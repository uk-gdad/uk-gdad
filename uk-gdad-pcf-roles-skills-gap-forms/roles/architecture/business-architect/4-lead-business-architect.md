# Lead business architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead business architect in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent
it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would
close them.

Lead is the top of this role. The framework makes you accountable for the
outputs of the business architecture team, directing how architectural
principles, methods and tools are applied, forming a view of the whole
enterprise that executives can actually use, providing the overall direction and
definition of business architecture in support of strategy, and building a
strong architecture capability through coaching, mentoring and training.

Two of those are worth pausing on. *Accountable for outputs produced by the
team* means the quality of work you did not write is yours. And *develop and
maintain a strong capability* is a standing job, not a project: capability
decays when people leave, and nobody sends a reminder.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the eight skills gets an explanation, a rating and some
questions. Expect about 45 minutes, and expect the useful answers to be the
uncomfortable ones.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job
  description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed

## About this role level

**The role: business architect.** A business architect develops an integrated
view of an enterprise and uses it to help the organisation put its strategy in
place — applying architectural principles and tools to business challenges,
aligning project and programme design with strategy, making sure business change
is implemented effectively, aggregating and disaggregating complex information,
and communicating it to stakeholders.

**Your level: lead business architect.** At this level you use expert knowledge
and experience to direct work on high-level, complex business scenarios, and you
support and lead other business architects. The framework expects that you:

- are accountable for outputs produced by the business architecture team
- lead and direct the application of architectural principles, methods and tools
  to business scenarios
- use architectural methods to form a view of the whole enterprise in a way that
  is easily understood by stakeholders
- work with stakeholders at executive levels
- provide overall direction, guidance and definition of business architecture to
  support strategy
- develop and maintain a strong business architecture capability through
  coaching, mentoring and training

**There is no level above this one.** The business architect role has four levels
and this is the highest. Where people go next is a question about other roles and
professions in the framework — enterprise architecture, strategy, or leadership
outside the profession — and the framework does not define that as a next step
for this role, so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Trainee business architect
2. Associate business architect
3. Business architect
4. **Lead business architect** — you are here

Four skills are worded exactly as they are at business architect: communicating
information, consultancy, making and guiding decisions, and strategic thinking.
Four are yours alone: business architecture at organisational maturity level,
methods and tools as direction-setting, stakeholder relationship strategy, and
the definition rather than support of principles and roadmaps. Where you sit
against those four is the real subject of this form.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Business architecture
2. Communicating information
3. Consultancy
4. Making and guiding decisions
5. Methods and tools
6. Stakeholder relationship management
7. Strategic design and business change
8. Strategic thinking

## Job skills

Eight skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the framework
expects, what that looks like in practice, and where it differs from business
architect.

### Skill: Business architecture

**What this means.** Making the discipline itself work in your organisation:
simple enough to act on, mature enough to survive turnover, and pointed at the
gaps between strategy and reality.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: simplify complex
business architecture frameworks into clear actions; develop business
architecture maturity in your organisation, such as through a community of
practice; lead or guide others in using best practice, such as industry
standards; and identify strategic misalignment within the organisation and make
recommendations for improvement.

**What that looks like here.** People act on your architecture without needing it
translated. There is a community of practice, or something like it, that exists
because you built it. You have told the organisation where its strategy and its
structure disagree — and said what to do about it.

**Different from business architect**, where the skill is applying and developing
frameworks and producing views, rather than maturing the discipline and naming
misalignment.

### Skill: Communicating information

professional community rather than yourself.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: mediate between

manage stakeholder expectations and host or moderate discussions about high risk

**What that looks like here.** You have repaired a relationship between
directorates. You run high-stakes conversations on deadlines. You speak for
architecture where architects are not in the room.

### Skill: Consultancy

**What this means.** Advising from deep expertise, and shaping how the whole
organisation approaches its work.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: provide advice and
recommendations to stakeholders based on your significant specialist knowledge
and experience; and lead the definition of guidance and inform how the
organisation approaches delivery or uses digital and data skills more
effectively.

**What that looks like here.** Your advice is sought before decisions are framed,
not after. The guidance the organisation works to is guidance you led. You have
changed how a programme works, not just what it chose.

**Same wording as business architect**, and there is no level above.

### Skill: Making and guiding decisions

**What this means.** Making consequential decisions, and getting independent
parties to agree where none of them has to.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: make decisions
characterised by medium levels of risk and complexity, and recommend decisions as
risk and complexity increase; and build consensus between services or
independent stakeholders.

**What that looks like here.** You decide within your remit, recommend beyond it,
and write the reasoning down where others can find it. You have got services with
different incentives to agree and hold to it.

**Same wording as business architect**, and there is no level above. What changes
is that you are accountable for your team's decisions as well as your own.

### Skill: Methods and tools

**What this means.** Setting the direction for how architecture is done, and
giving other people the standing to advise on it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: set direction and give
others the means and authority to advise on the best methodologies and tools for
the work, ensuring they are adopted consistently; and assess the effectiveness of
different approaches — such as Agile or plan-driven — and recommend tools to
support organisational collaboration and build capability.

**What that looks like here.** Architects other than you advise on method with
your backing, and people accept it. You have judged where an Agile approach fits
and where a plan-driven one does, on evidence rather than fashion. Tool choices
are made for collaboration, not for the architects' convenience.

**Different from business architect**, where the skill is building capability in
methods rather than setting direction and delegating the authority to advise.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What this means.** Directing how your teams build and hold relationships, and
negotiating at senior level where progress is blocked.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: direct the
stakeholder relationship strategy for your teams; ensure stakeholders' objectives
are set and support teams to meet them; and influence and negotiate with senior
stakeholders to resolve issues and enable progress.

**What that looks like here.** There is a deliberate relationship strategy rather
than a set of individual habits. Your architects know what their stakeholders are
trying to achieve, because someone made sure those objectives were stated. You
have unblocked something by negotiating at a level your team could not reach.

**Different from business architect**, where the skill is working with the team on
understanding and communications rather than directing the strategy and
negotiating at senior level.

### Skill: Strategic design and business change

**What this means.** Defining what the organisation's change should be guided by
— the principles, patterns, standards, policies, roadmaps and vision — and
keeping them alive as things move.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: demonstrate a strong
understanding of business issues, events and activities and their short to long
term impact; define principles, patterns, standards, policies, roadmaps and
vision statements; effectively focus on outcomes rather than solutions and
activities; and develop, maintain or update strategy in response to feedback and
findings.

**What that looks like here.** The principles your organisation designs to are
ones you wrote. There is a roadmap that people act on. When something is
learned, the strategy actually changes rather than the learning being noted.

**Different from business architect**, which *supports* defining these and *helps*
update strategy. Yours is the definition.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What this means.** Setting the direction other people's work follows, and
checking whether it is delivering.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: define strategies and
policies, providing guidance to others on working in the strategic context; and
evaluate current strategies to ensure business requirements are being met and
exceeded where possible.

**What that looks like here.** There is a strategy, you defined it, and people
outside architecture can describe it. Evaluation happens on a rhythm rather than
when someone asks. You have stopped something because the evaluation said so.

**Same wording as business architect**, and there is no level above.

## Legal compliance

Architecture decisions carry legal weight — what data services hold, how
accessible they are, how technology is bought, and how organisational change
affects people. At this level your assurance may be what the organisation relies
on. These questions ask about awareness, training and support, not about whether
you can state the law correctly, and not as a check on your conduct.
