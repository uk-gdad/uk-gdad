# Network architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a network architect in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, what you have not had the chance to do, and
what support would be most useful over the coming months.

At this level you own network designs and specifications that support business
strategy. The framework expects you to research new technologies and ensure
appropriate levels of assurance, to plan, direct and co-ordinate activities and
engage stakeholders to implement a programme, to agree service level agreements
and engage with third party vendors, to understand most of the network
technologies in the role's list, to coach associates, and to work with engineers
so that what you design can actually be run.

Agreeing service levels and dealing with vendors is the part that surprises
people arriving from engineering. It is negotiation, and the commitments you make
are hard to unwind.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Each of the five skills gets an
explanation, a note on what changes at lead, a rating and some questions. Expect
about 35 minutes.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.

  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |

| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**. A certification without recent
  hands-on work is a 1, however hard it was to get.
- **There is no target rating.** Network architects commonly sit at 3 in the
  technologies their estate uses and 0 or 1 in the ones it does not. That
  pattern is information, not a shortfall.

## About this role level

**The role: network architect.** A network architect is responsible for network
designs and specifications, including cloud networks.

**Your level: network architect.** At this level you have responsibility for
network designs and specifications that support business strategies, using common
tools. The framework expects that you:

- research new technologies and solutions, and ensure appropriate levels of
  assurance
- plan, direct and co-ordinate activities, engaging with other stakeholders to
  manage and implement a programme
- agree service level agreements and engage with third party vendors
- understand most of the network technologies in the role's list
- coach and mentor associate network architects where appropriate
- collaborate with engineers to ensure systems are practically designed

**Where this level sits.** The network architect role has three levels:

1. Associate network architect
2. **Network architect** — you are here
3. Lead network architect

Your step up is lead network architect. Three of the five skills change there:
governance becomes ensuring proportionate assurance is in place and continuously
improved and guiding teams through it; IT infrastructure becomes leading teams
and departments rather than designing yourself; and systems design rises from
medium to *high* risk, adds controlling design practice within an enterprise or
industry architecture, influencing industry models, and developing implementation
and procurement strategies. Systems integration and troubleshooting keep their
wording.

**The framework's breadth expectation.** Associates are expected to know one or
two of the role's network technologies, you are expected to know most of them,
and a lead all of them. There is a checklist below.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Governance and assurance
2. IT infrastructure
3. Systems design
4. Systems integration
5. Troubleshooting and problem resolution

## Job skills

Five skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at lead.

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What this means.** The processes that check designs before they become
expensive mistakes — and, at this level, improving those processes rather than
only passing through them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: develop appropriate
governance and assurance processes; identify and recommend improvements to them;
and support others in following or applying them.

**What that looks like here.** You have written or reshaped a review process
people actually use. You can say which assurance steps catch real problems and
which are ritual. You help colleagues get through governance rather than watching
them struggle.

**The next step.** At lead this becomes ensuring appropriate and *proportionate*
governance is in place and continuously improved, aligned to organisational and
government needs; guiding teams through processes such as service assessments; and
sharing your team's processes so stakeholders trust them.

### Skill: IT infrastructure

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of the kit that carries everything —
networks, hardware, software, cloud — and making it real.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design and implement
infrastructure solutions and services: networking and physical infrastructure,
commercial off-the-shelf software, open source packages, and virtual or cloud
computing.

**What that looks like here.** You decide the shape and then see it built. You
know what your estate costs to run, not only what it does. You can design for
both a physical data centre and a cloud provider without pretending they are the
same problem.

**The next step.** At lead you *lead teams and departments* in design,
implementation, administration and support — from doing it to running the
function that does it.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of systems at real risk, choosing the
standards by which such decisions are made, and reviewing other people's work.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: design systems
characterised by medium levels of risk, impact and business or technical
complexity; select appropriate design standards, methods and tools and ensure
they are applied effectively; and review the systems designs of others for
appropriate technology, efficient use of resources and integration across
multiple systems.

**What that looks like here.** You have designed something where being wrong
would have been expensive. You chose the standards your team designs to. You
review other people's designs and change their direction without simply
overruling them.

**The next step.** At lead this rises considerably: high levels of risk and
complexity, controlling design practice within an enterprise or industry
architecture, influencing industry-based models, developing implementation *and
procurement* strategies, and ensuring adherence to technical strategies and
policies.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Making separate systems work together — defining the build,
co-ordinating it across teams, and testing the seams. In networking, the seams
are usually where the outages are.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: define the
integration build; co-ordinate build activities across systems; and understand
how to undertake and support integration testing activities.

**What that looks like here.** You own the sequence when several teams have to
change things in the right order. You have co-ordinated a migration or a cutover
where the rollback plan mattered. You test the failure paths rather than assuming
them.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead network architect.

### Skill: Troubleshooting and problem resolution

**What this means.** Finding what is actually wrong across several technologies,
and getting to the cause rather than the symptom.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: break a problem down
into its component parts to identify and diagnose root causes; and troubleshoot
and identify problems across different technology capabilities.

**What that looks like here.** You decompose rather than guess. You can follow a
fault from application to network to platform to supplier. You have proved a
cause rather than asserting one, including when the answer was that it *was* the
network.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead network architect.
