# Lead network architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead network architect in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

Lead is the top of this role. The framework has you accountable for the designs
and networks representing your business area: being a point of escalation,
inputting into the governance forums that set IT strategy, cascading standards
and best practice from other architecture teams and design authorities into your
own, acting as subject matter expert, representing network architects internally
and externally, understanding *all* the network technologies in the role's list,
championing new technologies, coaching your architects, and communicating network
concepts to technical and non-technical people alike.

Two of those are easy to let slide and hard to recover: cascading what other
architecture teams decide into your team, and representing your architects
outside the organisation. Both are relationship work, and both stop happening the
moment escalations get heavy.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Each of the five skills gets an
explanation, a rating and some questions. Expect about 40 minutes.

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send
from here — your answers never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your
organisation uses for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so
answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"The team does this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer** at this level too.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you were expert in when
  you last had time to build something.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you and would stay changed if you left. Several are reasonable
  here.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Systems design**
>

>
> *Evidence:* I sign off high-risk designs and I set our standards. Procurement
> strategy is done by commercial with me consulted late, and influencing
> industry models is not something I have ever attempted.

Named practice, and a clear line about where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: network architect.** A network architect is responsible for network
designs and specifications, including cloud networks.

**Your level: lead network architect.** At this level you are accountable for
designs and networks representing your business area. The framework expects that
you:

- are a point of escalation
- actively input into governance forums that determine IT strategy
- cascade messages, standards and best practice from other architecture teams and
  design authorities to the network architecture team
- act as a subject matter expert for network architects
- represent network architects internally and externally
- understand all the network technologies in the role's list
- champion new technologies and solutions
- coach and mentor network architects and associate network architects
- are a strategic thinker who can communicate network concepts to technical and
  non-technical stakeholders

**There is no level above this one.** The network architect role has three levels
and this is the highest. Where people go next is a question about other roles —
enterprise or solution architecture, infrastructure leadership, or leadership
outside the profession — and the framework does not define it as a next step, so
this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Associate network architect
2. Network architect
3. **Lead network architect** — you are here

Three skills are yours alone rather than shared with the level below. Governance
becomes ensuring assurance is proportionate and continuously improved, guiding
teams through processes such as service assessments, and making your processes
transparent enough that stakeholders trust them. IT infrastructure becomes
leading teams and departments rather than designing personally. And systems design
rises to high risk and complexity, controlling design practice within an
enterprise or industry architecture, influencing industry-based models, and
developing implementation and *procurement* strategies.

**The framework's breadth expectation.** Your level is expected to understand
*all* of the role's network technologies. There is a checklist below. Very few
people can honestly tick every box, and saying which are thin is more useful than
claiming them.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Governance and assurance
2. IT infrastructure
3. Systems design
4. Systems integration
5. Troubleshooting and problem resolution

## Job skills

Five skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the framework
expects, what it looks like in practice, and where it differs from the level
below.

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What this means.** Making sure the checks that protect against expensive
mistakes are the right size, work, and are trusted.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: ensure appropriate
and proportionate governance and assurance is in place and continuously improved,
aligned to organisational and government needs; guide teams through governance
and assurance processes, such as service assessments, so standards are met; and
share your team's governance and assurance processes so stakeholders trust them
to deliver value in a sustainable way.

**What that looks like here.** Your assurance is sized to the risk rather than
applied uniformly. You get teams *through* assessments rather than sending them
in unprepared. Other people can see how your process works, which is what turns
governance from an obstacle into something colleagues use.

**Different from network architect**, which develops processes and recommends
improvements rather than owning proportionality and building trust in them.

### Skill: IT infrastructure

**What this means.** Leading the function that designs, builds, runs and supports
the kit — rather than doing it yourself.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you lead teams and departments
in the design, implementation, administration and support of infrastructure
solutions and services.

**What that looks like here.** You lead people who design rather than producing
designs. You know your estate's cost, risk and end-of-life profile. You can tell
when a technical answer from your own team is convenient rather than true — which
is what keeps you useful once you stop building.

**Different from network architect**, which designs and implements personally.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** The highest-risk design work, the standards by which design
is done across the organisation, and how technology gets bought.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: design systems
characterised by high levels of risk, impact and business or technical
complexity; control system design practice within an enterprise or industry
architecture; influence industry-based models for the development of new
technology applications; develop effective implementation and procurement
strategies consistent with business needs; and ensure adherence to relevant
technical strategies, policies, standards and practices.

**What that looks like here.** You own designs where being wrong would be public.
Design practice across your area follows an architecture you help control. You
shape how network technology is bought, not only how it is configured — and you
know which contracts your successors will be stuck with.

**Different from network architect**, which designs at medium risk and selects
standards for a team rather than controlling practice and shaping procurement.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Defining and co-ordinating how systems come together, and
testing the seams where outages start.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: define the
integration build; co-ordinate build activities across systems; and understand
how to undertake and support integration testing activities.

**What that looks like here.** You own the sequencing when several teams and
suppliers must change things in order. You have run a cutover where the rollback
plan was real. You know which integrations in your estate have never been tested
under failure.

**Same wording as network architect**, and there is no level above. What grows is
the size and consequence of what you co-ordinate.

### Skill: Troubleshooting and problem resolution

**What this means.** Getting to causes across technologies — and being the person
called when nobody else can.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: break a problem down
into its component parts to identify and diagnose root causes; and troubleshoot
and identify problems across different technology capabilities.

**What that looks like here.** You are brought into faults that have already
defeated other people, and you are useful without taking over. What you have
learned across years is written down somewhere other people can reach. You know
which recurring problems are structural rather than technical.

**Same wording as network architect**, and there is no level above.
