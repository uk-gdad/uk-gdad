# Associate network architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome to this form.

It is for you if you work as an associate network architect in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Someone in your organisation — human resources, or
your manager — has sent it to you so that you can say what you already do, what
you have not met yet, and what would help you most over the next few months.

Nothing here is scored, marked or graded, and nobody is comparing your answers
with anyone else's. Associate is a learning level: you work with network
architects on designs and specifications, with guidance. Gaps are expected.

One thing this role is unusually clear about is breadth. The framework expects an
associate to understand one or two network technologies, a network architect to
understand most of them, and a lead to understand all. There is a checklist below
so you can see where you stand — it is the most concrete progression measure in
the whole framework, and it is worth using honestly.

Most people take about half an hour. You can stop and come back.

## About this role level

**The role: network architect.** A network architect is responsible for network
designs and specifications, including cloud networks.

**Your level: associate network architect.** At this level you work with network
architects on designs and specifications, with guidance. The framework expects
that you:

- are aware and knowledgeable of new technology solutions, and produce network
  design policy
- draft recommendations for network support, create and maintain network plans
  and plan infrastructure runs
- ensure designs meet service level agreements, and develop an understanding of
  common tools
- understand one or two network technologies from the list below
- collaborate with engineers to make sure systems are practically designed

That last one is worth taking seriously. A design that cannot be built, patched
or recovered by the people who run it is not a good design, however elegant.

**Where this level sits.** The network architect role has three levels:

1. **Associate network architect** — you are here
2. Network architect
3. Lead network architect

Your next step is network architect. Every skill below changes there: governance
moves from following processes to developing them, infrastructure from building
to designing, systems integration from explaining to defining and co-ordinating,
and troubleshooting adds breaking problems down to root causes.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Governance and assurance
2. IT infrastructure
3. Systems design
4. Systems integration
5. Troubleshooting and problem resolution

## Job skills

Five skills, in the framework's order. Each one explains what the skill means,
what is expected of you now, what it looks like in real work, and what changes at
the next level.

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What this means.** The processes that check designs before they become
expensive mistakes — design authorities, security assurance, service assessments,
change approval.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: explain why governance
and assurance are important; and follow governance and assurance processes, under
supervision.

**What that looks like here.** You know which forums your designs have to pass
through and what each one is protecting against. You prepare what they need
rather than being chased for it. You can explain to a frustrated colleague why
the process exists.

**The next step.** At network architect you *develop* appropriate governance and
assurance processes, identify and recommend improvements to them, and support
others in following them.

### Skill: IT infrastructure

**What this means.** The physical and virtual kit that carries everything —
networks, hardware, off-the-shelf software, open source, and cloud services.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you build, configure,
administer and support infrastructure solutions and services — networking and
physical infrastructure, commercial off-the-shelf software, open source packages,
and virtual or cloud computing.

**What that looks like here.** You configure and support real kit rather than
only drawing it. You know what your infrastructure actually consists of, including
the parts nobody documents. You have fixed something you did not build.

**The next step.** At network architect this becomes *design and implement*
rather than build, configure and support — you move from working on the
infrastructure to deciding its shape.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Turning a logical intention into something that can actually
be built — the detail, the documentation, and the patterns.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: translate logical
designs into physical designs; produce detailed designs; document your work using
the required standards, methods and tools; design systems with managed levels of
risk, manageable complexity and meaningful impact; and work with well understood
technology, identifying appropriate patterns.

**What that looks like here.** You produce designs someone can build from without
ringing you. You reach for a known pattern rather than inventing one. Your
documentation matches what was actually built, which is rarer than it sounds.

**The next step.** At network architect the risk band rises to medium, you
*select* the design standards and methods rather than following them, and you
review other people's designs.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Making separate systems work together — and in networking,
that usually means change sequencing, dependencies, and what happens at the
boundaries between networks, suppliers, and organisations.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: explain the process and
principles of integrating systems; and describe the challenges of designing,
building and testing interfaces between systems.

**What that looks like here.** You can explain why two changes have to happen in
a particular order. You know which of your connections cross an organisational
boundary and who owns the other end. You understand why testing across a boundary
is harder than testing inside one.

**The next step.** At network architect this becomes defining the integration
build, co-ordinating build activities across systems, and understanding how to
undertake and support integration testing — a substantial jump from explaining to
running.

### Skill: Troubleshooting and problem resolution

**What this means.** Finding out what is actually wrong when a fault crosses
several technologies and nobody's monitoring shows the cause.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you troubleshoot and identify
problems across different technology capabilities.

**What that looks like here.** You can follow a problem from the application to
the network to the platform rather than stopping at your own boundary. You know
which tools tell you the truth and which are reassuring. You can say "it is not
the network" and be believed, because you have evidence.

**The next step.** At network architect this adds breaking a problem down into
its component parts to identify and diagnose root causes — from finding the fault
to explaining why it happened.

## Legal compliance

Network design decisions carry legal weight in ways that are easy to miss. Where
traffic goes decides where data is processed. Monitoring can capture personal
data and the content of communications. And network security sits inside a set of
government standards and duties.

These questions ask about **training and support**, not about whether you get the
law right.
