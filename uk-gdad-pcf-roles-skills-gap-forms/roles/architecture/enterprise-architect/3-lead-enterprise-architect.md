# Lead enterprise architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead enterprise architect in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent
it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would
close them.

The framework describes a lead enterprise architect as playing a high-level role
in ensuring the strategy is developed, agreed and followed: networking with
senior stakeholders across enterprises, supporting communities and teams, leading
teams of enterprise architects, horizon scanning for influences and risks,
supporting delivery of the long-term strategy, and taking a leading role in the
overall direction of business and digital capabilities.

Three of your skills turn outward at this level in a way they did not before. You
are expected to work with people *outside* your organisation to inform policies
and standards, to communicate and mediate *across* organisations, and — the
biggest change — to identify contractual frameworks and select suppliers.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Each of the eight skills gets an
explanation, a note on what changes at principal, a rating and some questions.
Expect about 45 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: enterprise architect.** Enterprise architects are leaders working
across different levels of an organisation to translate business strategy into
business change and technical delivery — owning the architecture vision, strategy
and roadmaps across business, technology and data, establishing principles,
policies and standards, developing the architecture community, and horizon
scanning across industry.

**Your level: lead enterprise architect.** At this level you play a high-level
role in ensuring the strategy is developed, agreed and followed. The framework
expects that you:

- network and communicate with senior stakeholders across enterprises, and seek
  opportunities for improvement
- support at least one community or team, or a combination of both
- find and use best practice and emerging technologies and approaches
- lead teams including enterprise architects and help them understand how to
  deliver the team's objectives to meet organisational goals
- horizon scan for influences or risks
- support successful delivery of the long-term strategy
- take a leading role in the overall direction of business and digital
  capabilities

**Where this level sits.** The enterprise architect role has four levels:

1. Enterprise architect
2. Senior enterprise architect
3. **Lead enterprise architect** — you are here
4. Principal enterprise architect

Your step up is principal enterprise architect, and only three skills change:
community collaboration becomes unblocking issues between teams or departments at
the highest level and coaching the organisation on team dynamics; enterprise
architecture becomes leading others who design architectures, working beyond the
organisation to influence structures and processes, and ensuring organisation-wide
change aligns with the architectural strategy; and making architectural decisions
rises to high risk and complexity, leading and evolving governance, and
representing architectural governance within wider governance such as legal or
commercial. The principal accountabilities also add responsibility for a Digital
Twin.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Enterprise architecture
6. Making architectural decisions
7. Problem definition and shaping
8. Strategy design

## Job skills

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What this means.** Looking beyond the organisation — at society, industry and
policy — and building architecture that survives what is coming.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: assess how trends in
society and industry practices might impact the organisation; work with people
outside your organisation to inform policies, strategies and standards;
anticipate changes to policy and build resilience through your architectural
work; and coach others in identifying important trends.

**What that looks like here.** You can name a societal or industry trend and say
what it would mean for your organisation's architecture in three years. You have
contributed to a policy or standard beyond your own organisation. Something you
designed absorbed a policy change that would otherwise have been expensive.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What this means.** Communicating and mediating across organisations, winning
support for high-risk decisions, and teaching others to do it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: communicate with
technical and non-technical stakeholders at all levels and across organisations,
using architecture communication techniques; mediate between people in difficult

coach and support others in architecture communication.

**What that looks like here.** You have mediated an architectural disagreement
where both parties had authority and neither had to concede. You have won backing
for something expensive and risky. Your architects present better because you
taught them, not because you rewrote their slides.

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What this means.** Choosing how and from whom your organisation buys — the
frameworks, the approaches, and the suppliers themselves.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things, and this is the largest
single jump in the role: identify appropriate contractual frameworks and
approaches; and identify, evaluate and select appropriate suppliers.

**What that looks like here.** You know which purchasing routes are available to
you and what each costs in time and flexibility. You have sat on an evaluation
and written criteria that discriminated between suppliers on things that actually
matter. You can tell when a requirement has been written around one product.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working across teams, giving feedback that lasts, and
dealing with the dynamics that stop work happening.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work collaboratively
in a group, actively networking with others; adapt feedback to ensure it is
effective and lasting; use your subject to identify problems or issues in the
team dynamic and rectify them; and identify issues through team health checks and
help stimulate the right responses.

**What that looks like here.** Your network reaches into other organisations, not
only other teams. Your feedback changes behaviour. You use architectural
conversations to surface problems that are really about how people work together.

**The next step.** At principal this becomes solving and unblocking issues
between teams or departments at the highest level, and coaching the organisation
on team dynamics and conflict resolution while building and growing the
community.

### Skill: Enterprise architecture

**What this means.** Designing how the organisation achieves its objectives
across technology, people and process — and making sure the design is used.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: design an architecture
that defines the technology, people, process and other elements needed to achieve
organisation objectives; and work with others across the organisation to ensure
your design is used to achieve those objectives.

**What that looks like here.** Your architecture describes an operating model,
not a system landscape. You stay with it into delivery, because a design nobody
follows is an opinion.

**The next step.** At principal you *lead others* designing architectures, work
beyond the organisation to influence structures, technology, people and
processes, and ensure organisation-wide changes align with the architectural
strategy.

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What this means.** Making and guiding consequential decisions, and using and
improving the governance that manages architectural risk.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: make and guide
architectural design decisions characterised by medium risk and complexity;
identify and address architectural risks that affect multiple teams or domains;
use architectural governance and assurance to make design decisions and manage
technical risks at the appropriate level; and contribute to the development of
architectural governance and assurance.

**What that looks like here.** You guide more decisions than you make. You can
name the cross-domain risks that nobody owns, and you have found owners for
some. You have improved governance rather than only complying with it.

**The next step.** At principal this rises to decisions of *high* risk and
complexity, risks across the organisation or wider government, leading and
evolving governance, and representing architectural governance as part of wider
governance such as legal or commercial.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What this means.** Leading the framing of the hardest problems, and teaching
others to frame their own.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: lead the framing of a
problem characterised by high complexity, complication or risk so that a solution
can be created; coach others in defining problems and describing appropriate
options for solutions; and help others challenge requirements and assumptions and
identify opportunities when defining problems and solution options.

**What that looks like here.** When something is a mess, you are the person asked
to state what the problem actually is. You coach rather than reframe on other
people's behalf. You teach people to challenge a requirement politely and
effectively.

**The next step.** Identical wording at principal enterprise architect.

### Skill: Strategy design

**What this means.** Defining and connecting strategy across the organisation and
beyond it, and clearing the way for it to happen.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: define and connect
strategies or visions across the organisation or wider government; and enable the
implementation of strategies or visions across the organisation or wider
government, for example by advocating for resources and removing blockers.

**What that looks like here.** You connect strategies that were written
separately and would otherwise conflict. You spend your credibility getting
resources moved and blockers removed — which is what "enable" means here, and it
is political work as much as architectural.

## Legal compliance

Enterprise architecture decisions carry legal weight, and at this level you
select suppliers — which brings procurement fairness and conflicts of interest
directly into your job. These questions ask about awareness, training and
support, not about whether you can state the law correctly, and not as a check on
your conduct.
