# Senior data architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior data architect in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. It has come from human resources or your manager,
and it exists to establish where the gap sits between what you do now and what
the organisation needs from someone at this level.

At senior the framework has you delivering the vision the chief data architect
sets: designing data models and metadata systems, helping interpret what the
organisation actually needs, overseeing and advising other data architects,
designing and supporting data dictionaries, making sure your teams work to the
organisation's standards, and working with technical architects so that systems
are built in line with the data architecture. In short, you are the layer where
data design meets everybody else's design decisions.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the ten skills gets an explanation, a note on what changes
at chief, a rating and some questions. Expect about 45 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: data architect.** A data architect sets the vision for the
organisation's use of data, through data design, so that data is managed
properly and meets the organisation's needs.

**Your level: senior data architect.** At this level you deliver the vision for
the organisation as set by the chief data architect. The framework expects that
you:

- design data models and metadata systems
- help chief data architects to interpret an organisation's needs
- provide oversight and advice to other data architects who are designing and
  producing data artefacts
- design and support the management of data dictionaries
- make sure that your teams are working to the standards set for the
  organisation by the chief data architects
- work with technical architects so that the organisation's systems are designed
  in accordance with the appropriate data architecture

The last one is where this job is won or lost. Systems get designed whether or
not the data architecture is in the room; being there early is the difference
between shaping a design and objecting to one.

**Where this level sits.** The data architect role has three levels:

1. Data architect
2. **Senior data architect** — you are here
3. Chief data architect

Your step up is chief data architect. Nearly every skill changes there, and the
direction is consistent: from doing it across your teams to deciding it for the
organisation and beyond. Data standards becomes deciding *where* standards need
to be set and how, in the wider context of government. Data modelling gains
cross-government reuse and the design of how models are categorised. Problem
management becomes anticipating problems and building problem-solving
capabilities in others. Data governance becomes making governance follow
organisational strategy and aligning it with wider governance such as budget.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating between the technical and non-technical
2. Communicating data
3. Data analysis and synthesis
4. Data governance (data architect)
5. Data innovation
6. Data modelling
7. Data standards
8. Metadata management
9. Problem management
10. Strategic thinking

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What this means.** Being understood by people who do not think in schemas, and
managing what senior people expect of the data work.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: listen to and
interpret the needs of technical and non-technical stakeholders and manage their
expectations; manage active and reactive communication; and support or host
difficult discussions within the team or with diverse senior stakeholders.

**What that looks like here.** You tell people about a data problem before they
find it. You have hosted a conversation where a director wanted something the
data could not support. You interpret a vague business need into a design
question without losing what was actually meant.

**The next step.** Identical wording at chief data architect.

### Skill: Communicating data

**What this means.** Choosing the right form for what you have to say about data
— a diagram, a paper, a demonstration, a number — and shaping it for whoever is
receiving it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: understand the
appropriate media to communicate findings; and shape communications for the
audience.

**What that looks like here.** You do not send the same artefact to a delivery
team and to a board. You know when a model diagram will lose a room and a single
example will not. Your material gets reused without you present.

**The next step.** At chief it becomes turning complex data into clear solutions
that can be acted on, sharing those communication skills across the team and
organisation, and communicating options together with their risks and
uncertainties.

### Skill: Data analysis and synthesis

**What this means.** Finding out what data actually contains rather than what
the documentation claims, and turning that into something colleagues can act on.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: undertake data
profiling and source system analysis; and present clear insights to colleagues to
support the end use of the data.

**What that looks like here.** You still profile personally, at least sometimes.
You know which of your sources are trustworthy and which are believed to be. You
present findings in terms of consequence rather than counts.

**The next step.** Identical wording at chief data architect — this is the one
skill the framework does not change anywhere in this role. What grows is scale
and consequence, not the description. Worth noticing: at chief you will still be
expected to be able to do this hands-on.

### Skill: Data governance (data architect)

**What this means.** Shaping the rules, roles and assurance that decide who may
hold what data, for what purpose, and who answers for it — and making sure data
gets considered in design at all.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: evolve and define data
governance; take responsibility for supporting and collaborating around wider
governance; assure and integrate data services to meet the needs of multiple
business services; and work proactively to ensure the organisation designs
architecture that considers data.

**What that looks like here.** Governance in your area has changed because you
changed it. You assure data services across more than one business area and can
see where they conflict. You get into design conversations before the data
questions become expensive.

**The next step.** At chief this becomes ensuring governance supports changes to
organisational strategy, aligning it with wider governance such as budget, and
assuring corporate services by understanding key risks and providing mitigation.

### Skill: Data innovation

**What this means.** Judging what emerging data tools and techniques would
actually mean for your organisation, rather than tracking them for interest.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you understand the impact on
the organisation of emerging trends in data tools, analysis techniques and data
usage.

**What that looks like here.** You can say what a trend would cost and require,
not just what it does. You have advised against something fashionable, or for
something unfashionable, with reasons.

**The next step.** At chief you identify areas of innovation and recognise the
appropriate *timing* for adoption — deciding not only whether but when.

### Skill: Data modelling

**What this means.** Representing information formally across the organisation,
choosing the right kind of model for the question, and reconciling models that
disagree.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: produce relevant data
models across multiple subject areas; explain which models to use for which
purpose; understand industry-recognised data modelling patterns and standards and
when to apply them; and compare and align different data models.

**What that looks like here.** You model in subject areas you did not start out
knowing. You can say why a conceptual model suits one conversation and a physical
one another. You have reconciled two models that described the same thing
differently — usually the hardest work in the job.

**The next step.** At chief it becomes recognising opportunities for reuse and
alignment across government and industry, and designing the method by which the
organisation categorises its models.

### Skill: Data standards

**What this means.** Creating the definitions, formats and policies that let data
be joined and trusted, getting them understood at senior level, and dealing with
breaches.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: create data standards
for different subjects and ensure senior leaders understand them; work with
subject matter experts across the organisation to introduce best practice;
monitor compliance with policies and standards across the organisation; and make
recommendations about how the organisation should resolve breaches.

**What that looks like here.** Standards exist because you wrote them, and
directors can explain why they matter. You know where compliance is real and
where it is claimed. When a standard is breached you propose what to do, rather
than only reporting it.

**The next step.** At chief you create standards for the organisation as a whole,
advocate for and oversee compliance, and decide *where* standards need to be set
and how to set them in the wider context of government.

### Skill: Metadata management

**What this means.** Designing and improving the systems that hold the data about
your data, and helping others use them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: design an appropriate
metadata repository; suggest changes to improve current repositories; understand
a range of tools for storing and working with metadata; and advise less
experienced colleagues about metadata management.

**What that looks like here.** You have designed a repository, or substantially
reshaped one, and can say what you optimised for. You know the tool landscape
well enough to say what yours is bad at. Junior colleagues come to you before
they make it worse.

**The next step.** At chief it becomes identifying how repositories can support
different areas of the organisation, communicating their value, and setting up
robust governance so they stay current.

### Skill: Problem management

**What this means.** Making sure recurring data problems get investigated,
resolved and prevented — and co-ordinating people to do it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: ensure that the right
actions are taken to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems; and co-ordinate
the team to investigate problems, implement solutions and take preventive
measures.

**What that looks like here.** Problems get worked on in the right order because
you decided the order. You co-ordinate rather than doing it all yourself. You
have anticipated a problem that had not happened yet and been believed.

**The next step.** At chief it becomes anticipating problems and defending
against them at the right time, understanding how a problem fits the larger
picture, helping others describe problems, and building problem-solving
capability in other people.

## Legal compliance

Data architecture sits closer to the law than almost any other technical role.
What data exists, how long it is kept, who it may be shared with and what may be
inferred from it are legal questions as much as design ones. At senior you are
often the person others assume has checked. These questions ask about awareness,
training and support — not about whether you can state the law correctly, and not
as a check on your conduct.
