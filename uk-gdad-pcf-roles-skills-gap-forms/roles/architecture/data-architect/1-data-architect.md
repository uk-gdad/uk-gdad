# Data architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a data architect in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, what you have not had the chance to do, and
what support would be most useful over the coming months.

Data architect is the first level of this role, but it is not a junior job. You
design and build the data models that meet the organisation's strategic data
needs, you guide the upgrade, management, decommissioning and archiving of data
in line with data policy, you feed the data dictionaries, and you define and
maintain the data technology architecture — metadata, integration, and the
business intelligence or warehouse layer. Real responsibility from day one.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the ten skills gets an explanation, a note on what changes
at senior, a rating and some questions. Expect about 45 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send
from here — your answers never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your
organisation uses for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so
answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Name a real model, system or standard** rather than describing what you
  generally do.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could pick up or did
  in a previous organisation.
- **There is no target rating.** Data architects commonly sit at 3 in modelling
  and metadata and at 1 in governance or innovation, simply because of what
  their organisation has asked for. That pattern is information, not a
  shortfall.

## About this role level

**The role: data architect.** A data architect sets the vision for the
organisation's use of data, through data design, so that data is managed
properly and meets the organisation's needs.

**Your level: data architect.** At this level you design and build data models
that fulfil the organisation's strategic data needs, as those needs are defined
by chief data architects. The framework expects that you:

- design, support and provide guidance for the upgrade, management,
  decommissioning and archiving of data in compliance with data policy
- provide input into data dictionaries
- define and maintain the data technology architecture, including metadata,
  integration and business intelligence or data warehouse architecture

Two of those are easy to under-weight. *Decommissioning and archiving* is where
most organisations are weakest and where the legal exposure is highest — data
kept because nobody decided to delete it. And *define and maintain* means the
architecture is a living thing you are answerable for, not a diagram produced
once.

**Where this level sits.** The data architect role has three levels:

1. **Data architect** — you are here
2. Senior data architect
3. Chief data architect

Your step up is senior data architect, where the pattern across nearly every
skill is the same: from working within the standards to shaping them, and from
your own models to overseeing other people's. Data governance makes the biggest
jump — from understanding what governance is required to evolving and defining
it, and working proactively so that the organisation's architecture considers
data at all.

One thing worth naming: the framework words *strategic thinking* at this level
in the same terms it uses for trainee roles elsewhere — explaining the strategic
context and supporting planning administratively. If you are already shaping
strategy, you are working above the published expectation, and that is worth
recording rather than assuming it is normal.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating between the technical and non-technical
2. Communicating data
3. Data analysis and synthesis
4. Data governance (data architect)
5. Data innovation
6. Data modelling

## Job skills

Ten skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What this means.** Being understood by people who do not think in schemas, and
understanding what they mean when they describe their work in their own terms.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: communicate
effectively with technical and non-technical stakeholders; support and host
discussions within a multidisciplinary team, including ones with difficult
dynamics; be an advocate for the team externally; and manage differing
stakeholder perspectives.

**What that looks like here.** You can explain why a field cannot simply be added
in terms of what it would cost the organisation, not in terms of normalisation.
You have run a meeting where two areas disagreed about what a term means. You
represent the data team to people who think of it as a bottleneck.

**The next step.** At senior it becomes listening to and interpreting needs and
managing expectations, managing active and reactive communication, and hosting
difficult discussions with *diverse senior stakeholders*.

### Skill: Communicating data

**What this means.** Presenting data — and facts about data — so that people can
use them, rather than so that they are technically complete.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: show an awareness that
data needs to be aligned to the needs of the end user; and create basic visuals
and presentations.

**What that looks like here.** Your diagrams get used in other people's decks.
You choose what to leave out. You can produce something a non-specialist can read
without you standing next to it.

**The next step.** At senior you understand which media suit which findings, and
you shape communications for the audience rather than producing one artefact for
everyone.

### Skill: Data analysis and synthesis

**What this means.** Looking at real data to find out what it actually contains,
rather than what the documentation claims — profiling, source system analysis,
and turning that into something colleagues can act on.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: undertake data
profiling and source system analysis; and present clear insights to colleagues to
support the end use of the data.

**What that looks like here.** You profile before you model. You have found the
field that is populated only 40% of the time, or the code list that grew three
undocumented values. You tell people what you found in terms of what it means for
their work.

**The next step.** The framework uses identical wording at senior *and* chief
data architect — this is the one skill that does not change anywhere in the role.
What grows is the scale and consequence of what you profile, not the description.

### Skill: Data governance (data architect)

**What this means.** The rules, roles and assurance that decide who may hold
what data, for how long, for what purpose, and who answers for it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: understand what data
governance is required; and take responsibility for the assurance of data
solutions, making recommendations to ensure compliance.

**What that looks like here.** You know which of your organisation's data has an
owner and which does not. You have reviewed a data solution and said what would
have to change for it to comply. You can tell the difference between a governance
requirement and a habit.

**The next step.** The largest jump at senior: evolving and defining data
governance, supporting wider governance, assuring and integrating data services
across multiple business services, and working proactively so the organisation
designs architecture that considers data in the first place.

### Skill: Data modelling

**What this means.** Representing information formally — entities, attributes,
relationships, keys — so that systems and people share a consistent
understanding of what things are.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: explain the concepts
and principles of data modelling; produce, maintain and update relevant data
models for the organisation's specific needs; and reverse-engineer data models
from a live system.

**What that looks like here.** You produce models people build from. You keep
them current rather than letting them drift. You can take an undocumented system
and derive what its data actually means — which is most of the job in a long-
lived estate.

**The next step.** At senior you model across *multiple subject areas*, explain
which model suits which purpose, apply industry-recognised patterns and
standards, and compare and align different models — including ones you did not
write.

### Skill: Data standards

**What this means.** The agreed definitions, formats, code lists and policies
that let data be joined, compared and trusted across an organisation — and
noticing when they are broken.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: use data policies,
processes and standards effectively; work with subject matter experts to develop
standards, policies and guidance to protect data; monitor compliance within a
team and act if needed; and analyse the impact if a standard is breached.

**What that looks like here.** You know which standards apply to your data and
follow them. You have worked with someone who knows the subject to write a
definition that survived contact with reality. You have traced what went wrong
when a standard was not followed.

**The next step.** At senior you *create* standards for different subjects and
make sure senior leaders understand them, work with experts across the whole
organisation, monitor compliance organisation-wide, and recommend how breaches
should be resolved.

### Skill: Metadata management

**What this means.** Keeping the data about your data — where it came from, what
it means, who owns it, what depends on it — accurate enough to answer questions
with.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: use metadata
repositories to complete complex tasks such as data and systems integration
impact analysis; and maintain a metadata repository so information stays accurate
and up to date.

**What that looks like here.** When someone asks "what breaks if we retire this
system", you can answer from the repository rather than from memory. The
repository is current because you keep it so, which is unglamorous and load-
bearing.

**The next step.** At senior you *design* an appropriate repository, suggest
improvements to existing ones, understand a range of metadata tools, and advise
less experienced colleagues.

### Skill: Problem management

**What this means.** Dealing with the underlying causes of recurring data
problems rather than the symptoms — investigating patterns, finding the
resolution, and preventing recurrence.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: initiate and monitor
actions to investigate patterns and trends to resolve problems; consult
specialists effectively where required; determine the appropriate resolution and
assist with its implementation; and determine preventative measures.

**What that looks like here.** You notice that the same reconciliation fails
every month and go after the cause. You know which specialist to call. You put
something in place so it stops happening rather than getting quicker at fixing
it.

**The next step.** At senior you ensure the right actions are taken to
investigate, resolve and *anticipate* problems, and you co-ordinate the team to
do it.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

commits to and how your models serve it. You have contributed material to a
planning round.

## Legal compliance

Data architecture sits closer to the law than almost any other technical role.
What data exists, how long it is kept, who it may be shared with and what may be
inferred from it are legal questions as much as design ones. These questions ask
about awareness, training and support — not about whether you can state the law
correctly, and not as a check on your conduct.
