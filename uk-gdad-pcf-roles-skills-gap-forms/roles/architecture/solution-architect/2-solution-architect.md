# Solution architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a solution architect in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, what you have not had the chance to do, and
what support would be most useful over the coming months.

At this level you are responsible for a single solution. The framework says you
usually work independently where risk is low, and often support or contribute to
work led by more senior solution architects — so your week is typically one thing
you own and several you are helping with.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the eight skills gets an explanation, a note on what changes
at senior, a rating and some questions. Expect about 40 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: solution architect.** A solution architect designs solutions for
problems that affect the organisation — defining the problem and outcomes,
scoping solutions to requirements, staying current on technology, understanding
organisational objectives and external drivers such as legislation or financial
constraints, working within constraints, designing and documenting solutions that
can actually be implemented, complying with standards and governance,
communicating with stakeholders, and managing risks and decisions transparently.

**Your level: solution architect.** At this level you are responsible for a
single solution, usually working independently where risk is low, and often
supporting work led by more senior architects. The framework expects that you:

- build relationships with stakeholders across different business or technical
  areas in the organisation
- are proactive in identifying opportunities to improve the organisation
- follow best practice for solution design
- use emerging technologies and approaches
- help your team achieve its objectives

**Where this level sits.** The solution architect role has five levels:

1. Associate solution architect
2. **Solution architect** — you are here
3. Senior solution architect
4. Lead solution architect
5. Principal solution architect

Your step up is senior solution architect, and it is narrower than you might
expect. Only three skills change: community collaboration moves to active
networking and lasting feedback; making architectural decisions moves from
working with others at managed risk to making and guiding decisions at medium
risk affecting multiple teams, and contributing to governance itself; and
technical design moves to medium risk with the addition of maintaining
architectural coherence in response to change. The rest of the step is in the
accountabilities — more complex solutions, several projects at once, and
supporting others to follow good practice.

**How this role relates to enterprise architect.** Seven of your eight skills are
worded as they are for enterprise architects. The published difference is the
eighth: yours is *technical design throughout the life cycle*, theirs is
*enterprise architecture*. You stay with a solution while it is built; they
design how the whole organisation achieves its objectives.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Making architectural decisions
6. Problem definition and shaping
7. Strategy design
8. Technical design throughout the life cycle

## Job skills

Eight skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at senior.

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What this means.** Making sure your solution fits with everything else that is
happening — other architects' work, other teams' delivery, and how technology and
policy are moving.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: align your work with
the work being done by other architects and technical professionals; track
emerging issues, strategies, roadmaps, patterns and technologies over time to
assess opportunities and risks to your work; and identify how other teams
contribute to delivering outcomes through change.

**What that looks like here.** You know what the other architects are designing
and where your solution touches theirs. You track things over time rather than
checking once. You can name the teams whose delivery your outcome depends on.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior solution architect. It changes at
lead, where it becomes supporting wider organisational objectives beyond your own
goals, tracking issues affecting teams across the organisation, and influencing
colleagues across it.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What this means.** Leading the difficult architectural conversations, and
making the message fit the audience.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: lead the communication
of complicated, complex or risky architecture topics with technical and
non-technical stakeholders; communicate with senior stakeholders across your
organisation; adapt your message and communication techniques to your audience;
advocate on behalf of a team to other stakeholders; and manage stakeholder
expectations effectively.

**What that looks like here.** You lead the conversation about the risky part of
your design rather than hoping nobody asks. You can present it to a delivery team
and to a director and have both act. You advocate for your team where they are
not present.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior. It changes at lead: communicating
across organisations, mediating in difficult discussions, gaining support for
high-risk topics, and coaching others.

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What this means.** Knowing how your organisation buys, because a solution
design usually becomes a purchase and a contract.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: understand commercial
processes and the appropriate internal contacts within a government department;
and understand different sourcing strategies and when to apply them.

**What that looks like here.** You involve commercial colleagues before your
design assumes a product. You can explain the difference between building,
buying, and reusing something the organisation already has — and when each makes
sense. You know what your existing contracts allow.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior. It changes considerably at lead:
identifying appropriate contractual frameworks and approaches, and identifying,
evaluating and selecting suppliers.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working well with the people around you, and making the
conditions in which good work happens.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: contribute to the work
of others; motivate and empower teams; create the right environment for teams to
work in and identify the best team makeup for the situation; and recognise and
deal with issues.

**What that looks like here.** You make other people's work better rather than
only producing your own. You know which mix of people a piece of work needs. You
say something when a team is not working well, rather than designing around it.

**The next step.** At senior the wording shifts to active networking, adapting
feedback so it lasts, using your subject to identify problems in team dynamics,
and stimulating the right responses to issues found in health checks.

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What this means.** Making choices that are hard to reverse, seeing the risks
they carry, and working within governance.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: work with others to
make architectural design decisions characterised by managed levels of risk and
complexity; identify and address architectural risks relevant to your team or
domain — business, data or security, for example; and engage with architectural
governance and assurance to manage decisions and risks, with support.

**What that looks like here.** You decide with others, and the reasoning is
written down where the next person will find it. You can name the architectural
risks in your solution. You take things to governance before they are
irreversible rather than after.

**The next step.** At senior you *make and guide* decisions at medium risk,
address risks affecting multiple teams or domains, use governance without
support, and contribute to developing that governance.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What this means.** Framing the problem so a solution can be built, and setting
out options in a way that lets people choose how to deliver.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: frame a problem
characterised by medium complexity, complication or risk so that a solution can
be created; produce architectural representations that enable different teams to
have a shared understanding of problems throughout the life cycle; and describe
options for solving problems so that appropriate delivery methods can be decided.

**What that looks like here.** Your framing survives contact with the teams who
have to act on it. Your diagrams get used by people who were not in the room. Your
options say what each would mean for how the work is delivered, not only for the
technology.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior. It changes at lead: leading the
framing of *high* complexity problems, coaching others in defining problems and
options, and helping others challenge requirements and assumptions.

### Skill: Strategy design

**What this means.** Supporting the direction the organisation is setting, and
using the principles and patterns that carry it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: support the
development of a strategy or vision that aligns with organisational objectives;
challenge requirements and assumptions and identify opportunities to develop
strategy; support the implementation of a strategy or vision, for example through
a roadmap or plan; and use architectural principles, patterns and constraints
when appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You have contributed to a strategy rather than
only working under one. You ask the question that exposes an untested assumption.
You use the principles that exist rather than reinventing them per solution.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior. It changes at lead: *defining*
strategies or visions across teams, directing implementation through roadmaps,
defining architectural principles and patterns, and maintaining strategy in
response to feedback.

### Skill: Technical design throughout the life cycle

**What this means.** Designing the technical solution and staying with it —
guiding the teams building it and adapting the design when reality disagrees.
This is the skill that distinguishes this role from enterprise architecture.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: create technical
designs characterised by managed levels of risk, impact and complexity; provide
guidance and support to teams using technical designs throughout the life cycle;
adapt a technical design if needed during delivery; and work with well-understood
technology, identifying appropriate patterns.

**What that looks like here.** Your design gets built. You are still involved in
month three. When an assumption turns out to be wrong, you change the design
rather than defending it — and you record why, so nobody re-litigates it later.

**The next step.** At senior the risk band rises to medium, and two things are
added: maintaining appropriate quality and architectural coherence in response to
change, and using feedback to optimise and refine designs throughout the life
cycle.

## Legal compliance

it is accessible, how it is bought, and what records it creates. These questions
ask about awareness, training and support, not about whether you can state the
law correctly, and not as a check on your conduct.
