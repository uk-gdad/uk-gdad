# Senior solution architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior solution architect in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. It has come from human resources or your
manager, and it exists to establish where the gap sits between what you do now
and what the organisation needs from someone at this level.

The framework describes a senior solution architect as responsible for a single,
more complex solution, usually where the risk is moderate, and possibly leading
and coaching other solution architects. You build relationships with senior
stakeholders, identify improvement opportunities proactively, support several
architecture projects, find and use emerging technologies, and support others to
follow good practice.

The phrase that changes most at this level is in technical design: you now
maintain *architectural coherence in response to change*. Solutions do not fail
because the first design was wrong; they fail because forty small changes were
each individually reasonable.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Each of the eight skills gets an
explanation, a note on what changes at lead, a rating and some questions. Expect
about 40 minutes.

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could pick up.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed

## About this role level

**The role: solution architect.** A solution architect designs solutions for
problems that affect the organisation — defining the problem and outcomes,
scoping to requirements, working within constraints such as legislation and
finance, designing and documenting solutions that can be implemented, complying
with standards and governance, and managing risks and decisions transparently.

**Your level: senior solution architect.** At this level you are responsible for
a single, more complex solution, usually where risk is moderate, and you may lead
and coach other solution architects. The framework expects that you:

- build relationships with senior stakeholders across different business or
  technical areas
- are proactive in identifying opportunities to improve the organisation
- support multiple architecture projects
- find and use emerging technologies and approaches
- support others to follow best practice for solution architecture

**Where this level sits.** The solution architect role has five levels:

1. Associate solution architect
2. Solution architect
3. **Senior solution architect** — you are here
4. Lead solution architect
5. Principal solution architect

Your step up is lead solution architect, and six of the eight skills change
there. Commercial perspective moves furthest — from understanding sourcing to
identifying contractual frameworks and selecting suppliers. Architecture
communication adds working across organisations, mediating and coaching. Problem
definition moves to leading the framing of high-complexity problems and coaching
others. Strategy design moves from supporting to defining strategies and
principles. Technical design rises to high risk and adds leading others and
refining the *standards* for design. And architect for the whole context turns
outward to organisation-wide objectives.

**How this role relates to enterprise architect.** Seven of your eight skills are
worded as they are for enterprise architects. The published difference is the
eighth: yours is technical design throughout the life cycle; theirs is enterprise
architecture.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Making architectural decisions
6. Problem definition and shaping
7. Strategy design
8. Technical design throughout the life cycle

## Job skills

Eight skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at lead.

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What this means.** Making sure your solution fits with everything else in
motion — other architects, other teams, and how technology and policy are moving.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: align your work with
the work being done by other architects and technical professionals; track
emerging issues, strategies, roadmaps, patterns and technologies over time to
assess opportunities and risks; and identify how other teams contribute to
delivering outcomes through change.

**What that looks like here.** You know where your solution touches others' and
have agreed the boundaries deliberately. You track things over time rather than
sampling them. You can name the teams whose delivery your outcome depends on, and
what you do when theirs slips.

**The next step.** At lead this turns outward: supporting wider organisational
objectives beyond your immediate goals, tracking issues that could affect teams
across the organisation, and influencing colleagues across it to solve or
mitigate problems.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What this means.** Leading the difficult architectural conversations and making
the message fit the audience.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: lead the communication
of complicated, complex or risky architecture topics with technical and
non-technical stakeholders; communicate with senior stakeholders across your
organisation; adapt your message and communication techniques to your audience;
advocate on behalf of a team to other stakeholders; and manage stakeholder
expectations effectively.

**What that looks like here.** You raise the risky part of the design yourself
rather than waiting to be asked. You can hold a senior audience without either
oversimplifying or drowning them. You advocate for your teams in rooms they are
not in.

**The next step.** At lead it becomes communicating *across organisations* using
architecture communication techniques, mediating in difficult architectural
discussions, gaining support for topics of high risk and complexity, and coaching
others in it.

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What this means.** Knowing how your organisation buys, because solutions become
purchases and contracts that outlast them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: understand commercial
processes and the appropriate internal contacts within a government department;
and understand different sourcing strategies and when to apply them. This is the
same wording as the two levels below — the framework does not raise it until
lead.

**What that looks like here.** You involve commercial colleagues before a design
assumes a product. You know what your contracts allow, and what a change would
cost. You can explain a sourcing choice in terms of risk and flexibility rather
than preference.

**The next step.** The largest change at lead: identifying appropriate
contractual frameworks and approaches, and identifying, evaluating and selecting
suppliers. If lead is where you are heading, this skill has the furthest to
travel and you cannot practise it alone.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working across teams, giving feedback that lasts, and
noticing when the way people work together is the real problem.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work collaboratively
in a group, actively networking with others; adapt feedback to ensure it is
effective and lasting; use your subject to identify problems or issues in the
team dynamic and rectify them; and identify issues through team health checks and
help stimulate the right responses.

**What that looks like here.** Your network reaches beyond the people you must
work with. Your feedback changes what someone does next time. You have used an
architectural conversation to surface a problem that was really about how a team
worked.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead solution architect. It changes at
principal: unblocking issues between teams or departments at the highest level,
and coaching the organisation on team dynamics while building the community.

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What this means.** Making and guiding consequential decisions, and using
governance to manage risk at the right level.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: make and guide
architectural design decisions characterised by medium risk and complexity;
identify and address architectural risks that affect multiple teams or domains;
use architectural governance and assurance to make design decisions and manage
technical risks at the appropriate level; and contribute to the development of
architectural governance and assurance.

**What that looks like here.** You decide and you help others decide. You can
name the risks that cross team boundaries, which are usually the ones nobody
owns. You have suggested an improvement to how governance works rather than only
complying with it.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead. It changes at principal: high risk
and complexity, risks across the organisation or wider government, leading and
evolving governance, and representing architectural governance within wider
governance such as legal or commercial.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What this means.** Framing problems so solutions can be built, representing
them so teams share an understanding, and setting out real options.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: frame a problem
characterised by medium complexity, complication or risk so a solution can be
created; produce architectural representations that enable different teams to
have a shared understanding throughout the life cycle; and describe options so
appropriate delivery methods can be decided.

**What that looks like here.** Your framing survives contact with delivery. Your
representations stay useful through the life cycle rather than being drawn once
for approval. Your options include what each would mean for how work is
delivered.

**The next step.** At lead you *lead* the framing of high-complexity problems,
coach others in defining problems and describing options, and help others
challenge requirements and assumptions.

### Skill: Strategy design

**What this means.** Supporting the direction the organisation sets, and using
the principles and patterns that carry it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: support the
development of a strategy or vision aligned with organisational objectives;
challenge requirements and assumptions, and identify opportunities to develop
strategy; support implementation, for example through a roadmap or plan; and use
architectural principles, patterns and constraints when appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You have contributed to a strategy rather than
only working under one. You ask the question that exposes an untested assumption.
You apply the principles that exist and say when one no longer fits.

**The next step.** At lead you *define* strategies or visions across teams,
direct implementation through roadmaps and plans, define architectural principles
and patterns, and maintain strategy in response to feedback.

### Skill: Technical design throughout the life cycle

**What this means.** Designing the solution, staying with it, and keeping it
coherent as it changes — which is the part that decides whether it is still
maintainable in three years.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: create technical
designs characterised by medium risk, impact and complexity; maintain appropriate
quality and architectural coherence of a technical design in response to change;
and use feedback to optimise and refine technical designs throughout the life
cycle.

**What that looks like here.** You hold the shape of the design while forty
reasonable-sounding changes arrive. You can say which changes you accepted and
which you refused, and why. You use what delivery teaches you rather than
defending the original drawing.

**The next step.** At lead the risk band rises to high, and you lead and guide
others in creating designs, and use feedback to refine the *standards* for design
rather than individual designs.

it is accessible, how it is bought, and what records it creates. These questions
ask about awareness, training and support, not about whether you can state the
law correctly, and not as a check on your conduct.
