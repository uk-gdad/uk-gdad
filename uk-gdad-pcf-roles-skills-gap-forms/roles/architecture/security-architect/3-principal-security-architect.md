# Principal security architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a principal security architect in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent
it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would
close them.

Principal is the top of this role. The framework has you working on services of
high complexity and risk and making the decisions that let the business achieve
what it needs: setting long-term strategy across the breadth of the organisation,
defining the vision, principles and strategy for security architects,
recommending security design across several projects or technologies up to
organisational and inter-organisational level, holding a deep and *evolving*
level of technical expertise so you can act as an exemplar, making and
influencing important business and architectural decisions, researching and
adopting new technologies and methodologies, solving unprecedented problems, and
furthering the profession inside and outside your organisation.

Two words in that list do the most work. *Evolving* — the framework explicitly
expects your technical depth to keep moving, not to rest on what made you senior.
And *unprecedented* — you are the person for problems with no known answer, which
is a different skill from being the person with the most answers.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Each of the seven skills gets an
explanation, a rating and some questions. Expect 45 minutes to an hour.

| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**. At this level the temptation is to
  rate the reputation; the framework's word *evolving* is the answer to that.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you and would stay changed if you left. Several are reasonable
  here; seven would be worth interrogating.

## About this role level

**The role: security architect.** A security architect designs and builds secure
solutions.

**Your level: principal security architect.** At this level you work on services
of high complexity and risk, making decisions that enable the business to achieve
its needs. The framework expects that you:

- work on projects with high strategic impact, setting a strategy usable in the
  long term and across the breadth of the organisation
- communicate with a broad range of senior stakeholders, and are responsible for
  defining the vision, principles and strategy for security architects
- recommend security design across several projects or technologies, up to
  organisational or inter-organisational level
- have a deep and evolving level of technical expertise, so you can act as an
  exemplar
- make and influence important business and architectural decisions
- research, identify, validate and adopt new technologies and methodologies
- are a recognised expert, demonstrating it by solving unprecedented issues and
  problems
- further the profession, demonstrating and sharing best practice within and
  outside the organisation

**There is no level above this one.** The security architect role has three
levels and this is the highest. Where people go next is a question about other
roles — chief information security officer, enterprise architecture, or
leadership outside the profession — and the framework does not define it as a
next step for this role, so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Security architect
2. Lead security architect
3. **Principal security architect** — you are here

Five skills are yours rather than shared with lead. Designing secure systems
becomes defining *and challenging* patterns and principles, creating precedents
and setting direction. Risk work becomes acting as the point of escalation and
being trusted by senior risk owners as an expert. Analysis adds watching the
technical environment to judge whether *previous decisions need revisiting* —
which is the hardest discipline in this job, because it means reopening things
you already signed off. Communication rises to the highest levels of complexity.
And understanding security implications of transformation becomes challenging and
leading changes to policy and process rather than influencing them.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Analysis
2. Communication (security architect)
3. Designing secure systems
4. Enabling and informing risk-based decisions
5. Research and innovation
6. Security technology
7. Understanding security implications of transformation

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the framework
expects, what it looks like in practice, and where it differs from lead.

### Skill: Analysis

**What this means.** Directing what feeds security analysis, and watching the
environment closely enough to know when a past decision has stopped being safe.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: provide direction and
lead on change regarding the factors that feed into analysis; monitor changes in
the technical environment and assess whether risks are still at acceptable levels
*or whether previous decisions need to be revisited*; and direct and influence
others on best practice and policy.

**What that looks like here.** You have reopened a decision you originally
approved, because the world changed. You know which of your organisation's
accepted risks were accepted under assumptions that no longer hold. You direct
what gets analysed rather than reacting to what arrives.

**Different from lead**, which monitors and reuses analysis and feeds back on
requirements — yours is direction, and the discipline of revisiting your own past
decisions.

### Skill: Communication (security architect)

**What this means.** Explaining security and risk at the top of an organisation,
at the highest levels of complexity and under time pressure.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: demonstrate expert
understanding of security concepts and apply them at a technical level at the
highest levels of risk complexity; effectively translate and accurately
communicate security and risk implications at the most senior levels across
technical and non-technical stakeholders; successfully respond to challenges; and
manage stakeholder expectations across high risk and complexity or under
constrained timescales.

**What that looks like here.** You can give a permanent secretary or a board an
honest answer in the time available without either alarming them or reassuring
them falsely. You hold up when challenged by people with more authority than you.
You are trusted precisely because you do not overstate.

**Different from lead**, which communicates deeply but not at the most senior
levels or the highest complexity.

### Skill: Designing secure systems

**What this means.** Setting how secure design is done — defining patterns,
challenging the ones that have stopped making sense, and creating precedents
others will follow.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: lead design and review
solutions to complex problems with system architectures by defining and
challenging patterns and principles; and create precedents and set direction.

**What that looks like here.** The patterns your organisation designs to are
yours, and you have retired some. When you approve something novel, you know you
are creating a precedent and you decide deliberately whether you want to. You
lead design on the problems nobody has a pattern for.

**Different from lead**, which develops patterns and principles rather than
challenging them and setting precedent.

### Skill: Enabling and informing risk-based decisions

**What this means.** Being the person the organisation's risk owners rely on when
the stakes are highest.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: act as a point of
escalation; be trusted by senior risk owners as an expert in security; and apply
risk methodologies at the most complex levels of risk.

**What that looks like here.** Senior risk owners come to you before deciding
rather than for endorsement afterwards. You handle the risks that do not fit any
methodology cleanly. You are clear, every time, that the decision is theirs — and
that clarity is why they trust you.

**Different from lead**, which advises on higher-impact risks and applies
methodologies proportionately, rather than being the escalation point and the
trusted expert.

### Skill: Research and innovation

standardised beyond your own organisation. You bring new technology in
deliberately, with the safe pattern designed rather than discovered afterwards.
Your community engagement is real rather than a conference badge.

### Skill: Security technology

**What this means.** Depth in the technology itself — judging exploitability, and
influencing how security technology develops.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: explain complex
system architectures; identify and explain how easy or difficult it will be to
exploit vulnerabilities; and lead and influence security technology in the
security industry.

**What that looks like here.** You can still take an unfamiliar architecture
apart. Your exploitability judgements stop the organisation panicking about the
wrong things and missing the right ones. The framework asks you to be an
exemplar, which means being visibly current rather than historically excellent.

**Same wording as lead security architect**, and there is no level above.

### Skill: Understanding security implications of transformation

**What this means.** Changing the policy and process that shape security, not
only working within them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you challenge and lead changes
to policy and processes to support business outcomes, business architecture, and
legal and political implications.

**What that looks like here.** You have got a policy changed because it produced
worse security or blocked a legitimate outcome. You can work with legal and
policy colleagues in their terms. You know which fights are worth having, because
you have lost some.

**Different from lead**, which *starts influencing* policy and process rather
than challenging and leading changes to it.

## Legal compliance

Security architecture carries statutory duties, government security policy, and
the boundary of what anyone may lawfully do to a computer system. At this level
you lead changes to the policy itself. These questions ask about awareness,
training and support, not about whether you can state the law correctly, and not
as a check on your conduct.
