# Lead security architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead security architect in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent
it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would
close them.

The framework describes a lead security architect as undertaking complex work at
a high risk level, often across several projects: interacting with senior
stakeholders across departments, reaching and influencing people across larger
teams and communities, researching and applying innovative solutions and
justifying the decisions, developing vision, principles and strategy for security
architects on a project or technology, working out subtle security needs,
understanding the impact of decisions and choosing between approaches, producing
patterns and supporting quality assurance, being the point of escalation for
architects below you, and leading the technical design of systems and services.

*Working out subtle security needs* is the phrase worth pausing on. The obvious
requirements are written down by somebody. The subtle ones — what an aggregation
of unremarkable data would enable, what an insider could do without breaking a
rule — are the ones this level exists to find.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Each of the seven skills gets an
explanation, a note on what changes at principal, a rating and some questions.
Expect about 45 minutes.

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you were expert in when
  you last had time to build something.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed

## About this role level

**The role: security architect.** A security architect designs and builds secure
solutions.

**Your level: lead security architect.** At this level you undertake complex work
of a high risk level, often across several projects. The framework expects that
you:

- interact with senior stakeholders across departments
- reach and influence a wide range of people across larger teams and communities
- research and apply innovative security architecture solutions, and justify and
  communicate the design decisions
- develop vision, principles and strategy for security architects for one project
  or technology
- work out subtle security needs
- understand the impact of decisions, balancing requirements and choosing between
  approaches
- produce particular patterns and support quality assurance
- are the point of escalation for architects in more junior roles
- lead the technical design of systems and services

**Where this level sits.** The security architect role has three levels:

1. Security architect
2. **Lead security architect** — you are here
3. Principal security architect

Your step up is principal security architect. Five skills change there, and the
direction is consistent: from doing the hardest work to setting direction and
being trusted as the organisation's authority. Designing secure systems becomes
*defining and challenging* patterns, creating precedents and setting direction.
Risk work becomes acting as the point of escalation and being trusted by senior
risk owners as an expert. Analysis adds monitoring the technical environment to
judge whether past decisions need revisiting. Understanding security implications
of transformation moves from starting to influence policy to *challenging and
leading changes* to it. Research and innovation and security technology keep
their wording.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Analysis
2. Communication (security architect)
3. Designing secure systems
4. Enabling and informing risk-based decisions
5. Research and innovation
6. Security technology
7. Understanding security implications of transformation

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at principal.

### Skill: Analysis

**What this means.** Making sure the analysis behind security decisions is done
well, reused where it applies, and fed back into the requirements that shaped it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: monitor the analysis of
a technical solution and ensure analysis is reused for similar problem sets;
review solutions and identify areas for change; drive the collection of the
information that is used and analysed; and feed back on policy and requirements.

**What that looks like here.** Analysis done once gets reused rather than
repeated. You ask for the information nobody was collecting. When a requirement
produces bad security, you say so to whoever wrote it rather than designing
around it.

**The next step.** At principal you provide direction and lead on change in what
feeds analysis, monitor changes in the technical environment to judge whether
risks are still acceptable *or whether previous decisions need revisiting*, and
direct and influence others on best practice and policy.

### Skill: Communication (security architect)

**What this means.** Explaining security and risk to people who do not share your
training, holding up under challenge, and reaching agreements that hold.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: demonstrate a deep
understanding of security concepts and apply them at a technical level;
effectively translate and accurately communicate security and risk implications
to technical and non-technical stakeholders; successfully respond to challenges;
and manage stakeholder expectations, adapting flexibly to reach consensus.

**What that looks like here.** You can hold a room where the security answer is
unwelcome and still leave with a decision people will keep. You explain
consequences rather than ratings. You adapt without conceding what actually
matters.

**The next step.** At principal this rises to expert understanding at the highest
levels of risk complexity, communicating at the most senior levels, and managing
expectations across high risk and complexity or under constrained timescales.

### Skill: Designing secure systems

**What this means.** Building security into architecture — and at this level,
creating the patterns and principles that others apply.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design and review system
architectures through the *development* of patterns and principles.

**What that looks like here.** You have written patterns that other architects
use without asking you. Your principles are followed because people understand
what they protect against. You review designs and change their direction without
taking them over.

**The next step.** At principal you lead design and review solutions to complex
problems by *defining and challenging* patterns and principles, and you create
precedents and set direction — including challenging patterns that have become
habits.

### Skill: Enabling and informing risk-based decisions

**What this means.** Helping people who own significant risk decide well, and
matching the method to the size of the risk.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: work with higher impact
or more complex risks, advising on the impact and whether it is within risk
tolerance; and apply different risk methodologies in proportion to the risk.

**What that looks like here.** You handle the risks where being wrong is
expensive and public. You choose the method — a full assessment, a threat model, a
conversation — according to what is at stake, rather than applying one process to
everything. You are clear about the difference between your advice and the risk
owner's decision.

**The next step.** At principal you act as a point of escalation, are trusted by
senior risk owners as an expert in security, and apply risk methodologies at the
most complex levels of risk.

### Skill: Research and innovation

### Skill: Security technology

**What this means.** Knowing the technology deeply enough to judge how hard a
weakness is to exploit, and to influence how security technology develops.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: explain complex
system architectures; identify and explain how easy or difficult it will be to
exploit vulnerabilities; and lead and influence security technology in the
security industry.

**What that looks like here.** You can talk an unfamiliar architecture through
end to end. You judge exploitability rather than repeating a severity score —
which is what stops an organisation panicking over the wrong things. The third
part takes you outside: vendor roadmaps, standards bodies, cross-government
groups.

**The next step.** Identical wording at principal security architect.

### Skill: Understanding security implications of transformation

**What this means.** Seeing what organisational change means for security, and
beginning to shape the policy and process that surround it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: interpret and apply
understanding across a complex area; and *start influencing* policy and process,
business architecture, and legal and political implications.

**What that looks like here.** You can hold a whole transformation in view and
say where security actually changes. You have got a policy or process altered
because it produced bad security — which usually means persuading people whose
job is the policy, not the security.

**The next step.** At principal you *challenge and lead changes* to policy and
processes to support business outcomes, business architecture, and legal and
political implications.

## Legal compliance

Security architecture carries statutory duties, government security policy, and
the boundary of what anyone may lawfully do to a computer system. At this level
you also influence the policy itself. These questions ask about awareness,
training and support, not about whether you can state the law correctly, and not
as a check on your conduct.
