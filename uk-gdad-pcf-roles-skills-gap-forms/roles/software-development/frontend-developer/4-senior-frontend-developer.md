# Senior frontend developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior frontend developer in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. It has come from human resources or your
manager, and it exists to find where the gap sits between what you do now and
what the organisation needs from someone at this level.

The framework describes a senior frontend developer as an experienced developer
who works with minimal support and can influence and mentor others. What it adds
at this level is direction rather than delivery: helping set direction and embed
good practice within teams, making decisions based on research, planning and
leading development across sets of related stories, holding the whole system in
your head, and teaching. The technical bar barely moves between frontend
developer and senior; the influence bar moves a long way.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the eleven skills the framework names for you gets an
explanation, a note on what changes at lead, a rating and some questions. Expect
about 45 minutes.

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into
whatever your organisation uses for development conversations. Every question
is numbered, so answers travel back on their own. Clear answers empties the

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Where a question asks what you did, name your part** rather than the team's.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: frontend developer.** A frontend developer designs, builds and
improves website software that meets user needs — writing clean, accessible code
following a progressive enhancement approach, creating code that is open by
default and easy to reuse, and understanding HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

**Your level: senior frontend developer.** An experienced developer who works
with minimal support and can influence and mentor others. The framework expects
that you:

- help set direction and embed good practice within teams
- make decisions based on research
- plan and lead development on sets of related stories, working with other
  disciplines to understand what needs to be built
- have an understanding of the whole system
- teach and mentor others

*Embed* is the operative word in the first one. Setting a direction is a
conversation; embedding it means the practice survives without you in the room.

**Where this level sits.** The frontend developer role has six levels:

1. Apprentice frontend developer
2. Junior frontend developer
3. Frontend developer
4. **Senior frontend developer** — you are here
5. Lead frontend developer
6. Head of frontend development

Your step up is lead frontend developer, and it is the largest single step in
this role. Five skills change, and one changes out of all recognition:
leadership and guidance goes from making medium-risk decisions to changing
organisational structures, leading strategy for a whole organisation, building
consensus between organisations, unblocking issues between departments, and
coaching the organisation on team dynamics and conflict resolution. Accessibility
adds external advocacy and design solutions; communicating information adds
mediation and speaking for the community to large audiences; modern development
standards and programming and build both move to setting standards and advising
others. If lead is where you are heading, the gap is mostly about influence
outside your own teams.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Accessibility
2. Communicating information

### Skill: Accessibility

**What this means.** Making sure what your teams build works for everyone, and
influencing decisions before they become accessibility problems.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: build user interfaces
that meet a predefined set of standards; champion accessibility to internal
stakeholders; offer design feedback to mitigate the risk of failing
accessibility testing; and offer recommendations on the best tools and methods
for accessibility testing.

**What that looks like here.** You catch problems in design review rather than
in an audit. You can explain to a product manager or a policy colleague why a
pattern will exclude people, in terms of consequences rather than rules. Your
teams' testing catches what automated tools cannot.

**The next step.** At lead this becomes advocacy for users with accessibility
needs in its own right, championing to *external* stakeholders as well as
internal ones, and offering design *solutions* rather than design feedback — you
bring the answer, not only the objection.

### Skill: Communicating information

**What this means.** Working with people whose priorities differ from yours,
managing expectations, and running the conversations nobody wants to run.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: work collaboratively
and build relationships; identify issues through team health checks and work
with others to address them; manage stakeholder expectations; be flexible and
capable of proactive and reactive communication; and host or moderate difficult
discussions within the team or with senior stakeholders.

**What that looks like here.** You tell people about problems before they
discover them. You have run a conversation between people who wanted
incompatible things and left them able to work together. You pitch detail to the
audience without either patronising or losing them.

**The next step.** At lead it becomes mediating and mending relationships,
hosting discussions about high risk and complexity under constrained timescales,
and speaking for the community to large audiences inside and outside government.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working beyond your team, giving feedback that lasts, and
recognising when the way a team works is the real problem.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work collaboratively
in a group, actively networking with others; adapt feedback so it is effective
and lasting; use your subject to identify problems in the team dynamic and
rectify them; and identify issues through team health checks and stimulate the
right responses.

**What that looks like here.** You know frontend people in other teams and
departments, and that network has saved you work. Your feedback changes what
someone does next time. You have named a team problem that everyone could feel
but nobody had said.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead frontend developer. It changes at
head of frontend development, where it becomes unblocking issues between

### Skill: Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Making technical decisions, judging how much risk they
carry, and knowing when to decide and when to recommend.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: make decisions
characterised by medium levels of risk and complexity, and recommend decisions
as risk and complexity increase; and contribute to best practice guidelines.

**What that looks like here.** You decide within your remit and escalate with a
recommendation rather than a question. When you recommend, you say what you
would do and why, not only what the options are. The team's guidelines have your
fingerprints on them.

**The next step.** The biggest jump in this role. At lead it becomes changing
organisational structures, leading strategy for the whole organisation, making
and justifying decisions of high risk and impact, building consensus between
organisations, unblocking issues between departments, and coaching the
organisation on team dynamics and conflict resolution. Almost none of that is
about code.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code understandable, testable and
safe to change — version control, small changes, review, automated testing,
continuous integration, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you apply modern development
standards and support others in applying them.

**What that looks like here.** Your reviews teach. You have set a convention and
explained the reasoning rather than asserting it. Junior colleagues write
differently since working with you.

**The next step.** At lead you identify and apply standards to support complex
projects and programmes, and lead others in applying them — supporting becomes
leading, and one team becomes several.

### Skill: Programming and build (frontend developer)

**What this means.** The craft: reviewing specifications, then building, testing
and documenting user interfaces of real complexity.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with others
when necessary to review specifications, and use the agreed specifications to
build, test and document user interfaces of medium to high complexity, using the
right standards and tools. This is the same wording as frontend developer.

**What that looks like here.** You take an interface with genuine states —
loading, error, empty, partial, offline, slow — and handle all of them. What you
build is reused by people who never spoke to you. Your work is the reference
others copy from.

**The next step.** At lead you advise on the right way to apply standards and
methods to ensure compliance, provide technical advice to stakeholders, set
team-based standards for tools and techniques, and build interfaces of *high*
complexity.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, disposably, so
decisions rest on evidence rather than seniority.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others;
establish design patterns and iterate them; and use a variety of prototyping
methods, choosing the most appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You ask for prototypes rather than opinions. You
have ended a circular debate by asking for two versions by Thursday. The
patterns your teams reuse get revised when they stop fitting.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead frontend developer.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What that looks like here.** You have written a policy or a technical strategy

**The next step.** At lead you lead the design and implementation of strategy,

### Skill: Systems integration (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Owning the path from source code to what a browser
downloads: the static assets build, the network configuration, delivery over the
internet, and the tests around the interface.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: define the static
assets build; co-ordinate all aspects of the integration and take responsibility
for the tests around the user interface; and configure the part of the system
that uses the network and test that it works as expected.

**What that looks like here.** The build is yours: you defined it, and you can
say why each step exists. You have configured caching, compression, a content
security policy or a content delivery network, and tested that it behaved. When
something works locally and fails in production, you are the person who finds
out why.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead frontend developer.

### Skill: User focus (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Representing the people who use the service inside the
team, and telling the difference between what they need and what they ask for.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: collaborate with user
researchers and interaction designers to represent user needs internally;
explain the difference between user needs and the desires of the user; champion
user research so it focuses on all users; effectively prioritise and define
approaches to understanding the user story, guiding others to do so; and offer
recommendations on the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You attend research and act on it. You have said
"that is a want, and here is the need underneath it" and been right. You push
for research with the users nobody is thinking about — assistive technology
users, people on poor connections, people doing this once under stress.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead frontend developer.

### Skill: Web performance optimisation

**What this means.** How quickly pages load and respond, what makes them slow,
and what that costs the people with the worst devices and connections.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: identify and resolve
web performance issues; collect data to monitor and resolve them; and guide
others on the best performance optimisation software and methods.

**What that looks like here.** Performance is measured continuously rather than
investigated after complaints. You can read a trace and say what is actually
slow. Other people come to you for the method, not just the fix.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead frontend developer. It changes at
head of frontend development: advocating for performance practice, coaching
others, and actively collecting data from multiple tools.

## Legal compliance

Government services carry legal duties, and frontend work carries one very
directly: public sector websites and apps must meet an accessibility standard in
law and publish an accessibility statement. At senior you are often the person
others assume has checked. These questions ask about awareness, training and
support — not about whether you can state the law correctly, and not as a check
on your conduct.
