# DevOps engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a DevOps engineer in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, what you have not had the chance to do, and
what support would be most useful over the coming months.

This is the level where the supervision drops away. The framework expects you to
transform technical requirements into DevOps processes, build complex stories
without additional support, manage live test environments, and identify and
resolve the issues that are preventing delivery. That last one is the interesting
part of the job and the hardest to evidence: much of your value shows up as
things that stopped blocking other people.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. The form takes each of the ten skills the framework names for you,
explains what it expects and what changes at senior, and asks for a rating and
an example. Expect about 40 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick
boxes tick, and each skill has a rating to choose. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into
whatever your organisation uses for development conversations. Every question
is numbered, so answers travel back on their own. Clear answers empties the
form and deletes the saved copy, which is worth doing on a computer other
people use.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **One named piece of work beats a paragraph of description.**
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: development operations (DevOps) engineer.** Commonly called DevOps
engineers, development operations engineers support the development and
operation of software through tools, environments and practices. In this role
you underpin good development processes: managing tools and testing
environments, central code control, maintaining development standards, and
writing software that automates systems.

**Your level: DevOps engineer.** At this level you deliver automated software
components that form part of a development operations toolchain. The framework
expects that you:

- transform technical requirements into DevOps processes
- build complex stories without additional support
- manage live test environments
- identify and resolve issues that are preventing delivery

Two of those are easy to skim past. *Transform technical requirements into
processes* means you turn "we need to release more safely" into an actual
pipeline, gate or practice — design work, not just build work. And *manage live
test environments* is a standing responsibility rather than a task: environments
drift, fill up, diverge from production and quietly stop being trustworthy.

**Where this level sits.** The DevOps engineer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice DevOps engineer
2. Junior DevOps engineer
3. **DevOps engineer** — you are here
4. Senior DevOps engineer
5. Senior DevOps engineer (management)
6. Lead DevOps engineer
7. Lead DevOps engineer (management)
8. Principal DevOps engineer
9. Principal DevOps engineer (management)

Your step up is senior DevOps engineer — and from that level onwards each level
splits into a technical role and a management role, so it is worth starting to
think about which you want.

Five of the ten skills below change at senior: modern development standards
(supporting others), service support (complex faults, and advising others),
systems design (a risk band up, and reviewing other people's designs), systems
integration (defining and co-ordinating rather than building), and development
process optimisation. The rest of the difference is in the accountabilities:
building a complete toolchain rather than components, coaching, making
deployment repeatable, scalable and highly available, and being the deep
technical support other teams call on.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

## Job skills

Ten skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether services stay up and cope with the load put on them
— both the services your organisation runs and the toolchain other engineers
depend on.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you manage service components
so they meet business needs and key performance indicators. This is a step up
from junior, where you only had to explain the processes.

**What that looks like here.** You know which indicators your services and tools
are judged on and roughly where they sit. You have changed something — a limit, a
scaling rule, a schedule, a cache — because a measurement told you to. You can
say which part of the estate would struggle first under load.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior DevOps engineer. The management
track at senior is where it changes: ensuring standards and procedures are
implemented, and initiating remedial action.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how software gets built and released — the
pipeline, the feedback loops, the review and release path. This skill is new at
your level, and it is close to the centre of what DevOps is for.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: explain why process
efficiency matters and the common ways processes get optimised; support specific
activities to improve development processes; and identify obvious deficiencies.

**What that looks like here.** You know your pipeline's real numbers — how long
it takes, how often it fails, how often a failure is the pipeline's own fault.
You have made a change that gave engineers time back. You can name the two worst
bottlenecks between commit and production.

**The next step.** At senior you work under guidance to identify optimisation
opportunities and contribute to implementing them — from spotting the obvious to
finding the non-obvious.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Building systems that resist attack and contain damage, by
designing the controls in rather than adding them afterwards. In DevOps this
includes the pipeline itself, which is often the most privileged thing in the
estate.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design solutions and
services with security controls included, specifically engineered to mitigate
security threats. "Specifically engineered" means controls chosen against threats
that apply, not a checklist applied uniformly.

**What that looks like here.** You can name the threats that apply to your
pipeline and estate, and the control that answers each. You have pushed back on
something because of what it exposed. You know what a compromised build agent
could reach.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior DevOps engineer. It changes at
principal, where you review and quality assure other people's solutions and lead
others in security practice.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code and configuration
understandable, testable and safe to change — version control, small changes,
review, automated testing, continuous integration, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can explain the most
important principles and how they apply to your own work, and apply them under
guidance. The framework keeps the junior wording here, which is worth noticing:
the step up in this skill comes at senior, not at your level.

**What that looks like here.** Infrastructure and pipeline changes go through the
same rigour as application code. Nothing important is configured by hand. Your
own work is consistent enough that someone could learn the conventions from it.

**The next step.** At senior you apply the standards *and support others in
applying them* — the first time this skill asks you to change what other people
do.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** Writing real software: reviewing the specification, then
designing, coding, testing and documenting programs of genuine complexity — in
DevOps, mostly automation, tooling and infrastructure code.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with others
when necessary to review specifications, and use the agreed specifications to
design, code, test and document programs or scripts of medium-to-high
complexity, using the right standards and tools.

**What that looks like here.** You write tools other teams use without asking you
how. Your automation is tested, not merely run. You take an ambiguous
requirement, ask the questions that settle it, and come back with an approach
before you come back with code.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior DevOps engineer, and at senior on
the management track. It changes at lead on the technical track.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, quickly and
disposably, so decisions rest on evidence rather than argument.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others;
establish design patterns and iterate them; and use a variety of prototyping
methods, choosing the most appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You stand up a scratch environment to settle a
disagreement rather than debating it. You ask other people for their rough
versions. You know when a prototype should be deleted, and you delete it — which
in infrastructure means it does not become a permanent unowned resource.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior DevOps engineer.

### Skill: Service support

**What this means.** Keeping live services and the toolchain working —
identifying faults, locating them and fixing them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you identify, locate and fix
service faults. The step from junior is from following a written procedure to
diagnosing something nobody has written a procedure for.

**What that looks like here.** You have led an investigation into something
genuinely puzzling. You can work from a symptom to a cause across several
systems. Your incident notes lead to changes rather than to filing.

**The next step.** At senior this becomes *complex* service faults, plus advising
others on different methodologies and types of service support — you become the
person other people bring their hard faults to.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of a system — its components, their
boundaries and how they fit together — and writing it down well enough for
others to build from.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: translate logical
designs into physical designs; produce detailed designs; document your work
using the required standards, methods and tools; design systems with managed
levels of risk, manageable complexity and meaningful impact; and work with well
understood technology, identifying appropriate patterns.

**What that looks like here.** You have designed a pipeline, an environment
topology or an automation system that someone else built from your description.
You reach for a known pattern and can say why it fits. Your documentation gets
used rather than asked about.

**The next step.** At senior the risk band rises, you select the standards and
methods rather than following them, and you review other people's designs.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Making separate systems work together — building the
interfaces, and handling what happens when the other side is slow, wrong or
absent.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you build and test simple
interfaces between systems, and work on more complex integration as part of a
wider team.

**What that looks like here.** You have integrated your toolchain with something
you do not control — an identity provider, an artefact store, a cloud API, a
security scanner — and tested the failure paths rather than only the happy one.
You know what your deployments do when a dependency times out.

**The next step.** At senior you define the integration build, co-ordinate build
activities across systems, and support integration testing — from building your
end to organising both ends.

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Building for the people who will actually use the thing.
For a DevOps engineer that is two audiences: the public who use the services your
organisation runs, and the engineers who use the toolchain you build.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things, and the framework
words this skill unusually for a technical role — closer to a researcher's
language than a developer's: identify and engage with users or stakeholders to
collect evidence of user needs; understand and define research that fits those
needs; and use quantitative and qualitative data about users to turn user focus
into outcomes.

**What that looks like here.** You ask the engineers who use your pipelines what
is painful, rather than assuming. You have measured something about their
experience — build wait times, failure rates, how often people work around a
tool — and acted on it. You can also say who the public users of your services
are and what your work does for them.

**The next step.** At senior the wording changes shape again, to collaborating
with user researchers, representing users internally, explaining the difference
between needs and desires, and championing research covering all users.

## Legal compliance

personal data lives in the environments you manage, access control is a security
matter, and logs and pipelines are records. These questions ask about awareness,

not as a check on your conduct.
