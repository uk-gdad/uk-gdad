# Senior DevOps engineer (management) - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior DevOps engineer on the management
track in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your
manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are,
and what would close them.

The framework gives both roles at this level the same accountabilities —
building a toolchain that enables delivery, coaching, making deployment
repeatable and scalable and highly available, and being the deep technical
support delivery teams call on. It then differentiates the two roles in three
skills, and yours is the governing side of each: you are answerable for
availability and capacity standards actually being implemented, and for
development process improvement as a programme of work with people and policy
behind it.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the ten skills gets an explanation, a note on what changes
at lead DevOps engineer (management), a rating and some questions. Expect about
45 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick
boxes tick, and each skill has a rating to choose. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into
whatever your organisation uses for development conversations. Every question
is numbered, so answers travel back on their own. Clear answers empties the
form and deletes the saved copy, which is worth doing on a computer other
people use.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **Answer for yourself, not for your team.** Where a question is about people
  you manage, it asks what you do, not how they are performing.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could pick up or did
  two jobs ago.
- **There is no target rating.** On the management track it is common for

  worth noticing rather than hiding: it tells you what you are becoming.

## About this role level

**The role: development operations (DevOps) engineer.** Commonly called DevOps
engineers, development operations engineers support the development and
operation of software through tools, environments and practices — managing tools
and testing environments, central code control, maintaining development
standards, and writing software that automates systems.

**Your level: senior DevOps engineer (management).** At this level you deliver
and integrate software to form a complete DevOps toolchain, and you are
responsible for planning and designing large groups of stories. The framework
expects that you:

- transform technical requirements into an effective DevOps toolchain to enable
  product delivery
- provide coaching and mentoring to more junior colleagues
- ensure that deployment strategies for products are repeatable, scalable and
  highly available
- have deep technical knowledge, providing support to delivery teams and solving
  complex problems

**The two roles at this level.** Identical accountabilities, three skills worded
differently:

| | Technical role | Management role (you) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Availability and capacity management | Manage service components to meet business needs and key performance indicators | Ensure the correct implementation of standards and procedures; identify capacity issues and implement required changes; initiate remedial action |
| Development process optimisation | Work under guidance to identify opportunities and contribute to implementation | Analyse current processes; identify and implement opportunities; lead and develop a team of experts to deliver service improvements; help establish requirements by setting policy and standards |
| Service support | Identify, locate and fix **complex** service faults, and advise others on methodologies | Identify, locate and fix service faults |

Read the third row carefully, because it runs against the usual assumption. The
deeper fault-finding expectation sits on the *technical* track, not yours. If
being the person who solves the impossible faults is what you want your job to
be, that is an argument for the technical role, and it is better raised now than
after a promotion. Use the
[senior DevOps engineer form](4-senior-devops-engineer.md) if your job is closer
to that description, and say so in Q46.

**Where this level sits.** The DevOps engineer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice DevOps engineer
2. Junior DevOps engineer
3. DevOps engineer
4. Senior DevOps engineer
5. **Senior DevOps engineer (management)** — you are here
6. Lead DevOps engineer
7. Lead DevOps engineer (management)
8. Principal DevOps engineer
9. Principal DevOps engineer (management)

Level 5 is not a step above level 4; it is the same level on a different track.
Your step up is lead DevOps engineer (management), at level 7 — and on the
published skills it is a remarkably narrow step. Only modern development
standards changes, from applying and supporting others to identifying and
applying across complex programmes and leading others. Everything else keeps its
wording. The whole of the rest of the step sits in the accountabilities: acting
as a technical product owner with medium to long term strategies for product
lines, providing technical leadership through coaching, and leading the sharing
of knowledge and good practice. If lead is where you are heading, that is where
the evidence has to come from.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

## Job skills

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether services and the toolchain stay up and cope with
load — and, in your version of this skill, whether the standards and procedures
for that are genuinely followed rather than merely written.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things, and this is one of
the skills that differ from the technical role: ensure the correct
implementation of availability and capacity management standards and procedures;
identify capacity issues and implement the required changes; and initiate
remedial action.

**What that looks like here.** You know whether the runbooks match what people
actually do at three in the morning, because you have checked. You have spotted
a capacity problem from a trend rather than an alert. You have called for
remedial work and defended what it cost in delivery time.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead DevOps engineer (management). It
changes at principal on your track, where it becomes leading policy and strategy
for service and capacity management.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how software gets built and released — and, on
your track, owning that improvement as a piece of work with people and
governance behind it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things, and this is the skill
your track exists for: analyse current processes; identify and implement
opportunities to optimise them; lead and develop a team of experts to deliver
service improvements; and help to evaluate and establish requirements for the
implementation of changes by setting policy and standards.

**What that looks like here.** You know your teams' delivery numbers as numbers,
not impressions. You have run an improvement as a piece of work with people
assigned to it rather than as a side project. The policy governing how your
teams release is one you helped write.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead DevOps engineer (management). It
changes at principal on your track, where it becomes setting the strategy and
managing resource allocation for solution development programmes, and working
with client functions to establish business requirements.

### Skill: Information security

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code and configuration
understandable, testable and safe to change, applied to infrastructure and
pipelines as much as to application code.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you apply modern development
standards and support others in applying them.

**What that looks like here.** Your reviews teach. Delivery teams write better
pipeline code since you started reviewing it. You have set a convention and
explained the reasoning rather than asserting it.

**The next step.** This is the *only* skill that changes at lead DevOps engineer
(management): identifying and applying standards to support complex projects and
programmes, and leading others in applying them. If you are aiming at lead, this
is the one skill the framework asks you to grow — everything else it asks you to
demonstrate at a larger scale.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, disposably, so
decisions rest on evidence rather than seniority.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others;
establish design patterns and iterate them; and use a variety of prototyping
methods, choosing the most appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You ask your team to stand something up rather
than argue about it. Patterns get revised rather than fossilised. Prototype
infrastructure gets removed instead of quietly becoming permanent.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead DevOps engineer (management). It
changes at principal: sharing best practice, coaching others, and looking at
strategic service design end to end.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of systems — components, boundaries,
technology — and the standards by which those decisions get made.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: design systems of
medium risk, impact and complexity; select appropriate design standards, methods
and tools and ensure they are applied effectively; and review other people's
designs for appropriate technology, efficient use of resources and integration
across systems.

**What that looks like here.** You have designed something substantial that
others built. You review designs and change their direction without overruling
their authors. You can say which of your own decisions you would reverse.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead and at principal on both tracks —

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Making separate systems work together — defining the build,
co-ordinating it across teams, and testing the seams.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you define the integration

undertake and support integration testing.

procedures across the service product life cycle and managing the resources for
the integration function — so if governing integration is what you want, the

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the people who use the service at the centre of
technical decisions — the public who use the services, and the engineers who use
your toolchain.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: collaborate with user
researchers and represent users internally; explain the difference between user
needs and user desires; champion user research so it covers all users;
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story, guiding others
in doing so; and recommend the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You treat your engineers' friction as evidence
rather than complaint, and you have measured it. You can connect a toolchain
decision to what it does for the public. You protect research time when delivery
pressure rises.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead and at principal on your track.

personal data lives in the environments you govern, access control is a security

awareness, training and support — not about whether you can state the law
