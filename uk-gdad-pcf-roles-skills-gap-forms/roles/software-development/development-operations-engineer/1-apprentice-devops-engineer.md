# Apprentice DevOps engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome to this form.

It is for you if you work as an apprentice DevOps engineer in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Someone in your organisation — human resources, or
your manager — has sent it to you so that you can say what you already do, what
you have not met yet, and what would help you most over the next few months.

This is not a test. Nothing here is scored, marked or graded, and nobody is
comparing your answers with anyone else's. An apprenticeship is a learning job,
and DevOps is a particularly wide one: pipelines, environments, monitoring,
security, scripting and the tools other developers depend on. Nobody knows all
of it, least of all at the start.

The form walks through the six skills the framework names for an apprentice
DevOps engineer. Each is explained before you are asked anything about it, so
you will not need another page open.

Most people take about half an hour. You can stop and come back.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

This page is a web page. You cannot type into it and there is no button to send
it. Nothing you write goes anywhere near this website.

So pick whichever suits you:

- Print the page and write on it.
- Copy the questions into a document or an email, and answer there.
- Answer in whatever tool your organisation already uses.

Every question has a number, like **Q7**, so your answers make sense on their
own without the questions attached.

Before you start:

- **"I don't know" is a real answer**, and a useful one — it tells people what
  to teach you.
- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a mark against you.
- **Small examples are fine.** One pipeline change, one alert, one script.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers or what
  they are used for, ask before you fill it in.

Some questions ask you to rate yourself. Use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |

| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It is about **what you do at work today**, not what you could probably manage
  or what a course covered. If you have only read about it, that is a 1.
- **There is no target rating.** An apprentice ticking mostly 1s and 2s is
  describing exactly what an apprenticeship looks like.

## About this role level

**The role: development operations (DevOps) engineer.** Commonly called DevOps
engineers, development operations engineers support the development and
operation of software through tools, environments and practices. In this role
you underpin good development processes: managing tools and testing
environments, central code control, maintaining development standards, and
writing software that automates systems.

That is worth reading twice, because it explains what the job is *for*. A DevOps
engineer's users are mostly other engineers. When you make a pipeline faster or
an environment more reliable, the benefit shows up in everybody else's work.

**Your level: apprentice DevOps engineer.** An apprentice DevOps engineer
attends certified training and develops skills on the job. The framework expects
that you:

- spend a significant proportion of your time shadowing others
- are involved in implementation but not planning
- build on your knowledge

"Implementation but not planning" is a real boundary and a helpful one. You are
expected to do the work once it has been decided; deciding what to do is
somebody else's job for now.

**Where this level sits.** The DevOps engineer role has nine levels:

1. **Apprentice DevOps engineer** — you are here
2. Junior DevOps engineer
3. DevOps engineer
4. Senior DevOps engineer
5. Senior DevOps engineer (management)
6. Lead DevOps engineer
7. Lead DevOps engineer (management)
8. Principal DevOps engineer
9. Principal DevOps engineer (management)

Your next step is junior DevOps engineer. Three new skills appear there —
systems design, systems integration and user focus — and several of the ones
below move from explaining to doing. You do not need them yet.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Information security
3. Modern development standards
4. Programming and build (software engineering)
5. Prototyping
6. Service support

## Job skills

Six skills, in the framework's order. Each explains the skill, what is expected
of you now, what it looks like in real work, and what changes at the next level.
Then rate yourself and answer a few questions.

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** "Availability" is whether a service is up and working for
the people using it. "Capacity" is whether it can cope with how much is asked of
it. In DevOps this covers the services your team runs and the tools other
engineers depend on — if the build server is down, everyone stops.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can *explain* the
processes. Explaining is the whole expectation; you are not expected to manage
them or be on call.

**What that looks like here.** You could tell a new starter what your team
watches and where. You know what happens when an environment runs out of disk or
a pipeline queue backs up, because you have seen it.

**The next step.** At junior the framework describes this skill in exactly the
same words, so the change comes later — at DevOps engineer it becomes managing
service components against business needs and key performance indicators.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Keeping systems and information safe from people who should
not reach them, and from accidents. In DevOps this is unusually concentrated:
pipelines hold credentials, environments hold data, and access to the toolchain
is often access to everything.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can *explain* information
security and the security controls available to protect solutions and services.

**What that looks like here.** You know why secrets do not go in code or in a
pipeline log. You know roughly who can deploy to production and how that is
controlled. You could name two or three controls your team relies on.

**The next step.** At junior this changes from explaining to doing: you *use*
information security practices and available controls to contribute to
protecting solutions and services.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The shared ways of working that keep code and configuration
understandable and safe to change — version control, small changes, review,
automated tests, automated builds, working in the open. In DevOps they apply to
infrastructure and pipelines as much as to application code.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can explain the
importance of using modern development standards.

**What that looks like here.** You could say why infrastructure changes go
through review rather than being made by hand on a server. You know what happens
when someone pushes a change, and roughly why each stage exists.

**The next step.** At junior you explain the most important principles *and how
they apply to your own work*, and you start applying them under guidance.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** Writing software, and understanding how the software your
organisation runs actually gets built.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you understand the process of
software development and have a basic knowledge of how services are built. Note
that this is knowledge rather than production: at apprentice you are expected to
understand the build, not to write complex software.

**What that looks like here.** You can describe how a change becomes a running
service in your organisation. You have written or edited a small script. You can
read someone else's pipeline configuration and follow what it does.

**The next step.** At junior this becomes production: you design, code, test,
correct and document simple programs or scripts under the direction of others.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building a rough version of something to learn from it — a
throwaway pipeline, a test environment, a spike. The point is the learning, not
the artefact.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can explain what
prototyping is, and why and when to use it, and that you understand how to work
in an open and collaborative environment — pair working, for example.

**What that looks like here.** You have seen your team try something rough
before committing to it. You can say why that was quicker than debating. You can
work out loud when pairing rather than going quiet.

**The next step.** At junior you understand when to use a *specific* technique
or method, and show the value of prototyping to your team.

### Skill: Service support

**What this means.** Keeping live services and the tools around them working —
investigating faults, fixing them, and doing the routine work that prevents
them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you help with the
investigation and fixing of service faults, completing defined activities under
direction. You take part; someone tells you which part.

**What that looks like here.** You are asked to check whether an error appears
in the logs, and you report what you found. You sit in on an incident and follow
what is happening. You pick up a small fix once the cause is known.

**The next step.** At junior you help fix faults *following agreed procedures*
rather than direct instruction, and you carry out maintenance tasks on service
support infrastructure.

## Legal compliance

Government services come with legal duties. DevOps work sits close to two of
them in particular: personal data lives in the environments you manage, and
access control is a security matter. At your level nobody expects you to be
responsible for these, but you should know they exist and know who to ask.

These questions ask about **training and support**, not about whether you get
the law right.
