# Lead DevOps engineer (management) - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead DevOps engineer on the management
track in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your
manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are,
and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as leading one or a small number of related
project teams and contributing to the strategic direction: acting as a technical
product owner with medium to long term strategies for product lines, providing
technical leadership through coaching and mentoring, and leading the sharing of
knowledge and good practice. On your track the published skills put two things
squarely in your hands — that availability and capacity standards are genuinely
implemented and remedial action gets initiated, and that development process
improvement is analysed, staffed and governed by policy you help write.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the ten skills gets an explanation, a note on what changes
at principal DevOps engineer (management), a rating and some questions. Expect
about 45 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send
from here — your answers never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your
organisation uses for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so
answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **Answer for yourself, not for your teams.** Where a question is about people
  you manage, it asks what you do, not how they are performing.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job
  description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you and would stay changed. On this track that is a fair claim in
  the skills you govern and an overreach in the ones you have delegated; being
  clear which is which is the point.

## About this role level

**The role: development operations (DevOps) engineer.** Commonly called DevOps
engineers, development operations engineers support the development and
operation of software through tools, environments and practices — managing tools
and testing environments, central code control, maintaining development
standards, and writing software that automates systems.

**Your level: lead DevOps engineer (management).** At this level you lead one or
a small number of related project teams and contribute to the development of the
strategic direction. The framework expects that you:

- act as a technical product owner, developing medium to long term strategies
  for product lines
- provide technical leadership and guidance through coaching and mentoring
- lead the sharing of knowledge and good practice

**The two roles at this level.** Identical accountabilities, four skills worded
differently:

| | Technical role | Management role (you) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Availability and capacity management | Manage service components against business needs and key performance indicators | Ensure the correct implementation of standards and procedures; identify capacity issues and implement required changes; initiate remedial action |
| Development process optimisation | Work under guidance to identify opportunities and contribute | Analyse processes; identify and implement opportunities; lead and develop a team of experts; help set policy and standards |
| Service support | Identify, locate and fix **complex** faults, and advise others on methodologies | Identify, locate and fix service faults |
| Systems integration | **Establish standards** across the service product life cycle and manage the resources for the integration function | Define the integration build; co-ordinate build activities; support integration testing |

Two of those run against the usual assumption about management tracks: the
deeper fault-finding and the *governance of integration* both sit on the
technical side. Your track governs capacity and owns process improvement as a
programme. If your job is closer to the technical description, use the
[lead DevOps engineer form](6-lead-devops-engineer.md) and say so in Q46.

**Where this level sits.** The DevOps engineer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice DevOps engineer
2. Junior DevOps engineer
3. DevOps engineer
4. Senior DevOps engineer
5. Senior DevOps engineer (management)
6. Lead DevOps engineer
7. **Lead DevOps engineer (management)** — you are here
8. Principal DevOps engineer
9. Principal DevOps engineer (management)

Level 7 is not a step above level 6; it is the same level on a different track.
Your step up is principal DevOps engineer (management), at level 9, and four
skills change there. Availability and capacity management moves from ensuring
implementation to *leading policy and strategy*. Development process optimisation
moves from analysing and improving to setting strategy and managing resource
allocation for solution development programmes, working with client functions to
establish what the business needs. Information security adds review and quality
assurance of others' work. Prototyping becomes coaching and strategic service
design end to end. The pattern is consistent: from running things well to
deciding which things exist and what they get.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

## Job skills

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether services and the toolchain stay up and cope with
load — and, on your track, whether the standards and procedures for that are
genuinely implemented rather than merely published.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: ensure the correct
implementation of availability and capacity management standards and procedures;
identify capacity issues and implement the required changes; and initiate
remedial action.

**What that looks like here.** You have tested whether the procedures survive
contact with a real incident, rather than assuming. You spot capacity problems
from trends rather than alerts. Remedial work gets started because you called
for it and defended the cost.

**The next step.** At principal (management) this becomes leading policy and
strategy for service and capacity management, and leading the identification and
implementation of required changes — from enforcing the standards to deciding
what they should be.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how software gets built and released, as a
governed piece of work with people and policy behind it rather than as a good
intention.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things, and this is the skill
your track exists for: analyse current processes; identify and implement
opportunities to optimise them; lead and develop a team of experts to deliver
service improvements; and help to evaluate and establish requirements for the
implementation of changes by setting policy and standards.

**What that looks like here.** You know your teams' delivery numbers as numbers.
Improvement work has named people and a place in the plan. The policy that
governs how your teams release is one you helped write, and you can say what it
prevents.

**The next step.** At principal (management) this becomes strategy and
resourcing: setting the strategy and managing resource allocation for solution
development programmes, and working with client functions to establish business
requirements and then initiate and lead those programmes.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Building systems that resist attack and contain damage, by
engineering the controls in against the threats that actually apply.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design solutions and
services with security controls included, specifically engineered to mitigate
security threats.

**What that looks like here.** You can name your estate's real threats and the
controls that answer them. You have stopped or delayed something over a security
concern. Your teams know which decisions they must not take alone.

**The next step.** At principal (management) this adds design, review *and
quality assurance* of solutions and services, and leading others in using
appropriate information security practices.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** Writing real software: reviewing specifications, then
designing, coding, testing and documenting programs of genuine complexity.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with others
when necessary to review specifications, and use the agreed specifications to
design, code, test and document programs or scripts of medium-to-high
complexity. This is the senior wording, and it stays this way at principal on
your track.

**What that looks like here.** You can still read and reason about what your
teams build, and you review specifications with judgement rather than deference.

**The next step.** No change at principal (management), and no change on the
technical track either — this skill tops out here in this role. If deepening
engineering practice is what you want, promotion will not supply it, and that is
worth saying in a career conversation.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, disposably, so
decisions rest on evidence rather than seniority.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others;
establish design patterns and iterate them; and use a variety of prototyping
methods, choosing the most appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You ask for evidence rather than opinions. The
patterns your teams share get revised. Prototype infrastructure is removed
rather than quietly kept.

**The next step.** At principal (management) it becomes sharing best practice and
coaching others in prototyping, and looking at strategic service design end to
end.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Making separate systems work together — defining the build,
co-ordinating it across teams, and testing the seams.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you define the integration
build, co-ordinate build activities across systems, and understand how to
undertake and support integration testing.

**What that looks like here.** You have owned integration with systems you do not

across teams with different release rhythms is work you have done.

*technical* track at your level goes further — establishing integration standards
across the product life cycle and managing the resources for that function. If

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the people who use the service at the centre of
technical decisions — the public using the services, and the engineers using
your toolchain.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: collaborate with user
researchers and represent users internally; explain the difference between user
needs and user desires; champion user research so it covers all users;
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story, guiding others
in doing so; and recommend the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** As technical product owner you have established
what your users need rather than assumed it. You protect your teams' time for
understanding users when delivery pressure rises. You can connect toolchain
decisions to what they do for the public.

**The next step.** Identical wording at principal (management).
