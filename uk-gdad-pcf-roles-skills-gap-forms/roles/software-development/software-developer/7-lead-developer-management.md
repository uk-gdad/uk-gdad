# Lead developer (management) - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead developer on the management track in
the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager
has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and
what would close them.

The framework gives the two roles at this level the same accountabilities —
guiding how teams work, deciding when software should be written, contributing
to the developer community, providing technical leadership, and promoting good
practice — and then differentiates them in the skills. Your version puts you
squarely in charge of two things: that availability and capacity standards are
genuinely implemented and remedial action gets initiated, and that development
processes are analysed, improved, staffed and governed by policy and standards
you help set. Those are the two questions this form pushes hardest on.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of your ten skills gets an explanation of what is expected, of
what changes at principal developer (management), a rating and some questions.
Expect about 45 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick
boxes tick, and each skill has a rating to choose. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into
whatever your organisation uses for development conversations. Every question
is numbered, so answers travel back on their own. Clear answers empties the
form and deletes the saved copy, which is worth doing on a computer other
people use.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **Answer for yourself, not for your teams.** Where a question is about people
  you manage, it asks what you do, not how they are performing.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could pick up or did
  two jobs ago.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people.** "I set the approach and develop others
  in it" means someone else's practice changed because of you. On the management
  track that is a fair claim in the skills you govern, and an overreach in the
  ones you now delegate — and being honest about which is which is the point of
  the exercise.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs — writing clean, secure code following a
test-driven approach, and creating code that is open by default and easy for
others to reuse.

**Your level: lead developer (management).** A lead developer leads development
on one or a small number of related project teams. The framework expects that
you:

- guide the ways in which the team works
- are proficient in a wide range of technical systems
- are involved in identifying appropriate technology and approaches, deciding
  when software should be written
- contribute to the developer community
- provide technical leadership, coaching and mentoring your team
- promote knowledge sharing and the adoption of good practice

**The two roles at this level.** The framework defines a technical role and a
management role, with identical accountabilities. Three skills are worded
differently:

| | Technical role | Management role (you) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Availability and capacity management | Manage service components to meet business needs and key performance indicators | Ensure the correct implementation of standards and procedures; identify capacity issues and implement required changes; initiate remedial action |
| Development process optimisation | Work under guidance to identify opportunities and contribute to implementation | Analyse current processes; identify and implement opportunities; lead and develop a team of experts to deliver service improvements; help establish requirements for change by setting policy and standards |
| Programming and build | Advise on applying standards and methods; hold technical responsibility across a project; set team standards for tools and techniques | Collaborate to review specifications; design, code, test and document programs of medium-to-high complexity |

The pattern is clear enough to plan a career by. Your track deepens governance
and improvement; the technical track deepens engineering responsibility. If your
job is closer to that description, use the
[lead developer form](6-lead-developer.md) and say so in Q46.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. Senior developer
5. Senior developer (management)
6. Lead developer
7. **Lead developer (management)** — you are here
8. Principal developer
9. Principal developer (management)

Level 7 is not a step above level 6; it is the same level on a different track.
Your step up is principal developer (management), at level 9.

Four skills change there. Development process optimisation moves furthest: from
analysing and improving processes to *setting the strategy and managing resource
allocation for solution development programmes*, and working with client
functions to establish requirements and then initiate and lead those programmes.
Information security adds review and quality assurance of other people's work,
and leading others in security practice. Prototyping becomes coaching others and
looking at strategic service design end to end. Systems design moves to high risk
and impact, controlling design practice within an enterprise architecture, and
procurement strategy. Programming and build stays where it is — on this track it
never advances beyond the senior wording, at either level.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether services stay up and cope with load — and, on your
track, whether the standards and procedures for that are genuinely implemented
rather than merely written.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you ensure the correct
implementation of availability and capacity management standards and procedures;
identify capacity issues and implement the required changes; and initiate
remedial action.

**What that looks like here.** You know whether the runbooks match what people
do at three in the morning, because you have checked rather than assumed. You
have spotted a capacity problem from a trend rather than an alert. You have
called for remedial work and defended what it cost in delivery.

**The next step.** Identical wording at principal developer (management). The
scope grows with the number of services and teams the standards must hold
across.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how software gets built — and, at your level,
owning that improvement as a programme of work with people and governance behind
it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things, and this is the
skill your track exists for: analyse current processes; identify and implement
opportunities to optimise them; lead and develop a team of experts to deliver
service improvements; and help to evaluate and establish requirements for the
implementation of changes by setting policy and standards.

**What that looks like here.** You know your teams' lead time and change failure
rate as numbers, not impressions. You have run an improvement as a piece of work
with people assigned to it. The policy or standard that governs how your teams
release is one you helped write.

**The next step.** At principal (management) this becomes strategy and
resourcing: setting the strategy and managing resource allocation for solution
development programmes, and working with client functions to establish business
requirements and then identify, propose, initiate and lead those programmes.
From running improvements to deciding which programmes exist and what they cost.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** The craft itself: reviewing specifications, then designing,
coding, testing, and documenting software of real complexity.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with others
when necessary to review specifications, and use the agreed specifications to
design, code, test and document programs or scripts of medium-to-high
complexity, using the right standards and tools. This is the senior wording, and
it stays this way at principal on your track.

**What that looks like here.** You still build, and what you build is picked up
by people who were not in the room. You ask the questions that settle an
ambiguous requirement before writing code against it.

**The next step.** No change at principal (management). If deepening engineering
responsibility is what you want — advising on standards and methods across a
project, holding technical responsibility for all its stages, setting the team's
tooling standards — that progression exists on the technical track, and this is

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, disposably, so
decisions rest on evidence rather than seniority.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others;
establish design patterns and iterate them; and use a variety of prototyping
methods, choosing the most appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You ask for prototypes rather than opinions. You
have ended a circular debate by asking for two versions by Thursday. Patterns
your teams reuse get revised rather than fossilised.

**The next step.** At principal (management) it becomes sharing best practice and
coaching others in prototyping, and looking at strategic service design end to
end — prototyping how a whole service should work, not how a component should.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of systems — components, boundaries,
technology choices — and the standards by which those decisions get made.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design systems of medium
risk, impact and complexity; select appropriate design standards, methods and
tools and ensure they are applied effectively; and review other people's designs
for appropriate technology, efficient use of resources and integration across
multiple systems.

**What that looks like here.** You have designed something substantial and had
it built by other people. You review designs and change their direction without
overruling their authors. You can name a decision of your own you would reverse.

**The next step.** A large change at principal (management): high risk, impact
and complexity; controlling design practice within an enterprise or industry
architecture; influencing industry-based models for new technology applications;
developing implementation and procurement strategies consistent with business
needs; and ensuring adherence to technical strategies, policies and standards.

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the people who use the service at the centre of
technical decisions, and knowing the difference between what they need and what
they ask for.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with user
researchers and can represent users internally; explain the difference between
user needs and user desires; champion user research so it covers all users;
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story and guide
others in doing so; and recommend the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You attend research rather than reading the
summary, and protect your teams' time for it when delivery pressure rises. You
have argued a technical decision on user grounds in a room where the loudest
argument was cost.
