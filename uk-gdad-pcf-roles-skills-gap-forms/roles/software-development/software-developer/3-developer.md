# Developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a developer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it to you so that you
can set out what you do now, what you have not had the chance to do, and what
support would be most useful over the coming months.

Developer is the level where the training wheels come off. You still have people
around you, but the framework stops describing your work as supervised: you
develop software to meet user needs, you write clean, secure and well-tested
code, you operate what you build and spot problems in production, and you coach
people junior to you. The interesting questions at this level are less about
whether you can write code and more about how far your responsibility now
reaches.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and nobody compares your answers with
a colleague's. The form takes each of the ten skills the framework names for a
developer, explains what it expects and what changes at senior, and asks you to
rate yourself and give an example. Expect to spend about 40 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick
boxes tick, and each skill has a rating to choose. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered so that answers
travel back on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy, which is worth doing on a computer other people use.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **One named piece of work beats a paragraph of description.**
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could pick up or what
  a course covered. Current practice, not ceiling.
- **There is no target rating.** A developer typically sits at 2 and 3 across
  the core skills, with 1s in whatever their particular service has never asked
  of them. That pattern is information, not a shortfall.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs — writing clean, secure code following a
test-driven approach, and creating code that is open by default and easy for
others to reuse.

**Your level: developer.** A developer delivers software components that form
part of a product. The framework expects that you:

- develop software to meet user needs
- follow best practice guidelines, and help to improve those guidelines
- write clean, secure and well-tested code
- coach and mentor more junior colleagues
- operate the services you build, and identify issues in production

Two of those are easy to skip over. *Help to improve those guidelines* means the
team's practice is now partly yours to shape, not just to follow. And *operate
the services you build* means the job no longer stops at merge: if it is yours,
its behaviour in production is yours too.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. **Developer** — you are here
4. Senior developer
5. Senior developer (management)
6. Lead developer
7. Lead developer (management)
8. Principal developer
9. Principal developer (management)

Your step up is senior developer — and from that level onwards the framework
splits each level into a technical role and a management role, so it is worth
starting to think about which of those you want.

The step to senior is an unusual one. Five of the ten skills below are described
in exactly the same words at senior as at developer: information security,
modern development standards, programming and build, prototyping and user focus.
The other five do change — service support, systems design, systems integration,
development process optimisation, and the surrounding accountabilities. Those
accountabilities are where most of the difference lives: planning and leading
work across sets of related stories, holding the whole system in your head and
teaching it to others, working with other disciplines to decide what gets built,
and improving robustness and resilience rather than only restoring service.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether the service stays up for the people using it, and
whether it copes with the load put on it — measured and managed rather than
discovered during an incident.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you manage service components
so they meet business needs and key performance indicators. This is a step up
from junior, where you only had to explain the processes.

**What that looks like here.** You know which indicators your service is judged
on and roughly where it sits against them. You have changed something — a query,
a limit, a scaling rule, a cache — because a measurement told you to. You can
say which part of your service would struggle first if usage doubled.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior developer. The skill grows in
scope with the size of what you own rather than in description. The management
track at senior is where it changes: implementing the standards, identifying
capacity issues and initiating remedial action.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how the team builds software — the build, the
tests, the review path, the release — so that good work reaches users faster and
more safely.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: that you can explain
why process efficiency matters and the common ways processes get optimised; that
you support specific activities to improve development processes; and that you
identify obvious deficiencies.

**What that looks like here.** You noticed the test suite had become slow enough
that people skipped it, and said so with numbers rather than a sigh. You took on
a piece of an improvement someone else was leading. You can name the two worst
bottlenecks between an idea and production.

**The next step.** At senior you work under guidance to identify optimisation
opportunities and contribute to implementing them — so it moves from spotting
the obvious to looking for the non-obvious, and from supporting to contributing.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Building services that resist attack and contain damage, by
designing the protections in rather than bolting them on.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design solutions and
services with security controls included, specifically engineered to mitigate
security threats. Note "specifically engineered": controls chosen against
threats that actually apply, not a checklist applied uniformly.

**What that looks like here.** You can name a threat that applies to your
service and the control that answers it. You think about what your component
logs and who can read it. You have changed a design because of what it exposed.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior developer. The growth is in the
size and risk of what you design, and in how much other people rely on your
judgement.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code understandable, testable and
safe to change — version control, small changes, review, automated testing,
continuous integration, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you apply modern development
standards *and support others in applying them*. The second half is the part
that makes this a developer-level skill rather than a junior one.

**What that looks like here.** Your reviews explain rather than just approve.
You have helped a junior colleague understand why a change should be split up.
You follow the team's conventions closely enough that someone could learn them
from your work.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior developer. It changes at lead,
where you identify and apply standards across complex projects and lead others
in applying them.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** The core craft: reviewing specifications, then designing,
coding, testing, and documenting software of real complexity with the right
standards and tools.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with others
when necessary to review specifications, and use the agreed specifications to
design, code, test and document programs or scripts of medium-to-high
complexity, using the right standards and tools. The jump from junior is both in
complexity and in being part of agreeing the specification rather than receiving
it.

**What that looks like here.** You take an ambiguous requirement, ask the
questions that settle it, and come back with an approach before you come back
with code. You test at the level that matters rather than the level that is
easy. What you leave behind is picked up by people who were not in the room.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior developer. It changes at lead:
advising on how standards should be applied, holding technical responsibility
across a whole project, and setting team standards for tools and techniques.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, quickly and
disposably, so decisions rest on evidence rather than on argument.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others; that
you establish design patterns and iterate them; and that you use a variety of
prototyping methods and choose the most appropriate one.

**What that looks like here.** You have settled a disagreement by building two
versions in a day. You ask other people for their rough versions rather than
producing them all yourself. You know when a prototype should be deleted, and
you delete it.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior developer. It changes at
principal, where prototyping becomes about coaching others and looking at
service design end to end.

### Skill: Service support

**What this means.** Keeping live services working — investigating faults,
fixing them, and doing the maintenance that stops them happening.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you help fix service faults
following agreed procedures, and carry out maintenance tasks on service support
infrastructure. Alongside it sits your accountability to operate the services
you build and identify issues in production, which is what makes this more than
a rota duty.

**What that looks like here.** You follow the runbook without needing a guide.
You have spotted a problem in production before a user reported it. You do the
unglamorous maintenance — dependencies, certificates, log noise — and can say
why it matters.

**The next step.** At senior this becomes *identify, locate and fix service
faults*: from following an agreed procedure to diagnosing something nobody has
written a procedure for.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of a system — its components, their
responsibilities and boundaries — and writing that down well enough for other
people to build from.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you translate logical designs
into physical designs; produce detailed designs; document your work using the
required standards, methods and tools, including prototyping tools where
appropriate; design systems with managed levels of risk, manageable complexity
and meaningful impact; and work with well understood technology, identifying
appropriate patterns.

**What that looks like here.** You have written a design that someone else
built. You reach for a known pattern and can say why it fits. Your documentation
is good enough that people use it rather than asking you.

**The next step.** At senior the risk and complexity go up a band, you select
the standards and methods rather than following them, and you review other
people's designs for technology choice, resource use and integration.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Making separate systems work together — building the
interfaces between them, and handling what happens when the other side is slow,
wrong or absent.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you build and test simple
interfaces between systems, and work on more complex integration as part of a
wider team.

**What that looks like here.** You have built an interface to a system you do
not control, and tested the failure paths rather than only the happy one. You
know what your service does when a dependency times out, because you have seen
it happen.

**The next step.** At senior you define the integration build, co-ordinate build
activities across systems, and support integration testing — so it moves from
building your end to organising both ends.

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the people who use the service at the centre of
technical decisions, and knowing the difference between what they need and what
they ask for.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with user
researchers and can represent users internally; explain the difference between
user needs and user desires; champion user research so that it covers all users;
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story, guiding others
in doing so; and recommend the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You attend research rather than reading the
summary. You have argued for a technical decision on the grounds of what it does
for users. When someone says "all users", you ask about the ones using assistive
technology, or on a poor connection, or doing this once under stress.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior developer, and at every level up
to principal. What deepens is the range of users you can speak for and the
weight your representation carries.
