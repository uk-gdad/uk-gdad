# Senior developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior developer in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. It has come to you from human resources or from
your manager, and its purpose is straightforward: to find out where the gap sits
between what you do now and what your organisation needs from someone at this
level, and to work out what would close it.

At your level the interesting gaps are rarely technical. The framework already
assumes you can design, build, test, and operate software of medium-to-high
complexity. What it adds at senior is everything around that: planning and
leading work across sets of related stories, holding the whole system in your
head and teaching it to others, working with other disciplines, coaching, and
improving robustness and resilience rather than merely restoring it. Those are
harder to evidence and easier to lose track of, which is why the questions push
on them.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and nothing is compared against a
colleague. The form walks through each skill the framework names for a senior
developer, explains what it expects at this level and what changes at lead, and
then asks you to rate yourself and give an example. Expect to spend 40 minutes
or so on it. You will get more out of it if you answer the awkward questions
rather than the comfortable ones.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick
boxes tick, and each skill has a rating to choose. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so answers travel
back on their own without the questions attached. Clear answers empties the
form and deletes the saved copy, which is worth doing on a computer other
people use.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"I don't know" and "I haven't had the chance" are both real answers**, and
  more useful than a generous reading of a thin example.
- **One concrete example beats a general claim.** A named piece of work, however
  small, tells a reader more than a paragraph of description.
- **Ask who reads it.** If you have not been told who sees your answers, what
  they are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could pick up, what
  you did two jobs ago, or what you have read about. Depth of current practice,
  not ceiling.
- **There is no target rating**, and a row of 4s is not the goal. Most senior
  developers sit at 3 across their core skills and at 1 or 2 in the ones their
  particular service has never demanded. That pattern is information, not a
  shortfall.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Systems integration**
>
> - [x] 2 — Working: I do it with support
>
> *Evidence:* I defined the build for the payments integration last quarter, but
> the co-ordination across the two teams was run by our tech lead rather than by
> me, and I would not have known where to start with the sequencing.

Note what makes it useful: it names the work, and it says where the support was.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs — writing clean, secure code following a
test-driven approach, and creating code that is open by default and easy for
others to reuse.

**Your level: senior developer.** A senior developer delivers and integrates
software to form a complete service. The framework expects that you:

- plan and lead development on sets of related stories
- have an understanding of the whole system, and take responsibility for
  teaching it to others
- work with other disciplines to understand what needs to be built
- coach and mentor more junior colleagues
- operate the production services you build
- find ways to improve system robustness, resilience and stability

Two of those are worth pausing on, because they are the ones most often left
implicit. *Take responsibility for teaching it to others* makes system knowledge
a duty to spread rather than a personal asset. And *find ways to improve
robustness, resilience and stability* is proactive: it is not the same as fixing
what breaks.

**The technical and management tracks.** The framework defines two roles at this
level — a technical one and a management one. This form covers the technical
one. The two share every accountability above; the management role's skill
expectations differ in one place, availability and capacity management, where it
adds implementing standards, spotting capacity issues and initiating remedial
action. If your job is closer to that description than to this one, say so in
Q46 and use the management level's form instead.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. **Senior developer** — you are here
5. Senior developer (management)
6. Lead developer
7. Lead developer (management)
8. Principal developer
9. Principal developer (management)

Your step up is lead developer, and it is an unusual step: eight of the ten
skills below are described in exactly the same words at lead as they are at
senior. Only modern development standards and programming and build change. The
rest of the difference sits in the accountabilities — guiding how the team
works, deciding when software should be written at all, contributing to the
developer community beyond your team, and providing technical leadership rather
than technical delivery. If you are aiming at lead, that is where to look, and
several questions below are pointed at it.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether the service stays up for the people using it, and
whether it copes with the load put on it — measured, planned for, and managed
rather than discovered during an incident.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you manage service components
so they meet business needs and key performance indicators.

**What that looks like here.** You know which indicators your service is judged
on and what the current numbers are. You can say which component would fail
first under load and why. You have changed something — a limit, a scaling rule,
a query, a cache — because a measurement told you to.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead developer. The management track at
your own level is where this skill grows: implementing the standards, spotting
capacity issues and initiating remedial action.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how the team builds software — the pipeline, the
feedback loops, the review and release path — so that good work gets out faster
and more safely.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you work under guidance to
identify optimisation opportunities, and contribute to implementing the
solutions. Note the "under guidance": at senior you are expected to spot and
contribute, not to own the programme of change.

**What that looks like here.** You noticed the build was slow enough that people
stopped running it locally, and you said so with numbers. You took a piece of a
larger improvement — flaky test triage, a caching step, a smaller deployment
unit — and delivered it.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead developer, which is unusual for a
skill this central. The growth is in scope rather than description: at lead you
guide how the team works, so process becomes yours to shape rather than to feed
into.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Building services that resist attack and contain damage, by
designing the controls in rather than adding them afterwards.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design solutions and
services with security controls included, specifically engineered to mitigate
security threats. That wording matters: engineered against identified threats,
not applied from a generic checklist.

**What that looks like here.** You can name the threats that actually apply to
your service and the control that answers each one. You have pushed back on a
design because of what it exposed. You know what your service logs, where the
logs go, and what would happen if they leaked.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead developer. What changes is that you

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code understandable, testable and
safe to change — version control, small changes, review, automated testing,
continuous integration, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you apply modern development
standards *and support others in applying them*. The second half is the senior
part, and it is the half most often missing from the evidence.

**What that looks like here.** Your reviews teach rather than merely approve.
You have set a convention in your team and explained the reasoning behind it
rather than asserting it. A junior colleague's work looks different since you
started pairing with them.

**The next step.** One of the two skills that genuinely changes at lead
developer: you identify and apply standards across complex projects and
programmes, and you lead others in applying them. Supporting becomes leading,
and one team becomes several.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** The core craft: reviewing specifications, then designing,
coding, testing, and documenting programs of real complexity with the right
standards and tools.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with others
to review specifications, and use the agreed specifications to design, code,
test and document programs or scripts of medium-to-high complexity, using the
right standards and tools.

**What that looks like here.** You take an ambiguous requirement, ask the
questions that resolve it, and come back with a design before you come back with
code. Your work is tested at the level that matters rather than at the level
that is easy. What you leave behind can be picked up by someone who was not in
the room.

**The next step.** The other skill that changes at lead: advising on how
standards and methods should be applied, holding technical responsibility across
all stages and iterations of a project, and setting team-based standards for
tools and techniques. Responsibility moves from your work to the project's work.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, quickly and
disposably, so decisions rest on evidence rather than argument.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others; that
you establish design patterns and iterate them; and that you use a variety of
prototyping methods and choose the most appropriate one.

**What that looks like here.** You have settled a disagreement by building two
versions in a day rather than debating for a week. You ask other people for
prototypes rather than producing them all yourself. You know when a prototype
should be thrown away, and you throw it away.

### Skill: Service support

**What this means.** Keeping live services working — finding faults, locating

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you identify, locate and fix

you build, and alongside improving robustness, resilience and stability — so the
expectation is not only that you fix, but that fewer things break.

**What that looks like here.** You have led the investigation on something
genuinely puzzling, not just applied a known remedy. Your incident write-ups

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of a system — its components, their
boundaries and how they fit together — and choosing the standards, methods and
tools that shape it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design systems of medium
risk, impact and complexity; that you select appropriate design standards,
methods and tools and ensure they are applied effectively; and that you review
other people's designs for appropriate technology, efficient use of resources
and integration across systems and technologies.

**What that looks like here.** You have written a design that someone else
built. You have reviewed a design and changed its direction. You can say why
your system is shaped the way it is, including the parts you would not choose
again.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Making separate systems work together — defining the build,
co-ordinating it across teams, and testing the seams where things actually
break.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you define the integration
build, co-ordinate build activities across systems, and understand how to
undertake and support integration testing.

**What that looks like here.** You have owned an integration with a system you
do not control, agreed the contract with the team who own it, and worked out
what happens when it is unavailable. You test the failure paths, not only the
successful ones.

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the people who use the service at the centre of
technical decisions — and knowing the difference between what they need and what
they ask for.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with user
researchers and can represent users internally; explain the difference between
user needs and user desires; champion user research so it covers all users;
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story and guide
others in doing so; and recommend the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You attend research rather than reading the
summary. You have argued for a technical decision on the grounds of what it does
for users, and against one for the same reason. When someone says "all users",
you ask about the ones who use assistive technology, or have no signal, or are
doing this once in their life under stress.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead developer.
