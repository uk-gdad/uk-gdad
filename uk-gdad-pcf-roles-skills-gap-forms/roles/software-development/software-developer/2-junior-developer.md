# Junior developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome to this form.

It is for you if you work as a junior developer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Someone in your organisation — human resources, or your manager
— has sent it to you so that you can say what you already do, what you have not
had a chance to try, and what would help you most over the next few months.

Nothing here is scored, marked or graded, and nobody is comparing your answers
with a colleague's. Junior is a learning level by design: you deliver real work,
but you do it under supervision, and gaps are exactly what supervision is for.

The form walks through the nine skills the Government Digital and Data
Profession Capability Framework names for a junior developer. For each one it
explains what the skill means, what is expected of you now, what it looks like
in real work, and what changes at the next level. Then it asks you to rate
yourself and give an example. You will not need any other page open.

Most people take about 30 to 45 minutes. You can stop and come back.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

This page is a web page. You cannot type into it and there is no button to send
it. Nothing you write goes anywhere near this website.

So pick whichever suits you:

- Print the page and write on it.
- Copy the questions into a document or an email, and answer there.
- Answer in whatever tool your organisation already uses.

Every question has a number, like **Q7**, so your answers make sense on their own
without the questions attached.

Before you start:

- **"I don't know" is a real answer**, and a useful one. It tells people what to
  teach you.
- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a mark against you.
- **One real example beats a general claim.** A single ticket or a single bug is
  plenty.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers or what
  they are used for, ask before you fill it in. That is a fair question.

## Rating scale

Some questions ask you to rate yourself. Use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It is about **what you do at work today**, not what you could probably manage
  or what a course covered. If you have only read about it, that is a 1.
- **There is no target rating.** At junior, a lot of 2s is the expected picture,
  because "I do it with support" is a fair description of the level.

Here is what a filled-in answer looks like:

> **Systems integration**
>
> - [x] 1 — Awareness: I know what it is and why it matters
>
> *Evidence:* I understand why our service calls the address lookup API and what
> happens when it times out, but I have not built or tested an interface myself.

One real thing, in a sentence or two. That is the level of detail to aim for.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs. In this role you write clean, secure
code following a test-driven approach, and you create code that is open by
default and easy for others to reuse.

**Your level: junior developer.** A junior developer learns on the job by
delivering software components. The framework expects that you:

- develop your skills while working under supervision to deliver stories in a
  multidisciplinary team
- understand and are proficient in different types of testing
- are aware of, but not responsible for, security
- coach and mentor more junior colleagues

That last one surprises people. Yes, at junior you are already expected to help
someone newer than you — an apprentice, a new starter, someone who has not seen
this part of the system. It counts, and this form asks about it.

Notice too what "aware of but not responsible for security" means. You are meant
to use the security practices your team has, and to ask when something looks
wrong. You are not meant to be deciding security architecture.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice developer
2. **Junior developer** — you are here
3. Developer
4. Senior developer
5. Senior developer (management)
6. Lead developer
7. Lead developer (management)
8. Principal developer
9. Principal developer (management)

Your next step is developer. Almost every skill below grows at that step, and
the pattern is the same each time: things you currently explain, you start to
do, and things you currently do with help, you start to do on your own.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Information security
3. Modern development standards
4. Programming and build (software engineering)
5. Prototyping
6. Service support
7. Systems design
8. Systems integration
9. User focus

## Job skills

Nine skills, in the framework's order. Each explains the skill, what is expected

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** "Availability" is whether the service is up and working for
the people using it. "Capacity" is whether it copes with how many people use it
and how much they ask of it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can *explain* the
processes. You are not expected to manage them.

**What that looks like here.** You could tell a new starter what your team
watches and where. You know what "the service is down" means in practice for
your service. You have seen a graph go the wrong way and understood why.

**The next step.** At developer this changes from explaining to doing: you
manage service components so they meet business needs and key performance
indicators. Worth knowing now, because it is one of the bigger jumps.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Keeping information and services safe from people who
should not reach them, and from accidents that expose them. "Security controls"
are the specific protections: sign-in, permissions, encryption, code review,
keeping secrets out of code.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you *use* information
security practices and the available controls, to contribute to protecting
solutions and services. You contribute — you are not responsible for the design.

**What that looks like here.** You keep credentials out of the code and know
where they belong instead. You follow the access rules rather than working round
them. You have raised something that looked wrong, even if it turned out fine.

**The next step.** At developer you design solutions and services with security
controls built in, engineered against specific threats. So it moves from using
what exists to designing what is needed.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The shared ways of working that keep code understandable,
testable and safe to change — version control, small changes, code review,
automated tests, automated builds, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: that you can explain
the most important principles and how they apply to your own work, and that you
apply them under guidance.

**What that looks like here.** You can say why the team keeps changes small, and
point at a time it saved you. Your branches, commits and pull requests follow
the team's conventions without someone reminding you. When a review comment says
"split this up", you know why.

**The next step.** At developer you apply the standards *and support others in
applying them* — so the step is from doing it yourself to helping someone else
do it.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** The craft itself: designing a piece of software, writing
it, testing it, correcting it and documenting it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design, code, test,
correct and document simple programs or scripts under the direction of others.
Working from someone else's steer is part of the level, not a shortfall in it.

**What that looks like here.** You take a well-described story and finish it,
including its tests. You respond to review comments and understand the reasons
behind them. When you break something, you can find your way back.

**The next step.** At developer this grows in two directions: you collaborate on
reviewing the specification rather than receiving it, and the work moves from
simple programs to medium-to-high complexity.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building a rough version of something to learn from it. The
point is the learning; the prototype itself is meant to be thrown away.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you understand when to use a
particular prototyping technique or method, and that you show the value of
prototyping to your team.

**What that looks like here.** You have built something rough to answer a
question rather than arguing about the answer. You can say why a paper sketch
suits one question and a working spike suits another. You have shown a rough
version to someone and got a useful reaction.

**The next step.** At developer prototyping becomes a team activity you help
run: soliciting prototypes from others, testing them together, establishing
design patterns and choosing between several methods.

### Skill: Service support

**What this means.** Keeping a live service working — investigating faults,
fixing them, and doing the routine maintenance that prevents them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you help fix service faults
following agreed procedures, and that you carry out maintenance tasks on service
support infrastructure. The move from apprentice is from "do this specific
thing" to "follow the procedure".

**What that looks like here.** You can follow a runbook without someone reading
it to you. You have picked up a support ticket and seen it through. You have
done the unglamorous maintenance — certificates, dependency updates, log tidying
— and know why it matters.

**The next step.** At developer the framework describes this skill in the same
words. What changes around it is that you are expected to operate the services
you build and identify issues in production, so support stops being a rota duty
and becomes part of owning the thing.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of software — which parts there are,
what each one is responsible for, and how they fit together.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you assist, as part of a
team, in designing components of larger systems. Assisting is the expectation:
you take part in the thinking, you are not holding the pen.

**What that looks like here.** You have been in the room when a design was
discussed, and asked a question that changed something. You can draw your part
of the system on a whiteboard. You understand why the piece you work on is
separate from the piece next to it.

**The next step.** At developer this grows considerably: translating logical
designs into physical ones, producing detailed designs, documenting them to a
standard, and designing systems with managed levels of risk using well
understood technology.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Making separate systems work together — the interfaces
between them, and what happens when the other side is slow, wrong or missing.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can explain the process
and principles of integrating systems, and describe the challenges of designing,
building and testing interfaces between them.

**What that looks like here.** You know which other systems yours talks to and
what it asks them for. You can say what happens when one of them does not
answer. You understand why testing across a boundary is harder than testing
inside one.

**The next step.** At developer you start building: simple interfaces yourself,
and more complex integration as part of a wider team.

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the person who will use the service in mind while
you build it. "User experience analysis" is finding out what people actually
need and where the service gets in their way. A "user story" describes a need
from that person's point of view.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you show awareness or
understanding of user experience analysis and its principles, and that you can
explain the purpose of user stories and the focus on user needs.

**What that looks like here.** You read the user story before starting and can
say who it is for. You have watched or heard about user research. When something
in the code seems strange, you can often connect it to a real person's need.

**The next step.** At developer this becomes much bigger: collaborating with
user researchers, representing users internally, explaining the difference
between a need and a want, championing research that covers all users, and
guiding others in understanding the user story.
