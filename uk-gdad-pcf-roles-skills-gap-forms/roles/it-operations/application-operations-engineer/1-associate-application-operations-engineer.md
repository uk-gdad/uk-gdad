# Associate application operations engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you work as an associate application operations engineer
in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can say what you do now, what you would like to get better at, and what would help.

The framework describes this level plainly: as a trainee, you work in an established team and support
application operations engineers in their daily work, under their direction.

Application operations is a keeping-things-working job. When an application that people depend on
stops behaving, someone has to notice, work out what changed, and get it back. Learning that craft
starts with watching how experienced people diagnose things.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. There are no right answers. Four skills — the smallest set
at any level of this role — each with a plain explanation, a rating, and a couple of questions. Take
about 20 minutes.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **You can skip anything.** Leaving a question blank does not count against you.
- **Answer about what you actually do**, not what you are supposed to be learning.
- **"I have not done this yet" is the right answer at a trainee level** for a lot of these.
- **Do not include anything sensitive about your systems** — no hostnames, credentials, or details of
  an unresolved weakness. Describe the work at a level you would be comfortable saying in a corridor.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who will see your answers, what they will be used for,
  or how long they will be kept, it is completely reasonable to ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: application operations engineer.** An application operations engineer supports, manages
and maintains a single application or a suite of applications.

**Your level: associate application operations engineer.** As a trainee, you work in an established
team and support application operations engineers in their daily work, under their direction.

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. **Associate application operations engineer** — you are here
2. Application operations engineer
3. Senior application operations engineer
4. Lead application operations engineer
5. Principal application operations engineer

Your next step is application operations engineer: maintaining services, conducting user, and security
administration, monitoring applications and being accountable for batch processes.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Your level has **four** skills. The level above has
**eleven**. That is the largest jump in the whole framework, and it is worth planning for rather than
discovering. The seven that appear are asset and configuration management, availability and capacity
management, change management, service focus, service management framework knowledge, technical
specialism, and testing.

One of those — service management framework knowledge — is worded as holding a **Level 3 service
management framework qualification**. It is one of very few places in the framework that names a
qualification, so it is worth asking now whether your organisation will fund it.

**The four skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Incident management
2. Ownership and initiative
3. Problem management
4. User focus

### Skill: Incident management

**What the framework expects at your level.** One thing: identify and register incidents, gathering
the required information and allocating them to the appropriate channel.

**What that means day to day.** Someone reports that something is broken, or a monitor fires. Your job
is to record it properly — what is affected, since when, who is impacted, what has changed recently —
and get it to the right people. A well-written incident record saves the next person twenty minutes.

**What changes at application operations engineer.** Nothing — the wording is **identical** at that
level. This skill first changes at senior, so incident handling is one thing you can get genuinely
good at without waiting for a promotion.

### Skill: Ownership and initiative

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: show an awareness of problem resolution
processes; and **pass problems on to your team**.

**What that means day to day.** At this level you are explicitly not expected to own a problem
through to resolution. Knowing when something is beyond you and handing it over cleanly — with what
you have already checked — is the skill.

**What changes at application operations engineer.** You would own an issue until a new owner has been
found or the problem has been mitigated or resolved. That is a real change: no more handing over and
moving on.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: investigate problems in systems, processes
and services, with an understanding of the level of a problem — strategic, tactical or operational;
and contribute to the implementation of remedies and preventative measures.

**What that means day to day.** An incident is one thing breaking now; a problem is the underlying
reason it keeps happening. *Operational* is today's failure, *tactical* is the pattern, *strategic* is
the design decision that makes the pattern inevitable.

**What changes at application operations engineer.** Nothing — the wording is **identical**.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: show an awareness or understanding of user
experience analysis and its principles; and explain the purpose of user stories and the focus on user
needs.

**What that means day to day.** The applications you keep running exist so that people can do
something — claim, apply, report, get paid. When one is slow or down, someone somewhere cannot do that
thing. Keeping that in view changes how you prioritise.

**What changes at application operations engineer.** A large jump: you would identify and engage with
users or stakeholders to collate evidence of user needs, understand and define research that fits
those needs, and use quantitative and qualitative data to turn user focus into outcomes.
