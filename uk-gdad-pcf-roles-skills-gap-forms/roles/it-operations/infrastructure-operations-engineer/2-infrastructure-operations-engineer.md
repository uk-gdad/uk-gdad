# Infrastructure operations engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as an infrastructure operations engineer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where
the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as responsible for the **preparation and support of IT operations
solutions and services, physical or virtual**, according to industry and organisational best practices,
standards, service requirements and key performance indicators throughout the product life cycle. At this
level you:

- create and maintain documentation
- perform all routine tasks according to process and checklists
- monitor infrastructure and applications services
- alert and take appropriate action

That is an unusually specific set of accountabilities, and they describe a discipline more than a
technology: doing it the same way every time, writing down what you did, and noticing early.

Twelve skills. Expect about 35 minutes.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you actually do at work**, not what you could probably manage.
- **The gap between 2 and 3 is the exceptions.** Following the checklist is a 2 or 3. Knowing what to do when
  the checklist does not fit the situation in front of you is a 3.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Availability and capacity management**
>

>
> *Evidence:* I watch the dashboards and raise it when something trends badly. I could not tell you what our
> key performance indicators actually are, or whether we are meeting them.

Names the practice and the gap in the same breath.

## About this role level

**The role: infrastructure operations engineer.** An infrastructure operations engineer supports, manages and
maintains the core infrastructure that underpins production services.

**Your level: infrastructure operations engineer.** You are responsible for the preparation and support of IT
operations solutions and services, physical or virtual, with the accountabilities listed in the introduction.

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. Associate infrastructure operations engineer
2. **Infrastructure operations engineer** — you are here
3. Senior infrastructure operations engineer
4. Lead infrastructure operations engineer
5. Principal infrastructure operations engineer

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, **you carry three times the skills of the level below you.** The associate has four; you have twelve.
Eight skills arrive at once, along with a **Level 3 service management framework qualification**. If you have
recently stepped up, that is why it feels like a lot — it genuinely is.

Second, **the qualification is a named requirement, not a suggestion.** *Service management framework
knowledge* at your level is simply "show you have a Level 3 service management framework qualification". If
you do not hold one, that is a concrete, fundable gap rather than a vague development area, and it is worth
raising in exactly those terms.

**The twelve skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Asset and configuration management
2. Availability and capacity management
3. Change management
4. Coding and scripting
5. Incident management
6. Ownership and initiative
7. Problem management
8. Service focus
9. Service management framework knowledge

11. Testing

### Skill: Asset and configuration management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: **maintain secure configuration and accurate
information**; **control IT assets in one or more areas**; and **verify the location and state of IT assets**.

**New at your level** — the associate does not have this skill.

**What that looks like here.** Knowing what exists, where it is, and what state it is in. Verifying is the
part that decays: records are accurate on the day they are written and drift quietly afterwards.

### Skill: Change management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **implement change requests**; and **apply change
control procedures under supervision**.

**New at your level**, and note "under supervision" — you are expected to follow the procedure, not own it.

**What that looks like here.** Making the change as approved, at the approved time, with a way back if it
goes wrong. The back-out plan is the part people skip and later regret.

**The next step.** At senior you implement changes and apply change control procedures without the
supervision clause.

### Skill: Technical specialism

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: **assist in technical support activities and carry
out agreed or routine maintenance and administration tasks.**

**New at your level.**

**What that looks like here.** The routine work done properly — patching, backups, account administration,
scheduled maintenance. Unglamorous and load-bearing.

**The next step.** At senior this skill changes shape entirely: using management system software and tools,
using logical schemata to investigate problems and create reports, carrying out routine configuration and
installation of **databases** and related products, and optimising performance and forecasting resource
needs. If databases are not part of your work now, that is worth knowing about early.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: **identify and engage with users or stakeholders
to collate user needs evidence**; **understand and define research that fits user needs**; and **use
quantitative and qualitative data about users to turn user focus into outcomes**.

**A large jump from associate**, where it was showing awareness of user experience analysis and explaining
user stories. Engaging, defining and using data, now.

**What that looks like here.** Finding out what the outage actually did to people, rather than how long it
lasted. Two hours in the middle of the night and two hours mid-morning are the same number and not the same
event.
