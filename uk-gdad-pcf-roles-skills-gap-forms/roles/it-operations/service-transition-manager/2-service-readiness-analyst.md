# Service readiness analyst - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a service readiness analyst in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where the
gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as **testing the products** and being **accountable for application
acceptance and checking whether security criteria have been met**. At this level you **flag any potential
risks and escalate these to the service transition manager**.

Accountable is a strong word, and the framework uses it deliberately here. You are answerable for whether an
application should be accepted and whether it meets the security criteria — usually while somebody with more
authority than you would like it to go live this week.

Eleven skills. Expect about 30 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: service transition manager.** IT service transition managers provide overall planning for service
transitions and co-ordinate the required resources. The role controls the product life cycle of all changes,
enabling beneficial changes to be made with minimum disruption to IT services.

**Your level: service readiness analyst.** You test the products and are accountable for application
acceptance and checking whether security criteria have been met, flagging potential risks and escalating them
to the service transition manager.

**Where this level sits.** This role has four levels:

1. Service acceptance analyst
2. **Service readiness analyst** — you are here
3. Service transition manager
4. Lead service transition manager

**Something worth knowing about the levels here.** Unlike most roles in this framework, the two lowest levels
have **different job names rather than a junior and senior version of the same one**. A service acceptance
analyst validates against acceptance criteria; you test products and own application and security acceptance.
Whether the step between them is a promotion or a sideways move is worth clarifying with your manager rather
than assuming.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, **community collaboration is set surprisingly high at your level.** It asks you to contribute to the
work of others, **motivate and empower teams**, create the right environment for teams to work in, identify
the best team makeup for a situation, and recognise and deal with issues. That is a set of leadership
expectations, and the wording is **identical at service transition manager and at lead service transition
manager** — all three levels read the same. If you have no team, rate honestly and say why; the gap is in
the framework's fit, not in you.

Second, **your security accountability is not matched by a security skill.** You are accountable for checking
whether security criteria have been met, and there is no information security skill in your list. Where you
learn what good looks like is therefore a real question, and a fair one to raise.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: **contribute to the work of others**; **motivate
and empower teams**; **create the right environment for teams to work in, and identify the best team makeup
depending on the situation**; and **recognise and deal with issues**.

**A large jump from service acceptance analyst**, where it was understanding the work of others and the

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: identify and engage with users or stakeholders to
collate user needs evidence; understand and define research that fits user needs; and use quantitative and
qualitative data about users to turn user focus into outcomes.

**Identical to service acceptance analyst.**

**What that looks like here.** You test whether a product works. Whether it works *for the people who will
use it* is a different question, and transition is one of the last places anyone can still ask it.

**Identical at service transition manager as well.** It changes only at lead, where it becomes collaborating
with user researchers, representing users internally, and championing user research for all users.
