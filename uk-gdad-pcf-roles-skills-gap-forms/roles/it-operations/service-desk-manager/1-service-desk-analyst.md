# Service desk analyst - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello. This form is for you if you work as a service desk analyst in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can say what you can do now, what you
cannot do yet, and what would help.

The framework describes this level in four words: **a service desk analyst interacts with customers**. At
this level you:

- perform tasks such as taking calls from customers and trying to resolve their queries
- **know when to escalate issues to your manager**

That is a short description for a demanding job. Everything difficult about the service desk — the person who
is angry, the person who is embarrassed, the query that turns out to be three queries — sits inside
"interacts with customers".

Six skills. Expect about 20 minutes.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page, or print it out, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation
uses for these conversations. Every question has a number, so your answers
make sense on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy. If other people use this computer, press it when you have finished.

A few things worth knowing:

- **You can skip questions.** A blank is not a mark against you.
- **"I do not know" is a real answer.** It often shows someone where to help you next.
- **Do not put anything confidential in here** — no passwords, no ticket details, no names of the people who
  contacted you.
- **Ask who will read it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or how long
  they are kept, it is completely fine to ask before you fill it in.

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you actually do at work**, not what you could probably manage.
- **A 2 is a good and normal answer** at this level. It means you do the thing and someone is there when it
  gets unusual. That is exactly how a service desk should work.

## About this role level

**The role: service desk manager.** A service desk manager is responsible for managing the first- and
second-line technical support for all departmental IT applications and services across sites, including end
user computing — multi-function devices and specialised IT equipment included. The role is also responsible
for ensuring support for existing and emerging information and communications technology services, including
providing technical advice to project teams.

**Your level: service desk analyst.** You interact with customers, taking calls and trying to resolve
queries, and you know when to escalate.

**Where this level sits.** This role has four levels:

1. **Service desk analyst** — you are here
2. Senior service desk analyst
3. Service desk manager
4. Head of service desk

Your next step is senior service desk analyst: monitoring inbound calls, supporting operators in service
resolution, intervening in difficult calls, owning and updating the script analysts work from, and coaching
one or more analysts.

**Two things worth knowing about that step.**

First, **it adds two skills you do not have yet** — problem management, and a **Level 3 service management
framework qualification**. The qualification takes time and usually money, so it is worth asking about early
rather than at a promotion conversation.

Second, **the step is mostly about other people.** Intervening in difficult calls, coaching analysts, owning
the script. If you want to move up, the useful preparation is helping colleagues rather than closing more
tickets yourself.

**The six skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Asset and configuration management
2. Customer service management
3. IT service reporting
4. Ownership and initiative
5. Service focus
6. User focus

### Skill: Asset and configuration management

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: **track, log and correct information to protect
assets and components.**

**In plain terms.** Keep the records straight as things move — a laptop reassigned, a phone returned, a
licence freed up. Correcting is the part that matters: fixing a record you notice is wrong rather than
working around it.

**Worded identically at every level of this role** — analyst, senior analyst, manager, and head of service
desk all read the same. It is the only skill here that never rises, so anything you build now counts all the
way up.

### Skill: Customer service management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: **resolve user requests to a minimum of the
agreed service level agreement**; **empathise with the end user**; and **use customer-focused metrics to
achieve the desired outcome**.

**This is the core skill of your level** — the only one where the published text describes the actual job
rather than a supporting activity.

**The empathy item is not decoration.** People contact a service desk when something has stopped them
working, often when they are already late for something. The technical fix and the way it is delivered are
both part of the resolution.

**A note on metrics.** Customer-focused metrics are the ones about the person's experience — was it resolved,
did they have to chase, did they have to explain twice — rather than the ones about your throughput.

**The next step.** At senior service desk analyst it becomes resolving requests while maintaining a high
level of customer service, **dealing with complex complaints**, and using empathy to understand and meet
customer needs.

### Skill: IT service reporting

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: **produce relevant reports in an appropriate
format and agreed timeframe**; work with stakeholders to discuss any changes in the reporting processes; and
**interpret a data set and communicate this to others**.

**Set surprisingly high for a first level** — this is exactly the same wording used at senior service desk
analyst. At service desk manager it collapses to one line about using data analytics to make decisions, so
producing reports is assumed from there upwards.

### Skill: Ownership and initiative

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: **own an issue until a new owner has been found or
the problem has been mitigated or resolved.**

**Identical at senior service desk analyst.** At service desk manager it becomes taking accountability for
issues and being proactive in searching for potential problems.

**In plain terms.** A ticket you passed on is still yours until someone confirms they have it. Transferring is
not the same as handing over.

### Skill: Service focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: **show an awareness of different products and
services.**

**In plain terms.** Know what your organisation runs, roughly what each thing is for, and who uses it.

**Why it helps you directly.** Half of diagnosing a query is knowing which service the person is actually
describing, which is rarely the name they use for it.

**The next step.** At senior service desk analyst it becomes taking inputs and establishing coherent
frameworks that work — from knowing about services to making the way you handle them consistent.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **show an awareness or understanding of user
experience analysis and its principles**; and **explain the purpose of user stories and the focus on user
needs**.

**In plain terms.** Understand that the point of all this is somebody being able to do their job, and that
what people ask for is not always what they need.

**A user story** is a short sentence describing what someone needs and why: "As a caseworker, I need my
laptop to connect from home so that I can work on the days I am not in the office." It keeps the reason
attached to the request.

**The next step.** A significant jump: at senior service desk analyst you identify and engage with users to
collate evidence of user needs, define research, and use quantitative and qualitative data.
