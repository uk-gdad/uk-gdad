# Product manager - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a product manager in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as being responsible for product quality and
value, working with the team to identify, implement and improve strategies and
tactics for your products — and possibly recruiting and managing people in the
product profession. It expects you to lead a product through the whole life
cycle, from discovery through development, delivery and continuous improvement to
retirement; to support lead and senior product managers; and to prioritise work
for your teams and be able to measure outcomes.

**This can be an entry-level role.** The framework says so explicitly — for
people who have been working in other digital roles. So this form sits in an
awkward place: some readers have been product managers for years, and some
arrived last month from delivery, design or analysis. Both are correctly at this
level. Answer for where you actually are.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Nine skills, each with an explanation, a
rating and some questions. Expect about 35 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: product manager.** A product manager makes sure that their products
provide value and achieve the right outcomes, by balancing user and business
needs. In this role you will:

- work with multidisciplinary teams, bringing together their views, user needs
  and business needs, to frame the problem
- work with multidisciplinary teams to provide the right outcomes for users and
  the organisation
- make decisions on priorities for your teams
- ensure teams comply with appropriate standards

**Your level: product manager.** You are responsible for product quality and
value, working with the team to identify, implement and improve strategies and
tactics for your products. You might recruit and manage people in the product
profession, making sure people with the right skills are hired, supported and
developed. The framework expects that you:

- lead a product through different phases of the product life cycle, from
  discovery, through development, delivery and continuous improvement, to
  retirement
- support lead and senior product managers
- prioritise work for your teams and be able to measure outcomes

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. Associate product manager
2. **Product manager** — you are here
3. Senior product manager
4. Lead product manager
5. Head of product

Your next step is senior product manager: a group of products sharing a value
proposition, or one complex, high-risk or sensitive product; managing associate
and product managers; and being involved in recruitment. On the published skills,
five of the nine are **identical** between your level and senior — Agile and Lean
practices, applying user-centred insights, and stakeholder relationship
management are word for word the same. What changes is creating value for money,
life cycle management, managing product outcomes, product leadership, product
management and strategic ownership.

That matters for a promotion conversation. On more than half the skills, the
distinction is not in the wording — it is in the scope of what you are doing and
the people you develop. Your accountabilities are where the case gets made.

**The nine skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile and Lean practices
2. Applying user-centred insights
3. Creating value for money
4. Life cycle management
5. Managing product outcomes
6. Product leadership
7. Product management
8. Stakeholder relationship management
9. Strategic ownership

## Job skills

Nine skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at senior product manager.

### Skill: Agile and Lean practices

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: compare and select the
most appropriate delivery methods and processes; recognise, reflect and adapt
when something does not work, encouraging experimentation; use a blended approach
depending on the context; help teams to measure, evaluate and visualise outcomes;
and encourage reflective practice to improve ways of working.

**What that looks like here.** You choose the way of working rather than
inheriting it, and you change it when it stops fitting. Retrospectives lead
somewhere. Experiments are allowed to fail without anyone being blamed for them.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior product manager. This skill does
not change until lead, where you coach and lead teams in it and act as a
recognised expert.

### Skill: Applying user-centred insights

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: use user insights to
represent users in internal discussions; advocate for research to be completed
with all types of users of the product or service; use user needs to agree the
work that needs to be done and determine priorities with the team; and work with
others to understand and recommend tools to get the appropriate user insight.

**What that looks like here.** In a meeting where nobody has met a user, you are
the one who has. "All types of users" is doing real work in that sentence — it
covers people using assistive technology, people in crisis, people who do not
speak English confidently, and staff on the other side of the service.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior product manager.

### Skill: Creating value for money

**What the framework expects at this level.** Six things: estimate the value of
each item on your roadmap; compare effort to value when deciding priorities;
decide when it is appropriate to reuse something and when it is not; decide how
and when to escalate risk in achieving value; discuss estimates with stakeholders
to validate understanding; and analyse the benefit of investment against
anticipated outcomes.

**What that looks like here.** Every roadmap item has a value you could defend
out loud, even roughly. You can say when reuse is the wrong answer — which is a
more senior judgement than saying when it is the right one. And when value is
slipping away, you say so early rather than at the review.

**The next step.** At senior you advocate for user needs against cost, write or
contribute to business cases, communicate business-value propositions, and help
others make value-based decisions.

### Skill: Life cycle management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: manage the delivery of
products and services that add value through different phases of the life cycle,
**with some support**; identify if the product should move to the next phase and
how to do this; ensure the product is developed in line with the appropriate
service standards for a phase; and use new information to adapt approaches to
life cycle management.

**What that looks like here.** You know which phase you are in and what it is
asking. You prepare for service assessments rather than being surprised by them.
"With some support" is in the framework's own wording — this level is not
expected to do it alone.

**The next step.** At senior you consult the right people at the right time, use
evidence to decide whether a team should continue, change direction or stop, and
guide colleagues and stakeholders through phases.

### Skill: Managing product outcomes

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work with others to
define key performance indicators that assess performance, value and outcomes;
analyse and use trusted data to make decisions and enable continuous improvement;
be open about your decision making; and identify and explain success metrics to
your stakeholders and how the product can contribute to outcomes.

**What that looks like here.** Your indicators measure something that matters
rather than something that is easy to count. "Trusted data" is the difficult
word — most product data has caveats, and knowing them is part of the skill.
Being open about decision making means people can see why, not just what.

**The next step.** At senior you lead product teams to create effective methods
for measuring outcomes, advocate for data-driven decision making, and use data to
drive continuous improvement.

### Skill: Product leadership

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: advocate for your
product and a product-led approach; seek opportunities for reuse and learning in
your area; protect the team, for example from unnecessary distractions or
requests to change direction; consult and mediate with stakeholders; and
participate in product management community activities.

**What that looks like here.** Protecting the team is the visible part — saying
no, or not yet, so the team can finish something. Mediating means holding a
disagreement between stakeholders rather than passing it to the team to absorb.

**The next step.** At senior you seek reuse across the whole organisation,
represent product management with senior stakeholders, coach and manage others,
and lead community activities rather than attending them.

### Skill: Product management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: work with your team to
investigate and validate what adds value now and what could add value in future;
create a prioritised roadmap and use it to discuss your product strategy with
stakeholders; address uncertainty while managing scope, ensuring critical
deadlines are met and value delivered; recommend a range of product management
methods; and work in the open and share your work.

**What that looks like here.** The roadmap is a conversation tool, not a
commitment schedule. Managing scope under uncertainty is the hardest item —
holding a deadline by changing what is in it rather than by hoping. Working in
the open means someone outside your team could find out what you are doing
without asking you.

**The next step.** At senior you set strategy and vision for products, plan
prioritised work across multiple teams while minimising dependencies, and coach
and mentor others.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work with the team to
develop and maintain an understanding of stakeholders; work with the team to
develop and implement stakeholder communications strategies; identify and resolve
issues, influence stakeholders and manage relationships effectively; and build
long-term strategic relationships, communicating clearly and regularly.

**What that looks like here.** Stakeholder knowledge lives in the team rather
than in your head. Communication runs on a rhythm rather than in a panic. You
resolve issues that would otherwise reach your team as noise.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior product manager. It changes at
lead, where you direct the stakeholder relationship strategy for your teams and
negotiate with senior stakeholders.

### Skill: Strategic ownership

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: engage and work with
users, stakeholders and others to develop a strategy and vision; ensure the
strategy sets out the value, benefits and outcomes needed to justify investment
in the feature, product or service; and regularly review and iterate the
strategy.

**What that looks like here.** There is a written product strategy, you wrote it
with others, and it says why the investment is worth making. It gets revisited
rather than written once and filed.

**The next step.** At senior you ensure the vision and strategy deliver the
intended benefits and policy intent, use evidence to challenge that when needed,
and get stakeholder agreement on the strategic approach.
