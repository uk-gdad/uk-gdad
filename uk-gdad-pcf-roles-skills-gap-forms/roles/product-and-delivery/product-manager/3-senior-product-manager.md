# Senior product manager - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior product manager in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as working on a group of products that share a
value proposition, or a single product that is complex, high risk or sensitive.
You are responsible for product quality and value across teams, and you may be
involved in parts of programme management. It expects you to lead products
through the whole life cycle; make decisions based on overall product quality and
value; manage associate product managers and product managers; and be involved in
recruiting them and contractors.

This is the level where the job stops being about one product and starts being
about other people's judgement. Four of your nine skills now say *coach*, *lead*
or *guide* where the level below said *do*.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Nine skills, each with an explanation, a
rating and some questions. Expect about 35 minutes.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"My teams do this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job
  description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you and would stay changed if you moved on. At senior, one, or two
  are reasonable and nine would not be believable.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Managing product outcomes**
>
> - [x] 3 — Practitioner: I do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions
>
> *Evidence:* I redesigned how two teams measure outcomes and both still use it.
> Connecting our product data to anyone else's is something I have proposed and
> not managed to land.

Named practice, and a clear line about where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: product manager.** A product manager makes sure that their products
provide value and achieve the right outcomes, by balancing user and business
needs. In this role you will:

- work with multidisciplinary teams, bringing together their views, user needs
  and business needs, to frame the problem
- work with multidisciplinary teams to provide the right outcomes for users and
  the organisation
- make decisions on priorities for your teams
- ensure teams comply with appropriate standards

**Your level: senior product manager.** You can work on a group of products that
share a value proposition, or a single product that is complex, high risk or
sensitive. You are responsible for product quality and value, working with teams
to identify, implement and improve strategies and tactics. You might also be
involved in parts of programme management activity. The framework expects that
you:

- lead products through different phases of the product life cycle, from
  discovery, through development, delivery and continuous improvement, to
  retirement
- make decisions based on the overall quality and value of your products
- manage associate product managers and product managers
- be involved in recruiting associate product managers, product managers and
  contractors

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. Associate product manager
2. Product manager
3. **Senior product manager** — you are here
4. Lead product manager
5. Head of product

Your next step is lead product manager: a product portfolio or an end-to-end
service, membership of the management team for your product area, the high-level
roadmap, and product leadership across multiple teams.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, four of your nine skills are worded **identically** at lead product
manager — creating value for money, managing product outcomes, product leadership
and product management. Those four are the same text at your level and the one
above. The difference is scope, not capability.

Second, the five that *do* change tell you exactly where lead differs: Agile and
Lean practices (you would coach and lead, as a recognised expert), applying
user-centred insights (advocacy across the organisation), life cycle management
(leading and coaching teams through phases, and assessing other teams),
stakeholder relationship management (directing the strategy and negotiating with
senior stakeholders), and strategic ownership (coaching others in creating
strategy and ensuring alignment across the organisation).

If you want the lead role, those five are the list.

**The nine skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile and Lean practices
2. Applying user-centred insights
3. Creating value for money
4. Life cycle management
5. Managing product outcomes
6. Product leadership
7. Product management
8. Stakeholder relationship management
9. Strategic ownership

## Job skills

Nine skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at lead product manager.

### Skill: Agile and Lean practices

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: compare and select the
most appropriate delivery methods and processes; recognise, reflect and adapt
when something does not work, encouraging experimentation; use a blended approach
depending on the context; help teams to measure, evaluate and visualise outcomes;
and encourage reflective practice to improve ways of working.

**Same wording as product manager.** This skill does not change between those two
levels, which is worth knowing if you are making a case at review.

**What that looks like here.** Different teams under you work differently
because their contexts differ, and you can explain why each choice was made
rather than defending a house style.

**The next step.** At lead you coach and lead teams in Agile and Lean good
practices, tailor and iterate the approach through the life cycle, experiment
with new ways of working to improve delivery across the organisation, and act as
a recognised expert.

### Skill: Applying user-centred insights

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: use user insights to
represent users in internal discussions; advocate for research to be completed
with all types of users of the product or service; use user needs to agree the
work that needs to be done and determine priorities with the team; and work with
others to understand and recommend tools to get the appropriate user insight.

**Same wording as product manager.**

**What that looks like here.** Across several teams, you are the person who
notices when a whole group of users has never been researched — and who protects
research budget when it is the easiest line to cut.

**The next step.** At lead you advocate for a range of research approaches across
the organisation, coach others in decisions that meet user needs across channels,
and use user insight to make strategic decisions.

### Skill: Creating value for money

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: advocate for user
needs, ensuring user value is balanced against cost and value for the
organisation; determine the benefit of a product and persuade others it is the
right product to use; write or contribute to business cases; communicate
business-value propositions; and help others make value-based decisions,
influencing the direction of development.

**What that looks like here.** You have written or contributed to a real business
case — the document that gets money released. You can hold the line on user value
in a conversation about cost, without pretending cost does not matter.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead product manager. It changes at head
of product, where you own a business case and iterate it through the life cycle,
and build the case for an organisational approach to value.

### Skill: Life cycle management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work and consult with
the right people at the right time to move through the life cycle and deliver
value; use evidence to decide when a team should continue, change direction or
stop; identify tools and techniques required at different phases; and guide
colleagues and stakeholders through different phases.

**What that looks like here.** *Stop* is the word that matters. Deciding on
evidence that a team should stop is one of the hardest things a senior product
manager does, and one of the most valuable. Guiding stakeholders through phases
usually means explaining, repeatedly, why beta does not mean nearly finished.

**The next step.** At lead you lead and coach teams through phases, ensure
effective support for products and users, predict and mitigate phase risks, and
contribute to assessing other teams.

### Skill: Product leadership

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: seek opportunities for
reuse and learning across your organisation; represent product management in your
area in conversations with senior stakeholders; protect teams from unnecessary
distractions and requests to change direction; coach and manage others in product
management; and lead some product management community activities and participate
in cross-government communities.

**What that looks like here.** You are the product voice in rooms your teams are
not in. You run something in the community rather than attending it. Protecting
teams is now plural and structural — a standing agreement rather than a case-by-
case rescue.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead product manager.

### Skill: Product management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: set the strategy and
vision for your products, collaborating with others; create a prioritised roadmap
for your products and use it to discuss product strategy at all levels; plan
prioritised work across multiple teams, minimising dependencies and risks;
support multiple teams in delivering quality outcomes in the right way; and coach
and mentor others in product management.

**What that looks like here.** Planning across teams while minimising
dependencies is the item that separates this from the level below. Most
cross-team pain is a dependency that could have been designed out earlier.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead product manager, and at head of
product. This skill does not change again in this role.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work with the team to
develop and maintain an understanding of stakeholders; work with the team to
develop and implement stakeholder communications strategies; identify and resolve
issues, influence stakeholders and manage relationships effectively; and build
long-term strategic relationships, communicating clearly and regularly.

**Same wording as product manager.**

**What that looks like here.** Across several teams, this becomes about
consistency — different teams telling the same stakeholder different things is
the failure mode you exist to prevent.

**The next step.** At lead you *direct* the stakeholder relationship strategy for
your teams, ensure stakeholders' objectives are set and support teams to meet
them, and negotiate with senior stakeholders to unblock progress.

### Skill: Strategic ownership

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: ensure that the
vision and strategy deliver the intended benefits and policy intent, and use
evidence to challenge this when needed; work with stakeholders to get agreement
on the strategic approach, build trust and adapt based on feedback; and
proactively review and iterate the strategy based on user and organisation
insights.

**What that looks like here.** *Challenge the policy intent with evidence* is the
demanding part. It means telling a policy colleague that what they asked for will
not produce what they want — and having the evidence to make that a conversation
rather than an argument.

**The next step.** At lead you support and coach others in creating and
implementing long-term strategy, influence stakeholders to support delivery, and
ensure strategic alignment across the organisation.
