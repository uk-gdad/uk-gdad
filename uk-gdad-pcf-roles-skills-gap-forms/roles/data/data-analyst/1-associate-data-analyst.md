# Associate data analyst - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you work as an associate data analyst
in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager
has sent it so that you can say what you do now, what you would like to get better
at, and what would help.

An associate data analyst supports data analysis activities and projects, and
develops their skills by working closely with other data professionals. The
framework expects you to support the development and delivery of data analysis and
data projects; develop analytical approaches and data visualisations, under
supervision; and work with others to understand the roles in a multidisciplinary
team and how data analysts work with them.

**Under supervision** appears twice in that list and several more times in the
skills below. It is in the framework's own wording, not a judgement about you.
Working with support is what this level is, so saying "I do this with help" is
the right answer rather than a weak one.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. There are no right answers. Nine skills,
each with a plain explanation, a rating, and a couple of questions. Take about 30
minutes, and stop and come back if you want to.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that
apply, and pick a rating where one is asked for. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

So: when you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so your answers
make sense on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy, which is worth doing on a computer other people use.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **You can skip anything.** Leaving a question blank does not count against you.
  If a question does not apply to your job, say so — that is useful in itself.
- **Answer about what you actually do**, not what your job description says. The
  gap between those two is the whole point.
- **"I have not had the chance to do this" is a good answer.** Much better than
  stretching a small example into a big one.
- **Be careful with data.** Do not paste real data, real records or anything
  identifying anyone into your answers. Describe the work, not the contents.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who will see your answers, what
  they will be used for, or how long they will be kept, it is completely
  reasonable to ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Some questions ask you to rate yourself. Use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could probably manage
  or what you covered on a course.
- **At this level, 1s and 2s are exactly right.** Five of your nine skills are
  written as *explain* rather than *do* — the framework is asking you to
  understand them before you own them. A form full of 4s would be less
  believable, not more impressive.

That is a good answer. It says what is done, who checks it, and where the gap is.

## About this role level

**The role: data analyst.** A data analyst collects, manages, explores and shares
data to support organisational objectives and deliver business impact. In this
role you will:

- identify, collect and move data between a range of systems
- apply data analysis and data visualisation tools and techniques
- prepare, manage and model data, following appropriate standards and practices
- communicate data, insights and conclusions in appropriate formats

**Your level: associate data analyst.** You support data analysis activities and
projects, and develop your skills by working closely with other data
professionals. The framework expects that you:

- support the development and delivery of data analysis and data projects
- develop analytical approaches and data visualisations, under supervision
- work with others to understand roles within a multidisciplinary team and how
  data analysts work with them

**Where this level sits.** This role has four levels:

1. **Associate data analyst** — you are here
2. Data analyst
3. Senior data analyst
4. Principal data analyst

Your next step is data analyst: preparing and managing data yourself, applying
techniques and tools to analyse it, and communicating data to stakeholders with
different needs. The supervision does not disappear at that level, but it thins
out considerably.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Five of your nine skills are worded
as *explain* — data ethics and privacy, data management, delivering business
impact through data, developing code for analysis, and managing a data project.
Being able to explain why data governance policies matter is a real achievement
at this level, and it is what the framework is asking for. Do not read those as
things you have failed to do.

**The nine skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Applying statistical and analytical tools and techniques
2. Communicating between the technical and non-technical
3. Data ethics and privacy
4. Data management
5. Data preparation and linkage
6. Data visualisation
7. Delivering business impact through data
8. Developing code for analysis
9. Managing a data project

### Skill: Applying statistical and analytical tools and techniques

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: support the
development of statistical and analytical insights and reports under supervision;
explain the benefits of basic statistical and analytical techniques; and explain
the value of quality assurance and best practices in developing statistical and
analytical outputs.

**What that means day to day.** Doing pieces of an analysis that someone else has
shaped, and being able to say why a technique was chosen. Quality assurance means
somebody checks the work before anyone relies on it — and the most useful habit
you can build now is checking your own before handing it over.

**What changes at data analyst.** You would contribute to insights and reports
rather than support them, apply techniques to answer real research questions, and
answer stakeholders' questions about the techniques themselves.

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain why it is
important to communicate technical concepts in non-technical language; and explain
the types of communication that can be used with internal and external
stakeholders, and their impact.

**What that means day to day.** Noticing when you have used a word — *outlier*,
*significant*, *confidence interval* — that means something different to a
non-specialist, and having another way to say it. *Significant* is the classic
trap: it means one thing in statistics and another in ordinary speech.

**What changes at data analyst.** You would communicate effectively with both
groups yourself, host discussions in a team with difficult dynamics, and manage
differing stakeholder perspectives.

### Skill: Data ethics and privacy

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain the importance
of using data ethics and privacy in your work; and identify appropriate channels
to discuss ethical issues, with support.

**What that means day to day.** Knowing that just because data can be joined,
analysed or published does not mean it should be — and knowing who to ask. The
second item is the practical one: at this level nobody expects you to resolve an
ethical question, but you should know where it goes.

**What changes at data analyst.** You would apply the fundamental principles
under supervision and raise ethics and privacy risks through the proper channels
yourself.

### Skill: Data management

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain the importance
of data governance policies; and explain how data management tools, procedures
and methods relate to a specific project.

**What that means day to day.** Data governance is the set of rules about who can
see what, how it is stored, how long it is kept and who is answerable. It exists
because the alternative is a spreadsheet of personal data on someone's laptop.

**What changes at data analyst.** You would follow the governance rather than
explain it — access, sharing, dissemination and protection — and use the
management tools with some support.

### Skill: Data preparation and linkage

**What the framework expects at your level.** Four things: explain the importance
of data models and their uses; identify appropriate channels in your organisation
to learn about data modelling and quality standards; identify data quality issues
and provide possible solutions; and prepare and cleanse data under supervision,
contributing to making it fit for purpose.

**What that means day to day.** Most analysis time is spent here, and nobody
warns you about that beforehand. Cleansing means fixing dates in three formats,
names entered five ways, and the row where someone typed a note into a number
field. Spotting a quality problem is genuinely valuable — analysts who do not,
publish confident nonsense.

**What changes at data analyst.** You would help plan a data model, make sure
models and pipelines can be reused, and resolve quality issues rather than
identifying them.

### Skill: Data visualisation

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: develop and use an
appropriate data visualisation under supervision to tell a meaningful story
relevant to set goals; explain the value of sharing a story effectively; and
explain why it is important to develop inclusive, accessible data visualisations
that recognise different user needs.

**What that means day to day.** Choosing the chart that answers the question
rather than the one that looks impressive. Accessible means it works for someone
using a screen reader, someone who cannot distinguish red from green, and someone
reading it printed in black and white — so never let colour be the only thing
carrying the meaning.

**What changes at data analyst.** You would choose the most appropriate
visualisation yourself, build accessible ones as a matter of course, and work with
the team to make sure it gives them what they need.

### Skill: Delivering business impact through data

product is something reusable — a dashboard, a regular report, a dataset others
build on — rather than a one-off answer.

priorities, help identify business requirements, and develop and deliver data

### Skill: Developing code for analysis

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain the importance
of coding for analysis; explain the value of maintaining analytical approach
documentation; and design an analytical approach with support, for example by
writing simple code.

**What that means day to day.** Writing code — usually R, Python or SQL — rather
than clicking through a spreadsheet, because code can be rerun, reviewed and
checked. Documentation here means writing down what you did and why, so that
someone else, or you in six months, can follow it.

**What changes at data analyst.** You would design the approach and the code for
simple research questions, explain why testing code matters, and write
documentation describing the code you wrote.

### Skill: Managing a data project

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: understand a problem
statement and explain the intended outcome of a data project; explain the
importance of data in solving a problem; and explain why it is important to adapt
your ways of working to deliver different project outcomes and requirements.

**What that means day to day.** A problem statement says what question is being
answered and for whom. Projects that skip it produce analysis nobody asked for.
At your level, being able to say what a project is *for* is the expectation.

**What changes at data analyst.** You would contribute to defining problem
statements and gathering requirements, and contribute to planning the stages of a
data project.

personal data, official information, or both. These questions are about awareness,
training and support — not about whether you can recite the law. Nobody expects
that at any level.
