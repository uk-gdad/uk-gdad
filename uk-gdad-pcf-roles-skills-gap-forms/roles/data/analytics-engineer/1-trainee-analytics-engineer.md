# Trainee analytics engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you are a trainee analytics engineer
in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager
has sent it so that you can say what you do now, what you would like to get better
at, and what would help.

The framework is refreshingly direct about this level: a trainee analytics
engineer attends training and develops skills on the job. It expects you to spend
time shadowing other analytics engineers, build your knowledge of the
organisation, learn skills for managing data, learn to use different applications
and tools, handle simple queries from users and document their data requirements,
and contribute to data documentation and user training.

**Learning is the job.** Not a preliminary to the job — the job. So "I have not
done that yet" is the expected answer to a lot of what follows, and writing it
down is exactly what this form is for.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. There are no right answers. Seven
skills, each with a plain explanation, a rating, and a couple of questions. Take
about 25 minutes, and stop and come back if you want to.

## Advisory

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that
apply, and pick a rating where one is asked for. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

So: when you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so your answers
make sense on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy, which is worth doing on a computer other people use.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **You can skip anything.** Leaving a question blank does not count against you.
- **Answer about what you actually do**, not what you are supposed to be learning.
- **"I have not done this yet" is the right answer at a trainee level** for a lot
  of these, and it is far more useful than stretching a small example.
- **Do not paste real data** into your answers — no records, no identifiers,
  nothing that would identify a person. Describe the work instead.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who will see your answers, what
  they will be used for, or how long they will be kept, it is completely
  reasonable to ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Some questions ask you to rate yourself. Use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you covered in training.
- **0s and 1s are completely normal here.** The framework's own wording for your
  level uses *show an awareness*, *explain*, and *under supervision*. A 1 means
  "I know what it is and why it matters" — for several of these skills, that is
  precisely the published expectation.

Here is what a filled-in answer looks like:

## About this role level

**The role: analytics engineer.** An analytics engineer transforms data relevant
to the organisation into structures that enable analysis and decision making. It
sits between data engineering, which moves data around, and data analysis, which
asks questions of it — your job is making the data usable. In this role you will:

- work with subject matter experts to understand organisational processes and
  translate these into data structures optimised for analysis
- work with data users to design and build data models they can use, using
  modelling techniques such as Kimball or Inmon
- support data quality improvement
- develop standards for data transformation
- create and maintain data documentation
- refine requirements in response to feedback and changes in the organisation
- provide ongoing support to users

**Your level: trainee analytics engineer.** You attend training and develop skills
on the job. The framework expects that you:

- spend time shadowing other analytics engineers
- build your knowledge of the organisation
- learn skills for managing data
- learn to use different applications, tools, templates and best practices
- handle simple queries from users and document their data requirements
- contribute to data documentation and user training

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. **Trainee analytics engineer** — you are here
2. Analytics engineer
3. Senior analytics engineer
4. Lead analytics engineer
5. Head of analytics engineering

Your next step is analytics engineer: developing and testing data models assigned
to you by more senior colleagues, drafting documentation to standard, and
supporting trainees yourself.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Your level has **seven** skills. Every
level above has eight — *Programming and build (data and analytics engineering)*
appears from analytics engineer upwards and is not expected of you yet. If you are
already writing scripts, you are working ahead of the published expectation, and
that is worth saying rather than assuming it goes without notice.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating between the technical and non-technical
2. Data analysis and synthesis
3. Data innovation
4. Data modelling, cleansing and enrichment
5. Metadata management
6. Problem management
7. Testing

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain why it is
important to communicate technical concepts in non-technical language; and explain
the types of communication that can be used with internal and external
stakeholders, and their impact.

**What that means day to day.** When someone asks "can I get the numbers for last
quarter", they are not asking about joins or schemas. Learning to answer the
question they asked, and only mentioning the technical detail that changes what
they should do, is a skill that takes years and starts now.

**What changes at analytics engineer.** You would communicate effectively with
both groups yourself, host discussions in a multidisciplinary team, advocate for
the team externally, and manage differing stakeholder perspectives.

### Skill: Data analysis and synthesis

Your level is the only one in this role where this skill is a single line, and it
is deliberately basic.

### Skill: Data modelling, cleansing and enrichment

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: show an awareness of
different data models and tools, and understand when they could be used; and show
an awareness of industry-recognised data modelling patterns and standards.

**What that means day to day.** A data model is how the data is organised —
which tables exist, what a row means, how they connect. Kimball and Inmon, named
in your role description, are two long-standing schools of thought about that.
Being able to say roughly what they are and how they differ is a good target for
your level.

**What changes at analytics engineer.** You would produce data models, compare
different types, and reverse-engineer a model from a live system.

### Skill: Metadata management

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain what metadata
is; and maintain the information stored in metadata repositories under the
direction of others.

**What that means day to day.** Metadata is data about the data — what a field
means, where it came from, who owns it, when it was last updated. It is what stops
someone using a column called `status` without knowing that `3` means cancelled.
Keeping it accurate is unglamorous and enormously valuable.

**What changes at analytics engineer.** You would use metadata repositories for
complex tasks such as impact analysis, and maintain a repository so it stays
accurate.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: investigate problems in
systems, processes and services, with an understanding of the level of a problem —
strategic, tactical or operational; and contribute to the implementation of
remedies and preventative measures.

**What that looks like day to day.** A pipeline fails, or a number looks wrong,
and someone has to work out why. *Operational* means it broke today; *tactical*
means it keeps breaking; *strategic* means it will keep breaking because of how
something was built. Learning to tell those apart is the useful part.

**What changes at analytics engineer.** Nothing — the wording is **identical** at
that level. It first changes at senior, where you would initiate and monitor
investigations into patterns and trends.

### Skill: Testing

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: correctly execute test
scripts under supervision; and understand the role of testing and how it works.

**What that means day to day.** Data has tests too — checking that a table has
roughly the number of rows expected, that a key is unique, that no dates fall in
the future. Untested data pipelines fail quietly, which is worse than failing
loudly, because the wrong numbers reach a report before anyone notices.

**What changes at analytics engineer.** Nothing — the wording is **identical** at
that level. It first changes at senior, where you would review requirements,
define test conditions and report on results.
