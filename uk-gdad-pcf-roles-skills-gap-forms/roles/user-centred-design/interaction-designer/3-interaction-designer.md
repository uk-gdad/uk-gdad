# Interaction designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as an interaction designer in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now,
where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as a confident and competent designer who can develop designs
based on evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes. It expects you to be trusted to make
good decisions; recognise when to ask for further guidance and support; contribute to the
development of design concepts; and interpret evidence-based research and incorporate it into your
work.

Four accountabilities, and two of them are about judgement rather than production: being trusted,
and knowing when to ask. Interaction design goes wrong most often when someone designs confidently
past the edge of what they actually know about the rules, the data or the system.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating and some
questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: interaction designer.** An interaction designer works out the best way to let users
interact with services, in terms of both overall flow and at the level of individual design
elements.

**Your level: interaction designer.** You are a confident and competent designer who can develop
designs based on evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes. The framework expects that
you:

- be trusted to make good decisions
- recognise when to ask for further guidance and support
- contribute to the development of design concepts
- interpret evidence-based research and incorporate this into your work

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate interaction designer
2. Junior interaction designer
3. **Interaction designer** — you are here
4. Senior interaction designer
5. Lead interaction designer
6. Head of interaction design

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, four of your seven skills are worded **identically at junior interaction designer** — design
communication, designing for everyone, evidence-based design and iterative design. What changed on
promotion was designing strategically, designing together and leading design.

Second: **the framework publishes the same seven skills, word for word, for interaction designers,
graphic designers and service designers** at every level. The skills cannot distinguish the three
disciplines, so what makes you an interaction designer rather than either of the others is entirely
in the work — flows, states, validation, transactions.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects, what it looks like
in interaction design, and what changes at senior interaction designer.

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: clearly explain problems and design ideas
to others; and clearly explain design decisions to others using appropriate tools and methods.

**Same wording as junior interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** The hardest thing to explain is usually why a journey needs *more*
steps than someone expects. Splitting questions across pages feels slower and tests better, and
having the evidence for that at hand is what wins the argument.

**The next step.** At senior you explain complex problems to stakeholders outside design, document
decisions along with risks and unresolved issues, and build consensus by asking difficult questions
and challenging assumptions.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: work with others to design and deliver
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services that meet the needs of all
users; and ensure a design meets appropriate standards, for example accessibility regulations,
**with support**.

**Same wording as junior interaction designer**, including *with support*.

**What that looks like here.** The interaction decisions that determine accessibility are yours:
focus management when content changes, error summaries that link to the field, session timeouts,
and whether a complex task can be paused. Assistive technology users are also disproportionately
affected by anything that requires precise timing or precise pointing.

**The next step.** At senior you advise teams on inclusive and sustainable design, design ethically
considering the personal and social context of users, and ensure a design meets standards — without
the *with support* qualifier.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: align your design work to both the goals
and vision of your team and the strategic objectives of your organisation; identify potential risks
and use design to mitigate them; identify opportunities and use design to take advantage of them;
and contribute to the development of design patterns and components.

**What that looks like here.** *Use design to mitigate risk* is very concrete in interaction design.
A confirmation step before an irreversible action, a clearer eligibility question early in a
journey, a validation rule that prevents a common mistake — each prevents casework, complaints or
harm downstream.

**The next step.** At senior you help teams understand how user-centred design meets their goals,
help them align to organisational vision, use risks and constraints in technology, systems and
policy to shape design, and identify and create new patterns.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: effectively plan and run a design
session with your team, users or stakeholders; identify and engage the right people throughout the
design process; and give and receive constructive design feedback.

**What that looks like here.** Mapping a journey on a wall with the whole team — including the
person who knows what the back-end system will and will not accept — surfaces constraints that would
otherwise appear after build.

**The next step.** At senior you advise others on planning and running sessions, adapt sessions to
achieve useful outcomes, involve the right people throughout, and work across profession boundaries
such as with policy teams.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: analyse, synthesise and clearly explain
evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics data; and work with researchers
or analysts to use evidence to inform, develop and test a design idea.

**Same wording as junior interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Your accountabilities say you *interpret evidence-based research and
incorporate it into your work*, which means being in the analysis rather than reading a summary.
Watching where people hesitate tells you more than where they succeed.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: apply iterative design principles and
agile methodologies to your work; iterate and improve a design based on successive rounds of
research; prototype your ideas at different fidelities to explore and test designs; use a range of
design tools and techniques; and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Same wording as junior interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Iterating a live transactional service is harder than iterating a
prototype — releases are constrained, and a change to one step can move the problem to the next one
rather than solving it.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: lead and co-ordinate design work in your
team, **with support**; communicate the value of user-centred design to your team; support other
designers; and work with digital and data leaders in your organisation, when needed.

**What that looks like here.** This is the first level where leading is doing rather than
explaining. Supporting other designers usually begins informally — being the person people show a
flow to before a critique.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior interaction designer. This skill does not change
between your level and the one above, which is worth knowing for a promotion conversation.
