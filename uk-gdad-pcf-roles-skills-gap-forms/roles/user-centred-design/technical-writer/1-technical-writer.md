# Technical writer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a technical writer in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do
now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes a technical writer as someone who takes a user-centred approach to
making complicated technical concepts easier to understand for a specialist audience,
focusing on specialist content and software documentation, and writing for technologists such
as developers, technical architects and technology leaders. At this level you are an **expert
practitioner with a deep understanding of technology and the needs of technologists**. It
expects you to explain how to use government products and services to a technical audience;
write blog posts for or on behalf of the technical community; and engage with and contribute
to the cross-government technical writing and content design communities.

Your users are experts in something you are writing about. That changes the job: they will
spot a hand-wave immediately, and the fastest way to lose them is to explain what they
already know while skipping what they came for.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating
and some questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **Answer about the job you do**, not the one on the job description.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a generous reading
  of a thin example.
- **Do not include anything from internal documentation that is sensitive.** Describe the
  work, not the system.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used
  for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: technical writer.** You take a user-centred approach to making complicated
technical concepts easier to understand for a specialist audience. You focus on specialist
content and software documentation, and write for technologists such as developers, technical
architects and technology leaders.

**Your level: technical writer.** You are an expert practitioner with a deep understanding of
technology and the needs of technologists. The framework expects that you:

- take a user-centred approach to explaining how to use government products and services to
  a technical audience, focusing on specialist content and software documentation
- write blog posts for or on behalf of the technical community
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government technical writing and content design
  communities

**Where this level sits.** This role has two levels only:

1. **Technical writer** — you are here
2. Lead technical writer

Your next step is lead technical writer: directing a team of technical writers, assuring the
quality of technical writing across teams and its alignment to strategy, standardising tools
and processes, working with service managers and technology leaders to resource teams, and
promoting the discipline.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** **Five of your seven skills are worded
identically at lead technical writer** — Agile working, prototyping, strategic thinking,
technical understanding, and user focus. Only stakeholder relationship management and
user-centred content design change. The published difference between the two levels is
therefore almost entirely about directing others and assuring their work, not about the craft
itself.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working
2. Prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management
4. Strategic thinking
5. Technical understanding (user-centred design)
6. User focus
7. User-centred content design

## Job skills

### Skill: Agile working

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: identify and compare the best
processes or delivery methods to use, including measuring and evaluating outcomes; help the
team to decide the best approach; and help teams to manage and visualise outcomes, prioritise
work and adhere to agreed minimum viable product, priorities and scope.

**Same wording at lead technical writer.**

**What that looks like here.** Documentation is usually the last thing in a sprint and the
first thing cut. Getting it treated as part of "done" rather than as follow-up work is the
practical version of this skill.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work with the team to develop and
maintain an understanding of stakeholders; work with the team to develop and implement
stakeholder communications strategies; identify and resolve issues, influence stakeholders
and manage relationships effectively; and build long-term strategic relationships,
communicating clearly and regularly.

**What that looks like here.** Your most important relationships are with the engineers whose
work you document. They are busy, the documentation is not their priority, and how easily you
can get twenty minutes from them determines the quality of everything you produce.

**The next step.** At lead you **direct** the stakeholder relationship strategy for your
teams, ensure stakeholders' objectives are set, and negotiate with senior stakeholders to
unblock progress. This is one of only two skills that change.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: lead the design and implementation
of strategy, directing the evaluation of strategies and policies to ensure business
requirements are being met.

**Same wording at lead technical writer**, and it is the framework's highest wording for this
skill anywhere. That is a striking expectation for a non-management level, and worth
noticing: as published, a technical writer and a lead technical writer are expected to lead
organisational strategy identically.

**What that looks like here.** In practice this usually means owning the documentation
strategy — what gets documented, where it lives, how it is maintained — rather than

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: give direction on which tools or
methods to use; demonstrate experience in meeting the needs of users across a variety of
channels; bring insight and expertise in how user needs have changed over time to ensure they
are met by the business; and apply strategic thinking to provide the best service for the end
user.

**Same wording at lead technical writer.**

**What that looks like here.** Your users are technologists, and their needs are specific:
they arrive mid-task, they scan rather than read, they want the working example first. Knowing
that from evidence rather than assumption is what separates this from writing for a general
audience.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Six things: show a deep understanding of
end-to-end journeys and how content is affected within these journeys; identify where journey
fixes or content improvements need to be made; take responsibility for assuring the quality of
content from more junior colleagues, and coaching and guiding them to improve; ensure content
is regularly reviewed and evaluated, contributing to continuous improvement and iteration;
encourage a continuous improvement mindset in teams and more junior content colleagues; and
deliver through others, and direct and critique their work.

**What that looks like here.** Several of these assume junior colleagues to assure and coach.
Many technical writers in government work alone, in which case they describe a remit you do
not have — worth saying plainly rather than reading as a gap in your practice.

**The next step.** At lead this becomes directing the content life cycle approach, overseeing
teams' work, extensive experience across multiple channels, being accountable for production
of high quality content, and identifying gaps in content design capability.
