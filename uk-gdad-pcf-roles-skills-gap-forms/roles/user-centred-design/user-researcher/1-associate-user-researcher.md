# Associate user researcher - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you work as an associate user researcher in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can
say what you do now, what you would like to get better at, and what would help.

The framework describes this level as a trainee in an entry-level role, working with more experienced
user researchers. You need an understanding of the role and the potential to grow into it, and you
will need guidance and training to produce good work and develop your skills.

**Guidance and training are written into the level.** That is not a soft expectation — it means being
properly supported is something you are entitled to, and if you are not getting it, that is a finding
about your organisation rather than about you.

User research in government reaches people at difficult moments — applying for support, appealing a
decision, proving something about themselves. Doing it well is a skill, and doing it safely is a
responsibility.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with a plain explanation, a rating and a
couple of questions. Take about 25 minutes.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that
apply, and pick a rating where one is asked for. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

So: when you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so your answers
make sense on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy, which is worth doing on a computer other people use.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **You can skip anything.** Leaving a question blank does not count against you.
- **Answer about what you actually do**, not what you are supposed to be learning.
- **"I have not done this yet" is the right answer at a trainee level** for a lot of these.
- **Never include anything about a research participant** in your answers — no names, no details, no
  quotes that could identify someone. Describe the work, not the people.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who will see your answers, what they will be used for,
  or how long they will be kept, it is completely reasonable to ask before you fill it in.

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you studied.
- **0s and 1s are completely normal here.** Every one of your seven skills is worded as *describe*,
  *explain* or *show an awareness*. A 1 is frequently the exact published expectation.

Here is what a filled-in answer looks like:

> **User research methods**
>

>

> research, and I have taken notes in six sessions. I have not run one.

That is a good answer. It says what is understood and exactly where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: user researcher.** User researchers plan, design and carry out research activities with
users that help teams get a deep understanding of the people who use government services. This
research informs policy, proposition, service, content and interaction design, so that services work
well for users and achieve policy intent.

**Your level: associate user researcher.** You are a trainee in an entry-level role, working with more
experienced user researchers. You need an understanding of the role and to show potential, and you
will need guidance and training to produce good work and develop your skills.

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. **Associate user researcher** — you are here
2. Junior user researcher
3. User researcher
4. Senior user researcher
5. Lead user researcher
6. Head of user research

Your next step is junior user researcher: embedded in a multidisciplinary team to carry out research
activities, with some practical experience, still working in combination with a more senior
researcher.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** **Five of your seven skills are worded identically at
junior user researcher.** Only *Analysis and synthesis* and *User research methods* change. So the
step to junior is specifically about starting to run and analyse research yourself — everything else
stays where it is.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile research practices
2. Analysis and synthesis
3. Inclusive research
4. Research management, leadership and assurance
5. Stakeholder relationship management
6. User research methods
7. User-centred practice and advocacy

### Skill: Agile research practices

**What the framework expects at your level.** One thing: describe agile working and the roles in a
multidisciplinary digital team.

**What that means day to day.** Knowing who does what — the product manager, the delivery manager, the
designers, the developers — and how research fits into a team that works in short cycles. Research in
an agile team has to produce something useful within days, not months.

**What changes at junior.** Nothing — the wording is identical. It first changes at user researcher,
where you explain the benefits and limitations of agile working and design research so findings can be
embedded into an agile workflow.

### Skill: Analysis and synthesis

**What the framework expects at your level.** One thing: show an awareness of the need for careful
analysis of research data to produce clear findings.

**What that means day to day.** Understanding that what someone said in a session is not yet a
finding. Findings come from looking across sessions for patterns, and being honest about how much a
small number of sessions can support.

**What changes at junior.** You would understand how to apply basic analysis and synthesis techniques,
involve your team in analysis, and present clear findings colleagues can understand and use. This is
one of only two skills that change.

### Skill: Inclusive research

**What the framework expects at your level.** One thing: demonstrate some awareness of the diversity
of users of government services and the need to make government services usable and accessible for
everyone.

**What that means day to day.** Government services are used by everyone, including people with
access needs, people in crisis, people with no fixed address and people who do not use the internet.
Research that recruits only people who are easy to reach describes only those people.

**What changes at junior.** Nothing — the wording is identical. It first changes at user researcher,
where you understand the diversity of users and work with colleagues to include many kinds of users in
appropriate research.

### Skill: Research management, leadership and assurance

**What the framework expects at your level.** One thing: explain the importance of working to a user
research plan and of following standards for user research.

**What that means day to day.** A plan says what question the research is answering, who you need to
talk to and how you will do it. Research without one tends to become a series of conversations that
confirm what the team already believed.

**What changes at junior.** Nothing — the wording is identical. It first changes at user researcher,
where you ensure research has a clearly defined scope and purpose.

### Skill: User research methods

**What the framework expects at your level.** One thing: explain some basic user research methods.

**What that means day to day.** Knowing roughly what each method is for: usability testing to see
whether people can do something, interviews to understand context and motivation, contextual research
to see how something is used in real life. Choosing the wrong method answers the wrong question well.

**What changes at junior.** You would plan and conduct a user research project with support,
understand when to use basic methods and apply them correctly, and involve your team in research
activities. This is the second of the two skills that change.

### Skill: User-centred practice and advocacy

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: describe how government services and
technology affect how people behave; and explain how user-centred practices are used to build services
that adapt to changing user behaviour and needs.

**What that means day to day.** Services shape behaviour: a confusing form produces phone calls, a
hard-to-find page produces complaints. Understanding that a service is not neutral — it changes what
people do — is the beginning of user-centred thinking.

**What changes at junior.** Nothing — the wording is identical.
