# Associate service designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you work as an associate service designer in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can
say what you do now, what you would like to get better at, and what would help.

The framework describes this level plainly: as a trainee in an entry-level position, working under
supervision, you will need design aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. That is the
whole level description — there is no separate list of accountabilities.

Service design is the widest of the design disciplines. Your role description says your work may
involve creating or changing transactions, products and content **across both digital and offline
channels provided by different parts of government**. The letter, the phone line, the caseworker's
screen and the appeal process are all inside the service, whether or not anyone has drawn them.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. There are no right answers. Seven skills, each with a
plain explanation, a rating, and a couple of questions. Take about 25 minutes.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that
apply, and pick a rating where one is asked for. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

So: when you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so your answers
make sense on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy, which is worth doing on a computer other people use.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **You can skip anything.** Leaving a question blank does not count against you.
- **Answer about what you actually do**, not what you are supposed to be learning.
- **"I have not done this yet" is the right answer at a trainee level** for a lot of these.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who will see your answers, what they will be used
  for, or how long they will be kept, it is completely reasonable to ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: service designer.** Service designers design the end-to-end journey of a service. This
helps a user complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. Your work may involve
creating or changing transactions, products and content across both digital and offline channels,
provided by different parts of government.

**Your level: associate service designer.** As a trainee in an entry-level position, working under
supervision, you will need design aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role.

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. **Associate service designer** — you are here
2. Junior service designer
3. Service designer
4. Senior service designer
5. Lead service designer
6. Head of service design

Your next step is junior service designer: explaining design decisions, working collaboratively,
having responsibility for a service, working independently after direction, and identifying user
issues and needs.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, three of your seven skills — designing strategically, designing together and leading design —
are worded **identically at junior service designer**.

Second, and useful to know early: **the framework publishes exactly the same seven skills, with the
same wording, for service designers, interaction designers and graphic designers**, at every level.
The only published difference between the three roles is the one-sentence role description. So the
skills cannot tell you what makes service design different — the difference is scope, and it is
enormous. This form asks about that scope.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain the importance of articulating
design decisions to others; and describe some methods for explaining design decisions.

**What that means in service design.** The main communication tool here is a map — of the journey,
of who does what, of where people fall out of the process. A map that shows a service honestly,
including the parts nobody owns, is often the first time anyone has seen the whole thing.

**What changes at junior.** You would clearly explain problems and design ideas to others, and
clearly explain design decisions using appropriate tools and methods.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain the importance of making
government content and services inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable; explain
different access needs that users can have, and ways to meet them; and describe how privilege and
power influence the design and delivery of content and services.

**What that means in service design.** At service scale, exclusion is usually structural rather than
technical: a service that requires documents some people do not have, assumes a fixed address, or
offers only one channel. The privilege and power item is particularly relevant here — services are
often designed around what is convenient for the organisation.

**What changes at junior.** You would work with others to design and deliver inclusive, accessible
and sustainable services, and ensure a design meets appropriate standards, with support.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain how research and analytics
inform user-centred design; frame your ideas as design hypotheses to be tested; and identify common
sources and types of data that inform user-centred design.

**What that means in service design.** The most revealing evidence is usually about failure demand —
the calls, complaints and repeat contacts a service generates because something did not work.
Those are recorded somewhere, and they describe the service as experienced rather than as intended.

**What changes at junior.** You would analyse, synthesise and clearly explain evidence relevant to
users or a service, and work with researchers or analysts to inform, develop and test a design idea.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: describe the basics of iterative design
methodologies such as agile; explain how iteration supports user-centred design; and describe some
ways to prototype ideas at different levels of realism, or fidelities, and when to use them.

**What that means in service design.** You can prototype a service without building anything — role
play the phone call, write the letter that would arrive, walk a colleague through the steps. It
feels unusual and it finds problems that a screen prototype never would.

**What changes at junior.** You would apply iterative design principles, iterate based on rounds of
research, prototype at different fidelities, use a range of tools, and use and iterate patterns.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain the role of user-centred design
in an organisation; describe typical leadership responsibilities in design; and identify leaders in
your organisation that designers need to work with.

**Same wording at junior service designer.**

**What that means in service design.** Services cross organisational boundaries, so the leaders who
matter are often in several places at once — the policy owner, the operations director, the digital
lead. Knowing who they are is the beginning of being able to change anything.
