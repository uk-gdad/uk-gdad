# Senior service designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior service designer in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now,
where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as an experienced designer who works with minimal support and can
influence and mentor others. It expects you to work with service managers and programme directors to
develop design concepts; potentially have responsibility across complex services; help set direction
and embed good practice within teams; and make important decisions based on research, understanding
how that research impacts others.

*Complex* in service design usually means the service crosses organisations — a benefit that depends
on another department's data, a licence that requires a local authority decision, an appeal handled
by a tribunal. Nobody owns the whole thing, and that is the problem you are there for.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating and some
questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"The team does this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a generous reading of a thin
  example.

  long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: service designer.** Service designers design the end-to-end journey of a service. This
helps a user complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. Your work may involve
creating or changing transactions, products and content across both digital and offline channels,
provided by different parts of government.

**Your level: senior service designer.** You are an experienced designer who works with minimal
support and can influence and mentor others. The framework expects that you:

- work with service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts
- potentially have responsibility across complex services
- help set direction and embed good practice within teams
- make important decisions based on research and understand how this research impacts others

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate service designer
2. Junior service designer
3. Service designer
4. **Senior service designer** — you are here
5. Lead service designer
6. Head of service design

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, **your published wording for *Leading design* is identical to the service designer's below
you** — including "lead and co-ordinate design work in your team, **with support**" — despite your
level description saying you work with minimal support and mentor others.

Second, designing strategically and designing together are worded identically at lead service
designer, so those two will not distinguish you at the next step either.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects, what it looks like
in service design, and what changes at lead service designer.

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: clearly explain complex problems and
ideas to teams or stakeholders outside of design; **clearly document and communicate design
decisions, related risks and any unresolved issues**; and build consensus around a design approach,
for example by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions.

**What that looks like here.** Cross-organisation services accumulate unresolved issues that everyone
knows about and nobody records — the handover that fails twice a month, the data that arrives late.
Writing those down with named owners is how they eventually get fixed.

**The next step.** At lead you gain support for design decisions and strategy from senior leaders,
communicate design strategy across organisational boundaries, coach others in communicating design,
and introduce more effective methods of communicating and working in the open.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: advise teams on how to design inclusive,
accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services; design and deliver ethical content or
services that consider the personal and social context of users; and ensure a design meets
appropriate standards, for example accessibility regulations.

**What that looks like here.** The *with support* qualifier has gone. And in service design, the
personal and social context is often the whole problem: a service that requires someone to prove
something they cannot prove, or to disclose something in front of family, is unusable in ways no
audit reports.

**The next step.** At lead you measurably improve inclusive, accessible and sustainable design
practice across teams or your organisation, and help others understand and mitigate the influence of
privilege and power on design.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: help a team understand how user-centred
design helps it meet its goals; help teams align their work to the goals and vision of their
organisation; use risks, opportunities and constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape
design; and identify and create new design patterns and components.

**Same wording at lead service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Service patterns are organisational: how identity is proved once
rather than four times, how a decision is communicated, how someone is handed between organisations.
Creating one that other services adopt is high-value and rare.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: advise others how to effectively plan and
run design sessions with a team, users or stakeholders; adapt a design session to ensure you achieve
a useful outcome; effectively involve the right people throughout the design process; and **work
across team or profession boundaries, for example with policy teams**.

**Same wording at lead service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Running a session across two organisations, where one has no incentive
to change, is the hardest facilitation in government design. Finding the shared interest — usually
avoided work or avoided complaints — is what makes it possible.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: analyse, synthesise and clearly explain
complex evidence relevant to users or a service, for example large data sets; help your team use
design hypotheses effectively; and use complex research and data to develop and test design ideas.

**What that looks like here.** Service evidence is scattered across systems that were never meant to
be joined — contact centre logs, casework systems, complaints, appeals. Assembling a picture of what
actually happens to people is often the most valuable analysis nobody has done.

**The next step.** At lead you embed hypothesis-driven design in a team, guide the organisation in
using evidence to improve services, and ensure user insights are shared across government.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: help other designers apply iterative design
principles and agile methodologies to their work; iterate and improve complex designs based on
successive rounds of research; **independently prototype complex ideas at an appropriate fidelity**;
and adapt designs quickly to changes in requirements, priorities or user needs.

**What that looks like here.** Prototyping a complex service means simulating a process — a trial in
one office, a shadow run alongside the real thing, a walkthrough with real cases. That is harder to
arrange than a clickable prototype and far more convincing.

**The next step.** At lead you embed iterative design across teams or the organisation, lead on
prototyping complex ideas at any fidelity, help teams adapt, and iterate delivery processes or team
structures.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: lead and co-ordinate design work in your
team, **with support**; communicate the value of user-centred design to your team; support other
designers; and work with digital and data leaders in your organisation, when needed.

**Identical wording to service designer**, the level below — including *with support*, despite your
level description saying you work with minimal support and mentor others.

**What that looks like here.** In practice senior service designers lead without support and often
across organisations. If you do, you are beyond this wording.

**The next step.** At lead you lead and co-ordinate design work in a team without the qualifier,
advocate for user-centred design with leaders outside digital and data, create an inclusive and
productive environment for designers, mentor and coach, and iterate design processes.
