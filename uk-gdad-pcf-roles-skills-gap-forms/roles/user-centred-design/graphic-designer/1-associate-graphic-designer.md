# Associate graphic designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you work as an associate graphic designer in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you
can say what you do now, what you would like to get better at, and what would help.

The framework describes this level plainly: as a trainee in an entry-level position, working
under supervision, you need aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. That is the
whole level description — there is no separate list of accountabilities, which makes the first
section of this form more important than usual.

Graphic design in government is not decoration. Your role description is specific: you use
layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to make sure content is legible and readable, and
that users see and understand interactions. Whether someone notices the one button that matters
on a page is a graphic design outcome.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. There are no right answers. Seven skills, each with a
plain explanation, a rating, and a couple of questions. Take about 25 minutes.

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that

So: when you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so your answers
make sense on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy, which is worth doing on a computer other people use.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **You can skip anything.** Leaving a question blank does not count against you.
- **Answer about what you actually do**, not what you are supposed to be learning.
- **"I have not done this yet" is the right answer at a trainee level** for a lot of these.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who will see your answers, what they will be used
  for, or how long they will be kept, it is completely reasonable to ask before you fill it in.

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you studied.
- **0s and 1s are completely normal here.** Every one of your seven skills is worded as
  *explain* or *describe*. At this level the framework is asking you to understand the practice
  before you own it, so a 1 is frequently the exact published expectation.

## About this role level

**The role: graphic designer.** A graphic designer creates the graphic elements that underpin
interaction and service design. You use layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure
that content is legible and readable and that users see and understand interactions.

**Your level: associate graphic designer.** As a trainee in an entry-level position, working
under supervision, you need aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role.

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. **Associate graphic designer** — you are here
2. Junior graphic designer
3. Graphic designer
4. Senior graphic designer
5. Lead graphic designer
6. Head of graphic design

Your next step is junior graphic designer: explaining design decisions, working collaboratively,
holding responsibility as part of a service, working independently after direction from more
senior designers, and identifying user issues and needs.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Three of your seven skills — designing
strategically, designing together, and leading design — are worded **identically at junior
graphic designer**. They do not change when you are promoted. What changes are design
communication, designing for everyone, evidence-based design and iterative design, which all move
from *explain* to *do*.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain the importance of articulating
design decisions to others; and describe some methods for explaining design decisions.

**What that means day to day.** In government design, "I think it looks better" is not an
argument. Being able to say *why* — this spacing groups related things, this weight makes the
primary action findable — is what turns a preference into a decision other people can agree
with.

**What changes at junior.** You would clearly explain problems and design ideas to others, and
clearly explain design decisions using appropriate tools and methods.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain the importance of making
government content and services inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable; explain
different access needs that users can have, and ways to meet them; and describe how privilege and
power influence the design and delivery of content and services.

**What that means day to day.** For a graphic designer this is unusually concrete: colour
contrast, text size, not using colour alone to carry meaning, and not relying on an icon whose
meaning is obvious only to people who already know. Government services are used by everyone,
including people with low vision, in bright sunlight, on an old phone.

**What changes at junior.** You would work with others to design and deliver inclusive,
accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services, and ensure a design meets
appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations, with support.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain the importance of aligning to
the strategy of an organisation; describe how your design work aligns to the wider goals and
vision of your team; and explain the value of using patterns and components in design.

**Same wording at junior graphic designer** — this skill does not change between the first two
levels.

**What that means day to day.** The last item is the practical one. Using an existing pattern
rather than designing something new is usually the better decision: it has been tested, people
recognise it, and it costs less to maintain. New is not automatically better.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain how research and analytics
inform user-centred design; frame your ideas as design hypotheses to be tested; and identify
common sources and types of data that inform user-centred design.

**What that means day to day.** Framing an idea as a hypothesis — "if we make this heading
larger, more people will notice the deadline" — turns a preference into something that can be
checked. Getting comfortable with being wrong about your own designs is part of the craft.

**What changes at junior.** You would analyse, synthesise and clearly explain evidence relevant
to users or a service, and work with researchers or analysts to use evidence to inform, develop
and test a design idea.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: describe the basics of iterative
design methodologies such as agile; explain how iteration supports user-centred design; and
describe some ways to prototype ideas at different levels of realism, or fidelities, and when to
use them.

**What that means day to day.** *Fidelity* means how finished something looks. A rough sketch
invites people to comment on the structure; a polished mock-up invites them to comment on the
colours. Choosing the wrong fidelity gets you the wrong feedback — which is a genuinely useful
thing to understand early.

**What changes at junior.** You would apply iterative design principles and agile methodologies,
iterate a design based on successive rounds of research, prototype at different fidelities, use a
range of tools and techniques, and use and iterate design patterns and components.
