# Junior graphic designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a junior graphic designer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do
now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject, or someone
with some relevant work experience, or both. It expects you to explain design decisions; work
collaboratively; have a responsibility as part of a service; work independently after being
given direction by more senior designers; and identify user issues and important needs.

**Responsibility as part of a service** is the phrase that matters. Your work is not a standalone
artefact — the icon, the spacing, the type scale all sit inside something people use to get
something done, often something that matters to them a great deal.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating and
some questions. Expect about 25 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: graphic designer.** A graphic designer creates the graphic elements that underpin
interaction and service design. You use layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure
that content is legible and readable and that users see and understand interactions.

**Your level: junior graphic designer.** You are a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject,
or someone with relevant work experience, or both. The framework expects that you:

- explain design decisions
- work collaboratively
- have a responsibility as part of a service
- work independently after being given direction by more senior designers
- identify user issues and important needs

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate graphic designer
2. **Junior graphic designer** — you are here
3. Graphic designer
4. Senior graphic designer
5. Lead graphic designer
6. Head of graphic design

Your next step is graphic designer: a confident and competent designer who develops designs based
on evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes, trusted to make good decisions, and
leading multiple or highly complex services.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Four of your seven skills are worded **identically at
graphic designer**, the level above — design communication, designing for everyone,
evidence-based design and iterative design. Only designing strategically, designing together and
leading design change. So the next step is less about new craft and more about judgement, scope
and trust, which is exactly what its accountabilities describe.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: clearly explain problems and design
ideas to others; and clearly explain design decisions to others using appropriate tools and
methods.

**Same wording at graphic designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Explaining a *problem* is as important as explaining an idea.
Showing a stakeholder why the current layout buries the thing people need often does more than
presenting a better version of it.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: work with others to design and deliver
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services that meet the needs of
all users; and ensure a design meets appropriate standards, for example accessibility
regulations, **with support**.

**Same wording at graphic designer.**

**What that looks like here.** For graphic design the accessible choices are specific and
checkable: contrast ratios, text size and line length, target sizes for touch, never relying on
colour alone, and icons that are labelled rather than assumed to be self-explanatory.
Sustainability shows up in image weight and page size, which matter most to people on poor
connections.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: explain the importance of aligning to
the strategy of an organisation; describe how your design work aligns to the wider goals and
vision of your team; and explain the value of using patterns and components in design.

**Same wording as associate graphic designer.** This skill does not change between the first two
levels.

**What that looks like here.** The practical version is knowing when to reach for an existing
pattern and when a case genuinely needs something new — and being able to say which, rather than
defaulting either way.

**The next step.** At graphic designer you align your work to team goals and organisational
objectives, identify risks and use design to mitigate them, identify opportunities and use design
to take advantage of them, and contribute to developing patterns and components.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: explain different techniques for
bringing together perspectives from multiple people to inform a design; and explain the
importance of constructive feedback in the design process.

**Same wording as associate graphic designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Design in government happens with content designers, interaction
designers, researchers, and developers in the room. The graphic decisions and the words are not
separable — type size and line length change how readable content is as much as the sentences
do.

**The next step.** At graphic designer you plan and run design sessions with your team, users or
stakeholders, identify and engage the right people throughout, and give and receive constructive
feedback.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: analyse, synthesise and clearly explain
evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics data; and work with
researchers or analysts to use evidence to inform, develop and test a design idea.

**Same wording at graphic designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Graphic decisions are testable. Whether people notice a warning,
find the primary action, or read past the first section can all be observed rather than argued
about.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: apply iterative design principles and
agile methodologies to your work; iterate and improve a design based on successive rounds of
research; prototype your ideas at different fidelities to explore and test designs; use a range
of design tools and techniques; and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Same wording at graphic designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Choosing fidelity deliberately is the craft here: a greyscale
wireframe gets you comments on hierarchy, a finished-looking mock-up gets you comments on colour.
Both are useful, at different moments.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: explain the role of user-centred
design in an organisation; describe typical leadership responsibilities in design; and identify
leaders in your organisation that designers need to work with.

**Same wording as associate graphic designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Knowing who decides what, and who needs convincing for design work
to happen at all. At your level that is orientation rather than influence, but it is the
groundwork for both.

**The next step.** At graphic designer you lead and co-ordinate design work in your team with
support, communicate the value of user-centred design to your team, support other designers, and
work with digital and data leaders when needed.
