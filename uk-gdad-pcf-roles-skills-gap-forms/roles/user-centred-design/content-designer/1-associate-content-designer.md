# Associate content designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you work as an associate content designer in
the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it
so that you can say what you do now, what you would like to get better at, and what would
help.

The framework describes this level plainly: as a trainee in an entry-level position,
working under supervision, you need aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role,
and you will perform basic standard checks and copy edits.

That is the whole level description. There is no separate list of accountabilities — this
is one of the few levels in the framework published without one. So the first section of
this form matters more than usual, because what you actually do has probably never been
written down anywhere.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. There are no right answers. Six skills, each with
a plain explanation, a rating, and a couple of questions. Take about 25 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational purposes. If

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that
apply, and pick a rating where one is asked for. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

So: when you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so your answers
make sense on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy, which is worth doing on a computer other people use.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **You can skip anything.** Leaving a question blank does not count against you.
- **Answer about what you actually do**, not what you are supposed to be learning.
- **"I have not done this yet" is the right answer at a trainee level** for a lot of
  these, and much more useful than stretching a small example.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who will see your answers, what they will be
  used for, or how long they will be kept, it is completely reasonable to ask before you
  fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for people to
understand and use. That can mean working on a single piece of content or on the end-to-end
journey of a service, to help users complete their goal and government deliver a policy
intent. Your work may involve creating or changing a transaction, a product, or a single
piece of content that stretches across digital and offline channels. In this role you will:

- make sure appropriate content is shown to a user in the right place and in the best
  format
- start from discovery and work closely with user researchers, service designers and
  interaction designers

**Your level: associate content designer.** As a trainee in an entry-level position, working
under supervision, you need aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. You will
perform basic standard checks and copy edits.

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels — one of the longest ladders in the
framework:

1. **Associate content designer** — you are here
2. Junior content designer
3. Content designer
4. Senior content designer
5. Lead content designer
6. Head of content design

Your next step is junior content designer: explaining content decisions, working
independently after direction from more senior designers, identifying user issues and needs
yourself, and supporting content team administration and publishing.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** **Five of your six skills are worded
identically at junior content designer.** Only *Strategic thinking (content design)*
changes. That means the published skills barely distinguish the first two levels, and what
actually changes is the independence described in the accountabilities. Good to know: you do
not need to become a different kind of content designer to move up, you need to be able to
do more of it without someone checking.

**The six skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at your level.** Four things: demonstrate experience of working
in Agile teams, including an awareness of Agile tools and how to use them; advise colleagues
on how and why Agile methods are used and provide a clear, open and transparent framework in
which teams can deliver; adapt and reflect and be responsive to feedback; and see outside of
the process.

**A note on the wording.** *Advise colleagues* is demanding for a trainee level, and this
exact text is also used at junior content designer. If it reads as more than you do, that is
the framework being ambitious rather than you falling short. What is realistic at your level
is the third item — responding well to feedback, which in content design arrives constantly.

**What changes at junior.** Nothing — the wording is identical.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: show an awareness of prototyping
and explain why and when to use it; and understand how to work in an open and collaborative
environment, by pair working for example.

**What that means day to day.** Content can be prototyped — writing the words into a
realistic-looking page and putting it in front of someone, rather than discussing the wording
in a document. Pair writing, where two people draft together, is one of the fastest ways to
learn this craft.

**What changes at junior.** Nothing — the wording is identical.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at your level.** One thing: understand who your stakeholders
are, what evidence is relevant to them and the importance of managing relationships with
them.

**What that means day to day.** Content usually has someone who feels ownership of the words
— a policy team, a subject expert, a lawyer. Knowing who they are and what convinces them is
half of getting good content published.

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: show an awareness of the strategic
context of your work and understand why it is important; and support strategic planning in an
administrative capacity.

**This is the only skill that changes at junior**, where it becomes supporting the creation
and development of strategies and policies, contributing to iterating processes and
guidelines, and understanding how activities meet strategic goals.

**What that means day to day.** Knowing why the content exists — what the organisation is
trying to achieve, and what users are trying to do — rather than treating each page as a
task.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: show an awareness or understanding
of user experience analysis and its principles; and see the purpose of user stories and focus
on user needs.

**What that means day to day.** Writing for the person who arrives confused, in a hurry, and
possibly on a phone with a poor connection — rather than for the colleague who will approve
it. Watching user research is the fastest way to learn what that really means.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: demonstrate basic but decent
editorial standards; show an awareness of style standards; and work well under supervision.

**What that means day to day.** Knowing the style guide and using it — including the parts
that feel counterintuitive, like short sentences and words people actually say. Government
style guidance exists because it has been tested with users, not because someone preferred
it.
