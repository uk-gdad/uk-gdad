# Content designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a content designer in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do
now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as being responsible for creating, evaluating, updating and
reviewing content at all stages of the end-to-end user journey. It expects you to be
comfortable using evidence, data and research to make content decisions; build relationships
across government to focus on user needs and influence stakeholders; contribute to and use the
style guides and content patterns; and engage with and contribute to the cross-government
content community.

This is the level where the job becomes fully itself. All six skills change from the level
below, several substantially, and *User-centred content design* becomes the longest skill
description in the role — nine separate expectations, ending with *work autonomously*.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Six skills, each with an explanation, a rating and
some questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for people to understand
and use. That can mean working on a single piece of content or on the end-to-end journey of a
service, to help users complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. In this
role you will:

- make sure appropriate content is shown to a user in the right place and in the best format
- start from discovery and work closely with user researchers, service designers and
  interaction designers

**Your level: content designer.** You are responsible for creating, evaluating, updating and
reviewing content at all stages of the end-to-end user journey. The framework expects that
you:

- be comfortable using evidence, data and research to make content decisions
- build relationships across government to focus on the needs of the user and to influence
  stakeholders
- contribute to and use the style guides and content patterns
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government content community

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate content designer
2. Junior content designer
3. **Content designer** — you are here
4. Senior content designer
5. Lead content designer
6. Head of content design

Your next step is senior content designer.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** The step you have just made was the largest in
this role. Associate and junior share five of six skills word for word; every one of them
changes at your level. If you have recently been promoted into this and it feels like a bigger
jump than the title suggests, the framework's own wording agrees with you.

**The six skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Job skills

Six skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects and what it

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Six things: understand and demonstrate awareness
of Agile methodology and apply an Agile mindset to your work; work in a fast-paced, evolving
environment and use an iterative and flexible approach to enable rapid delivery; appreciate the
importance of Agile project delivery to digital projects in government; be unafraid to take
risks and willing to learn from mistakes; ensure the team has situational awareness of each
other's work and how it relates to objectives and user needs; and use a range of tools and
platforms to plan and manage your work.

**What that looks like here.** Content that is iterated after launch, based on what people
actually do with it, rather than perfected before. That requires being comfortable publishing
something you know will change.

**Identical wording at senior content designer**, so this is a skill you can already be building to
the level above.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: understand different prototyping
techniques, from paper sketches to coded prototypes, and use them to visualise content in
context; choose the best option to make content understandable to different audiences and to
demonstrate a proposed approach to content changes or improvements; and show the value of
prototyping to the team.

**What that looks like here.** Showing a stakeholder a realistic page usually ends an argument
that a document would have prolonged. Prototyping is as much a persuasion tool as a design
one.

**The next step.** At senior content designer you build a variety of prototypes from paper sketches to
coded ones, choose the most appropriate way to visualise content in context for different audiences,
combine prototypes with other outputs to test with users and show stakeholders, and establish content
patterns, testing and iterating them.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: identify important stakeholders and
communicate with them clearly and regularly; tailor communication to stakeholders' needs and
work with them to build relationships while also meeting user needs; build and reach
consensus; and work to improve stakeholder relationships, using evidence to explain decisions
made.

**What that looks like here.** *While also meeting user needs* is the tension in one phrase.
A good relationship with a stakeholder who wants worse content is not a success, and holding
both is the job.

**The next step.** At senior you negotiate with and influence stakeholders and manage relationships
effectively, influence decisions, deal with challenging situations and remove blockers, and build
long-term strategic relationships.

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: contribute to content strategies and
policies; create content patterns or standards; provide support for content improvement
projects; and effectively focus on outcomes rather than solutions and activities.

**What that looks like here.** Creating a pattern means solving a recurring problem once —
how error messages are written, how eligibility is explained — so nobody has to solve it
again. It is the highest-leverage thing available at your level.

**The next step.** At senior you help lead the design and implementation of strategies, evaluating
their impact and progress, and lead and direct strategic content improvement projects, focusing effort
where priority is greatest.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: identify tasks that will provide
insights into a problem; formulate hypotheses, gain insights from data and user research and
make decisions on findings; understand the range of different users who might access content
and services, and identify their needs based on evidence; translate user stories into content
that meets users' needs, and propose suitable design approaches; and use quantitative and
qualitative data about users to turn user insights into outcomes.

**What that looks like here.** *The range of different users* covers people using assistive
technology, people in distress, people whose first language is not English, and people using an
old phone on a bad connection. Content that works only for a calm, capable reader has not met
this expectation.

**The next step.** At senior it broadens considerably: collaborating with user researchers and
performance analysts, understanding how research and data are used at alpha, beta and live, forming
hypotheses and explaining decisions, championing research covering all users, putting accessibility at
the heart of content design, and guiding others on tools and methods.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Nine things: design content to meet user needs
and make complex language and processes easy to understand; present the right information in
the right format for what users need; show robust experience in writing, editing and
publishing digital content, and in using content management systems and production processes;
write in plain language in a way users understand, making information accessible to all;
understand government accessibility requirements and design content that works with common
assistive technologies; use data, research and evidence to review and evaluate content to make
improvements; consistently and effectively apply content standards and style guidelines; work
to continuously improve content and understand why content life cycle management is important;
and work autonomously.

**What that looks like here.** This is the longest skill description in the role, and the
accessibility item is the one people most often assume is someone else's job. Content decisions
— heading structure, link text, tables, how a complex process is broken up — determine
accessibility as much as any code does.

**The next step.** At senior you show a deep understanding of end-to-end journeys, identify where
journey fixes or content improvements are needed, assure the quality of more junior colleagues' content
and coach them, ensure content is regularly reviewed, and deliver through others — directing and
critiquing their work.
