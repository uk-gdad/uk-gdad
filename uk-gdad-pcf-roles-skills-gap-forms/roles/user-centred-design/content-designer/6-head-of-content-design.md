# Head of content design - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a head of content design in a UK government organisation. Human
resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps
are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this role as an expert practitioner with broad industry experience who
can define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others. It expects
you to develop a strategy for content that meets the organisation's objectives; lead a team
capable of executing that strategy; work with senior stakeholders to influence organisational
strategy; prioritise and collaborate with counterpart colleagues across government; and champion
good content design practice within government and industry.

*Influence organisational strategy* is the accountability that separates this from running a
content team. Content design at its best changes what an organisation decides to do, not only
how it explains what it has already decided.

Head of content design is the top of this role. Six skills. Expect about 30 minutes.

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational purposes. If you

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send from here — your
answers never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"The team does this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer** at this level too.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or
  how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of you and
  would stay changed if you left. The honest test here is whether content design practice would
  survive your departure.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Content concepts and prototyping**
>
> - [x] 1 — Awareness: I know what it is and why it matters
>
> *Evidence:* I make the case for prototyping and fund the tools. I have not built one in years,
> which — as the note below explains — is exactly what the published wording for my level
> expects.

Named practice, and a clear line about where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for people to understand
and use. That can mean working on a single piece of content or on the end-to-end journey of a
service, to help users complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. In this role
you will:

- make sure appropriate content is shown to a user in the right place and in the best format
- start from discovery and work closely with user researchers, service designers and interaction
  designers

**Your level: head of content design.** You are an expert practitioner with broad industry
experience who can define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring
others. The framework expects that you:

- develop a strategy for content that meets the organisation's objectives
- lead a team capable of executing that strategy
- work with senior stakeholders to influence organisational strategy
- prioritise and collaborate with counterpart colleagues across government
- champion good content design practice within government and industry

**There is no level above this one.** This role has six levels and this is the highest. Where
people go next is a question about other roles — user-centred design leadership, digital
leadership, or leadership outside the profession — and the framework does not define it as a next
step, so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Associate content designer
2. Junior content designer
3. Content designer
4. Senior content designer
5. Lead content designer
6. **Head of content design** — you are here

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, **five of your six skills are worded identically at lead content designer.** The
published difference between the two levels rests entirely on the accountabilities —
organisational strategy, and championing the discipline in government and industry.

Second, and more strikingly: **your published wording for *Content concepts and prototyping* is
the same as an associate or junior content designer's** — showing an awareness of prototyping
and explaining why and when to use it. Senior content designers are expected to build coded
prototypes and establish tested content patterns; you are expected to be aware of prototyping.
That is how the framework publishes it. A low rating on that skill is not a finding about you.

**The six skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Job skills

Six skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the framework expects and what it
looks like in practice. There is no level above, so these say what the skill demands rather than
what comes next.

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects.** Five things: coach and lead teams in Agile and Lean practices,
determining the right approach; evaluate and review the approach through the life of a project
and iterate and pivot accordingly; think of new and innovative ways of working; act as a
recognised expert and advocate the approaches proposed, continuously reflecting on the team's
work and constructively challenging them to improve; and regularly assess and review capability
within teams and ensure individuals have the skills needed to deliver.

**Same wording as lead content designer.**

**What that looks like here.** *Recognised expert* means recognised by other people. At head
level the useful version is whether other disciplines and other organisations ask you how
content design should work.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects.** Two things: show an awareness of prototyping and explain why and
when to use it; and understand how to work in an open and collaborative environment, by pair
working for example.

**A note on the wording.** This is the **associate and junior content designer** wording — the
lowest in the role, and far below the senior content designer's, which covers coded prototypes
and establishing tested content patterns. That is how the framework publishes it, recorded here
rather than adjusted.

**What that looks like here.** In practice your contribution is making prototyping possible —
funding tools, protecting the time, and refusing to accept wording decisions made in documents
when a prototype would settle them.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects.** Five things: manage long-term strategic relationships with
stakeholders, identifying where new connections need to be made and existing ones nurtured;
direct the strategic approach for stakeholder relationships, establishing stakeholder objectives
and ensuring they are clearly represented; act as a point of escalation if stakeholder
relationships break down or become challenging for more junior members of the team; influence
important senior stakeholders and arbitrate when blockers are escalated; and facilitate
discussions across high-risk and complex areas or projects under constrained timelines.

**Same wording as lead content designer.**

**What that looks like here.** At head level the constrained-timeline discussion is often about
words that will be read by millions the same day. Being the calm, clear voice in that room —
and knowing which battles to fight — is the job.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects.** Five things: give direction on which tools or methods are best

variety of channels; understand complex user journeys and direct solutions to meet different

**Same wording as lead content designer.**

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects.** Six things: direct the approach to content life cycle management
so content is regularly reviewed and evaluated by teams; oversee teams' work to ensure the right
content is being produced to meet user needs; demonstrate extensive experience in creating,
iterating, managing and overseeing content across multiple channels; encourage teams to review
and evaluate the effectiveness of processes and systems and support them to iterate; be
accountable for the production of high quality, user-focused content; and identify gaps in
content design skills and capability, and help teams grow and develop.

**Same wording as lead content designer.**

**What that looks like here.** You are accountable for content quality across an organisation
you cannot read all of. That accountability is only meaningful if the life cycle approach runs
without you — otherwise it is a title rather than a control.
