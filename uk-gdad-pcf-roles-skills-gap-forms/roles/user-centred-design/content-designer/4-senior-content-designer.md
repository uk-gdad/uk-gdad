# Senior content designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior content designer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you
do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as an expert practitioner who develops content strategy and
solutions for **large-scale problems and high-profile events**. It expects you to take
responsibility for content quality, manage small teams and mentor colleagues; write and map
user stories; review the work of others to assure quality; lead on cross-government content
projects; and engage with and contribute to the cross-government content community.

High-profile events are worth pausing on. A good deal of senior content design in government
happens under time pressure with an audience — a policy change announced, a service that
suddenly matters, a page that will be read by millions in a week. The craft is the same; the
conditions are not.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Six skills, each with an explanation, a rating and
some questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"The team does this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a generous reading of a
  thin example.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for,
  or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of you

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for people to understand
and use. That can mean working on a single piece of content or on the end-to-end journey of a
service, to help users complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. In this role
you will:

- make sure appropriate content is shown to a user in the right place and in the best format
- start from discovery and work closely with user researchers, service designers and
  interaction designers

**Your level: senior content designer.** You are an expert practitioner who develops content
strategy and solutions for large-scale problems and high-profile events. The framework expects
that you:

- take responsibility for content quality, manage small teams and mentor content colleagues
- write and map user stories
- review the work of others to assure quality
- lead on cross-government content projects
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government content community

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate content designer
2. Junior content designer
3. Content designer
4. **Senior content designer** — you are here
5. Lead content designer
6. Head of content design

Your next step is lead content designer: directing a team, assuring content quality across
whole teams, aligning content to strategy, and working with service managers and programme
directors to resource teams and resolve problems.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, *Agile working (content design)* is worded **identically at your level and at content
designer** below you. It rises at lead, where it becomes coaching and leading teams in Agile
and Lean practices.

Second, *Strategic thinking (content design)* is worded **identically at your level, at lead,
and at head of content design** — all three top levels. It will not distinguish you at any
point above here, so the case rests on the other skills and on your accountabilities.

**The six skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Job skills

Six skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects, what it looks
like in practice, and what changes at lead content designer.

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Six things: understand and demonstrate awareness
of Agile methodology and apply an Agile mindset; work in a fast-paced, evolving environment
using an iterative and flexible approach for rapid delivery; appreciate the importance of Agile
project delivery to digital projects in government; be unafraid to take risks and willing to
learn from mistakes; ensure the team has situational awareness of each other's work; and use a
range of tools and platforms to plan and manage your work.

**Same wording as content designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Under high-profile pressure the iterative approach is the first
thing abandoned. Holding some version of it — publishing something honest and improving it —
rather than delaying for perfection is a senior judgement.

**The next step.** At lead you coach and lead teams in Agile and Lean practices, determine the
right approach, evaluate and pivot through a project's life, think of innovative ways of
working, act as a recognised expert, and assess capability within teams.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: build and create a variety of
different prototypes, from paper sketches to coded prototypes; choose the most appropriate
option to visualise content in context for different audiences; effectively combine prototypes
with other content and design outputs to test with users and show to stakeholders; and
**establish content patterns, and test and iterate them**.

**This is the most demanding prototyping wording in the entire role** — more than at lead, and
far more than at head of content design, whose published text is the same as a trainee's.
Yours is the technical peak of this skill.

**What that looks like here.** Establishing a pattern and testing it means the fix outlives
the project. Coded prototypes are the item most senior content designers have not done and
would like to.

**The next step.** At lead this drops back to the content designer wording — understanding
techniques, choosing the best option, showing the value of prototyping.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: negotiate with and influence
stakeholders, and manage relationships effectively; influence decisions, deal with challenging
situations and remove blockers; and build long-term strategic relationships, communicating
clearly and regularly, supporting mutual needs and commitments while focusing on user needs.

**What that looks like here.** *Challenging situations* in high-profile content work usually
means a senior person who wants specific wording for reasons that have nothing to do with
users. Handling that without either capitulating or escalating unnecessarily is the skill.

**The next step.** At lead you manage long-term strategic relationships and identify new
connections, direct the strategic approach, act as a point of escalation when relationships
break down for junior colleagues, influence senior stakeholders and arbitrate escalated
blockers, and facilitate discussions across high-risk areas under constrained timelines.

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: help lead the design and
implementation of strategies, evaluating their impact and progress to ensure business
objectives and user needs are met; and lead and direct strategic content improvement projects,
focusing effort in areas of greatest priority and ensuring goals and objectives are met.

**Identical at lead content designer and at head of content design.** This skill does not rise
again anywhere in this role.

**What that looks like here.** *Focusing effort in areas of greatest priority* means choosing
which content not to improve — which is harder than it sounds when every team believes theirs
matters most.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Seven things: collaborate with user researchers
and performance analysts to advocate effectively for users; understand different types of
research, evidence and data and how they are used at alpha, beta and live; formulate
hypotheses, gain insights from research and make decisions, explaining clearly how decisions
were made; evaluate quantitative and qualitative data and prioritise approaches to understand
users; champion user research to focus on all users; **put accessibility requirements at the
heart of approaches to designing content, and encourage others to do the same**; and guide
others and make recommendations on the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** Accessibility being *at the heart* rather than checked at the
end is named explicitly at your level and nowhere else in this role. That makes it yours to
establish.

**The next step.** At lead you give direction on tools and methods for teams, demonstrate
extensive experience across channels, direct solutions within complex journeys, bring insight
into how needs have changed, and apply strategic thinking across the cross-government context.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Six things: show a deep understanding of
end-to-end journeys and how content is affected within them; identify where journey fixes or
content improvements need to be made; take responsibility for assuring the quality of content
from more junior colleagues, and coaching and guiding them to improve; ensure content is
regularly reviewed and evaluated, contributing to continuous improvement; encourage a
continuous improvement mindset in teams and junior colleagues; and **deliver through others,
and direct and critique their work**.

**What that looks like here.** *Deliver through others* is the shift. Critiquing someone's
writing so they improve, rather than rewriting it yourself, takes longer and is the only
version that scales.

**The next step.** At lead you direct the content life cycle approach, oversee teams' work,
demonstrate extensive experience across multiple channels, encourage teams to evaluate
processes, be accountable for high quality content, and identify capability gaps.
