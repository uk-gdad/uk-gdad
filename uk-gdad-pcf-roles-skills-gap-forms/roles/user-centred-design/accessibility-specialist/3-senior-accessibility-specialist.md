# Senior accessibility specialist - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior accessibility specialist in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can
set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as working with people at a range of role levels to
**embed** accessibility into the output of teams. It expects you to coach and mentor more
junior colleagues; input into accessibility strategy with business areas or teams; create
and deliver accessibility training; engage with teams across different levels and
capabilities; potentially manage or lead individuals or sub-teams; and engage with and
contribute to the cross-government accessibility community.

*Embed* is the word that changes the job. Auditing finds problems after they exist;
embedding means teams stop creating them. The second is slower, less visible, and worth
far more.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating
and some questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of

## About this role level

**The role: accessibility specialist.** You provide support, advice and guidance to other
roles in the Government Digital and Data profession about how to create accessible digital
services. You use knowledge of accessibility guidelines, provide technical expertise, and
bring the voice of disabled users to ensure that product teams make digital services that
can be used by everyone.

**Your level: senior accessibility specialist.** You work with people at a range of role
levels to embed accessibility into the output of teams. The framework expects that you:

- coach and mentor more junior colleagues
- input into accessibility strategy with business areas or teams
- create and deliver accessibility training
- engage with teams across different levels and capabilities
- potentially manage or lead individuals or sub-teams
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government accessibility community

**Where this level sits.** This role has four levels:

1. Junior accessibility specialist
2. Accessibility specialist
3. **Senior accessibility specialist** — you are here
4. Head of accessibility

Your next step is head of accessibility: creating an accessibility vision or strategy for
the organisation, influencing organisational strategy and priorities, ensuring the right
conditions for specialists to work effectively, developing departmental capability, and
owning the overall approach to accessibility risk.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, **your published wording for *Technical understanding (accessibility)* is the most
demanding in the whole role** — more demanding than the head of accessibility's, which
matches the accessibility specialist wording two levels below. Yours names excellent
knowledge including edge cases, deep knowledge of technologies such as ARIA, understanding
how usability influences accessibility, and confidence with a range of assistive
technologies. That is how the framework publishes it, and it means your level is the
technical peak of this role.

Second, **head of accessibility has no *Consultancy* skill at all.** Yours is the last
level where it appears. That is unusual, since advising is much of what a head does.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating information
2. Consultancy
3. Governance and assurance (accessibility)
4. Leadership and guidance
5. Technical understanding (accessibility)
6. Testing
7. User focus

## Job skills

### Skill: Communicating information

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: work collaboratively in a group
and build relationships with others; identify issues through Agile health checks and work
with others to address them; manage stakeholder expectations; be flexible and capable of
proactive and reactive communication; and host or moderate difficult discussions within the
team or with senior stakeholders.

**What that looks like here.** The difficult discussion in accessibility work is usually
about a launch date and a known barrier. Holding that conversation so the decision is made
consciously — rather than by nobody mentioning it — is the job.

**The next step.** At head you mediate between people and mend relationships at all levels,
host discussions about high risk and complexity under time pressure, and represent the
community to large audiences inside and outside government.

### Skill: Governance and assurance (accessibility)

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: challenge teams, make full use of

### Skill: Leadership and guidance

**What the framework expects at this level.** Six things: make decisions characterised by
medium levels of risk and complexity and recommend decisions as risk and complexity
increase; build consensus between services or independent stakeholders; identify problems or
issues in the team dynamic and rectify them; engage in varying types of feedback, choosing
the right type at the right time and ensuring the discussion and decision stick; bring
people together to form a motivated team and help create the right environment; and
facilitate the best team makeup depending on the situation.

**What that looks like here.** This is a substantial people-skills list for a specialist
role, and it is there because embedding accessibility is a change management problem, not a
technical one.

**The next step.** At head you change organisational structures, lead strategy for the whole
organisation, make decisions with high risk and impact, build consensus between
organisations, unblock issues between departments at the highest level, and coach the
organisation on team dynamics and conflict resolution.

### Skill: Technical understanding (accessibility)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Eight things: demonstrate excellent knowledge
of accessibility standards and legislation, **including edge cases**; communicate
requirements to a range of stakeholders; actively share deep knowledge of how to select and
use appropriate technology, such as Accessible Rich Internet Applications, to provide an
excellent experience; perform detailed audits and provide solutions for others to work from;
understand and communicate **how usability influences accessibility, and the boundary
between them**; advocate removing barriers across all your interactions; provide training on
a range of accessibility issues; and demonstrate confidence using a range of assistive
technologies.

**This is the most demanding technical wording in the role** — more than at head of
accessibility, whose published text matches the level two below yours.

**What that looks like here.** The usability boundary is the sophisticated part. Something
can meet every criterion and still be miserable to use with a screen reader. Knowing where
compliance ends and usability begins — and being able to explain that to a team who thought
they were finished — is senior work.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: collaborate with user researchers
and represent users internally; explain the difference between user needs and the desires of
the user; **champion user research to focus on all users**; prioritise and define approaches
to understand the user story, guiding others in doing so; and offer recommendations on the
best tools and methods to use.

**What that looks like here.** *All users* is doing the work in that sentence. Research that
never includes disabled participants produces services that were never tested by the people
most likely to be excluded, and championing that inclusion is a core part of your role.

**The next step.** At head you give direction on tools and methods, demonstrate experience
meeting user needs across channels, bring insight into how needs have changed over time, and
apply strategic thinking to providing the best service.
