# Junior accessibility specialist - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you work as a junior accessibility specialist
in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent
it so that you can say what you do now, what you would like to get better at, and what
would help.

The framework describes this level as learning on the job by assisting with accessibility
testing. It expects you to develop skills while working under supervision to provide
accessibility testing; understand and use different types of testing; **be aware of but not
responsible for assistive technology testing**; and engage with and contribute to the
cross-government accessibility community.

That third one is unusually explicit and worth reading carefully. Testing with assistive
technology is skilled work — a screen reader in the hands of someone who has used one for
years behaves nothing like one in the hands of a beginner — and the framework deliberately
does not make you responsible for it yet.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. There are no right answers. Five skills, each
with a plain explanation, a rating and a couple of questions. Take about 25 minutes.

## How to fill in this form

This is a web page you can read, not one you can type into. Nothing you write here would be
saved or sent — the page has no way to do that.

So: print it, or copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your
organisation uses for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so your
answers make sense on their own.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **You can skip anything.** Leaving a question blank does not count against you.
- **Answer about what you actually do**, not what you are supposed to be learning.
- **"I have not done this yet" is the right answer at a junior level** for a lot of these,
  and much more useful than stretching a small example.
- **You do not have to disclose anything about yourself.** Some accessibility specialists
  have lived experience of disability and some do not; nothing here asks, and nothing here
  should be read as asking.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who will see your answers, what they will be
  used for, or how long they will be kept, it is completely reasonable to ask before you
  fill it in.

## Rating scale

Some questions ask you to rate yourself. Use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you learned on a course.
- **0s and 1s are completely normal here.** Your level's own wording is *under supervision*,
  *show an understanding*, *be aware of*. A 1 is frequently the exact published expectation.

Here is what a filled-in answer looks like:

> **Technical understanding (accessibility)**
>
> - [x] 2 — Working: I do it with support
>
> *Evidence:* I run automated checks and do the basic manual ones — headings, alt text,
> contrast, keyboard navigation — and write up what I find. My senior checks anything that
> needs a judgement call about whether something genuinely fails.

That is a good answer. It names real practice and says exactly where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: accessibility specialist.** You provide support, advice and guidance to other
roles in the Government Digital and Data profession about how to create accessible digital
services. You use knowledge of accessibility guidelines, provide technical expertise, and
bring the voice of disabled users to ensure that product teams make digital services that
can be used by everyone.

**Your level: junior accessibility specialist.** You learn on the job by assisting with
accessibility testing. The framework expects that you:

- develop skills while working under supervision to provide accessibility testing
- understand and use different types of testing
- be aware of but not responsible for assistive technology testing
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government accessibility community

**Where this level sits.** This role has four levels:

1. **Junior accessibility specialist** — you are here
2. Accessibility specialist
3. Senior accessibility specialist
4. Head of accessibility

Your next step is accessibility specialist: coaching more junior colleagues, supporting
training materials, and performing accessibility audits yourself.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Your level has **five** skills. Every level
above has more — *Governance and assurance (accessibility)* and *Leadership and guidance*
both appear from accessibility specialist upwards. If you are already commenting on
governance documents or resolving disagreements between teams, you are working ahead of the
published expectation.

**The five skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating information
2. Consultancy
3. Technical understanding (accessibility)
4. Testing
5. User focus

### Skill: Communicating information

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: listen to the needs of design and
business stakeholders and interpret information; and take part in discussions within a
multidisciplinary team.

**What that means day to day.** Accessibility findings land better when they explain who is
affected and how, rather than citing a criterion number. "This heading structure means
someone using a screen reader cannot skim the page" is more useful than a reference code,
even though the reference code also matters.

**What changes at accessibility specialist.** You would also advocate for the team
externally, manage differing perspectives, and shape communications for the audience.

### Skill: Consultancy

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: show an understanding of the
need for consulting in your role and its purpose; work under guidance to identify what
advice, guidance and recommendations might be appropriate, usually by referring to previous
work by others; and show an understanding of how to frame problems that need to be solved.

**What that means day to day.** *Referring to previous work by others* is good advice, not
a limitation. Most accessibility problems have been solved before, and knowing where the
established patterns and guidance live is faster and safer than inventing a fix.

**What changes at accessibility specialist.** You would provide advice and recommendations
from your own specialist knowledge, propose methodologies and implementation approaches, and
frame problems so they are easily understood.

### Skill: Technical understanding (accessibility)

**What the framework expects at your level.** Five things: demonstrate knowledge of some
details of the standards and legislation, and know where to find more information;
demonstrate knowledge of basic HTML, CSS and JavaScript and related common accessibility
issues; perform basic automated and manual checks on websites and documents, and communicate
findings and fixes; be an advocate for the people affected by accessibility barriers within
the context of your work; and demonstrate knowledge of using some assistive technology.

**What that means day to day.** Automated tools catch perhaps a third of issues, and the
rest need a person. Knowing enough HTML to see why something is announced wrongly is what
makes your findings actionable rather than a list of complaints.

**What changes at accessibility specialist.** You would communicate the requirements of
standards and legislation, advocate technical solutions, perform detailed audits, provide
introductory training, and be confident with one or more assistive technologies.

### Skill: Testing

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: correctly execute test scripts
under supervision; and understand the role of testing and how it works.

**What that means day to day.** Working through a defined set of checks carefully and
recording what you find, including the things you were not sure about. A test result that
says "unclear, needs review" is more useful than a guess either way.

**What changes at accessibility specialist.** You would review requirements and
specifications and define test conditions, identify issues and risks, and analyse and
report on test activities and results.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: show an awareness or understanding
of user experience analysis and its principles; and explain the purpose of user stories and
the focus on user needs.

**What that means day to day.** Your role description says you bring the voice of disabled
users to product teams. That is only possible if you have heard it — watching research with
disabled participants teaches things no guideline can, and asking to observe some is a
reasonable request at any level.

**What changes at accessibility specialist.** You would identify and engage with users or
stakeholders to collate evidence of user needs, define research that fits those needs, and
use quantitative and qualitative data to turn user focus into outcomes.
