# Senior Graphic Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A graphic designer creates graphic elements that underpin interaction and service design. You will use layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure that content is legible and readable and that users see and understand interactions. A senior graphic designer is a designer who works with minimal support and can influence and mentor others.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same five-point
scale. This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well someone does the
thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel
about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Design communication.** The framework expects a senior
graphic designer to "clearly explain complex problems and ideas to teams or
stakeholders outside of design" and to "build consensus around a design
approach, for example, by asking difficult questions and challenging
assumptions." Someone who can explain a simple design decision but
struggles with a complex, multi-part problem, or avoids stakeholders outside
design, is showing at most **2 — Intermediate**. Someone who clearly
explains a complex problem to a non-design stakeholder, documents the
decision and any unresolved issues, and builds consensus by challenging an
assumption in the room is showing **3 — Advanced**, the baseline this level
expects. Someone who goes further and gains support for a whole design
strategy from senior leaders is showing evidence closer to **4 — Expert**,
above this level's baseline.

## About this role level

The User-centred design profession's Graphic designer role creates graphic
elements that underpin interaction and service design. Across the role, a
graphic designer uses layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure
that content is legible and readable, and that users see and understand
interactions.

The role has six levels, in order: Associate graphic designer, Junior graphic
designer, Graphic designer, Senior graphic designer, Lead graphic designer, and
Head of graphic design. This assessment is for the fourth of them.

A **senior graphic designer** is a designer who works with minimal support and
can influence and mentor others. At this role level, they will work with
service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts,
potentially hold responsibility across complex services, help set direction
and embed good practice within teams, and make important decisions based on
research, understanding how that research impacts others.

The framework names seven skills for this role level: Design communication,
Designing for everyone, Designing strategically, Designing together,
Evidence-based design, Iterative design, and Leading design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior graphic designer is expected to clearly explain complex problems and
ideas to teams or stakeholders outside of design, clearly document and
communicate design decisions, related risks and any unresolved issues, and
build consensus around a design approach, for example by asking difficult
questions and challenging assumptions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Explaining *complex* problems to people outside
design, and actively building consensus by challenging assumptions, is
independent, senior-level communication, well beyond the graphic designer's
baseline of explaining a single decision clearly.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain a design decision to someone outside the design
  team.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a simple design decision to a design colleague, with
  support for anything more complex.
- 2 — Intermediate: Clearly explains a straightforward design problem to a
  non-design stakeholder.
- 3 — Advanced: Clearly explains a complex, multi-part design problem to a
  stakeholder outside design, documents decisions, risks and unresolved
  issues, and builds consensus by asking difficult questions.
- 4 — Expert: Gains support for design strategy from senior leaders and
  coaches others in effective design communication.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to describe a complex piece of design work involving
  several constraints (for example, brand, accessibility and a tight
  deadline) and how you explained the trade-offs to a non-design
  stakeholder.
- Look back at a design decision document or risk log you produced.
- Ask about a time you challenged an assumption in a meeting to build
  consensus around a design approach.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior graphic designer is expected to advise teams on how to design
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services,
design and deliver ethical content or services that consider the personal and
social context of users, and ensure a design meets appropriate standards, for
example accessibility regulations.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Advising other teams, and delivering ethical design
that accounts for personal and social context, is independent practice with
influence beyond the designer's own work — the "with support" qualifier that
capped the graphic designer's baseline has gone.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what it means for a design to consider a user's
  personal or social context.
- 1 — Beginner: Applies a basic accessibility checklist to their own work,
  with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently ensures their own design work meets
  accessibility standards.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises other teams on inclusive, accessible and sustainable
  design, and designs ethically, accounting for users' personal and social
  context.
- 4 — Expert: Measurably improves inclusive and sustainable design practice
  across multiple teams or the organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the designer advised another team on making a design
  accessible or inclusive, and what changed as a result.
- Think about how you'd describe a design decision that had to account for a user's
  social or personal circumstances, such as low digital literacy or limited
  data.
- Review evidence that a design you produced or advised on meets a named
  accessibility standard.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior graphic designer is expected to help a team understand how
user-centred design helps it meet its goals, help teams align their work to
the goals and vision of their organisation, use risks, opportunities and
constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design, and identify
and create new design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Helping a team understand and align, and creating
*new* patterns rather than contributing to existing ones, is independent,
influencing work that goes beyond the graphic designer's own alignment of
their work to team goals.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain how design connects to organisational goals.
- 1 — Beginner: Aligns their own work to team goals, with guidance.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently aligns their own work to team and
  organisational goals and contributes to existing patterns.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps a team understand how design meets its goals, aligns
  team work to organisational vision, and creates new design patterns and
  components informed by risk and opportunity.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds design as part of organisational strategy development.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the designer helped a team see how a design choice
  connected to a wider organisational goal.
- Look back at a new pattern or component you created and the reasoning behind it.
- Ask how you have used a technology, policy or system constraint to shape
  a design rather than being blocked by it.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior graphic designer is expected to advise others how to effectively
plan and run design sessions with a team, users or stakeholders, adapt a
design session to ensure a useful outcome, effectively involve the right
people throughout the design process, and work across team or profession
boundaries, for example with policy teams.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Advising *others* on running sessions, and adapting a
session in the room, is influencing practice beyond running your own
sessions well, and working across profession boundaries, such as with policy
teams, is a distinctly senior reach.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how to plan a design session.
- 1 — Beginner: Takes part in and contributes to a design session someone
  else runs.
- 2 — Intermediate: Plans and runs their own design sessions competently.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises other designers on planning and running sessions,
  adapts a session that is not delivering a useful outcome, and works across
  team or profession boundaries such as with policy teams.
- 4 — Expert: Plans and runs sessions that include senior leaders and coaches
  others in involving stakeholders of any level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the designer advised a colleague on how to run a better
  design session.
- Think about how you'd describe a session that was not working and how you adapted
  it in the moment.
- Ask about a project where you worked with a team outside design, such as
  policy, and how you made that collaboration work.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior graphic designer is expected to analyse, synthesise and clearly
explain complex evidence relevant to users or a service, for example large
data sets, help their team use design hypotheses effectively, and use complex
research and data to develop and test design ideas.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Handling *complex* evidence, such as large data sets,
and helping a team use hypotheses well, is independent, teaching-adjacent
practice beyond analysing straightforward evidence for a single design.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain what a piece of research or data evidence shows.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains simple evidence with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently analyses and explains straightforward
  evidence relevant to a design.
- 3 — Advanced: Analyses and explains complex evidence such as large data
  sets, and helps a team frame and use design hypotheses effectively.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds hypothesis-driven design practice and guides the
  organisation on using evidence to improve services.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to talk through a piece of complex evidence, for example
  a large analytics data set, and what it changed about a design.
- Ask about a time you helped a colleague frame a testable design
  hypothesis.
- Look back at a research-informed design decision and the evidence behind it.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior graphic designer is expected to help other designers apply
iterative design principles and agile methodologies to their work, iterate
and improve complex designs based on successive rounds of research,
independently prototype complex ideas at an appropriate fidelity, and adapt
designs quickly to changes in requirements, priorities or user needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Helping *other* designers apply iterative principles,
and handling complex designs and fast-changing requirements, is independent
practice with a coaching element, beyond iterating your own straightforward
work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how a design changed across iterations.
- 1 — Beginner: Iterates a simple design with guidance on what to change.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently iterates straightforward designs across
  rounds of research.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps other designers apply iterative principles, iterates
  complex designs, and adapts quickly when requirements or priorities
  change.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds iterative practice across teams or the wider
  organisation and leads prototyping of complex ideas at any fidelity.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the designer helped a colleague iterate your design more
  effectively.
- Think about how you'd describe a complex design that changed significantly across
  several rounds of research, and why.
- Ask about a time requirements or priorities changed suddenly, and how
  quickly your design adapted.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior graphic designer is expected to lead and coordinate design work in
their team, with support, communicate the value of user-centred design to
their team, support other designers, and work with digital and data leaders
in their organisation when needed.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework repeats this skill's wording
unchanged from the graphic designer level below, including the "with
support" qualifier, so the baseline stays at Intermediate rather than
rising with the rest of the matrix — the accountability bullets at this
level describe growing leadership through "helping set direction," but the
skill text itself has not moved on yet.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe why user-centred design matters to their team.
- 1 — Beginner: Describes the value of user-centred design when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: With support, leads or coordinates a piece of design work
  in the team, communicates the value of user-centred design, and supports
  another designer.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads and coordinates design work without needing support,
  advocates for user-centred design outside the design team, and mentors
  other designers.
- 4 — Expert: Sets a vision for design leadership across multiple teams or
  the organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the designer led or coordinated design work, and how much
  support you needed to do it.
- Ask how you have supported another designer's development.
- Ask about a time you communicated the value of user-centred design to
  someone outside the design team, for example a service manager or
  programme director.
