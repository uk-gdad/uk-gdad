# Senior developer (management) - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A software developer designs, runs and improves software that meets user needs. A senior developer delivers and integrates software to form a complete service.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale.
This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it measures how well
someone performs against the evidence available, not how often they do the
thing or how they say they feel about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example: Availability and capacity management.** A person rated
**1 — Beginner** might be able to say what an availability standard is for, but
has only ever followed one that someone else wrote, and would need close
supervision to change it. A person rated **3 — Advanced** owns the correct
implementation of availability and capacity standards for their own service,
independently spots a capacity issue before it causes an incident, and
initiates the remedial action themselves rather than escalating it and waiting
to be told what to do. A person rated **4 — Expert** does all of that and is
also someone other teams' capacity plans and availability standards get
checked against — they can explain, to someone else who owns a different
service, what "correct implementation" means and why a particular remedial
action is the right one, and other people's standards improve after talking to
them.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs. In this role, a developer is
responsible for writing clean, secure code following a test-driven approach,
and for creating code that is open by default and easy for others to reuse.

**The level: senior developer (management).** A senior developer delivers and
integrates software to form a complete service. At this role level, a senior
developer plans and leads development on sets of related stories; has an
understanding of the whole system and takes responsibility for teaching this
to others; works with other disciplines to understand what needs to be built;
coaches and mentors more junior colleagues; operates the production services
they build; and finds ways to improve system robustness, resilience and
stability.

**This is the management track.** The framework publishes two roles at this
level of seniority: a technical role and a management role. This document
assesses the **management** track. Its sibling, the **technical** track at the
same seniority, is `4-senior-developer` in this repository's role index — a
different, separately assessed role level, not a lower or a higher one. The two
tracks share almost every skill and almost every word of accountability; where
this document's wording differs from the technical track's, it is because the
framework itself gives the management track a more standards-owning,
action-initiating form of a skill, and this document says so explicitly (see
Availability and capacity management, below).

**Where this level sits in the software developer ladder**, in order:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. Senior developer (the technical track at this same seniority)
5. **Senior developer (management)** — this level
6. Lead developer
7. Lead developer (management)
8. Principal developer
9. Principal developer (management)

**The skills this level names**, in the order the framework lists them:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

Nine of these ten skills carry identical expectations to the technical senior
developer track — the framework's own wording does not distinguish the two
tracks for them. The tenth, **Availability and capacity management**, is
worded differently for this management track: it asks someone to ensure the
*correct implementation of* availability and capacity management standards and
procedures, to identify capacity issues and *implement the required changes*,
and to *initiate remedial action* — a more accountable, process-owning form of
the skill than the technical track's expectation to manage service components
against business needs and KPIs.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to ensure the correct
implementation of availability and capacity management standards and
procedures, to identify capacity issues and implement the changes those issues
require, and to initiate remedial action themselves rather than waiting for
someone else to direct it.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is the one skill where the framework's wording for
the management track is materially more accountable than the technical track
at the same seniority: it is not framed around meeting KPIs on the components
you personally own, but around ensuring correct implementation of *standards
and procedures* and initiating remedial action off your own judgement. A person
operating production services and coaching junior colleagues, as this level's
accountabilities require, needs to be independently trusted with that
standard-owning, action-initiating form of the skill.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never worked with an availability or capacity standard, and
  cannot describe what "correct implementation" of one would mean for a
  service they have worked on.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe what an availability or capacity standard is for,
  and has followed one someone else wrote, but has not judged whether it was
  implemented correctly and would need close supervision to change it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Follows the team's availability and capacity procedures
  correctly for their own components, and can identify an obvious capacity
  issue when it is pointed out, but relies on someone more senior to decide
  what remedial action to take.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently checks that availability and capacity standards
  are being implemented correctly across a service, not just their own part of
  it; spots a developing capacity issue before it causes an incident; and
  initiates the remedial action themselves, choosing and starting the fix
  rather than escalating and waiting.
- 4 — Expert: Writes or substantially revises the availability or capacity
  standard other people are implementing, teaches other developers what
  "correct implementation" looks like, and is someone consulted when a
  capacity issue is ambiguous or contested.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the person to walk through a real capacity issue you identified: how
  you noticed it, what remedial action you initiated, and what you checked
  afterwards to confirm the standard was correctly implemented.
- Review an availability standard, capacity plan, or runbook you wrote or
  updated, and ask what problem with the previous version it fixed.
- Present a scenario — a service is trending towards a capacity limit next
  month, with no incident yet — and ask what standard you would check, what
  remedial action you would initiate, and at what point you would escalate
  rather than act alone.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to work under guidance
to identify opportunities to optimise the development process, and to
contribute to putting proposed solutions into practice.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Work under guidance" is the framework's own ceiling on
this skill at this level — it does not yet ask someone to own process change
unsupervised — but a person coaching junior colleagues and operating production
services is expected to notice friction in the team's process and contribute
real fixes, not just follow whatever process exists.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not identified or contributed to a change in how their team
  develops software.
- 1 — Beginner: Can name a frustration with the current development process
  when asked, but has not proposed or helped build a fix for it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Raises a process problem with a lead or manager and helps
  implement a fix once someone else has decided what it should be.
- 3 — Advanced: Under guidance from a lead, identifies a genuine optimisation
  opportunity in the development process — for example in how code moves from
  review to release — and contributes substantively to building and rolling
  out the fix, checking with colleagues that it actually helped.
- 4 — Expert: Independently identifies and drives process optimisation across
  more than one team without needing guidance, and other developers adopt
  changes because this person proposed them.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a development process change you helped bring about:
  what the problem was, what guidance you worked under, and what you
  personally contributed to the fix.
- Ask how you would tell whether a proposed process change actually made
  things better, rather than just feeling different.
- Look back at a retrospective, process document, or team wiki page you contributed
  to that proposes or records a process change.

### Skill: Information security

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to design solutions and
services with security controls built in from the start, specifically
engineered to mitigate the security threats those solutions and services
actually face.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Designing controls in — rather than adding them after
the fact, or applying a checklist without judging the actual threat — is a
senior, independent expectation, and this level's accountability for
operating production services makes that judgement a live, recurring one.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not considered security threats when designing a solution or
  service.
- 1 — Beginner: Applies a security checklist or a standard someone else wrote
  when told to, but cannot explain which threat a given control mitigates.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies common threats to a service they are building
  and adds standard controls for them, with review from someone more senior.
- 3 — Advanced: Designs a solution's security controls from the start based on
  the specific threats that solution actually faces, explaining the reasoning
  to the rest of the team rather than presenting the controls as a given.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the security design approach other developers follow for
  their services, and is someone who spots a threat a standard checklist
  would have missed.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the person to describe a service you helped design and the specific
  threats its security controls were chosen to mitigate, not a generic list of
  controls.
- Present a design scenario for a new service handling sensitive data and try to identify the threats and propose controls, then explain your
  reasoning.
- Look back at a design document, architecture decision record, or threat model you
  authored or contributed to.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to apply modern
development standards themselves, and to support other people in applying
them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The second half of the expectation — supporting others
in applying the standards, not just applying them personally — matches this
level's coaching and mentoring accountability directly, so the baseline sits
above simple personal competence.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Does not follow current development standards and practices in
  their own work.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows modern development standards when told which ones to
  use, but cannot explain why a given standard exists or help someone else
  apply it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies modern development standards consistently in their
  own work, and can answer a colleague's question about them when asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Applies modern development standards independently and
  proactively helps junior colleagues adopt them — for example by pairing on a
  change, or reviewing a pull request with the standard in mind and explaining
  the reasoning behind a comment, not just the fix.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes what the team's modern development standards are, and is
  sought out by other developers deciding how to apply a standard to an
  unusual case.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review code the person has written recently against the team's current
  standards, and try to explain a choice you made that a standard
  informed.
- Ask for an example of helping a more junior colleague adopt a standard you
  were not yet using, and what changed as a result.
- Ask a structured interview question about a standard the team has adopted
  recently and how you would help a resistant colleague come round to it.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to collaborate with
others when necessary to review specifications, and to use the agreed
specifications to design, code, test and document programs or scripts of
medium-to-high complexity, using the right standards and tools.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Medium-to-high complexity" and working independently
from agreed specifications are the framework's own words for this level, and
they describe someone who no longer needs specifications broken down for them
before they can start.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot design, code, test and document a program or script from a
  specification without significant help.
- 1 — Beginner: Codes to a specification for simple, low-complexity work with
  close supervision, and needs help choosing the right standards and tools.
- 2 — Intermediate: Delivers medium-complexity work from an agreed
  specification with some guidance, and follows the team's chosen standards
  and tools once told which apply.
- 3 — Advanced: Reviews specifications with others when needed, then
  independently designs, codes, tests and documents programs or scripts of
  medium-to-high complexity, choosing the right standards and tools for the
  job without being told which to use.
- 4 — Expert: Handles the highest-complexity work in the team unsupervised,
  and is someone other developers bring an ambiguous or contested
  specification to before they start building.

**Evidence to gather**

- Look back at a substantial piece of code the person designed, built, tested and
  documented, and try to walk through the decisions behind it.
- Give a specification with a genuine ambiguity in it and ask how you would
  resolve it before writing any code.
- Ask for an example of reviewing a specification with someone else before
  building against it, and what changed as a result of that conversation.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to approach prototyping
as a team activity — actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with
others — to establish design patterns and iterate them, and to use a variety of
prototyping methods, choosing the most appropriate one for the situation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Treating prototyping as something to actively solicit
from a team, and choosing among several methods, is a level above simply
building a prototype when asked, and fits this level's cross-discipline
accountability to work out what needs to be built.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not built or tested a prototype of a solution before building
  it in full.
- 1 — Beginner: Builds a prototype when specifically asked to, using whichever
  method someone else suggests.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses a reasonable prototyping method for a given
  problem and tests it with a colleague, with some guidance on what "good"
  looks like.
- 3 — Advanced: Actively asks other people for prototypes rather than waiting
  to be asked, tests them with the team, establishes a design pattern from
  what works and iterates it, and picks the right prototyping method — a
  sketch, a clickable mock-up, a working spike — for the question at hand.
- 4 — Expert: Sets how the team prototypes as a discipline, and other
  developers' prototyping practice improves from working alongside them.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a prototype you built or solicited from others, what
  method you chose and why, and what changed after testing it with the team.
- Ask how you decided a design pattern was worth establishing from a
  prototype, rather than treating it as a one-off.
- Present a new, ambiguous feature and ask which prototyping method you would
  reach for first, and why that one rather than another.

### Skill: Service support

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to identify, locate and
fix service faults.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This level operates the production services it builds,
so fault-finding has to be an independent, reliable capability rather than
something done with a colleague looking over their shoulder.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not diagnosed or fixed a service fault.
- 1 — Beginner: Can fix a fault once someone else has identified where it is,
  but needs help locating the cause.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies and locates common, previously-seen faults with
  some support, and fixes them correctly.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies, locates and fixes faults in
  production services, including ones they have not seen before, working from
  symptoms through to root cause.
- 4 — Expert: Diagnoses the hardest, most ambiguous faults in the service, and
  is someone other developers bring an unresolved fault to.

**Evidence to gather**

- Walk through a real fault the person diagnosed and fixed: the symptoms you
  started from, how you located the cause, and how you confirmed the fix
  worked.
- Present a description of an unfamiliar fault — symptoms only, no cause — and
  ask how you would go about locating it.
- Ask a referee or colleague for an example of the person fixing a fault
  nobody else on the team could immediately explain.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to design systems
characterised by medium levels of risk, impact, and business or technical
complexity; to select appropriate design standards, methods and tools and
ensure they are applied effectively; and to review other people's systems
designs to check they select the right technology, use resources efficiently
and integrate multiple systems and technologies correctly.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Reviewing other people's designs, not just producing
your own, is explicit in the framework's wording at this level and matches its
coaching accountability — a person cannot review someone else's design
competently while still needing their own reviewed step by step.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not designed or reviewed a system design.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to part of a system design under close direction,
  and cannot yet review someone else's design unsupported.
- 2 — Intermediate: Designs low-risk, low-complexity systems with guidance,
  and can point out an obvious problem in someone else's design when asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs systems of medium risk, impact and
  complexity, selects the design standards, methods and tools to use, and
  reviews a colleague's design for correct technology choice, efficient use of
  resources and correct integration — giving feedback that changes the design.
- 4 — Expert: Designs the highest-complexity systems in the team's remit, and
  is the reviewer other developers' designs are checked against before they
  are trusted.

**Evidence to gather**

- Look back at a system design the person authored, and try to justify the
  design standards, methods and tools you chose over the alternatives.
- Think about how you'd review a colleague's design — real or a prepared example — and
  observe what you check for and what feedback you give.
- Ask for an example of reviewing someone else's design and finding a problem
  with resource use or system integration that the original author had
  missed.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to define the
integration build, co-ordinate build activities across systems, and
understand how to undertake and support integration testing activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Defining an integration build and co-ordinating across
systems is inherently a whole-system responsibility, matching this level's
accountability to understand the whole system and teach that understanding to
others.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not worked on integrating one system with another.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an integration build someone else has defined, with
  supervision.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes to defining an integration build for a
  straightforward case, and supports integration testing when asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently defines the integration build for a set of
  related systems, co-ordinates the build activities across the teams or
  components involved, and undertakes or supports integration testing without
  needing it explained.
- 4 — Expert: Defines integration approaches across the most complex,
  multi-system builds, and is someone other developers check their
  integration plan against before starting.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the person to describe an integration build you defined: which systems
  were involved, how you co-ordinated build activities across you, and how
  integration testing was carried out.
- Present a scenario with two or three systems that need to work together and
  ask how you would define and sequence the integration build.
- Review an integration plan, interface specification, or test plan you
  authored.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a senior developer (management) to collaborate with user
researchers and represent users internally; to explain the difference between
user needs and the desires of the user; to champion user research so that it
focuses on all users; to prioritise and define approaches to understand the
user story, guiding others in doing so; and to offer recommendations on the
best tools and methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Guiding others" and "championing" user research are
this level's own words, not an aspiration for the level above — a management
track developer coaching junior colleagues is expected to be the one setting
that standard, not still learning to represent users themselves.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not engaged with user research or represented users in a
  decision about what to build.
- 1 — Beginner: Attends user research sessions when invited, but cannot yet
  explain the difference between what a user asks for and what they actually
  need.
- 2 — Intermediate: Collaborates with user researchers on their own piece of
  work, and represents users in team discussions with some support.
- 3 — Advanced: Represents users internally without prompting, explains the
  difference between user needs and user desires to colleagues who conflate
  them, champions research covering the full range of users rather than the
  easiest to reach, and guides a junior colleague through defining their own
  approach to understanding a user story.
- 4 — Expert: Sets how the wider team approaches user focus, and is sought out
  for a recommendation on which research tool or method fits an unusual
  situation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of guiding a more junior colleague through understanding
  a user story, and what the colleague did differently afterwards.
- Think about how you'd explain, using a real example from your work, the difference
  between a user's stated request and your underlying need.
- Ask how you have championed research into users who are harder to reach or
  easy to overlook, and what changed in what got built as a result.
