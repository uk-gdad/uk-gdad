# Service Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities required for the User-centred design role of Service designer, at
the Service designer role level. It measures demonstrated skill against the
UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of this role
level, for a hiring decision, a promotion board, onboarding, or a capability
check.

A service designer at this level is a confident and competent designer who
develops designs based on evidence of user needs and organisational
outcomes, is trusted to make good decisions, and knows when to ask for
support. This assessment looks for independent, evidence-grounded practice
across a whole service journey — not the supervised first steps of a junior
designer, and not yet the cross-team influence expected of a senior service
designer.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of
it. Read `## Advisory` before using it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, then gather whatever
evidence exists: a portfolio of service blueprints, journey maps or
end-to-end service work spanning digital and offline channels, a structured
interview, a design task (for example, asking the candidate to explain the
decisions behind a real piece of service design), direct observation in a
design session, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. Where you have no
evidence for a skill, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way. For a
hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Every rating and every piece of evidence you gather must be
about demonstrated, job-related behaviour — see Fair and lawful use in the
project specification. Never let a rating be coloured by anything other than
what the person actually did, said, or produced.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same five-point
scale. This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well the person does the
thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel
about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Designing strategically.** The framework expects a
service designer to "align your design work to both the goals and vision of
your team and the strategic objectives of your organisation" and "identify
potential risks and use design to mitigate them." A candidate who can only
say, when asked, how a piece of work supports a team goal is showing at most
**1 — Beginner**. A candidate who independently aligns their service design
choices to team and organisational goals, and contributes new pattern ideas
to a shared library, is showing **2 — Intermediate** — the baseline this
level expects. A candidate who goes further and helps a whole team
understand how service design meets its goals is showing evidence closer to
**3 — Advanced**, above this level's own baseline.

## About this role level

The User-centred design profession's Service designer role designs the
end-to-end journey of a service. This helps a user complete their goal and
government deliver a policy intent. The role's work may involve creating or
changing transactions, products and content across both digital and offline
channels provided by different parts of government.

The role has six levels, in order: Associate service designer, Junior
service designer, Service designer, Senior service designer, Lead service
designer, and Head of service design. This assessment is for the third of
them.

A **service designer** at this level is a confident and competent designer
who can develop designs based on evidence of user needs and organisational
outcomes. At this role level, they will be trusted to make good decisions,
recognise when to ask for further guidance and support, contribute to the
development of design concepts, and interpret evidence-based research and
incorporate it into their work.

The framework names seven skills for this role level: Design communication,
Designing for everyone, Designing strategically, Designing together,
Evidence-based design, Iterative design, and Leading design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

A service designer is expected to clearly explain problems and design
ideas to others, and clearly explain design decisions to others using
appropriate tools and methods.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Clearly explain" describes independent,
competent communication, a step beyond the guided description expected of a
junior designer, but short of the cross-boundary, consensus-building
communication a senior designer is expected to manage.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain a design decision, even when asked directly what
  problem it solves.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a design decision when prompted, but the
  explanation is vague or needs a colleague to fill gaps.
- 2 — Intermediate: Clearly explains a design problem and the reasoning
  behind a service decision, choosing an appropriate way to show it, such
  as an annotated journey map or a short walkthrough.
- 3 — Advanced: Explains complex design problems to stakeholders outside
  design, documents decisions and unresolved issues, and builds consensus by
  asking searching questions.
- 4 — Expert: Gains support for design strategy from senior leaders and
  coaches others in communicating design effectively.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to walk through a real service blueprint or journey map
  and explain the decisions behind it, as if to a stakeholder unfamiliar
  with design.
- Review a design rationale document or presentation they have produced.
- Ask about a time a design decision was challenged, and how they explained
  or defended it.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

A service designer is expected to work with others to design and deliver
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services
that meet the needs of all users, and to ensure a design meets appropriate
standards, for example accessibility regulations, with support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Work with others to design and deliver" is
applied, collaborative practice, while "with support" on the standards
bullet keeps full, independent assurance out of reach.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what makes a service accessible or inclusive, for
  example an assisted digital channel or plain language content.
- 1 — Beginner: With guidance, checks part of a service against a basic
  accessibility or inclusion checklist.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently applies accessible and inclusive design
  practice across a service journey, and asks for support to confirm it
  meets formal standards.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises other designers on inclusive design and ensures a
  service meets accessibility regulations without prompting.
- 4 — Expert: Measurably improves inclusive design practice across multiple
  teams.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a journey map or service element the designer produced and ask how
  they checked it works for users with different access needs, including
  offline or assisted digital users.
- Ask about a specific accessibility standard and how they check for it.
- Ask about a design choice they changed because it excluded some users.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

A service designer is expected to align their design work to both the
goals and vision of their team and the strategic objectives of their
organisation, identify potential risks and use design to mitigate them,
identify opportunities and use design to take advantage of them, and
contribute to the development of design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This moves beyond explaining why alignment
matters — a more junior framing — into actually aligning work and spotting
risk and opportunity, short of the senior designer's coaching role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot connect a piece of service design work to the team's
  goals.
- 1 — Beginner: Says, when asked, how a specific piece of work supports the
  team's goals.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently aligns service design choices to team
  goals, and contributes new pattern or component ideas to a shared
  library.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps a team understand how design contributes to its
  goals, and shapes design using risk, opportunity and constraints in
  technology, systems and policy.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds design in organisational strategy.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to describe a project where their service design choices
  were driven by a team or policy goal, not just personal preference.
- Review a contribution they made to a shared pattern library or component
  set.
- Ask about a risk they spotted in a service design and what they did about
  it.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

A service designer is expected to effectively plan and run a design
session with their team, users or stakeholders, identify and engage the
right people throughout the design process, and give and receive
constructive design feedback.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Planning and running a session independently,
and handling feedback both ways, is competent practice — a step up from
taking part in a session someone else runs, and short of advising others on
how to do it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what a design session or blueprinting workshop
  is for.
- 1 — Beginner: Takes part in a design session and gives feedback when
  prompted.
- 2 — Intermediate: Plans and runs a design session, identifies who should
  be in the room, and both gives and receives constructive feedback.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises others on running sessions, adapts a session that
  is not working, and works across team or profession boundaries.
- 4 — Expert: Runs sessions that include senior leaders and coaches others
  in collaborative design practice.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to describe a design session or blueprinting workshop
  they planned and ran, including who they invited and why.
- Observe them giving feedback on a colleague's work, if possible.
- Ask about a time they received difficult feedback on their design and what
  they did with it.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A service designer is expected to analyse, synthesise and clearly explain
evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics data,
and to work with researchers or analysts to use evidence to inform, develop
and test a design idea.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Analysing and explaining evidence, and working
with a researcher or analyst to test an idea, is applied, collaborative
practice — a step beyond explaining what evidence shows with support, and
short of handling complex evidence or framing hypotheses for a team.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what evidence, such as analytics or user research,
  might tell them about a service.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains what a piece of evidence shows, with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Analyses and explains evidence relevant to a service,
  and works with a researcher or analyst to test a design idea.
- 3 — Advanced: Analyses and explains complex evidence, for example large
  data sets, and helps a team frame and use design hypotheses.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds hypothesis-driven design practice and guides the
  organisation on using evidence.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to talk through a piece of user research or service
  usage data that shaped a service design decision.
- Ask how they worked with a user researcher or analyst on a project.
- Review a design that changed as a result of evidence, and ask what the
  evidence showed.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A service designer is expected to apply iterative design principles and
agile methodologies to their work, iterate and improve a design based on
successive rounds of research, prototype their ideas at different
fidelities to explore and test designs, use a range of design tools and
techniques, and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Iterating across rounds of research, working at
more than one fidelity, and adjusting existing patterns is competent,
independent practice — short of iterating complex designs or adapting
quickly to fast-changing requirements.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what changed between two versions of a service
  design and why.
- 1 — Beginner: Makes a small change to a service element following
  feedback, with guidance on what "iteration" means in practice.
- 2 — Intermediate: Iterates a design across successive rounds of research,
  prototypes at more than one fidelity, and uses and adjusts existing
  patterns and components.
- 3 — Advanced: Iterates complex service designs, prototypes independently
  at the right fidelity for the question being asked, and adapts quickly
  when requirements change.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds iterative practice across teams and leads prototyping
  of complex ideas at any fidelity.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to show two or three versions of a service journey and
  explain what changed and why, referencing research or feedback.
- Ask about the range of tools they use for different fidelities, for
  example a paper journey map versus a piloted service change.
- Review a pattern or component they reused or adapted from a library.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A service designer is expected to lead and coordinate design work in their
team, with support, communicate the value of user-centred design to their
team, support other designers, and work with digital and data leaders in
their organisation when needed.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "With support" caps full independence, but
leading or coordinating a piece of work, even with support, is a step beyond
simply describing leadership responsibilities in the abstract, so
Intermediate is the right baseline rather than Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe why user-centred design matters to their team.
- 1 — Beginner: Describes the value of user-centred design when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: With support, leads or coordinates a piece of design
  work in the team, communicates the value of user-centred design, and
  supports another designer.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads and coordinates design work without needing support,
  advocates for user-centred design outside the design team, and mentors
  other designers.
- 4 — Expert: Sets a vision for design leadership across multiple teams or
  the organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the designer led or coordinated a piece of work, even a
  small one, and what support they needed.
- Ask how they have supported a more junior designer.
- Ask about a time they explained the value of user-centred design to
  someone outside the design team, such as a policy colleague.
