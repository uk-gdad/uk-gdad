# Associate Service Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities required for the User-centred design role of Service designer, at
the Associate service designer role level. It measures demonstrated skill
against the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of
this role level, for a hiring decision, a promotion board, onboarding, or a
capability check.

An associate service designer is a trainee in an entry-level position,
working under supervision, who brings design aptitude, potential and an
understanding of the role rather than independent delivery. This assessment
looks for a candidate who can explain and describe the ideas behind service
design, not one who can yet apply them unsupervised.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of
it. Read `## Advisory` before using it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, then gather whatever
evidence exists: a CV or portfolio (which may be coursework or a personal
project rather than professional work), a structured interview, a short,
guided task (for example, talking through how they would approach mapping a
simple service journey), direct observation of the candidate shadowing or
pairing, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. For an associate-level
candidate, "unknown" is a normal and expected answer for several skills —
record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way. For a
hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Every rating and every piece of evidence you gather must be
about demonstrated, job-related behaviour — see Fair and lawful use in the
project specification. Never let a rating be coloured by anything other than
what the person actually did, said, or produced.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same five-point
scale. This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well the person does the
thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel
about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Evidence-based design.** The framework expects an
associate service designer to "explain how research and analytics inform
user-centred design" and "identify common sources and types of data that
inform user-centred design." A candidate who cannot name a source of
evidence, such as user research or analytics, even when prompted, is
showing **0 — None**. A candidate who explains how research or analytics can
inform a service design decision, names a couple of common data sources, and
can frame a simple idea as a hypothesis to test is showing **1 — Beginner** —
the baseline this level expects. A candidate who goes further and,
unprompted, analyses a straightforward piece of evidence and explains what
it means for a service is showing evidence closer to **2 — Intermediate**,
above this level's own baseline.

## About this role level

The User-centred design profession's Service designer role designs the
end-to-end journey of a service. This helps a user complete their goal and
government deliver a policy intent. The role's work may involve creating or
changing transactions, products and content across both digital and offline
channels provided by different parts of government.

The role has six levels, in order: Associate service designer, Junior
service designer, Service designer, Senior service designer, Lead service
designer, and Head of service design. This assessment is for the first of
them.

An **associate service designer** is a trainee in an entry-level position.
Working under supervision, they need design aptitude, potential and an
understanding of the role — the framework sets no separate list of
accountabilities for this level beyond the skills themselves, because this is
a supervised, learning-first level.

The framework names seven skills for this role level: Design communication,
Designing for everyone, Designing strategically, Designing together,
Evidence-based design, Iterative design, and Leading design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate service designer is expected to explain the importance of
articulating design decisions to others, and describe some methods for
explaining design decisions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Both bullets ask for explanation and description of
an idea, not for demonstrated communication of a real design decision, which
fits the first rung above None.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why explaining a design decision to someone else
  matters.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains why articulating design decisions matters, and
  describes a method for doing so, such as a journey map or a short
  walkthrough.
- 2 — Intermediate: Clearly explains a real design decision they made, using
  an appropriate method, with only light guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently explains complex problems to stakeholders
  outside design and builds consensus around a design approach.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches others in effective design communication.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain why it matters that a designer can say why
  they made a particular choice, using an everyday example if they have no
  professional one.
- Ask them to describe a method for explaining a design decision, such as a
  service blueprint, a journey map or a short write-up.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate service designer is expected to explain the importance of
making government content and services inclusive, accessible and
environmentally sustainable, explain different access needs that users can
have and ways to meet them, and describe how privilege and power influence
the design and delivery of content and services.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Explaining importance and describing influence is
awareness, not yet applied practice, so the baseline sits at Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name an access need a user might have, such as low
  digital confidence or no reliable internet connection.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains why inclusive, accessible and sustainable design
  matters, and names a couple of different access needs and how they might
  be met across a service.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies a basic accessibility or inclusion check to a
  simple part of a service, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises a team on inclusive and accessible service design
  without needing support.
- 4 — Expert: Measurably improves inclusive design practice across multiple
  teams.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain why a service should work for a user who is
  offline, has low digital confidence, or needs an assisted digital
  channel.
- Ask them to describe how privilege or power might affect who gets a good
  service and who does not, using an example if they have one.

- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate service designer is expected to explain the importance of
aligning to the strategy of an organisation, describe how their design work
aligns to the wider goals and vision of their team, and explain the value of
using patterns and components in design.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. All three bullets ask for explanation of an idea
rather than demonstrated strategic alignment, so the baseline sits at
Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why a service designer's work should connect to
  their team's goals.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains why aligning to organisational strategy matters,
  and the value of reusing patterns and components, in general terms.
- 2 — Intermediate: With some support, describes how a specific piece of
  their own work aligns to a team goal.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently aligns their own work to team and
  organisational goals.
- 4 — Expert: Helps teams understand how design meets organisational goals
  and creates new patterns.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain, in their own words, why it matters that
  service design work supports what an organisation is trying to achieve,
  including the policy intent behind a service.
- Ask why reusing a design pattern or component might be better than
  designing something from scratch each time.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate service designer is expected to explain different techniques
for bringing together perspectives from multiple people to inform a design,
and explain the importance of constructive feedback in the design process.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Explaining techniques and importance is knowledge
rather than demonstrated facilitation or feedback practice, so the baseline
sits at Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why a service design session might involve more
  than one person's perspective.
- 1 — Beginner: Names a technique for bringing together different
  perspectives, such as a workshop or a service blueprinting session, and
  explains why constructive feedback matters.
- 2 — Intermediate: Plans and runs a simple design session, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently plans and runs design sessions and adapts
  them when they are not working.
- 4 — Expert: Runs sessions that include senior leaders and coaches others
  in collaborative design practice.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to name a way of bringing several people's perspectives
  into a service design, and why that might produce a better result than
  one person working alone.
- Ask them to explain what makes feedback on a design "constructive" rather
  than just critical.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate service designer is expected to explain how research and
analytics inform user-centred design, frame their ideas as design
hypotheses to be tested, and identify common sources and types of data that
inform user-centred design.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Explaining, framing and identifying are all
understanding-level activities rather than demonstrated analysis of real
evidence, so the baseline sits at Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a source of evidence, such as user research or
  service usage data, that might inform a service design.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains how research or analytics can inform a design
  decision, names a couple of common data sources, and can frame a simple
  idea as a hypothesis to test.
- 2 — Intermediate: Analyses and explains a straightforward piece of
  evidence relevant to a service, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently analyses and explains complex evidence.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds hypothesis-driven design practice in a team.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain what a piece of user research or service
  usage data might tell a designer, using a simple example.
- Ask them to turn a service design idea into a testable hypothesis, for
  example "if we do X, users will do Y."

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate service designer is expected to describe the basics of
iterative design methodologies such as agile, explain how iteration
supports user-centred design, and describe some ways to prototype ideas at
different levels of realism, or "fidelities," and when to use them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Describing and explaining the basics of iteration and
fidelity is understanding, not yet demonstrated iterative practice, so the
baseline sits at Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what "iteration" means in a service design context.
- 1 — Beginner: Describes, in their own words, roughly how agile working
  iterates and why that helps design meet user needs, and names a couple of
  prototype fidelities and when each might be used, for example a paper
  journey map versus a piloted service.
- 2 — Intermediate: Iterates a simple part of a service across rounds of
  feedback, with guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently iterates complex service designs and adapts
  quickly to changing requirements.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds iterative practice across teams and leads prototyping
  of complex ideas at any fidelity.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain, in their own words, what agile or iterative
  working means and why it might suit service design work.
- Ask them to describe the difference between a low-fidelity idea, such as a
  sketch of a journey, and a high-fidelity one, such as a piloted service,
  and when they would use each.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate service designer is expected to explain the role of
user-centred design in an organisation, describe typical leadership
responsibilities in design, and identify leaders in their organisation that
designers need to work with.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Explaining, describing and identifying are all
awareness-level activities rather than demonstrated leadership, so the
baseline sits at Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why user-centred design matters to an organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains the role of user-centred design in an
  organisation, and describes typical leadership responsibilities in a
  design team.
- 2 — Intermediate: With support, leads or coordinates a small piece of
  design work.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads and coordinates design work in a team independently
  and mentors other designers.
- 4 — Expert: Sets a vision for and leads design across multiple teams or a
  whole organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain, in their own words, why user-centred design
  matters to a government service.
- Ask them to name a role, such as a service manager or policy lead, that a
  service designer might need to work with, and why.
