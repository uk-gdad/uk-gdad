# Lead Content Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — typically a senior manager or a panel —
judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline abilities the
UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) Profession Capability Framework
expects of a **lead content designer**. Use it for a hiring decision, a
promotion board from senior content designer, or a general capability check
against the framework.

The framework describes this level as an expert practitioner and leader who
directs a team of content designers, assures content quality across whole
teams, and makes sure content aligns to strategy and objectives while
meeting the needs of users.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole
of one. Read `## Advisory` before you use it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, including the note on
skill wording that repeats from content designer. Then gather whatever
evidence you can: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample
or task, direct observation, or references — for a leadership role, evidence
about how a team performed under the candidate matters as much as evidence
of their own individual practice.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace back to
something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
and for a hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score
independently before comparing notes.

**Stay fair.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact between candidates.
Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using Agile working (content design), a skill this level
names:

- A candidate who applies an Agile mindset well to their own work but has
  not coached a team in it sits at **2 — Intermediate**, well below this
  assessment's baseline.
- A candidate who coaches and leads teams in Agile and Lean practices,
  determines the right approach for a project, regularly assesses capability
  within teams, and acts as a recognised expert who constructively
  challenges teams to improve sits at **4 — Expert** — the baseline this
  assessment sets for this skill.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for
people to understand and use. Their work can involve a single piece of
content or the end-to-end journey of a service, helping users complete their
goal and government deliver a policy intent. The role makes sure appropriate
content is shown to a user in the right place and the best format, starting
from discovery and working closely with user researchers, service designers
and interaction designers.

**This level: lead content designer.** The framework describes this level as
an expert practitioner and leader who directs a team of content designers.
At this level, the framework expects the person to:

- work closely with service managers, programme directors and senior
  stakeholders to resource teams, resolve problems and develop future
  projects
- promote the content design discipline, engage with the cross-government
  community and keep up to date with industry changes

**Where this level sits.** Content designer has six levels:

1. Associate content designer
2. Junior content designer
3. Content designer
4. Senior content designer
5. **Lead content designer** — assessed here
6. Head of content design

**A note on Content concepts and prototyping.** The framework's published
skill text for this skill at lead content designer repeats, word for word,
the text published for content designer (level 3) — it does not build on
senior content designer's more advanced "build and create a variety of
prototypes" wording. This assessment sets that skill's baseline from the
framework's actual published text rather than assuming the level's general
seniority applies equally to every skill, and says so here rather than
smoothing the anomaly away.

**The six skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to coach and lead teams in Agile and Lean
practices, determining the right approach for the team or the project;
evaluate and review the approach through the life of a project and iterate
and pivot accordingly; think of new and innovative ways of working to
achieve the right outcomes; act as a recognised expert and advocate the
approaches proposed, continuously reflecting on the work of the team and
constructively challenging them to improve processes and delivery; and
regularly assess and review capability within teams and ensure individuals
have the skills needed to deliver.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Coaching and leading teams, and assessing capability
within teams, matches the rating scale's own definition of Expert — mastery
demonstrated by teaching or guiding others — and reflects this level's
accountability to resource and develop teams.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never coached anyone else in Agile or Lean practice.
- 1 — Beginner: Suggests an Agile improvement to a colleague when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies an Agile mindset well to their own work but has
  not led a team's approach.
- 3 — Advanced: Determines the right Agile or Lean approach for one project
  and reviews it through the project's life — one step below this level's
  baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches and leads more than one team in Agile and Lean
  practice, regularly assesses capability gaps, and is recognised as an
  expert whose challenge improves how teams deliver.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a team's Agile or Lean approach they set,
  and how they knew it needed to change partway through a project.
- Ask them to describe a capability gap they identified in a team and what
  they did about it.
- Ask a referee, ideally someone they have coached, what changed in how the
  team worked.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to understand different prototyping
techniques, from paper sketches to coded prototypes, and use them to
visualise content in context; choose the best option to make content
understandable to different audiences and demonstrate a proposed approach to
content changes or improvements; and show the value of prototyping to the
team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. As noted above, this skill's published text
repeats content designer's exactly rather than building further, so this
assessment sets its baseline to match rather than assuming a higher bar the
text does not state.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain the difference between a low- and high-fidelity
  prototype.
- 1 — Beginner: Builds a simple prototype with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses a prototyping technique appropriate to an
  audience unsupported, and demonstrates the value of prototyping to a team.
- 3 — Advanced: Builds a variety of prototype types independently and
  establishes reusable content patterns from them — the senior content
  designer expectation, above this level's published baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the prototyping standard a whole discipline works to —
  well above what this skill's text asks for at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: ask the candidate to sketch or build a quick prototype for a
  given piece of content, and explain the technique they chose.
- Ask them to describe a time they demonstrated the value of prototyping to
  a sceptical stakeholder or team member.
- Review a prototype or mock-up they have produced.

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to manage long-term strategic relationships
with stakeholders, identifying where new connections need to be made and
existing ones nurtured; direct the strategic approach for stakeholder
relationships, establishing stakeholder objectives; act as a point of
escalation if stakeholder relationships break down or become challenging for
more junior members of the team; influence important senior stakeholders and
arbitrate when blockers are escalated; and facilitate discussions across
high risk and complex areas or projects under constrained timelines.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Acting as the point of escalation for junior
colleagues and directing the strategic approach for a whole discipline
describes someone others rely on, matching this level's leadership
accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never been the point of escalation for a stakeholder
  relationship a colleague could not resolve.
- 1 — Beginner: Manages their own strategic stakeholder relationships
  competently.
- 2 — Intermediate: Occasionally supports a colleague with a difficult
  stakeholder, with guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Manages long-term strategic relationships independently and
  identifies where new connections are needed — one step below this level's
  baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Acts as the escalation point when a colleague's stakeholder
  relationship breaks down, arbitrates escalated blockers, and facilitates
  discussions across high-risk, time-constrained work.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time a colleague escalated a broken
  stakeholder relationship to them, and how they resolved it.
- Ask them to describe a strategic stakeholder relationship they identified
  the need for and built from scratch.
- Ask a referee, ideally a senior stakeholder, how the candidate is regarded
  under time pressure.

- [ ] 0 — None

- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to help lead the design and implementation
of strategies, evaluating their impact and progress to ensure business
objectives and the needs of users are being met, and to lead and direct
strategic content improvement projects, focusing effort in the areas of
greatest priority and ensuring goals and objectives are met.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This skill's published text is identical to senior
content designer's, and this assessment keeps the same baseline for it
rather than inventing a difference the text does not support, while noting
that this level's own broader accountability — resourcing teams and
developing future projects with programme directors — sits above senior
content designer's in scope, which is worth probing directly in the evidence
log even where this specific skill's baseline has not moved.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Contributes to strategy only when told exactly what to write.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes meaningfully to a strategy someone else leads.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads a small, contained strategic improvement with
  support.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps lead the design and implementation of a strategy,
  evaluates its impact against business objectives and user needs, and
  directs a strategic content improvement project by prioritising where
  effort matters most.
- 4 — Expert: Leads content strategy for the whole organisation — the head
  of content design expectation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a strategy they helped lead across more than
  one team, and how they evaluated its impact.
- Ask them to describe how they prioritised effort on a strategic content
  improvement project with competing demands.
- Review a strategy document or evaluation they have authored or
  co-authored.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to give direction on which tools or
methods are best for teams to use; demonstrate extensive experience in
meeting the needs of users across a variety of channels; understand complex
user journeys and direct solutions to meet different needs within them;
bring insight and expertise in how user needs have changed over time to
ensure these are still relevant and met; and apply strategic thinking to
providing the best service for the user while considering business needs
and the wider cross-government context.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Give direction on which tools or methods are best for
teams" and "direct solutions" describe setting the approach other content
designers follow, which is this assessment's Expert point.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how user needs have changed over time for a
  service they have worked on.
- 1 — Beginner: Meets user needs for one channel with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Meets user needs across more than one channel, with some
  support directing a solution for a complex journey.
- 3 — Advanced: Meets user needs across a variety of channels with extensive
  experience, but has not yet directed a team's overall approach — one step
  below this level's baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Directs a team's approach to a complex user journey, gives
  direction on tools and methods across teams, and applies strategic
  thinking that balances user needs against business needs and the wider
  cross-government context.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a complex user journey they directed a
  solution for, involving more than one team.
- Ask them to describe a time they directed which tools or methods teams
  should use, and how they knew it was right.
- Ask a referee how the candidate balances user needs against business
  pressure.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to direct the approach to content life
cycle management to ensure content is regularly reviewed and evaluated by
teams; oversee teams' work to ensure the right content is being produced to
meet the needs of users; demonstrate extensive experience in creating,
iterating, managing and overseeing content across multiple channels;
encourage teams to review and evaluate the effectiveness of processes and
systems and support them to iterate for improvements; be accountable for the
production of high quality, user-focused content; and identify gaps in
content design skills and capability, helping teams to grow and develop.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Directing content life cycle management across teams,
being accountable for a whole team's output, and identifying capability gaps
to help teams grow describes leadership of the discipline, not individual
practice — this assessment's Expert point.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never overseen another content designer's output for a
  service, only their own.
- 1 — Beginner: Reviews a colleague's content when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: Oversees a small piece of a team's work with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Takes responsibility for the quality of a small team's
  content and coaches individuals directly — the senior content designer
  expectation, one step below this level's baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Directs the approach to content life cycle management across
  more than one team, is accountable for the quality of the content those
  teams produce, and identifies capability gaps to develop the teams as a
  whole.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe how they directed content life cycle
  management across more than one team.
- Ask them to describe a capability gap they identified across a team, and
  what they did to close it.
- Ask a referee, ideally someone who reported to or worked under the
  candidate's direction, how content quality changed under their oversight.
