# Associate Content Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a
reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities the UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) Profession Capability
Framework expects of an **associate content designer**. Use it for a hiring
decision, an onboarding check, or a general capability review against the
framework.

The framework describes this level as an entry-level trainee position:
someone working under supervision who needs aptitude, potential and an
understanding of the role, performing basic standard checks and copy edits.
The matrix below is built to that description, not to a more senior one — do
not mark someone down for needing supervision; the framework says they
should.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole
of one. Read `## Advisory` before you use it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what this
level actually expects — and, just as importantly, what it does not.
Because this is a trainee level, evidence may come from academic work,
placements or a small portfolio rather than paid content design experience;
weigh that appropriately rather than expecting a work history the level does
not assume. Gather whatever evidence exists: a CV or portfolio, a structured
interview, a short work sample or task, direct observation, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating you give must trace back
to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
and for a hiring decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Stay fair.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact between candidates.
Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using User-centred content design, a skill this level
names:

- A candidate who cannot spot an obvious spelling, grammar or plain-language
  problem in a short piece of copy sits at **0 — None**.
- A candidate who can perform a basic copy edit and apply obvious style rules
  when someone points them out, but needs support to catch anything less
  obvious, sits at **1 — Beginner** — the baseline this assessment sets for
  this skill.
- A candidate who applies style standards consistently without prompting is
  already working above this level's baseline, at **2 — Intermediate**.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for
people to understand and use. Their work can involve a single piece of
content or the end-to-end journey of a service, helping users complete their
goal and government deliver a policy intent. The role makes sure appropriate
content is shown to a user in the right place and the best format, starting
from discovery and working closely with user researchers, service designers
and interaction designers.

**This level: associate content designer.** The framework describes this
level as a trainee in an entry-level position, working under supervision,
who needs aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. At this
level, the framework's whole accountability statement is: perform basic
standard checks and copy edits.

**Where this level sits.** Content designer has six levels:

1. **Associate content designer** — assessed here
2. Junior content designer
3. Content designer
4. Senior content designer
5. Lead content designer
6. Head of content design

**The six skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to demonstrate experience of working in
Agile teams, including an awareness of Agile tools and how to use them;
advise colleagues on how and why Agile methods are used and provide a clear,
open and transparent framework for delivery; adapt, reflect and be
responsive to feedback; and see outside of the process.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. "Demonstrate experience" and "an awareness" describe
early exposure, and this level's overall accountability — basic checks and
edits under supervision — does not support a higher bar for a discipline as
broad as Agile working.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a basic Agile tool or ceremony the team uses.
- 1 — Beginner: Has worked in an Agile team, knows the common tools by name,
  and responds constructively when given feedback.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains to a colleague why the team uses a particular
  Agile method, not just what it is.
- 3 — Advanced: Notices when the team's process is getting in the way of the
  outcome and says so — already working towards junior content designer.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches a team in Agile practice and decides the right
  approach for a project — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an Agile ceremony they took part in and what
  it was for.
- Observe them in a stand-up or sprint planning session, if practical.
- Ask a referee, such as a placement supervisor or tutor, how they respond
  to feedback.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to show an awareness of prototyping and
explain why and when to use it, and understand how to work in an open and
collaborative environment, for example by pair working.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. "Show an awareness" and "explain why and when" are
understanding-level bullets, matching a trainee who has not yet built
prototypes independently.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain what a content prototype is or why a team might
  make one.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains what prototyping is and gives a plausible reason to
  use it, and has taken part in pair working.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has helped build a simple prototype — a paper sketch or
  similar — as part of a team.
- 3 — Advanced: Chooses between prototyping techniques for a given problem
  and explains the choice — already working towards junior content designer.
- 4 — Expert: Builds a range of prototypes independently and shows their
  value to a team — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain, in their own words, why a team might
  prototype a piece of content before finalising it.
- Ask about a time they worked in a pair or small group on a design problem.
- Review any prototype or sketch they contributed to, if one exists.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to understand who their stakeholders are,
what evidence is relevant to them, and the importance of managing
relationships with them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. "Understand" describes conceptual grasp rather than
active relationship management, which the framework does not expect
independently until later levels.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name who the stakeholders are for a piece of content
  work they have done.
- 1 — Beginner: Names the stakeholders for a piece of work and explains, in
  general terms, why the relationship with them matters.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies what evidence a specific stakeholder would
  find relevant, with some support.
- 3 — Advanced: Communicates with a stakeholder directly and clearly on a
  straightforward matter — already working towards junior content designer.
- 4 — Expert: Manages complex, long-term stakeholder relationships
  independently — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a piece of work and name who the
  stakeholders were and why.
- Ask what kind of evidence they think would matter to a named stakeholder
  in a hypothetical scenario.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate understands who needs to be kept
  informed on a piece of work.

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to show an awareness of the strategic
context of their work and understand why it is important, and to support
strategic planning in an administrative capacity.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. "Show an awareness" and "support... in an
administrative capacity" describe a supporting, learning role rather than
contributing to strategy itself.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why the content work they do connects to any wider
  goal.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain, broadly, why their work matters to the team's
  wider goals, and has helped with administrative tasks around strategic
  planning, such as note-taking or collating input.
- 2 — Intermediate: Connects a specific piece of their own work to a stated
  team goal without being told to.
- 3 — Advanced: Contributes an idea to a strategic planning discussion that
  gets taken up — already working towards junior content designer.
- 4 — Expert: Leads strategic planning for content — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain how a piece of work they have done connects
  to a wider team or service goal.
- Ask about a time they supported a planning process — taking notes,
  collating feedback, preparing materials.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate understands the "why" behind their
  tasks, not just the "what".

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to show an awareness or understanding of
user experience analysis and its principles, and to see the purpose of user
stories and the focus on user needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. "Show an awareness" and "see the purpose" are
understanding-level bullets, consistent with a trainee who has not yet
independently identified user needs from evidence.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain what a user story is or why it matters to
  content.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains, in general terms, what user experience analysis is
  for and can describe the purpose of a user story.
- 2 — Intermediate: Connects a specific user story to the content decision
  it should influence, with some support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies a user need from a piece of
  evidence, such as feedback or a support ticket — already working towards
  junior content designer.
- 4 — Expert: Gathers and synthesises user evidence to shape content
  decisions independently — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to read a short user story and explain what content
  decision it should influence.
- Ask them to explain, in their own words, what "user focus" means in
  content design.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate shows curiosity about who is using the
  content they work on.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to demonstrate basic but decent editorial
standards, show an awareness of style standards, and work well under
supervision.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. This is the level's own most direct accountability —
"perform basic standard checks and copy edits" — so this is the one skill
where a genuine trainee should already be doing real, if supervised, work,
but "basic but decent" still describes Beginner rather than independent
practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Misses obvious spelling, grammar or plain-language problems in a
  short piece of copy.
- 1 — Beginner: Performs a basic copy edit competently, and applies a named
  style rule when it is pointed out; works well when supervised.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies the organisation's style standards consistently
  without being reminded, and catches issues beyond spelling and grammar,
  such as unclear plain language.
- 3 — Advanced: Writes original short-form content that meets style
  standards with only light review — already working towards junior content
  designer.
- 4 — Expert: Designs content to meet complex user needs independently,
  publishes it, and reviews others' work — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: give a short piece of copy with deliberate errors and ask the
  candidate to edit it.
- Ask them to explain a style rule from the organisation's style guide, if
  they have seen one, or a general plain-language principle.
- Ask a referee how the candidate responds to editorial feedback and whether
  they apply it consistently afterwards.

here), consider how coachable the shortfall looks, and record your reasoning
in the evidence log rather than relying on the number alone.
