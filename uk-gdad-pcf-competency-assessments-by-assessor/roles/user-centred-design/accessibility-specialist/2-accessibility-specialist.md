# Accessibility Specialist - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a
reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities the UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) Profession Capability
Framework expects of an **accessibility specialist**. Use it for a hiring
decision, a promotion board from junior accessibility specialist, or a
general capability check against the framework.

The framework describes this level as someone who can support teams in
creating accessible services: coaching junior colleagues, supporting
training materials, performing accessibility audits, and — unlike the level
below — taking on assistive technology testing with real confidence.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole
of one. Read `## Advisory` before you use it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what this
level expects and how it differs from junior accessibility specialist below
it and senior accessibility specialist above it. Then gather whatever
evidence you can: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or
task, direct observation, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace back to
something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
and for a hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score
independently before comparing notes.

**Stay fair.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact between candidates.
Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This rates **how well** the person does the thing, on the evidence available,
not how often they do it.

**Worked example**, using Technical understanding (accessibility), a skill
this level names:

- A candidate who can only run basic automated checks and still needs help
  interpreting an edge case sits at **2 — Intermediate**, the level below
  this assessment's baseline.
- A candidate who performs detailed audits of websites, services and
  documents unsupported, documents findings clearly enough for a developer
  to act on, and is confident using at least one assistive technology, sits
  at **3 — Advanced** — the baseline this assessment sets for this skill.
- A candidate who additionally trains others in the department and is
  sought out to resolve edge cases nobody else can settle is already working
  towards senior accessibility specialist, at **4 — Expert**.

## About this role level

**The role: accessibility specialist.** An accessibility specialist provides
support, advice and guidance to other roles in the GDAD profession about how
to create accessible digital services, using knowledge of accessibility
guidelines, technical expertise, and the voice of disabled users, so that
product teams make services everyone can use.

**This level: accessibility specialist.** The framework describes this level
as someone who can support teams in creating accessible services. At this
level, the framework expects the person to:

- coach and mentor more junior colleagues
- support in providing training materials and learning content
- perform accessibility audits
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government accessibility community

**Where this level sits.** Accessibility specialist has four levels:

1. Junior accessibility specialist
2. **Accessibility specialist** — assessed here
3. Senior accessibility specialist
4. Head of accessibility

Two skills appear for the first time at this level and nowhere at junior
accessibility specialist: Governance and assurance (accessibility) and
Leadership and guidance. Both assume the person is now trusted to work
without close supervision, which is the central change from the level below.

**The seven skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating information
2. Consultancy
3. Governance and assurance (accessibility)
4. Leadership and guidance
5. Technical understanding (accessibility)
6. Testing
7. User focus

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Communicating information

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to listen to the needs of design and
business stakeholders and interpret information, take part in discussions
within a multidisciplinary team, act as an advocate for the team externally
while managing differing perspectives, and shape and share communications
relevant to the audience.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Advocating for the team externally and shaping
communications for an audience is real, unsupervised work, but the framework
does not yet ask for the mediation between senior stakeholders that appears
at senior accessibility specialist.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot restate a stakeholder's need accurately and does not
  represent the team's position when asked.
- 1 — Beginner: Restates stakeholder needs accurately with support; needs
  coaching to represent the team externally.
- 2 — Intermediate: Represents the team to external stakeholders and manages
  differing perspectives without support, and tailors written or spoken
  communication to who is receiving it.
- 3 — Advanced: Proactively resolves a disagreement between stakeholders
  before it escalates, and is asked by colleagues to review how they should
  frame a difficult message.
- 4 — Expert: Sets how the wider team communicates externally, and mediates
  at senior level — above this level's expectation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they represented the team's position
  to an external stakeholder who disagreed with a finding.
- Review a piece of written communication — an audit summary or a briefing —
  they produced for a non-technical audience.
- Structured interview: "Tell me about two stakeholders who wanted different
  things. How did you manage that?"

### Skill: Consultancy

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to provide advice, guidance and
recommendations based on their specialist knowledge and experience, propose
methodologies to follow and approaches to implementation, and frame problems
so they can easily be understood and troubleshoot where required.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework has dropped junior accessibility
specialist's "under guidance" and "referring to previous work by others" —
this level originates advice from its own specialist knowledge, but leading
the definition of organisation-wide guidance is reserved for senior
accessibility specialist.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot give advice on an accessibility problem without simply
  repeating something someone else told them.
- 1 — Beginner: Gives advice on familiar problems but still checks it against
  documented guidance before offering it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Gives sound advice drawn from their own knowledge and
  experience on problems without an obvious precedent, and proposes a
  workable approach to implementation.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads a piece of work defining how the organisation should
  approach an accessibility problem, informing wider practice — already
  working towards the level above.
- 4 — Expert: A recognised authority whose recommendations shape strategy
  across departments — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give the candidate an accessibility problem with no documented precedent
  and ask them to propose an approach.
- Ask them to describe advice they gave that a team acted on, and what would
  have happened if they had been wrong.
- Review a piece of guidance or a recommendation document they authored.

### Skill: Governance and assurance (accessibility)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to analyse and provide feedback on
governance and project documents, and at governance boards, according to a
predefined framework, assessing them against accessibility standards.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This skill first appears at this level, and the
framework's own wording — "according to a predefined framework" — describes
applying a structure someone else has set, which fits Intermediate rather
than the independent judgement Advanced would require.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot use a governance checklist to assess a document against
  accessibility standards, even with the checklist in front of them.
- 1 — Beginner: Applies a predefined governance framework to a document with
  support, and needs help interpreting an ambiguous case.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies the predefined framework to governance and
  project documents unsupported, and gives clear, standards-referenced
  feedback at a governance board.
- 3 — Advanced: Spots a gap in the predefined framework itself and raises it
  — already working towards challenging governance arrangements, which is
  the senior accessibility specialist expectation.
- 4 — Expert: Designs governance and assurance frameworks for others to
  apply — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give the candidate a sample project document and the organisation's
  governance checklist, and ask them to assess it.
- Ask them to describe feedback they gave at a governance board and how it
  was received.
- Review written feedback they have given on a real governance or project
  document, with identifying details removed if needed.

### Skill: Leadership and guidance

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to contribute to best practice guidelines,
understand the sustainability and consequences of their decisions and make
decisions characterised by managed levels of risk and complexity, and resolve
technical disputes between wider peers and indirect stakeholders, taking into
account all views and opinions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is a new skill at this level, and "managed
levels of risk and complexity" is a deliberately more contained scope than
senior accessibility specialist's "medium levels of risk and complexity" or
head of accessibility's "high levels".

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Avoids or escalates every dispute between colleagues rather than
  attempting to resolve it.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes an example or a suggestion to best practice
  guidelines when asked, but needs support to make a decision involving risk.
- 2 — Intermediate: Makes decisions involving managed risk and complexity
  unsupported, and resolves a technical dispute between peers by hearing all
  sides fairly.
- 3 — Advanced: Recommends decisions as risk and complexity increase beyond
  what they would decide alone — already working towards senior accessibility
  specialist.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches the organisation on team dynamics and resolves disputes
  at the highest level — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a decision they made that carried real risk,
  and how they judged the level of risk was acceptable.
- Ask them to describe a technical dispute they resolved between two
  colleagues, and how they made sure both were heard.
- Ask a referee how the candidate is regarded when a disagreement needs
  settling.

### Skill: Technical understanding (accessibility)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to demonstrate knowledge and communicate
the requirements of accessibility standards and legislation, advocate
appropriate technical solutions to a range of accessibility issues, perform
detailed audits of websites, services and documents and document findings
clearly for others to work from, be an advocate for the people affected by
accessibility barriers across the department, provide introductory awareness
and training about accessibility, and demonstrate confidence using one or
more assistive technologies with knowledge of others.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is the central technical skill of the role, and
the framework's wording moves clearly beyond junior accessibility
specialist's basic checks to detailed, unsupported audits and real
confidence with assistive technology — this level is trusted to be
independently responsible for the testing the level below was explicitly not
responsible for.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot perform an audit without a checklist someone else wrote,
  and cannot explain an accessibility standard's requirement in their own
  words.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a standard's requirement with help, and performs
  only basic automated checks unsupported.
- 2 — Intermediate: Performs basic manual and automated checks confidently
  but still needs support with a detailed audit or an unfamiliar assistive
  technology.
- 3 — Advanced: Performs a detailed audit of a website, service or document
  unsupported, documents findings clearly enough for a developer to fix
  without further explanation, and uses at least one assistive technology
  with genuine confidence.
- 4 — Expert: Advocates removing barriers across every interaction, and is
  the person others bring edge cases to — the senior accessibility specialist
  or head of accessibility expectation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: give a real or representative service and ask for a detailed
  audit with documented findings.
- Observe the candidate using an assistive technology they claim confidence
  with, on an unfamiliar page.
- Ask them to deliver a short piece of introductory training to a mock
  audience of non-specialists.
- Review a training resource or awareness material they have produced.

### Skill: Testing

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to review requirements and specifications
and define test conditions, identify issues and risks associated with work,
and analyse and report test activities and results.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Defining test conditions from requirements and
analysing results is a step up from junior accessibility specialist's
supervised script execution, but managing testing activities across a whole
programme of work is reserved for senior accessibility specialist.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot turn a requirement into a test condition, and only
  executes scripts someone else has written.
- 1 — Beginner: Defines a simple test condition from a clear requirement with
  support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Reviews requirements and specifications and defines test
  conditions unsupported, identifies risks in the work, and reports results
  clearly.
- 3 — Advanced: Manages testing activities across more than one piece of
  work at once — already working towards senior accessibility specialist.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the testing approach for a whole programme and manages
  preventative action when risk becomes unacceptable — well above this
  level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give the candidate a written requirement and ask them to define test
  conditions for it.
- Ask them to describe a risk they identified during testing and what they
  did about it.
- Review a test report they have written.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to identify and engage with users or
stakeholders to collate evidence of user needs, understand and define
research that fits those needs, and use quantitative and qualitative data
about users to turn user focus into outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This level moves from junior accessibility
specialist's awareness of user experience principles to actively engaging
users and turning evidence into outcomes, without yet the champion-level
research advocacy expected at senior accessibility specialist.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how a specific user need shaped a decision they
  made.
- 1 — Beginner: Collates user needs evidence that someone else has already
  gathered, but does not yet engage users directly.
- 2 — Intermediate: Engages users or stakeholders directly to collate
  evidence of user needs, and uses quantitative or qualitative data to shape
  a recommendation.
- 3 — Advanced: Champions user research to make sure all users, including
  those with less common access needs, are represented — already working
  towards senior accessibility specialist.
- 4 — Expert: Applies strategic thinking to how the whole organisation meets
  user needs — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a finding that came directly from engaging a
  disabled user or stakeholder, and what changed because of it.
- Ask how they decide which research method fits a given question about user
  needs.
- Review a piece of analysis where they turned qualitative or quantitative
  user data into a recommendation.
