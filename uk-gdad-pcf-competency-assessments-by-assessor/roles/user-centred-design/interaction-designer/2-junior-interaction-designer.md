# Junior Interaction Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities required for the User-centred design role of Interaction designer,
at the Junior interaction designer role level. It measures demonstrated skill
against the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of this
role level, for a hiring decision, a promotion board, onboarding, or a
capability check.

A junior interaction designer is typically a graduate in a relevant subject,
or someone with some relevant work experience, or both, who works
independently once given direction and can identify user issues without
being told exactly what to look for. This assessment looks for demonstrated,
if still developing, practice — a step beyond the associate interaction
designer's supervised understanding, and short of the fully independent,
evidence-driven work expected of an interaction designer.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of
it. Read `## Advisory` before using it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, then gather whatever
evidence exists: a portfolio of coursework or early professional work, a
structured interview, a design task (for example, asking the candidate to
sketch and explain an approach to a simple interaction problem), direct
observation, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. Where you have no
evidence for a skill, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way. For a
hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Every rating and every piece of evidence you gather must be
about demonstrated, job-related behaviour — see Fair and lawful use in the
project specification. Never let a rating be coloured by anything other than
what the person actually did, said, or produced.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same five-point
scale. This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well the person does the
thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel
about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Design communication.** The framework expects a junior
interaction designer to "clearly explain problems and design ideas to
others" and "clearly explain design decisions to others using appropriate
tools and methods." A candidate who can only explain a decision vaguely, or
needs a colleague to fill the gaps, is showing at most **1 — Beginner**. A
candidate who clearly explains a design problem and the reasoning behind it,
choosing an appropriate way to show it such as an annotated wireframe, is
showing **2 — Intermediate** — the baseline this level expects. A candidate
who does this for complex, multi-part problems with stakeholders outside
design is showing evidence closer to **3 — Advanced**, above this level's own
baseline.

## About this role level

The User-centred design profession's Interaction designer role works out the
best way to let users interact with services, in terms of both overall flow
and the level of individual design elements.

The role has six levels, in order: Associate interaction designer, Junior
interaction designer, Interaction designer, Senior interaction designer, Lead
interaction designer, and Head of interaction design. This assessment is for
the second of them.

A **junior interaction designer** is a graduate with a degree in a relevant
subject or an individual with some relevant work experience, or both. At this
role level, they will explain design decisions, work collaboratively, hold
responsibility as part of a service, work independently after being given
direction by more senior designers, and independently identify user issues
and important needs.

The framework names seven skills for this role level: Design communication,
Designing for everyone, Designing strategically, Designing together,
Evidence-based design, Iterative design, and Leading design. For three of
these — Designing strategically, Designing together, and Leading design —
the framework repeats the wording used for the associate interaction
designer level below unchanged; for the other four, the wording matches the
interaction designer level above. This assessment reflects that pattern
directly rather than flattening every skill to the same baseline.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior interaction designer is expected to clearly explain problems and
design ideas to others, and clearly explain design decisions to others using
appropriate tools and methods.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's wording for this skill matches
the interaction designer level above rather than the associate level below,
so this is treated as genuine, demonstrated communication practice rather
than the associate's "explain the importance of" framing.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain a design decision, even when asked directly what
  problem it solves.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a design decision when prompted, but the
  explanation is vague or needs a colleague to fill gaps.
- 2 — Intermediate: Clearly explains a design problem and the reasoning
  behind a decision, choosing an appropriate way to show it, such as an
  annotated wireframe or a short walkthrough.
- 3 — Advanced: Explains complex design problems to stakeholders outside
  design and builds consensus around a design approach.
- 4 — Expert: Gains support for design strategy from senior leaders and
  coaches others in communicating design effectively.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a wireframe or interaction flow they
  produced and explain the decisions behind it.
- Review a design rationale or annotated design they have produced.
- Ask about a time a design decision was questioned, and how they explained
  or defended it.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior interaction designer is expected to work with others to design and
deliver inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or
services that meet the needs of all users, and to ensure a design meets
appropriate standards, for example accessibility regulations, with support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Work with others to design and deliver" is
applied, collaborative practice, matching the interaction designer level
above; "with support" on the standards bullet keeps full independent
assurance out of reach.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what makes an interaction accessible, for example
  keyboard navigation or a clear focus state.
- 1 — Beginner: With guidance, checks a design against a basic accessibility
  checklist.
- 2 — Intermediate: Works with others to design an inclusive, accessible
  interaction, and, with support, checks it against a named standard.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises other designers on inclusive design and ensures a
  design meets accessibility regulations without prompting.
- 4 — Expert: Measurably improves inclusive design practice across multiple
  teams.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review an interaction flow or prototype the candidate produced and ask how
  they made it work for users with different access needs.
- Ask about a specific accessibility standard, for example keyboard
  operability, and how they check for it.
- Ask about a time they changed a design because it excluded some users.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior interaction designer is expected to explain the importance of
aligning to the strategy of an organisation, describe how their design work
aligns to the wider goals and vision of their team, and explain the value of
using patterns and components in design.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework repeats this skill's wording unchanged
from the associate interaction designer level below, so the baseline stays
at Beginner even though the accountability bullets at this level describe
more independent working overall.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why a designer's work should connect to their team's
  goals.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains why aligning to organisational strategy matters,
  and the value of reusing patterns and components.
- 2 — Intermediate: With some support, describes how a specific piece of
  their own work aligns to a team goal.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently aligns their own work to team and
  organisational goals.
- 4 — Expert: Helps teams understand how design meets organisational goals
  and creates new patterns.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain how a piece of work they did connects to what
  their team or service is trying to achieve.
- Ask why reusing a design pattern or component might be better than
  designing something from scratch each time.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior interaction designer is expected to explain different techniques
for bringing together perspectives from multiple people to inform a design,
and explain the importance of constructive feedback in the design process.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework repeats this skill's wording unchanged
from the associate interaction designer level below, so the baseline stays
at Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why a design session might involve more than one
  person's perspective.
- 1 — Beginner: Names a technique for bringing together different
  perspectives, such as a workshop or a crit, and explains why constructive
  feedback matters.
- 2 — Intermediate: Plans and runs a simple design session, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently plans and runs design sessions and adapts
  them when they are not working.
- 4 — Expert: Runs sessions that include senior leaders and coaches others
  in collaborative design practice.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a design session or crit they took part in,
  and what made the feedback useful or not.
- Ask them to name a technique for bringing together different people's
  views on a design.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior interaction designer is expected to analyse, synthesise and clearly
explain evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics
data, and to work with researchers or analysts to use evidence to inform,
develop and test a design idea.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's wording for this skill matches
the interaction designer level above rather than the associate level below,
so this is treated as genuine, applied evidence analysis rather than the
associate's understanding of where evidence comes from.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what evidence, such as analytics or user research,
  might tell them about a design.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains what a piece of evidence shows, with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Analyses and explains evidence relevant to a design, and
  works with a researcher or analyst to test a design idea.
- 3 — Advanced: Analyses and explains complex evidence and helps a team
  frame design hypotheses.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds hypothesis-driven design practice and guides the
  organisation on using evidence.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to talk through a piece of user research or analytics
  data that shaped an interaction design decision.
- Ask how they worked with a user researcher or analyst on a project.
- Review a design that changed as a result of evidence, and ask what the
  evidence showed.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior interaction designer is expected to apply iterative design
principles and agile methodologies to their work, iterate and improve a
design based on successive rounds of research, prototype their ideas at
different fidelities to explore and test designs, use a range of design
tools and techniques, and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's wording for this skill matches
the interaction designer level above rather than the associate level below —
this is applying iteration in practice, not describing what it is.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what changed between two versions of a design
  and why.
- 1 — Beginner: Makes a small change to a design following feedback, with
  guidance.
- 2 — Intermediate: Iterates a design across successive rounds of research,
  prototypes at more than one fidelity, and uses and adjusts existing
  patterns and components.
- 3 — Advanced: Iterates complex designs, prototypes independently at the
  right fidelity, and adapts quickly when requirements change.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds iterative practice across teams and leads prototyping
  of complex ideas at any fidelity.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to show two or three versions of an interaction design
  and explain what changed and why, referencing research or feedback.
- Ask about the range of tools they use for different fidelities, for
  example a paper sketch versus a clickable prototype.
- Review a pattern or component they reused or adapted from a library.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior interaction designer is expected to explain the role of
user-centred design in an organisation, describe typical leadership
responsibilities in design, and identify leaders in their organisation that
designers need to work with.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework repeats this skill's wording unchanged
from the associate interaction designer level below, so the baseline stays
at Beginner — leadership is still awareness at this stage, even though the
role's other accountabilities describe more independent working.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why user-centred design matters to an organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains the role of user-centred design in an
  organisation, and describes typical leadership responsibilities in a
  design team.
- 2 — Intermediate: With support, leads or coordinates a small piece of
  design work.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads and coordinates design work in a team independently
  and mentors other designers.
- 4 — Expert: Sets a vision for and leads design across multiple teams or a
  whole organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain, in their own words, why user-centred design
  matters to a government service.
- Ask them to name a role, such as a service manager or a design lead, that
  a designer might need to work with, and why.
