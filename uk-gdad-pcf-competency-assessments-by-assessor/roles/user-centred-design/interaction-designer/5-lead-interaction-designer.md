# Lead Interaction Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities required for the User-centred design role of Interaction designer,
at the Lead interaction designer role level. It measures demonstrated skill
against the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of
this role level, for a hiring decision, a promotion board, onboarding, or a
capability check.

A lead interaction designer is an expert practitioner who influences and
mentors others, sets direction across teams, and gains support from senior
leaders. This assessment looks for organisation-level influence and
mentoring, not the individual, minimally-supported delivery expected of a
senior interaction designer.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of
it. Read `## Advisory` before using it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, then gather whatever
evidence exists: a portfolio and track record across multiple services, a
structured interview, references from designers this person has mentored or
teams they have set direction for, or direct observation of them leading a
design session with senior stakeholders.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing, ideally over a track
record rather than a single task. Where you have no evidence for a skill,
record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way. For a
hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Every rating and every piece of evidence you gather must be
about demonstrated, job-related behaviour — see Fair and lawful use in the
project specification. Never let a rating be coloured by anything other than
what the person actually did, said, or produced.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same five-point
scale. This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well the person does the
thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel
about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Evidence-based design.** The framework expects a lead
interaction designer to "embed a practice of hypothesis-driven design in a
team," "guide the organisation in how to use evidence to improve services,"
and "ensure user insights are shared with and used by the wider organisation
and across government." A candidate who can analyse and explain complex
evidence for their own work, but has not embedded that practice in a team or
guided an organisation, is showing at most **3 — Advanced**. A candidate who
has built a lasting hypothesis-driven design practice in a team and can show
how the organisation uses evidence differently as a result is showing
**4 — Expert**, the baseline this level expects.

## About this role level

The User-centred design profession's Interaction designer role works out the
best way to let users interact with services, in terms of both overall flow
and the level of individual design elements.

The role has six levels, in order: Associate interaction designer, Junior
interaction designer, Interaction designer, Senior interaction designer, Lead
interaction designer, and Head of interaction design. This assessment is for
the fifth of them.

A **lead interaction designer** is an expert practitioner who influences and
mentors others. At this role level, they will work with service managers and
programme directors to develop design concepts, set direction and assure the
quality of design delivery across teams, and lead multiple or highly complex
services.

The framework names seven skills for this role level: Design communication,
Designing for everyone, Designing strategically, Designing together,
Evidence-based design, Iterative design, and Leading design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead interaction designer is expected to gain support for design
decisions and design strategy from senior leaders, communicate design
strategy across organisational boundaries, coach others in how to
effectively communicate design, and develop or introduce more effective
methods of communicating design and working in the open.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Gaining support from senior leaders, communicating
across organisational boundaries, and coaching others is full mastery
exercised for the benefit of others, not just independent delivery of your
own communication.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain a design decision beyond their immediate team.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a design decision clearly within their own team,
  with support for wider audiences.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains complex design problems to stakeholders
  outside design.
- 3 — Advanced: Builds consensus on a design approach and documents risks
  and unresolved issues without needing support.
- 4 — Expert: Gains support for design strategy from senior leaders,
  communicates it across organisational boundaries, and coaches others in
  effective design communication.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the candidate gained support for a design strategy from a
  senior leader, and how they made the case.
- Ask about a design communication method or approach they introduced that
  others adopted.
- Ask them to describe how they have coached a colleague to communicate
  design more effectively.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead interaction designer is expected to measurably improve the practice
of inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable design in multiple
teams or across their organisation, and help others understand and mitigate
the influence of privilege and power on the design and delivery of content
and services.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Measurably improve... across multiple teams" and
helping others understand privilege and power are organisation-level,
teaching-and-influencing outcomes, beyond a senior designer's advice to a
single team.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how privilege or power might influence a design
  decision.
- 1 — Beginner: Applies accessible design practice to their own work.
- 2 — Intermediate: Advises a single team on inclusive or accessible
  design.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises multiple teams on inclusive, accessible and
  sustainable design.
- 4 — Expert: Measurably improves inclusive, accessible and sustainable
  design practice across multiple teams or the organisation, and helps
  others understand and mitigate the influence of privilege and power.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a concrete example of an improvement in inclusive or accessible
  design practice across more than one team, and how it was measured.
- Ask about a time the candidate helped a colleague or a team recognise how
  privilege or power was shaping a design decision.
- Review any standard, guide or training the candidate created that other
  teams now use.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead interaction designer is expected to help a team understand how
user-centred design helps it meet its goals, help teams align their work to
the goals and vision of their organisation, use risks, opportunities and
constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design, and identify
and create new design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework repeats this skill's wording
unchanged from the senior interaction designer level below, so the baseline
stays at Advanced — the lead role's added strategic weight shows up in the
other skills in this matrix rather than in a re-worded version of this one.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain how design connects to organisational goals.
- 1 — Beginner: Aligns their own work to team goals, with guidance.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently aligns their own work to team and
  organisational goals.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps a team understand how design meets its goals, aligns
  team work to organisational vision, and creates new design patterns and
  components informed by risk and opportunity.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds design as part of organisational strategy development
  and enables the use of patterns and components across the organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the candidate helped a team see how a design choice
  connected to a wider organisational goal.
- Review a new pattern or component they created and the reasoning behind
  it.
- Ask how they have used a technology, policy or system constraint to shape
  a design rather than being blocked by it.

- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead interaction designer is expected to advise others how to
effectively plan and run design sessions with a team, users or
stakeholders, adapt a design session to ensure a useful outcome, effectively
involve the right people throughout the design process, and work across
team or profession boundaries, for example with policy teams.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. As with Designing strategically, the framework
repeats this skill's wording unchanged from the senior interaction designer
level below, so the baseline stays at Advanced rather than rising further.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how to plan a design session.
- 1 — Beginner: Takes part in and contributes to a design session someone
  else runs.
- 2 — Intermediate: Plans and runs their own design sessions competently.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises other designers on planning and running sessions,
  adapts a session that is not delivering a useful outcome, and works
  across team or profession boundaries such as with policy teams.
- 4 — Expert: Plans and runs sessions that include senior leaders and
  coaches others in involving stakeholders of any level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the candidate advised a colleague on how to run a better
  design session.
- Ask them to describe a session that was not working and how they adapted
  it in the moment.
- Ask about a project where they worked with a team outside design, such as
  policy, and how they made that collaboration work.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead interaction designer is expected to embed a practice of
hypothesis-driven design in a team, guide the organisation in how to use
evidence to improve services, analyse, synthesise and use evidence to
improve the way the organisation works, and ensure user insights are shared
with and used by the wider organisation and across government.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Embedding a lasting practice in a team and guiding a
whole organisation is full mastery exercised at scale, well beyond a senior
designer's own handling of complex evidence.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain what a piece of research or data evidence shows.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains simple evidence with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently analyses and explains evidence relevant to
  a design.
- 3 — Advanced: Analyses and explains complex evidence and helps a team
  frame and use design hypotheses effectively.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds a hypothesis-driven design practice in a team, guides
  the organisation on using evidence to improve services, and ensures
  insights are shared across the organisation and government.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a practice or process the candidate introduced that made
  hypothesis-driven design part of how a team routinely works.
- Ask for an example of guidance they gave that changed how the wider
  organisation uses evidence.
- Ask how they have shared user insight beyond their own team, including
  with other government organisations.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead interaction designer is expected to embed the practice of iterative
design and agile working in teams or the wider organisation, lead on
prototyping complex ideas at any fidelity, help teams adapt quickly to
changes in requirements, priorities or user needs, and iterate delivery
processes or team structures to help teams work effectively.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Embedding practice across teams or the organisation,
and iterating delivery processes and team structures themselves, is
system-level mastery, beyond a senior designer's own complex, individual
iteration.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how a design changed across iterations.
- 1 — Beginner: Iterates a simple design with guidance on what to change.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently iterates straightforward designs across
  rounds of research.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps other designers apply iterative principles and
  iterates complex designs.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds iterative design and agile practice across teams or
  the organisation, leads prototyping of complex ideas at any fidelity, and
  iterates delivery processes or team structures.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a change the candidate made to a delivery process or team
  structure that helped a team iterate more effectively.
- Ask them to describe leading the prototyping of a complex idea at a
  fidelity they judged was right for the question.
- Ask about a time requirements changed suddenly across more than one team,
  and how they helped those teams adapt.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead interaction designer is expected to lead and coordinate design work
in a team, advocate for user-centred design with leaders outside of digital
and data, create an inclusive, productive environment for designers to work
in, mentor and coach other designers, and iterate and improve design
processes within their team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Leading a team without the "with support" qualifier
that capped the level below, advocating with leaders outside digital and
data, and mentoring other designers is independent leadership — a clear
step up from the senior designer's supported coordination.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe why user-centred design matters to their
  organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Coordinates a piece of design work with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads a piece of design work in a team with some
  support, and communicates the value of user-centred design.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads and coordinates design work in a team independently,
  advocates for user-centred design with leaders outside digital and data,
  creates an inclusive environment for designers, and mentors other
  designers.
- 4 — Expert: Sets a vision for and leads design across multiple teams or a
  whole organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the candidate advocated for user-centred design to a
  leader outside digital and data, and what changed as a result.
- Ask for an example of how they created a more inclusive or productive
  environment for their design team.
- Ask about a designer they have mentored, and what changed for that
  person.
