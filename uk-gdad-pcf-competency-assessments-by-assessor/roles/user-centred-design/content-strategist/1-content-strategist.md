# Content Strategist - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a
reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities the UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) Profession Capability
Framework expects of a **content strategist**. Use it for a hiring decision, a
promotion board, or a general capability review against the framework.

The framework describes this level as an expert practitioner in content
strategy and taxonomy design: someone who develops governance, processes and
workflows from strategy and policy intent, manages relationships and
interdependencies across content roles, shapes and structures the architecture
of content, and works with lead content designers to organise and manage teams
to deliver outcomes. The matrix below is built to that description — a
genuinely senior bar, not a stepping-stone one.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of
one. Read `## Advisory` before you use it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what this
level actually expects. Evidence at this level should come from real
delivery — a body of work directing content strategy or taxonomy for a
service or a portfolio, not a single task — because the framework's own
accountabilities are about directing, shaping and delivering through others.
Gather whatever evidence exists: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a
work sample or task, direct observation, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating you give must trace back
to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way.
For a hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score
independently before comparing notes — this matters even more at a senior
level, where the pool of directly comparable evidence for any one candidate
is often thin.

**Stay fair.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact between candidates.
Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using Strategic thinking, a skill this level names:

- A candidate who cannot connect a piece of content work to any wider
  organisational strategy sits at **0 — None**.
- A candidate who contributes ideas to a strategic discussion that get taken
  up, but has not led a strategy's design themselves, sits at **2 —
  Intermediate**.
- A candidate who leads the design and implementation of a content strategy
  end to end, and directs the evaluation of strategies and policies against
  business requirements, sits at **4 — Expert** — the baseline this
  assessment sets for this skill.

## About this role level

**The role: content strategist.** A content strategist takes an overview of
content and how it is produced. In this role, the framework expects someone
to design governance, workflows and taxonomies in a user-centred context, and
to work with senior stakeholders, user researchers, and content, service and
interaction designers.

**This level: content strategist.** The framework describes this level as an
expert practitioner in content strategy and taxonomy design. At this level,
the framework's accountabilities are: develop governance, processes and
workflows based on delivering strategy and policy intent; manage
relationships and interdependencies with other content roles; shape and
structure the architecture of content based on user-centred design
principles; and work with lead content designers to organise and manage
teams to deliver outcomes.

**Where this level sits.** This repository's role summaries carry one level
for content strategist — the level assessed here. The framework may describe
other roles working alongside it in the same profession, but this document
describes only what content strategist's own summary states, and does not
invent a ladder of further levels for this specific role.

**The six skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working
2. Prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management
4. Strategic thinking
5. User focus
6. User-centred content design

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to identify and compare the best processes
or delivery methods to use, including measuring and evaluating outcomes;
help the team decide the best approach; and help teams manage and visualise
outcomes, prioritise work, and adhere to agreed minimum viable product (MVP),
priorities and scope.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The bullets describe advising and helping teams
decide and prioritise their own delivery approach — genuine, independent
practice at team level — but they stop short of the level's most senior
bullets, which describe leading or directing across multiple teams, so this
skill sits a point below the level's top baseline.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how a delivery method affects content strategy
  outcomes.
- 1 — Beginner: Names common delivery approaches but needs support comparing
  them for a specific piece of work.
- 2 — Intermediate: Compares delivery approaches for a straightforward piece
  of work and recommends one, with review.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently compares and selects delivery approaches
  across a programme of content work, measures outcomes, and helps a team
  stick to an agreed MVP and scope.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the delivery approach standard across multiple teams and
  coaches other leads in how to prioritise and evaluate outcomes.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they helped a team choose between two
  delivery approaches for a content programme, and how they measured the
  outcome.
- Work sample: review a prioritisation or scoping decision document they
  produced or contributed to.
- Ask a referee how the candidate handled scope creep or MVP discipline on a
  live piece of work.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to understand when to use a specific
prototyping technique or method, and to show the value of prototyping to
their team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The bullets describe judgement about technique
choice and persuading a team of prototyping's value — independent practice —
but the skill's scope is narrower than the level's headline strategic
accountabilities, so it does not reach Expert on the evidence the framework
gives.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why a team would prototype a piece of content
  strategy or taxonomy work before committing to it.
- 1 — Beginner: Names a prototyping technique but needs help judging when to
  use it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses a reasonable prototyping technique for a
  straightforward case, with review.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently judges which prototyping technique fits a
  given strategy or taxonomy problem, and persuades a sceptical team of its
  value.
- 4 — Expert: Introduces prototyping practice across teams that had not used
  it before, and shows measurable value from it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a case where they chose a specific
  prototyping technique for a taxonomy or content architecture problem, and
  why.
- Ask about a time they had to convince a stakeholder or team that
  prototyping was worth the time it took.
- Review any prototype, or a description of one, they produced for a
  strategic piece of work.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None

- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to direct the stakeholder relationship
strategy for their teams; ensure stakeholders' objectives are set and support
teams to meet them; and influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to
resolve issues and enable progress.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Direct the stakeholder relationship strategy" and
"influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to resolve issues" describe
setting the approach for others and operating directly at senior level, which
is the framework's own definition of expert practice for this skill.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe who the senior stakeholders are for a piece of
  content strategy work, or what they need.
- 1 — Beginner: Identifies senior stakeholders and their objectives, with
  support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Manages a stakeholder relationship for a defined piece
  of work, escalating disagreements to someone more senior.
- 3 — Advanced: Sets stakeholders' objectives and supports a team to meet
  them, resolving straightforward disagreements without escalation.
- 4 — Expert: Directs the stakeholder relationship strategy across teams,
  and personally negotiates with senior stakeholders to resolve a genuine
  impasse and unblock progress.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they negotiated directly with a
  senior stakeholder to resolve a disagreement that was blocking progress.
- Ask how they set stakeholder objectives for a team, and how they supported
  the team to meet them.
- Ask a referee, ideally a stakeholder or a peer, how the candidate handles
  disagreement at senior level.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to lead the design and implementation of
strategy, directing the evaluation of strategies and policies to ensure
business requirements are being met.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This is the single bullet the framework gives for this
skill at this level, and it describes leading strategy design end to end and
directing evaluation — the clearest statement of expert-level ownership
anywhere in this level's summary.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot connect a piece of content work to any wider
  organisational strategy.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains how their own work connects to a stated strategy,
  without having shaped it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes ideas to a strategic discussion that get
  taken up.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads part of a strategy's design, or evaluates whether a
  strategy is meeting business requirements, with oversight.
- 4 — Expert: Leads the design and implementation of a content strategy end
  to end, and directs the evaluation of strategies and policies against
  business requirements.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a strategy they led the design of, from
  intent to implementation.
- Work sample: review a strategy document or evaluation they led or
  directed.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's strategic recommendations were
  adopted, and what difference they made.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to give direction on which tools or methods
to use; demonstrate experience in meeting the needs of users across a
variety of channels; bring insight and expertise in how user needs have
changed over time to ensure they're met by the business; and apply strategic
thinking to provide the best service for the end user.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The bullets describe directing others' tool and
method choices and bringing longitudinal insight into user need — independent
expert practice — but the wording frames the person as informing and
directing others' work on users, rather than owning strategy outright the way
Strategic thinking does, so it sits a point below the level's top baseline.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how user needs have changed for the service they
  work on.
- 1 — Beginner: Describes current user needs, but not how they have changed
  over time.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies a known user-focused method to a piece of work,
  with review.
- 3 — Advanced: Gives a team direction on which user research tool or
  method to use, and explains how user needs have shifted over time in a way
  that changes what the business does.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the user-focus approach across multiple teams and
  services, and is the person others in the organisation turn to for
  judgement on user need trends.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a case where they directed a team to use a
  particular tool or method to meet a specific user need, and why.
- Ask them to describe how user needs for their service have changed over
  time, and what the business changed as a result.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate applying strategic thinking
  to improve the end-to-end service for users.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to show a deep understanding of end-to-end
journeys and how content is affected within these journeys; identify where
journey fixes or content improvements need to be made; take responsibility
for assuring the quality of content from more junior colleagues, and
coaching and guiding them to improve; ensure that content is regularly
reviewed and evaluated, contributing to continuous improvements and
iteration; encourage a continuous improvement mindset in teams and more
junior content colleagues; and deliver through others, and direct and
critique their work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Take responsibility for assuring the quality of
content from more junior colleagues" and "deliver through others, and direct
and critique their work" describe accountability for other people's output —
the clearest marker of expert practice the framework offers anywhere in this
file, and it matches the level's own description as "an expert practitioner
in content strategy and taxonomy design".

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe an end-to-end user journey for a service they
  have worked on, or where content sits within it.
- 1 — Beginner: Describes an end-to-end journey and names a content problem
  within it, with support identifying the fix.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies a journey fix or content improvement
  independently for a straightforward case.
- 3 — Advanced: Runs a regular content review and evaluation process for a
  piece of work, and encourages a continuous improvement mindset in a small
  team.
- 4 — Expert: Takes responsibility for the quality of junior colleagues'
  content, coaches and guides them, and delivers through others by directing
  and critiquing their work across a whole journey.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through an end-to-end user journey they have
  worked on, and identify where they made a journey fix or content
  improvement.
- Ask about a time they coached or guided a more junior colleague on a piece
  of content, and what changed.
- Ask a referee, ideally someone the candidate has directed or critiqued,
  how the candidate gives feedback on content.

processes and workflows based on delivering strategy and policy intent" and
"work with lead content designers to organise and manage teams"), consider
whether the shortfall is coachable quickly after appointment, and record your
