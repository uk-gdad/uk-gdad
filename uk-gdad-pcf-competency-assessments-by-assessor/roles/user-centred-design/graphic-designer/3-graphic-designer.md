# Graphic Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities required for the User-centred design role of Graphic designer, at
the Graphic designer role level. It measures demonstrated skill against the UK
GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of this role level, for
a hiring decision, a promotion board, onboarding, or a capability check.

A graphic designer at this level is a confident and competent designer who
develops designs based on evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes,
is trusted to make good decisions, and knows when to ask for support. This
assessment looks for independent, evidence-grounded practice — not the
supervised first steps of a junior designer, and not yet the cross-team
influence expected of a senior designer.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of
it. Read `## Advisory` before using it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, then gather whatever
evidence exists: a portfolio of layout, iconography, type or colour work, a
structured interview, a design task (for example, asking the candidate to
explain the decisions behind a real piece of work), direct observation in a
design session or crit, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. Where you have no
evidence for a skill, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way. For a
hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Every rating and every piece of evidence you gather must be
about demonstrated, job-related behaviour — see Fair and lawful use in the
project specification. Never let a rating be coloured by anything other than
what the person actually did, said, or produced.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same five-point
scale. This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well the person does the
thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel
about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Design communication.** The framework expects a graphic
designer to "clearly explain problems and design ideas to others" and "clearly
explain design decisions to others using appropriate tools and methods." A
candidate who cannot say what problem a piece of work solves, even when asked
directly, is showing **0 — None**. A candidate who can explain a decision only
vaguely, or needs help filling the gaps, is showing **1 — Beginner**. A
candidate who clearly explains the reasoning behind a layout, colour, type or
iconography decision, and chooses an appropriate way to show it — an annotated
design, a short walkthrough — is showing **2 — Intermediate**, the baseline
this level expects. A candidate who explains a complex design problem to
stakeholders outside design and builds consensus by asking searching questions
is showing evidence closer to **3 — Advanced**, above this level's baseline.

## About this role level

The User-centred design profession's Graphic designer role creates graphic
elements that underpin interaction and service design. Across the role, a
graphic designer uses layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure
that content is legible and readable, and that users see and understand
interactions.

The role has six levels, in order: Associate graphic designer, Junior graphic
designer, Graphic designer, Senior graphic designer, Lead graphic designer, and
Head of graphic design. This assessment is for the third of them.

A **graphic designer** at this level is a confident and competent designer who
can develop designs based on evidence of user needs and organisational
outcomes. At this role level, they will be trusted to make good decisions,
recognise when to ask for further guidance and support, contribute to the
development of design concepts, interpret evidence-based research and
incorporate it into their work, support the quality of design delivery across
teams, and lead multiple or highly complex services.

The framework names seven skills for this role level: Design communication,
Designing for everyone, Designing strategically, Designing together,
Evidence-based design, Iterative design, and Leading design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

A graphic designer is expected to clearly explain problems and design ideas to
others, and clearly explain design decisions to others using appropriate
tools and methods.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Clearly explain" describes independent,
competent communication, a step beyond the guided description expected of a
junior designer, but short of the cross-boundary, consensus-building
communication a senior designer is expected to manage.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain a design decision, even when asked directly what
  problem it solves.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a design decision when prompted, but the explanation
  is vague or needs a colleague to fill gaps.
- 2 — Intermediate: Clearly explains a design problem and the reasoning behind
  a layout, colour, type or iconography decision, choosing an appropriate way
  to show it, such as an annotated design or a short walkthrough.
- 3 — Advanced: Explains complex design problems to stakeholders outside
  design, documents decisions and unresolved issues, and builds consensus by
  asking searching questions.
- 4 — Expert: Gains support for design strategy from senior leaders and
  coaches others in communicating design effectively.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to walk through a real piece of work — a layout, an icon
  set, a style guide page — and explain the decisions behind it as if to a
  stakeholder unfamiliar with design.
- Review a design rationale document or presentation they have produced.
- Ask about a time a design decision was challenged, and how they explained
  or defended it.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

A graphic designer is expected to work with others to design and deliver
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services
that meet the needs of all users, and to ensure a design meets appropriate
standards, for example accessibility regulations, with support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Work with others to design and deliver" is
applied, collaborative practice, while "with support" on the standards bullet
keeps full, independent assurance out of reach, so Intermediate fits better
than Advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what makes a graphic design accessible, for example
  colour contrast or a colour-blind-safe palette.
- 1 — Beginner: With guidance, checks a design against a basic accessibility
  checklist, such as contrast or text alternatives for icons.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently applies accessible and inclusive design
  practice to typography, colour and iconography, and asks for support to
  confirm a design meets formal standards.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises other designers on inclusive and sustainable design
  and ensures a design meets accessibility regulations without prompting.
- 4 — Expert: Measurably improves inclusive and sustainable design practice
  across multiple teams.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a colour palette or icon set the designer produced and ask how they
  checked it works for users with low vision or colour blindness.
- Ask about a specific accessibility standard, for example a contrast ratio,
  and how they apply it to graphic assets.
- Ask about a design choice they changed because it excluded some users.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

A graphic designer is expected to align their design work to both the goals
and vision of their team and the strategic objectives of their organisation,
identify potential risks and use design to mitigate them, identify
opportunities and use design to take advantage of them, and contribute to the
development of design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This moves beyond explaining why alignment
matters — a more junior framing — into actually aligning work and spotting
risk and opportunity, so the baseline sits at Intermediate, short of the
senior designer's coaching role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot connect a piece of graphic design work to the team's
  goals.
- 1 — Beginner: Says, when asked, how a specific piece of work supports the
  team's goals.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently aligns layout and visual design choices to
  team goals, and contributes new pattern or component ideas to a shared
  library.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps a team understand how design contributes to its goals,
  and shapes design using risk, opportunity and constraints in technology,
  systems and policy.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds design in organisational strategy.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to describe a project where their layout or visual choices
  were driven by a team goal, not personal preference.
- Review a contribution they made to a shared pattern library or component
  set.
- Ask about a risk they spotted in a design and what they did about it.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

A graphic designer is expected to effectively plan and run a design session
with their team, users or stakeholders, identify and engage the right people
throughout the design process, and give and receive constructive design
feedback.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Planning and running a session independently, and
handling feedback both ways, is competent practice — a step up from taking
part in a session someone else runs, and short of advising others on how to
do it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what a design session or crit is for.
- 1 — Beginner: Takes part in a design session and gives feedback when
  prompted.
- 2 — Intermediate: Plans and runs a design session, identifies who should be
  in the room, and both gives and receives constructive feedback.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises others on running sessions, adapts a session that is
  not working, and works across team or profession boundaries.
- 4 — Expert: Runs sessions that include senior leaders and coaches others in
  collaborative design practice.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to describe a design session or crit they planned and ran,
  including who they invited and why.
- Observe them giving feedback on a colleague's work, if possible.
- Ask about a time they received difficult feedback on their design and what
  they did with it.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A graphic designer is expected to analyse, synthesise and clearly explain
evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics data, and
to work with researchers or analysts to use evidence to inform, develop and
test a design idea.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Analysing and explaining evidence, and working
with a researcher or analyst to test an idea, is applied, collaborative
practice — a step beyond explaining what evidence shows with support, and
short of handling complex evidence or framing hypotheses for a team.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what evidence, such as analytics or user research,
  might tell them about a design.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains what a piece of evidence shows, with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Analyses and explains evidence relevant to a design, and
  works with a researcher or analyst to test a design idea.
- 3 — Advanced: Analyses and explains complex evidence, for example large
  data sets, and helps a team frame and use design hypotheses.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds hypothesis-driven design practice and guides the
  organisation on using evidence.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to talk through a piece of user research or analytics
  data that shaped a graphic design decision.
- Ask how they worked with a user researcher or analyst on a project.
- Review a design that changed as a result of evidence, and ask what the
  evidence showed.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A graphic designer is expected to apply iterative design principles and agile
methodologies to their work, iterate and improve a design based on
successive rounds of research, prototype their ideas at different
fidelities to explore and test designs, use a range of design tools and
techniques, and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Iterating across rounds of research, working at
more than one fidelity, and adjusting existing patterns is competent,
independent practice — short of iterating complex designs or adapting
quickly to fast-changing requirements.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what changed between two versions of a design and
  why.
- 1 — Beginner: Makes a small change to a design following feedback, with
  guidance on what "iteration" means in practice.
- 2 — Intermediate: Iterates a design across successive rounds of research,
  prototypes at more than one fidelity, and uses and adjusts existing
  patterns and components.
- 3 — Advanced: Iterates complex designs, prototypes independently at the
  right fidelity for the question being asked, and adapts quickly when
  requirements change.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds iterative practice across teams and leads prototyping
  of complex ideas at any fidelity.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to show two or three versions of the same design and
  explain what changed and why, referencing research or feedback.
- Ask about the range of tools they use for different fidelities, for
  example sketches versus a high-fidelity prototype.
- Review a pattern or component they reused or adapted from a library.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A graphic designer is expected to lead and coordinate design work in their
team, with support, communicate the value of user-centred design to their
team, support other designers, and work with digital and data leaders in
their organisation when needed.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "With support" caps full independence, but
leading or coordinating a piece of work, even with support, is a step beyond
simply describing leadership responsibilities in the abstract, so
Intermediate is the right baseline rather than Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe why user-centred design matters to their team.
- 1 — Beginner: Describes the value of user-centred design when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: With support, leads or coordinates a piece of design work
  in the team, communicates the value of user-centred design, and supports
  another designer.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads and coordinates design work without needing support,
  advocates for user-centred design outside the design team, and mentors
  other designers.
- 4 — Expert: Sets a vision for design leadership across multiple teams or
  the organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the designer led or coordinated a piece of work, even a
  small one, and what support they needed.
- Ask how they have supported a more junior designer.
- Ask about a time they explained the value of user-centred design to
  someone outside the design team.
