# Associate Graphic Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a
reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities the UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) Profession Capability
Framework expects of an **associate graphic designer**. Use it for a hiring
decision, an onboarding check, or a general capability review against the
framework.

The framework describes this level as an entry-level trainee position:
someone working under supervision who needs aptitude, potential and an
understanding of the role. The matrix below is built to that description,
not to a more senior one — do not mark someone down for needing supervision;
the framework says they should.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole
of one. Read `## Advisory` before you use it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what this
level actually expects — and, just as importantly, what it does not. Because
this is a trainee level, evidence may come from academic work, placements or
a small portfolio rather than paid design experience; weigh that
appropriately rather than expecting a work history the level does not
assume. Gather whatever evidence exists: a CV or portfolio, a structured
interview, a short work sample or task, direct observation, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating you give must trace back
to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
and for a hiring decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Stay fair.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact between candidates.
Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using Designing for everyone, a skill this level names:

- A candidate who cannot name an access need a user might have sits at
  **0 — None**.
- A candidate who explains why inclusive, accessible and environmentally
  sustainable design matters, and can name a couple of access needs and
  plausible ways to meet them, sits at **1 — Beginner** — the baseline this
  assessment sets for this skill.
- A candidate who independently spots and fixes an accessibility problem in
  their own design work is already working above this level, at
  **2 — Intermediate**.

## About this role level

**The role: graphic designer.** A graphic designer creates graphic elements
that underpin interaction and service design, using layout, spacing,
colour, type and iconography to ensure that content is legible and
readable, and that users see and understand interactions.

**This level: associate graphic designer.** The framework describes this
level as a trainee in an entry-level position, working under supervision,
who needs aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. The
framework does not give a separate accountability list for this level
beyond that description — its expectations are carried entirely in the
skill bullets below, which are consistently pitched at explaining and
describing, rather than doing the work unsupervised.

**Where this level sits.** This repository's summaries carry two levels for
graphic designer: associate graphic designer, assessed here, and junior
graphic designer, the level above it. The framework publishes further
levels above junior graphic designer, but this repository's summaries do
not name them, so this note does not enumerate a ladder beyond the two
levels it can see.

**The seven skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to explain the importance of articulating
design decisions to others, and to describe some methods for explaining
design decisions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Both bullets are "explain" and "describe" —
understanding-level, matching a trainee who has not yet been asked to
communicate design decisions independently.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why a designer would need to explain a design
  decision to someone else.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains, in general terms, why articulating design
  decisions matters, and names a method — such as a rationale document or a
  walkthrough — for doing it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses an appropriate method to explain a specific
  design decision to a named audience, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Explains a design decision clearly to a non-designer
  stakeholder without support — already working towards junior graphic
  designer.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches others in how to communicate design decisions — well
  above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain why a graphic designer might need to justify
  a colour or layout choice to a non-designer.
- Ask them to name and describe a method for explaining a design decision,
  such as annotated mock-ups or a short rationale.
- Ask a referee, such as a tutor or placement supervisor, whether the
  candidate can talk through their own design choices when asked.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to explain the importance of making
government content and services inclusive, accessible and environmentally
sustainable; explain different access needs that users can have, and ways
to meet them; and describe how privilege and power influence the design and
delivery of content and services.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. All three bullets are explain or describe, matching
a trainee who understands the principles but has not yet been asked to
apply them unsupervised.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name an access need a user might have, or say why
  inclusive design matters.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains why inclusive, accessible and environmentally
  sustainable design matters, names some access needs and plausible ways to
  meet them, and describes, in general terms, how privilege and power can
  shape a design.
- 2 — Intermediate: Points out a specific accessibility problem in a piece
  of design work, with support fixing it.
- 3 — Advanced: Designs a straightforward piece of content or service that
  meets basic accessibility standards without prompting — already working
  towards junior graphic designer.
- 4 — Expert: Leads inclusive design practice across a team — well above
  this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain what inclusive, accessible and
  environmentally sustainable design means for a government service, in
  their own words.
- Ask them to name two different access needs and a way a design could meet
  each one.
- Ask them to describe an example of how privilege or power might shape
  whose needs get designed for.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to explain the importance of aligning to
the strategy of an organisation; describe how their design work aligns to
the wider goals and vision of their team; and explain the value of using
patterns and components in design.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Explain and describe throughout — the trainee is
expected to understand why strategy and patterns matter, not yet to shape
either.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why a design should align with an organisation's
  strategy.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains why aligning to organisational strategy matters,
  describes in general terms how their own work connects to the team's
  goals, and explains why using patterns and components is valuable.
- 2 — Intermediate: Connects a specific piece of their own work to a stated
  team goal without being told to.
- 3 — Advanced: Chooses an existing pattern or component for a piece of
  work and explains why it fits the team's strategy — already working
  towards junior graphic designer.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes design strategy for a team or organisation — well
  above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain how a piece of design work they have done
  connects to a wider team or organisational goal.
- Ask them to explain the value of using existing patterns and components
  rather than designing from scratch each time.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate shows awareness of the "why" behind
  design decisions, not just the "how".

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to explain different techniques for
bringing together perspectives from multiple people to inform a design, and
to explain the importance of constructive feedback in the design process.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Explain-only bullets, matching a trainee who has not
yet run a collaborative design activity themselves.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a technique for bringing multiple people's
  perspectives into a design.
- 1 — Beginner: Names and explains a technique for gathering multiple
  perspectives, such as a design crit or a workshop, and explains why
  constructive feedback matters.
- 2 — Intermediate: Takes part constructively in a design crit or workshop
  and applies feedback received.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps facilitate a small collaborative design session —
  already working towards junior graphic designer.
- 4 — Expert: Runs collaborative design practice across a team — well above
  this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain a technique for bringing together different
  people's perspectives on a design, such as a crit or a co-design
  workshop.
- Ask them to explain why constructive feedback matters in a design
  process, and describe a time they received some.
- Ask a referee how the candidate responds when their design work is
  critiqued in a group setting.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to explain how research and analytics
inform user-centred design; frame their ideas as design hypotheses to be
tested; and identify common sources and types of data that inform
user-centred design.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Two of the three bullets are explain and identify;
"frame your ideas as design hypotheses" is a small step towards
application, but the block as a whole still describes conceptual grounding
rather than independently testing a hypothesis, so it stays at Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what a design hypothesis is, or why data might
  inform a design.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains how research and analytics inform user-centred
  design, names common sources and types of data, and can frame a simple
  idea as a hypothesis when prompted.
- 2 — Intermediate: Frames their own design idea as a testable hypothesis
  without prompting.
- 3 — Advanced: Uses a specific piece of research or analytics to change a
  design decision — already working towards junior graphic designer.
- 4 — Expert: Designs and runs evidence-gathering activity to test design
  hypotheses independently — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain how research or analytics data might change
  a design decision, using an example.
- Ask them to frame a simple design idea as a hypothesis that could be
  tested.
- Ask them to name a couple of sources of data or evidence a designer might
  use.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to describe the basics of iterative design
methodologies such as agile; explain how iteration supports user-centred
design; and describe some ways to prototype ideas at different levels of
realism, or 'fidelities', and when to use them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Describe and explain throughout, matching a trainee
who understands the idea of iteration but has not yet been asked to run an
iterative cycle themselves.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what an iterative design methodology is.
- 1 — Beginner: Describes the basics of an iterative methodology such as
  agile, explains why iteration supports user-centred design, and describes
  a couple of prototyping fidelities and roughly when each is used.
- 2 — Intermediate: Takes part in one round of iteration on a piece of
  design work, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Runs a full cycle of prototyping, testing and iterating a
  design independently — already working towards junior graphic designer.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches a team in iterative practice — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe, in their own words, what an iterative
  design methodology like agile involves.
- Ask them to explain why iterating on a design tends to produce a better
  result than designing once.
- Ask them to describe two different prototype fidelities and when each
  would be useful.

- [ ] 0 — None

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to explain the role of user-centred design
in an organisation; describe typical leadership responsibilities in design;
and identify leaders in their organisation that designers need to work
with.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Explain, describe and identify — orientation-level
bullets appropriate to someone new to the discipline, not yet in any
leadership capacity.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what user-centred design contributes to an
  organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains the role user-centred design plays in an
  organisation, describes what a design leader typically does, and
  identifies at least one leader their own team would need to work with.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains how a specific design leadership decision
  affected a piece of work they were part of.
- 3 — Advanced: Takes on a small piece of informal leadership, such as
  coordinating a task within a team — already working towards junior
  graphic designer.
- 4 — Expert: Leads design for a team or organisation — well above this
  level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain why user-centred design matters to an
  organisation delivering public services.
- Ask them to describe what a design lead or head of design typically does
  day to day.
- Ask them to name a role, such as a service owner or product manager, that
  a designer in their team would need to work with, and why.

accountability to weigh centrality against, so consider instead how
coachable the shortfall looks, whether it clusters with other low ratings,
and record your reasoning in the evidence log rather than relying on the
