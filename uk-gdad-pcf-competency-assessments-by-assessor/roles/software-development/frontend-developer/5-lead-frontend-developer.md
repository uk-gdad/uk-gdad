# Lead frontend developer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the measuring — a line manager, an
interview panel, or a reviewer conducting a capability check — evaluating a
candidate or an employee against the lead frontend developer role level of the
UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) Profession Capability Framework.

A lead frontend developer is an expert practitioner who sets direction and
assures the quality of development across teams, leads multiple or highly
complex services, and provides technical leadership, coaching and mentoring to
the wider community. This document turns that description into a competency
matrix: eleven skills, each rated against observable behaviour, that together
answer one question — does this person possess the baseline abilities required
to do this job?

This is one input among several to a hiring, promotion or development
decision, never the whole of it. Read `## Advisory` below before using it.

## Advisory

> This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
> educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
> not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
> form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
> disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human

> colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so the skills in the
matrix make sense against the role and the level they belong to. Then gather
whatever evidence already exists before you score anything: a CV or
portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task, direct observation
of the person's work, or references from people who have worked with them.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating in the matrix must trace
back to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If
you cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess — a guess dressed up as a rating is worse than an honest gap.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, using
the same kinds of evidence where you can. Where possible, have more than one
assessor score independently and compare notes afterwards, especially for a
hiring or promotion decision — independent scoring followed by calibration is
one of the most effective ways to catch a single assessor's blind spots.

**Staying fair.** This is an unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument. Used
inconsistently, or scored on impression rather than evidence, it can produce
outcomes that unlawfully disadvantage some candidates over others, even
without anyone intending it. Consistent, evidence-based administration by more
than one assessor materially reduces that risk. Every rating and every
evidence prompt below is about demonstrated, job-related behaviour only —
never let a rating be influenced by anything else, including a candidate's
protected characteristics, health, or personal circumstances — and this
assessment must never be used to compare one candidate against another, only
to measure one person against the framework's own description of this role
level.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale.
This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or
how they feel about doing it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using the Accessibility skill named at this level:

- A candidate who can describe WCAG success criteria but has never applied
  them to a real interface, and needs a colleague to point out failures, is
  rated **1 — Beginner**.
- A candidate who runs accessibility testing on their own work, fixes what
  they find, and can explain the reasoning behind a fix when asked, is rated
  **3 — Advanced**: independent, competent, not yet advising others.
- A candidate who has changed how their organisation approaches
  accessibility — writing the testing standard other teams now follow,
  briefing non-technical stakeholders on why it matters, and coaching other
  developers through their first accessibility audit — is rated **4 —
  Expert**.

## About this role level

**The role.** A frontend developer designs, builds and improves website
software that meets user needs. In this role, a person is responsible for
writing clean, accessible code following a progressive enhancement approach;
creates code that is open by default and easy for others to reuse; designs
software which meets user needs and creates meaningful interactions and
relationships with users; and has an understanding of the three fundamental
frontend technologies — HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

**This level: Lead frontend developer.** A lead frontend developer is an
expert practitioner who influences and mentors others. At this role level, a
person sets direction and assures the quality of development across teams;
leads multiple or highly complex services; contributes to the community by
providing technical leadership, coaching and mentoring; promotes knowledge
sharing and the adoption of good practice; and is proficient in a wide range
of technical systems, able to identify the appropriate technology and
approach for a given problem.

**Where this sits in the frontend developer ladder**, in order:

1. Apprentice frontend developer
2. Junior frontend developer
3. Frontend developer
4. Senior frontend developer
5. **Lead frontend developer — this level**
6. Head of frontend development

**The skills this level names**, in the order the framework lists them:

1. Accessibility
2. Communicating information
3. Community collaboration
4. Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)
5. Modern development standards
6. Programming and build (frontend developer)
7. Prototyping
8. Strategic thinking
9. Systems integration (frontend developer)
10. User focus (frontend developer)
11. Web performance optimisation

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Accessibility

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer shows a strong understanding of accessibility and
is an advocate for users with accessibility needs. They build user interfaces
that meet a predefined set of standards, champion accessibility to internal
and external stakeholders, offer design solutions to mitigate the risk of
failing accessibility testing, and recommend the best tools and methods to
use when accessibility testing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework asks this level to champion accessibility
to stakeholders and to recommend testing tools and methods to others, which is
advisory and teaching work, not only personal application of a standard.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain what an accessibility standard is for, or point to
  any accessibility work they have done.
- 1 — Beginner: Knows some accessibility basics, such as alt text or colour
  contrast, but needs close direction to apply them and cannot run an
  accessibility test unsupervised.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies a known accessibility standard to their own
  interfaces with some guidance, and can fix issues a testing tool flags.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently builds interfaces that meet a defined
  accessibility standard, runs accessibility testing unprompted, and can
  explain a fix's rationale to a colleague.
- 4 — Expert: Has changed how a team or organisation approaches
  accessibility — writing a standard others follow, briefing non-technical
  stakeholders on the business case, or recommending the testing tools a
  wider group now uses.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of an accessibility failure the candidate found and
  fixed, and what they changed about the team's process afterwards.
- Give a short interface mock-up with two or three accessibility defects and
  ask the candidate to identify them and propose fixes.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has ever been asked to advise another
  team on accessibility, and what came of it.
- Review a design document or pull request where the candidate raised or
  resolved an accessibility concern.

### Skill: Communicating information

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer mediates between people and mends relationships,
communicating with stakeholders at all levels. They manage stakeholder
expectations and host or moderate discussions about high risk and complexity,
even within constrained timescales, and speak on behalf of and represent the
community to large audiences inside and outside of government.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework explicitly describes representing the
community to large audiences and mediating relationships at all levels, which
is organisation-wide communication work rather than day-to-day technical
reporting.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe a time they explained technical work to a
  non-technical stakeholder.
- 1 — Beginner: Communicates within their own immediate team, but struggles to
  adapt a message for a stakeholder outside it, and needs coaching before a
  difficult conversation.
- 2 — Intermediate: Updates stakeholders clearly on their own work with some
  guidance on tone and framing, particularly under time pressure.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently manages stakeholder expectations on a
  complex piece of work, and can moderate a discussion between people who
  disagree without needing someone more senior present.
- 4 — Expert: Has mediated a relationship that had broken down, or has
  represented the frontend community to a large internal or external audience
  — a conference talk, a cross-government forum, or a board-level briefing.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they mediated between two stakeholders
  who disagreed about a technical decision, and what they did.
- Ask for an example of a presentation or talk they have given to an audience
  outside their immediate team, and what the audience took away.
- Ask a referee how the candidate handles a stakeholder who is unhappy about
  timescales or risk.
- Observe the candidate explaining a technically complex trade-off to a
  non-technical panel member during the interview itself.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer works collaboratively in a group, actively
networking with others. They adapt feedback to ensure it is effective and
lasting, use their topic to identify problems or issues in the team dynamic
and rectify them, and identify issues through Agile "health checks" with the
team, helping to stimulate the right responses.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's own wording here is about strong,
independent facilitation within a team's dynamic rather than the
organisation-wide reach it gives to the leadership and communication skills
above, so the bar is a full grasp of the skill rather than evidence of having
taught it to others.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe having noticed or acted on a problem in how a
  team was working together.
- 1 — Beginner: Takes part in team activities such as retrospectives, but
  needs prompting to raise a concern about team dynamics.
- 2 — Intermediate: Gives feedback to colleagues with some guidance on how to
  make it land well, and follows a team's existing health-check process.
- 3 — Advanced: Notices a team dynamic problem — friction between two people,
  a pattern of missed handovers — through a health check or day-to-day
  observation, and takes independent action to address it.
- 4 — Expert: Has changed how more than one team runs its own health checks
  or feedback process, based on what they learned facilitating one.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a team dynamic problem they identified and
  what they did about it.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has run or contributed meaningfully to
  a retrospective or Agile health check, and what changed as a result.
- Observe how the candidate handles a scenario, posed during the interview,
  describing a team where feedback is not landing.
- Ask for an example of feedback the candidate gave that was hard to deliver,
  and how they adapted it to be heard.

### Skill: Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer changes organisational structures to fixable and
sustainable designs, leads on the strategy for the whole organisation,
marrying business needs with innovative analysis, and makes and justifies
decisions characterised by high levels of risk, impact and complexity. They
build consensus between organisations — private or public — or highly
independent stakeholders, solve and unblock issues between teams or
departments at the highest level, understand the psychology of a team and
have strong mediation skills, and coach the organisation on team dynamics and
conflict resolution.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Every bullet the framework gives for this skill is
framed at organisational scale — changing structures, building consensus
between whole organisations, coaching the organisation itself — which is the
clearest statement in this level's summary of what "expert practitioner who
influences and mentors others" means in practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe having led or unblocked a decision that affected
  more than their own immediate team.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes ideas to organisational decisions when asked, but
  needs someone more senior to actually make and justify a high-risk call.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads a decision within their own team with high risk or
  complexity, with support from a more senior colleague on the justification.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently makes and justifies a high-risk, high-complexity
  decision, and unblocks a disagreement between two teams without needing to
  escalate it.
- 4 — Expert: Has changed an organisational structure or way of working
  across more than one team, built consensus between highly independent
  stakeholders who started out in disagreement, or coached an organisation on
  resolving a conflict rather than resolving it for them.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a decision they made that carried high risk
  and high complexity, who else it affected, and how they justified it.
- Ask for an example of building consensus between two teams or organisations
  that started out with conflicting positions.
- Ask a referee about a time the candidate coached someone else through
  resolving a conflict, rather than resolving it themselves.
- Review a document — a design decision record, a business case, a strategy
  paper — the candidate authored or led that shows this kind of reasoning.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer identifies and applies modern development standards
to support complex projects and programmes, and leads others in applying
modern development standards.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework names leading others in applying
standards as an explicit part of this skill at this level, not only applying
them personally.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a modern development standard relevant to frontend
  work, such as a linting, testing or accessibility standard.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a development standard set by someone else, with
  reminders when they drift from it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies known modern development standards to their own
  work consistently, with some guidance when a standard is ambiguous.
- 3 — Advanced: Identifies which modern development standard fits a complex
  project unprompted, and applies it without support.
- 4 — Expert: Has led a team or programme in adopting a modern development
  standard — writing the guidance, running the training, or being the person
  others check with when a standard is unclear.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a development standard the candidate introduced or
  changed, and how they got other developers to adopt it.
- Give a small code sample and ask which modern development standards it
  falls short of, and why those matter for a complex programme.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate is the person their team turns to for a
  ruling on how something should be built.
- Review documentation or a style guide the candidate has authored.

### Skill: Programming and build (frontend developer)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer advises on the right way to apply standards and
methods to ensure compliance, provides technical advice to stakeholders and
sets team-based standards for programming tools and techniques, collaborates
with others when required to review specifications, and uses specifications
to build, test and document user interfaces of high complexity, using the
appropriate standards and tools.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The skill explicitly includes setting team-based
standards for tools and techniques and advising stakeholders, on top of
personally building high-complexity interfaces, which is teaching-and-guiding
work in the framework's own terms.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe having built a user interface from a
  specification without close supervision.
- 1 — Beginner: Builds simple interfaces from a clear specification, with
  regular review and correction.
- 2 — Intermediate: Builds, tests and documents interfaces of moderate
  complexity from a specification, with some guidance on tool choice.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently builds, tests and documents user interfaces of
  high complexity, choosing the appropriate standards and tools without
  support.
- 4 — Expert: Has set the programming standards or tool choices a whole team
  now works to, and has advised a non-technical stakeholder on the right
  technical approach for a piece of work.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a high-complexity interface they built,
  including the standards and tools they chose and why.
- Ask for an example of a team-wide standard for programming tools or
  techniques they introduced.
- Give a specification with an ambiguity in it and ask how they would resolve
  it before building.
- Ask a referee whether stakeholders have come to the candidate directly for
  technical advice, and on what.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer approaches prototyping as a team activity, actively
soliciting prototypes and testing with others. They establish design patterns
and iterate them, and use a variety of prototyping methods, choosing the most
appropriate one for the situation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The skill asks this level to run prototyping as a
team activity and choose methods well, which is strong independent practice;
it does not, in the framework's own wording, ask this level to teach
prototyping method choice to others the way the leadership and standards
skills above do.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe a prototype they built or tested with someone
  else.
- 1 — Beginner: Builds a prototype when asked to, using one method they were
  shown, without testing it with anyone else.
- 2 — Intermediate: Builds and tests a prototype with some guidance on which
  method fits the question being asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently chooses the right prototyping method for the
  situation, runs it as a team activity by actively soliciting input, and
  establishes a design pattern from what they learn.
- 4 — Expert: Has iterated a design pattern across more than one project
  based on prototyping evidence, and is the person a team turns to for which
  prototyping method suits a given question.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a prototype they ran as a team activity, who
  they involved, and what they changed as a result.
- Ask for an example of a design pattern they established and later iterated
  based on evidence.
- Give a scenario — a new interaction the team is unsure about — and ask
  which prototyping method they would choose and why.
- Review a prototype artefact and the notes or findings that came out of
  testing it.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer leads the design and implementation of strategy,
directing the evaluation of strategies and policies to ensure business
requirements are being met.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's wording is explicitly about leading and

- 4 — Expert: Has led the design and implementation of a strategy — not just

- Ask for an example of directing an evaluation of a strategy or policy

- Ask a referee whether the candidate has been asked to set direction for

### Skill: Systems integration (frontend developer)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer defines the static assets build, co-ordinates all
aspects of the integration and takes responsibility for the tests around the
user interface, and configures the part of the system that uses the network,
testing that it works as expected.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This skill's wording is about defining and owning
the integration and network-facing configuration work independently; it does
not, unlike the standards and leadership skills above, explicitly ask this
level to teach or set integration approaches for other teams.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe having integrated a frontend build with a wider
  system.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an existing static assets build and integration
  process set up by someone else, needing help when it breaks.
- 2 — Intermediate: Makes small changes to a build or integration
  configuration with some guidance, and can run the tests around it.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently defines a static assets build, co-ordinates the
  integration end to end, takes responsibility for the interface's tests, and
  configures and tests the network-facing parts of the system.
- 4 — Expert: Has designed an integration or build approach that more than
  one service or team now relies on, and is the person others check with
  when a build or network configuration problem is hard to diagnose.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a static assets build they defined, and
  a network integration problem they diagnosed and fixed.
- Give a scenario describing a failing integration test and ask how they
  would isolate the cause.
- Review a build configuration or integration test suite the candidate wrote
  or owns.
- Ask a referee who owns the integration and build decisions on the
  candidate's current team.

### Skill: User focus (frontend developer)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer collaborates with user researchers and interaction
designers to represent user needs internally, explains the difference between
user needs and the desires of the user, champions user research to focus on
all users, effectively prioritises and defines approaches to understand the
user story while guiding others to do so, and offers recommendations on the
best tools and methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The skill explicitly includes guiding others to define
approaches for understanding the user story and recommending tools and
methods, which the framework frames as teaching, not only applying user focus
to one's own work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe a user need they represented in a technical
  decision.
- 1 — Beginner: Repeats what a user researcher tells them about user needs,
  without being able to explain the reasoning behind it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Represents user needs in their own work with some
  guidance, and can explain the difference between a stated desire and an
  underlying need when prompted.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently collaborates with user researchers and
  interaction designers, champions research covering all users rather than
  the loudest ones, and prioritises which user story to understand next.
- 4 — Expert: Has guided other developers in defining their own approach to
  understanding a user story, and is the person others ask which research
  method or tool fits a given question.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they distinguished a user's stated
  desire from their underlying need, and what changed as a result.
- Ask for an example of guiding another developer through understanding a
  user story for the first time.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has championed research covering users
  who are easy to overlook, such as users with limited access or connectivity.
- Review a piece of work where the candidate's user focus visibly shaped a
  technical decision.

### Skill: Web performance optimisation

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead frontend developer identifies and resolves web performance issues,
collects data to monitor and resolve web performance issues, and guides
others on the best web performance optimisation software and methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's wording explicitly extends this skill
to guiding others on tools and methods, not only diagnosing and fixing
performance problems personally.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe having diagnosed or fixed a web performance
  problem.
- 1 — Beginner: Recognises that a page is slow, but needs help identifying
  the cause or the right tool to investigate it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Uses a known performance tool to diagnose an issue with
  some guidance, and applies a suggested fix.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently collects performance data, identifies the
  cause of a performance issue, and resolves it without support.
- 4 — Expert: Has guided another developer or a whole team on which
  performance optimisation software or method to use, and has changed how a
  team monitors performance rather than only fixing a single issue.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a web performance issue they diagnosed,
  including the data they collected and how they confirmed the fix worked.
- Give a scenario with a slow page and ask which data they would gather
  first and why.
- Ask for an example of recommending a performance tool or method that a
  wider team then adopted.
- Review a performance audit, monitoring dashboard, or optimisation write-up
  the candidate produced.

The baseline is normally met when every skill above is rated at or above its
baseline target. One or two skills below target is not automatically a fail:
weigh how central that skill is to this level's accountabilities — leading
across teams, setting direction, coaching and mentoring — consider whether the
shortfall looks quickly coachable once the person is in post, and record that
reasoning in the evidence log below rather than relying on the number alone.
A wide gap across several skills, or a gap on a skill this level's own
description treats as central — Leadership and guidance, or Strategic
thinking, for instance — deserves more weight than a single narrow gap on a
more supporting skill.
