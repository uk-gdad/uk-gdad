# Principal DevOps Engineer (management) - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This competency assessment is for the Software development role of
Development operations (DevOps) engineer, at the Principal DevOps engineer -
management role level. It is written for an assessor — a line manager, a
panel, or a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee holds the
baseline abilities this role level requires, whether the context is a hiring
decision, a promotion board, onboarding into the role, or a periodic
capability check against the framework.

This document covers the **management** track of Principal DevOps engineer.
The framework publishes two roles at this level — a technical role and a
management role — and this assessment is for the management one. A separate
document, for the technical track, covers the same numbered level.

This assessment is one input among several into whatever decision it
supports. It is never the whole of that decision. Read `## Advisory` before
using it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you are working against what this role level actually requires rather than
what you assume a "principal" title implies. Gather whatever evidence exists
before you start: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or
task, direct observation of the person at work, or references. The more of
these you have, the more confidently you can rate.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating in the competency
matrix must trace to something the person did, said, produced, or was
observed doing. If you cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as
unknown rather than guess — a guess dressed up as a score is worse than an
honest gap.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
using the same evidence types where possible. For a hiring or promotion
decision, have more than one assessor score independently and compare notes
afterwards, rather than scoring together from the start.

**Fairness.** This is an unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument. Used
inconsistently, it can produce unlawful adverse impact — different people
held to different standards without anyone intending it. Consistent,
evidence-based administration, ideally by more than one assessor, materially
reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a proficiency scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example**, using Development process optimisation: a rating of 2
might be evidenced by someone who has, under guidance, spotted a process
inefficiency on their own team and helped implement a fix someone else
designed. A rating of 3 might be evidenced by someone who has independently
analysed a development process across a team, identified where it was
failing, and led the change themselves. A rating of 4, the baseline this
skill sits at for this level, would be evidenced by someone who has set the
strategy and resourcing for a solution development programme spanning
several teams, working with client functions to establish the business
requirements the programme answers to, and can describe how they proposed,
initiated and led it from that first conversation through to delivery.

## About this role level

A DevOps engineer — commonly called that, in full "development operations
engineer" — supports the development and operation of software through
tools, environments and practices. The role is responsible for underpinning
good development processes: managing tools and testing environments, central
code control, maintaining development standards, and writing software that
automates systems.

This role has nine published levels, in order: Apprentice DevOps engineer,
Junior DevOps engineer, DevOps engineer, Senior DevOps engineer (with a
parallel Senior DevOps engineer - management track), Lead DevOps engineer
(with a parallel Lead DevOps engineer - management track), and Principal
DevOps engineer (with a parallel **Principal DevOps engineer - management**
track — the level this document assesses, and the top of this role's
ladder).

A principal DevOps engineer leads and plans development across large or
multiple teams, defining the strategic vision for delivery. At this role
level, in the management track, you will:

- identify, test and champion the adoption of emerging technologies
- ensure that security, stability and capacity are embedded in the
  development and deployment of services
- develop a team's capability by rewarding high performers
- shape career paths and recruit the right talent
- identify skills gaps and important dependencies within technical teams

This level names ten skills: Availability and capacity management,
Development process optimisation, Information security, Modern development
standards, Programming and build (software engineering), Prototyping,
Service support, Systems design, Systems integration, and User focus.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a principal DevOps engineer in the management track to
lead policy and strategy for service and capacity management, and to lead
the identification and implementation of required changes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This is the top of the role's ladder, and the skill is
framed here entirely in terms of leading policy and strategy rather than
applying it — that is an expert-level accountability, not an advanced one.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how availability or capacity management applies
  to a development operations toolchain.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain the purpose of availability and capacity
  management processes but has not set or changed any.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has managed individual service components against
  agreed business needs and key performance indicators, with guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Has identified capacity issues independently, implemented
  the required changes, and initiated remedial action without needing it
  escalated to them.
- 4 — Expert: Has set the policy and strategy for availability and capacity
  management across more than one team or service, and can describe how
  that strategy changed practice, not just how it was written.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a document or policy the candidate wrote or led that set
  availability or capacity standards for more than one team.
- Structured interview question: "Describe a time capacity planning failed
  somewhere in your organisation. What strategy did you put in place
  afterwards, and how did you get other teams to adopt it?"
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has set capacity or availability
  policy that outlived their direct involvement in a project.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a principal DevOps engineer in the management track to
set the strategy and manage resource allocation for solution development
programmes, and to work with client functions to establish business
requirements and identify, propose, initiate and lead these programmes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Setting strategy, allocating resource across a
programme and leading it from proposal to delivery is programme-level
leadership, matching the top of this role's ladder.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has no experience improving how a development team works.
- 1 — Beginner: Can name common ways development processes are optimised
  but has not led any change.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has supported specific process-improvement activities
  and identified obvious deficiencies on a team, under direction.
- 3 — Advanced: Has analysed current processes independently and led a
  team through a service improvement, working to policy and standards set
  by someone else.
- 4 — Expert: Has set the strategy and resourcing for a solution
  development programme, worked with client functions to establish its
  business requirements, and proposed, initiated and led it themselves.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a development-process programme the candidate
  proposed and resourced, not just one they improved locally.
- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a business requirement you
  worked out with a client function, and the programme you built to meet
  it."
- Work sample: ask the candidate to sketch how they would resource and
  sequence a multi-team process-optimisation programme from a short brief.

### Skill: Information security

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the candidate to design, review and quality assure
solutions and services, ensuring they are specifically engineered to
mitigate security threats, and to lead others in using appropriate
information security practices.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Reviewing and quality assuring other people's designs,
and leading others' practice, is a mastery-level accountability at the top
of this role's ladder.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how security controls are engineered into a
  service.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain available security controls but has not applied
  or reviewed them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has designed a solution with security controls
  included, with guidance from a more experienced colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Has independently designed and reviewed solutions
  specifically engineered to mitigate identified security threats.
- 4 — Expert: Has quality-assured other engineers' security-engineered
  designs and led a team's adoption of stronger information security
  practice, with evidence the practice stuck after they moved on.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a design review the candidate led that changed how
  a service mitigated a specific security threat.
- Structured interview question: "Describe a piece of security practice you
  introduced to a team. How did you know it had actually taken hold rather
  than lapsing once you stopped checking?"
- Document to review: a design or architecture document the candidate
  quality-assured for security, with their annotations if available.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the candidate to identify and apply modern development
standards to support complex projects and programmes, and to lead others in
applying modern development standards.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Leading others' application of standards across
complex programmes, not just applying them personally, is the expert end of
this skill.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a modern development standard relevant to a DevOps
  toolchain.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain why modern development standards matter but
  applies them only under guidance.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies modern development standards to their own work
  with some guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Applies modern development standards independently and
  supports colleagues in applying them on request.
- 4 — Expert: Has identified which standards a complex programme needed and
  led a team or teams in adopting them, with a track record spanning more
  than one programme.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a standard the candidate identified as necessary
  for a programme and led others to adopt.
- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you had to persuade
  a team to change a working standard. How did you make the case?"
- Ask a referee how the candidate's standards work was received by the
  teams asked to change.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the candidate to collaborate with others when
necessary to review specifications, and to use agreed specifications to
design, code, test and document programs or scripts of medium-to-high
complexity, using the right standards and tools.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's own wording for this skill at this
level describes independent delivery of medium-to-high complexity work
against agreed specifications — advanced, hands-on competence — rather than
leading or setting strategy for other engineers' programming, which is why
it sits below the strategic skills in this block.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot design, code or test a script or program without close
  supervision.
- 1 — Beginner: Writes simple scripts or programs under the direction of
  others.
- 2 — Intermediate: Designs, codes, tests and documents programs of
  moderate complexity with some guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs, codes, tests and documents programs
  or scripts of medium-to-high complexity, using the right standards and
  tools, and reviews specifications collaboratively when needed.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the standards and tooling choices other engineers use
  for medium-to-high complexity work, and is sought out to review the
  hardest specifications.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: a piece of medium-to-high complexity automation code the
  candidate wrote and documented, with tests.
- Structured interview question: "Walk me through a specification you
  reviewed collaboratively before building it out. What changed as a
  result of that review?"
- Ask a referee to describe the complexity of work the candidate is
  typically trusted to deliver without close review.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the candidate to use a variety of prototyping methods,
to share best practice and coach others, and to look at strategic service
design end to end.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Look at strategic service design end to end" and
"coach others" are both expert-level accountabilities, well beyond applying
one prototyping method to one piece of work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not used a prototyping technique or method.
- 1 — Beginner: Understands what prototyping is and why it is used, in
  general terms.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has used a specific prototyping method when directed to
  and can explain its value to a team.
- 3 — Advanced: Chooses between a variety of prototyping methods for a
  given piece of work and applies the chosen method independently.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches other engineers in prototyping practice and has used
  prototyping as part of looking at a service's design strategically, end
  to end, not just at one component of it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of coaching the candidate gave someone on prototyping
  method choice.
- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you looked at a
  service's design end to end rather than just the part you were building.
  What did prototyping tell you that you would have missed otherwise?"
- Work sample: a prototyping artefact the candidate produced or directed,
  with a note on why that method was chosen over another.

### Skill: Service support

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the candidate to identify, locate and fix service
faults.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's wording for this skill at this level
is plainer than for the other skills in this block — independent fault
diagnosis and resolution, without the "advise others" or "lead" language the
technical track carries for the same skill — so the baseline sits at
independent, hands-on competence rather than at leading others through it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot diagnose the cause of a service fault without help.
- 1 — Beginner: Helps fix service faults under close direction, following
  agreed procedures.
- 2 — Intermediate: Fixes straightforward service faults with some
  guidance from a more experienced colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies, locates and fixes service faults,
  including ones whose cause is not immediately obvious.
- 4 — Expert: Is the person other engineers escalate the hardest, most
  ambiguous service faults to, and can trace a fault back through several
  interacting components unaided.

**Evidence to gather**

- Structured interview question: "Describe the hardest service fault you
  have located and fixed. What made it hard to find?"
- Scenario to observe: present a symptom of a live service fault and ask
  the candidate to talk through how they would locate its cause.
- Ask a referee for an example of a fault the candidate resolved that
  others had struggled with.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the candidate to design systems characterised by
medium levels of risk, impact, and business or technical complexity, to
select appropriate design standards, methods and tools and ensure they are
applied effectively, and to review the systems designs of others to ensure
the selection of appropriate technology, efficient use of resources, and
integration of multiple systems and technology.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Reviewing other people's systems designs, not only
producing your own, is a mastery-level accountability, and matches the top
of this role's ladder.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how a systems design accounts for risk or
  complexity.
- 1 — Beginner: Assists with parts of a systems design under close
  direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: Produces detailed designs for well-understood systems,
  with guidance on appropriate patterns.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs systems of medium risk, impact and
  complexity, selecting appropriate standards, methods and tools.
- 4 — Expert: Reviews other engineers' systems designs for appropriate
  technology choice, efficient resourcing and correct integration across
  multiple systems, and can point to designs materially improved by that
  review.

**Evidence to gather**

- Document to review: a systems design the candidate reviewed, with their
  feedback on technology choice or integration.
- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a design review where you
  changed the direction of someone else's systems design. What was wrong
  with the original approach?"
- Work sample: ask the candidate to review a sample systems design for
  risk, complexity and resource use.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the candidate to define the integration build,
co-ordinate build activities across systems, and understand how to
undertake and support integration testing activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's wording for this skill at this level
is execution-focused — defining and co-ordinating a build, and understanding
integration testing — rather than the standards-setting, resource-managing
language used for this skill elsewhere in this role's ladder, so the
baseline sits at independent delivery rather than leading a function.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how systems are integrated or tested together.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains the process and principles of integrating systems
  in general terms.
- 2 — Intermediate: Builds and tests simple interfaces between systems,
  with support from others on more complex integration.
- 3 — Advanced: Defines an integration build and co-ordinates build
  activities across more than one system, and understands how to undertake
  and support integration testing.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the standards or procedures other engineers follow for
  integration work across a whole service or programme.

**Evidence to gather**

- Structured interview question: "Describe an integration build you
  defined that spanned more than one system. How did you co-ordinate the
  teams involved?"
- Work sample: a build or integration plan the candidate authored.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has co-ordinated integration testing
  across systems owned by different teams.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the candidate to collaborate with user researchers and
represent users internally, explain the difference between user needs and
the desires of the user, champion user research to focus on all users,
prioritise and define approaches to understand the user story while guiding
others in doing so, and offer recommendations on the best tools and methods
to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Guiding others in how they approach the user story,
and championing user research on their behalf, are leadership
accountabilities that match the top of this role's ladder.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot distinguish a user need from what a user says they want.
- 1 — Beginner: Shows an awareness of user experience analysis and can
  explain the purpose of user stories.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies and engages with users or stakeholders to
  collect evidence of their needs, and uses that evidence in their own
  work.
- 3 — Advanced: Collaborates with user researchers, represents users
  internally, and explains to others the difference between user needs and
  user desires.
- 4 — Expert: Champions user research on behalf of all users across a team
  or programme, guides other engineers in how they approach the user
  story, and recommends which tools and methods a team should use.

**Evidence to gather**

- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you had to explain
  the difference between what a user asked for and what they actually
  needed. How did that change the work?"
- Ask for an example of guidance the candidate gave another engineer on
  approaching a user story.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has championed user research at a
  level beyond their own immediate work.
