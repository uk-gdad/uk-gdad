# Lead Security Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the assessing — a line manager, a
hiring panel member, or a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or employee
possesses the baseline abilities the lead security architect role level
requires. A lead security architect undertakes complex work of a high risk
level, often working across several projects at once, interacts with senior
stakeholders across departments, and is the point of escalation for architects
in lower grade roles.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then complete
the overall determination. This assessment is one input among several into a
hiring, promotion or development decision, never the whole of it — see
`## Advisory`.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before scoring, so you are
assessing against this level's own accountabilities rather than security
architect below it or principal security architect above it. Gather whatever
evidence exists first: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work
sample or task, direct observation, or references — for this level, a track
record across more than one project matters more than a single example.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, and
where possible have a second assessor score independently before you compare
notes — this matters most for a hiring or promotion decision, and materially
reduces the risk of an unlawful adverse impact from an unvalidated,
AI-drafted instrument used inconsistently.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence — see
[Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification.

**Handling security material.** This role level develops security patterns
and assesses how exploitable vulnerabilities are across complex system
architectures. When you record evidence in `## Assessor notes and evidence
log`, keep it at a level you would be comfortable discussing outside a secure
room: describe what the person did, not the live vulnerability, the exploit
technique, or any detail that would help an attacker. Never record material
above the classification this document will be handled and stored at.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

A worked example, using **Security technology**:

- A candidate rated **2 — Intermediate** can explain a known vulnerability's
  effect on one design with support, but cannot yet judge how easy it would
  be for an attacker to exploit it.
- A candidate rated **3 — Advanced** independently explains complex system
  architectures and reasons about exploitability across more than one
  system type, unsupported.
- A candidate rated **4 — Expert** has led or influenced security technology
  decisions beyond their own organisation — a conference talk, a contribution
  to a standard, a role advising another organisation — and can point to
  where their judgement on exploitability changed a decision.

## About this role level

**The role: security architect.** A security architect designs and builds
secure solutions.

**This level: lead security architect.** A lead security architect undertakes
complex work of a high risk level, often working on several projects. The
role develops vision, principles and strategy for security architects on one
project or technology, works out subtle security needs, and leads the
technical design of systems and services.

**Where this level sits.** The security architect role has three levels:

1. Security architect
2. **Lead security architect** — this level
3. Principal security architect

The step down from principal security architect is scope: principal works
across several projects or technologies at organisational level and sets
long-term strategy; lead works on one project or technology at a time. The
step up from security architect below is depth: lead develops the patterns
that security architect only applies, and reaches senior stakeholders across
departments rather than within a team.

**Accountabilities at this level.** The framework expects a lead security
architect to:

- interact with senior stakeholders across departments
- reach and influence a wide range of people across larger teams and
  communities
- research and apply innovative security architecture solutions to new or
  existing problems and be able to justify and communicate design decisions
- develop vision, principles and strategy for security architects for one
  project or technology
- work out subtle security needs
- understand the impact of decisions, balancing requirements and deciding
  between approaches
- produce particular patterns and support quality assurance
- be the point of escalation for architects in lower grade roles
- lead the technical design of systems and services

**The skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Analysis
2. Communication (security architect)
3. Designing secure systems
4. Enabling and informing risk-based decisions
5. Research and innovation
6. Security technology
7. Understanding security implications of transformation

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

Monitor the analysis of a technical solution and ensure analysis is reused
for similar problem sets. Review solutions and identify areas for change.
Drive the collection of information that is used and analysed. Feed back on
policy and requirements.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The shift from "apply" at security architect to
"monitor" and "drive" here means the person is expected to run analysis
across a piece of work unsupported, and to improve how others analyse —
squarely Advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not overseen analysis carried out by someone else.
- 1 — Beginner: Reviews someone else's analysis when asked, but does not yet
  drive the collection of information it depends on.
- 2 — Intermediate: Drives information-gathering for a piece of analysis with
  support, and can review a solution to find obvious gaps.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently monitors analysis across a project, spots where
  reused analysis would save duplicated effort, reviews solutions to find
  the areas that genuinely need to change, and feeds findings back into
  policy or requirements.
- 4 — Expert: Sets how analysis is structured and reused across an
  organisation's security architecture practice.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe a time you noticed analysis
  done for one project could be reused for another. What did you do?"
- A document review: a review report or feedback the candidate gave on
  someone else's technical solution.
- A reference question: "Has this person's feedback on policy or
  requirements changed either of them?"

### Skill: Communication (security architect)

**What the framework expects at this level**

Demonstrate a deep understanding of security concepts and can apply them to a
technical level. Effectively translate and accurately communicate security
and risk implications to technical and non-technical stakeholders.
Successfully respond to challenges. Manage stakeholder expectations and be
flexible, adapting to stakeholder reactions to reach consensus.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework repeats the same skill wording as
security architect, but this level's accountabilities put that skill to work
with senior stakeholders across departments and under escalation, which is a
materially higher bar for the same words — a candidate meeting only the
Intermediate reading of this text is not ready for lead.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not communicated a security position to a senior stakeholder
  outside their own team.
- 1 — Beginner: Communicates well with familiar stakeholders but has not yet
  been tested with a senior or unfamiliar audience.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains security and risk to senior stakeholders with
  support available, and holds their ground under mild challenge.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently reaches consensus with senior, resistant
  stakeholders across departments, adapting the approach to the audience, and
  is the person other architects bring in when a conversation has stalled.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches other architects in stakeholder communication and is
  trusted with the organisation's hardest security conversations.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe reaching agreement with a
  senior stakeholder who initially disagreed with your security position.
  What changed their mind, and what did not?"
- A reference question from a senior stakeholder the candidate has worked
  with: "How did they handle disagreement?"
- Direct observation: where possible, observe the candidate present a
  security position to a mixed technical and non-technical audience.

### Skill: Designing secure systems

**What the framework expects at this level**

Design and review system architectures through the development of patterns
and principles.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's word changes from "application" at
security architect to "development" here — this level is expected to create
the patterns other architects apply, which is the definition of teaching or
guiding others in the skill.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not created a security pattern others have used.
- 1 — Beginner: Adapts an existing pattern for a new context, with review.
- 2 — Intermediate: Develops a variant of an existing pattern for a real
  system, with a senior architect confirming it before wider use.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently develops a new pattern or principle that
  another team has adopted, and supports quality assurance of designs built
  on it.
- 4 — Expert: Has developed patterns that other architects across the
  organisation reach for by default, and sets the precedent others design
  against.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: a security pattern, principle, or reference architecture
  the candidate authored, with evidence of it being adopted by someone else.
- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a pattern you developed.
  What problem did the existing patterns not solve?"
- A reference question: "Has this person's pattern or principle been reused
  by other teams?"

### Skill: Enabling and informing risk-based decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

Work with higher impact or more complex risks, advising on the impact and
whether it's within risk tolerance. Apply different risk methodologies in
proportion to the risk.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The move from "describe" methodologies at security
architect to "apply" them here, on higher-impact risk, is a clear step to
independent, Advanced practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not advised on a high-impact or complex risk decision.
- 1 — Beginner: Advises on straightforward risk decisions but needs support
  for complex or high-impact ones.
- 2 — Intermediate: Advises on moderately complex risk with support, applying
  a chosen methodology reasonably well.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently advises risk owners on high-impact or complex
  risk, applying different methodologies proportionately rather than
  defaulting to one.
- 4 — Expert: Is the point of escalation for the organisation's most complex
  or contested risk decisions.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe the most complex or high-impact
  risk decision you have advised on. Which methodology did you use, and
  why that one?"
- A reference question, put to a risk owner: "Did they correctly judge when
  a lighter-touch approach was proportionate, and when it was not?"
- A work sample: a written risk assessment or briefing the candidate produced
  for a genuinely complex situation.

### Skill: Research and innovation

**What the framework expects at this level**

Contribute to and inform developments on security properties in technology.
Identify new technologies and design the use of these in the business context
across the organisation. Engage with the broader security community.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Contribute to and inform" is a step beyond
"advising on" developments, and "across the organisation" widens the scope
from a single team — both point to independent, Advanced practice, short of
the organisation-wide authority principal security architect needs.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not engaged with the wider security community or contributed
  to a technology development beyond their immediate team.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows the broader security community's output but has not
  yet contributed to it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Designs the use of a new technology for more than one
  team with some support, and occasionally engages externally.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs the organisation-wide use of new
  technology, and actively engages with the broader security community —
  a working group, a conference, a shared advisory list.
- 4 — Expert: Is recognised outside their own organisation as contributing to
  how security properties of technology develop.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: a talk, publication, working-group contribution, or
  internal cross-organisation briefing the candidate produced.
- A structured interview question: "How have you engaged with the wider
  security community, and what has that changed about how you work?"
- A reference question: "Has this person's assessment of a new technology
  shaped a decision beyond their own team?"

### Skill: Security technology

**What the framework expects at this level**

Explain complex system architectures. Identify and explain how easy or
difficult it will be to exploit vulnerabilities. Lead and influence security
technology in the security industry.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Leading and influencing security technology "in the
security industry" — beyond the organisation — is explicitly a
teach-or-guide-others bar, and judging exploitability rather than only
effect is a materially harder technical skill than security architect
requires.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot reason about how exploitable a vulnerability is, only
  whether it exists.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains simple system architectures but needs support for
  complex ones, and defers to others on exploitability.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains complex system architectures with some support,
  and can give a rough view on exploitability that a more senior colleague
  checks.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently explains complex system architectures and
  judges exploitability accurately across more than one system type.
- 4 — Expert: Leads or influences security technology practice beyond their
  own organisation, and their judgement on exploitability is treated as
  authoritative.

**Evidence to gather**

- A work sample: present a complex, realistic (or redacted) system
  architecture and ask the candidate to explain it and assess exploitability
  of a named weakness.
- A portfolio item: evidence of influence in the security industry — a
  published assessment, a standards contribution, an invited talk.
- A reference question: "Do other technical staff defer to this person's
  judgement on how exploitable a weakness is?"

### Skill: Understanding security implications of transformation

**What the framework expects at this level**

Interpret and apply understanding across a complex area. Start influencing
policy and process, business architecture, and legal and political
implications.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's own word is "start" influencing —
this level is beginning to shape policy and process rather than only
applying an understanding of it, which is a step up from security architect's
"assist" but not yet the independent influence principal security architect
exercises.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot apply an understanding of policy or legal implications to
  a complex transformation.
- 1 — Beginner: Applies this understanding to straightforward situations, and
  needs support for complex ones.
- 2 — Intermediate: Interprets and applies this understanding across a
  complex transformation with some support, and has made at least one
  attempt to influence policy or process rather than only respond to it.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently interprets complex transformation implications
  and is starting to see that influence land — a process changed, a policy
  question raised and answered.
- 4 — Expert: Challenges and leads changes to policy and process where they
  affect security outcomes.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe a time you tried to influence a
  policy or process because of its security implications. What happened?"
- A document review: a briefing or business case the candidate wrote that
  addresses legal, political or business-architecture implications.
- A reference question: "Has this person changed a policy or process, or only
  worked within it?"
