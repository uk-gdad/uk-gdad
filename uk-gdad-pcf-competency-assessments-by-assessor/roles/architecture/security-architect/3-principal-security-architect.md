# Principal Security Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the assessing — a line manager, a
hiring panel member, or a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or employee
possesses the baseline abilities the principal security architect role level
requires. A principal security architect works on services of high complexity
and risk, sets strategy across the breadth of the organisation, and is a
recognised expert who demonstrates that expertise by solving unprecedented
problems. This is the top of the security architect ladder — there is no
level above it to defer to.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then complete
the overall determination. This assessment is one input among several into a
hiring, promotion or development decision, never the whole of it — see
`## Advisory`.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before scoring, so you are
assessing against this level's own accountabilities rather than lead security
architect. At this level, evidence should show a track record across
projects or technologies, not a single strong example — read
`## About this role level` for what distinguishes principal from lead.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess — this matters
more, not less, for a candidate whose seniority makes it tempting to assume
competence.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, and
where possible have a second assessor score independently before you compare
notes — this matters most for a hiring or promotion decision, and materially
reduces the risk of an unlawful adverse impact from an unvalidated,
AI-drafted instrument used inconsistently.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence — see
[Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification.

**Handling security material.** This role level sets security strategy and
recommends design across an organisation or beyond it, and reasons about
exploitability at the highest levels of risk complexity. When you record
evidence in `## Assessor notes and evidence log`, keep it at a level you
would be comfortable discussing outside a secure room: describe what the
person did, not the live vulnerability, the exploit technique, or any detail
that would help an attacker. Never record material above the classification
this document will be handled and stored at.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

A worked example, using **Designing secure systems**:

- A candidate rated **3 — Advanced** independently develops new patterns that
  another team has adopted, but has not yet set precedents that shape design
  across the organisation.
- A candidate rated **4 — Expert** has created precedents and set direction
  for security design across multiple projects or technologies, and other
  architects — including lead security architects — design against the
  standard they set.

## About this role level

**The role: security architect.** A security architect designs and builds
secure solutions.

**This level: principal security architect.** A principal security architect
works on services of high complexity and risk, making decisions to enable the
business to achieve its needs. This level has strategic reach across the
organisation, deep and evolving technical expertise, and is a recognised
expert who solves unprecedented problems.

**Where this level sits.** The security architect role has three levels:

1. Security architect
2. Lead security architect
3. **Principal security architect** — this level, the top of the ladder

The step up from lead security architect is scope and horizon: lead works on
one project or technology and produces particular patterns; principal
recommends design across several projects or technologies at organisational
or inter-organisational level, and sets strategy for the long term. Two
skills — Research and innovation and Security technology — carry identical
framework wording at lead and principal; the difference at this level shows
up in the accountabilities around them, not in new bullet points, and an
assessor should weigh that when scoring those two skills.

**Accountabilities at this level.** The framework expects a principal
security architect to:

- work on projects with high strategic impact, setting a strategy that can be
  used in the long term and across the breadth of the organisation
- communicate with a broad range of senior stakeholders and be responsible
  for defining the vision, principles and strategy for security architects
- recommend security design across several projects or technologies, up to
  an organisational or inter-organisational level
- have a deep and evolving level of technical expertise, so you can act as an
  exemplar
- make and influence important business and architectural decisions
- research, identify, validate and adopt new technologies and methodologies
- be a recognised expert and demonstrate this expertise by solving
  unprecedented issues and problems
- further the profession, demonstrating and sharing best practice within and
  outside the organisation

**The skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Analysis
2. Communication (security architect)
3. Designing secure systems
4. Enabling and informing risk-based decisions
5. Research and innovation
6. Security technology
7. Understanding security implications of transformation

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

Provide direction and lead on change regarding factors that feed into
analysis. Monitor changes in the technical environment and assess whether
risks are still at acceptable levels or whether previous decisions need to be
revisited. Direct and influence others on best practice and policy.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Directing and influencing others' best practice and
policy, and deciding when a past risk decision needs revisiting across a
changing technical environment, is by definition teaching and guiding others
in the skill.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not directed a change to how analysis is done beyond their
  own work.
- 1 — Beginner: Reviews analysis produced by others but does not yet direct
  changes to how it is done.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes to changing an analytical approach with
  support, and monitors part of the technical environment for risk drift.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently monitors the technical environment and
  revisits past risk decisions when circumstances change, across a defined
  area.
- 4 — Expert: Sets and directs analytical best practice and policy across the
  organisation, and other architects change their approach because of it.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe a past risk decision you
  revisited because the technical environment changed. What triggered the
  review, and what changed?"
- A portfolio item: a policy, standard, or best-practice document the
  candidate authored or substantially shaped.
- A reference question: "Has this person changed how your organisation
  approaches security analysis, beyond their own project?"

### Skill: Communication (security architect)

**What the framework expects at this level**

Demonstrate expert understanding of security concepts and apply them to a
technical level, at the highest levels of risk complexity. Effectively
translate and accurately communicate security and risk implications at the
most senior levels across technical and non-technical stakeholders.
Successfully respond to challenges. Manage stakeholder expectations across
high risk and complexity or under constrained timescales.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's wording is explicit here: "expert
understanding," "the most senior levels," "the highest levels of risk
complexity" — this is the ceiling of the scale by the framework's own words,
not by inference.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not communicated a security position at the most senior
  levels of an organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Communicates well at team and department level, but has not
  been tested at board or equivalent level.
- 2 — Intermediate: Communicates with senior stakeholders on moderately
  complex risk, with support for the highest-stakes conversations.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently communicates complex risk to senior
  stakeholders, but has not yet held the line under the most constrained
  timescales or the highest complexity.
- 4 — Expert: Manages stakeholder expectations at the most senior levels
  under high risk, complexity, and constrained timescales, and is the person
  the organisation turns to for the hardest of these conversations.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe communicating a high-risk
  security position to the most senior stakeholders you have worked with,
  under a tight deadline. What happened?"
- A reference question, ideally from a senior stakeholder: "Did this person's
  communication hold up under pressure and challenge?"
- Direct observation, where possible: the candidate presenting to a senior or
  simulated senior audience.

### Skill: Designing secure systems

**What the framework expects at this level**

Lead design and review solutions to complex problems with system
architectures by defining and challenging patterns and principles. Create
precedents and set direction.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Creating precedents and setting direction is, by
definition, guiding others through the skill rather than practising it
independently.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not led the design of a solution to a complex, high-risk
  problem.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to complex design work under someone else's
  direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads design for a moderately complex problem with
  support, and can challenge an existing pattern when it does not fit.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently leads design for complex, high-risk problems,
  and has defined a pattern others have questioned and defended.
- 4 — Expert: Has created precedents that set the direction for how security
  is designed across the organisation or beyond it.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: a precedent-setting design, standard, or architectural
  decision the candidate led, with evidence of adoption beyond their own
  project.
- A structured interview question: "Describe a time you challenged an
  existing pattern or principle rather than working within it. What
  happened?"
- A reference question: "Where has this person's design work set a
  precedent others now follow?"

### Skill: Enabling and informing risk-based decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

Act as a point of escalation. Be trusted by senior risk owners as an expert
in security. Apply risk methodologies at the most complex levels of risk.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Being the named point of escalation and being trusted
by senior risk owners as an expert is the definition of Expert on this scale
— there is nobody more senior to escalate this skill to.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not been the point of escalation for a risk decision.
- 1 — Beginner: Advises on complex risk with regular support from someone
  more senior.
- 2 — Intermediate: Handles complex risk decisions with occasional support,
  and is trusted by some risk owners.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently advises on the most complex risk decisions
  they encounter, though escalation for the very hardest cases still goes
  elsewhere.
- 4 — Expert: Is the named point of escalation for the organisation's most
  complex risk decisions, and senior risk owners seek them out directly.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe being the point of escalation
  for a risk decision nobody else could resolve. What made it hard, and how
  did you resolve it?"
- A reference question, put to a senior risk owner: "Do you treat this
  person as the final word on security risk in their area?"
- A document review: a risk assessment or escalation record for a genuinely
  complex, high-stakes decision the candidate led.

### Skill: Research and innovation

**What the framework expects at this level**

Contribute to and inform developments on security properties in technology.
Identify new technologies and design the use of these in the business context
across the organisation. Engage with the broader security community.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework repeats lead security architect's
wording for this skill word for word — record this rather than smoothing it
into a higher number. What changes at principal is scope, carried elsewhere
in this level's accountabilities ("further the profession... within and
outside the organisation"), so an assessor should look for organisation-wide
or cross-organisation influence as the evidence that separates a principal's
Advanced from a lead's, while recognising the framework itself does not
mandate Expert for this specific skill's text.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not engaged with the broader security community or
  contributed to a technology development.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows developments in the field but has not yet
  contributed to them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Designs the use of new technology across parts of the
  organisation, with some support, and engages occasionally externally.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs the organisation-wide use of new
  technology, and actively and regularly engages with the broader security
  community.
- 4 — Expert: Is recognised outside the organisation as shaping how security
  properties of technology develop — a standards body, a cross-government
  forum, an industry role.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: publications, standards contributions, or cross-
  organisation briefings the candidate produced.
- A structured interview question: "Where has your engagement with the
  wider security community changed a decision inside your organisation?"
- A reference question: "Does this person's research shape decisions beyond
  their immediate team?"

### Skill: Security technology

**What the framework expects at this level**

Explain complex system architectures. Identify and explain how easy or
difficult it will be to exploit vulnerabilities. Lead and influence security
technology in the security industry.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This wording is identical to lead security architect's,
and it already sits at the ceiling of the scale there — leading and
influencing security technology in the industry is an Expert-level bar
wherever it appears, and principal is expected to sustain it, not merely
repeat it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot judge exploitability of a vulnerability against a complex
  architecture.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains architectures and judges exploitability with
  regular support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains complex architectures independently, but
  exploitability judgements are checked by someone else.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently explains complex architectures and judges
  exploitability accurately, without external industry influence yet.
- 4 — Expert: Leads or influences security technology practice in the
  security industry, and their exploitability judgement is treated as
  authoritative across the organisation and beyond.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: evidence of industry influence — a published assessment,
  a standards contribution, an advisory board role, an invited talk.
- A work sample: present a complex, realistic (or redacted) architecture with
  a named weakness and ask for an exploitability judgement.
- A reference question: "Do people outside this person's own organisation
  seek out their judgement on security technology?"

### Skill: Understanding security implications of transformation

**What the framework expects at this level**

Challenge and lead changes to policy and processes to support business
outcomes, business architecture, and legal and political implications.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Lead changes to policy" is an explicit step beyond
lead security architect's "start influencing" — this level owns the outcome,
not just the attempt.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not led a change to policy or process arising from security
  implications.
- 1 — Beginner: Raises concerns about policy or process but has not led a
  change to either.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes to a policy or process change with support
  from someone more senior.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently challenges a policy or process and secures a
  change, though not yet at organisational scale.
- 4 — Expert: Leads changes to policy and process at organisational or
  inter-organisational scale, balancing legal, political and business-
  architecture implications.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe leading a change to policy or
  process because of its security implications. What resistance did you
  meet, and how did you resolve it?"
- A portfolio item: a policy or process document the candidate authored or
  materially changed.
- A reference question: "Has this person changed how the organisation, or a
  wider group of organisations, handles a policy or process?"

quickly after appointment, and record the reasoning in the evidence log
rather than relying on the number alone. Research and innovation carries a
lower baseline because the framework repeats lead security architect's
wording for it; do not treat a 3 there as a shortfall on its own.
