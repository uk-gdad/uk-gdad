# Senior Enterprise Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an existing employee has the
baseline abilities required for the senior enterprise architect role level in
the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's architecture profession. Use it
for a hiring decision, a promotion or transfer into the role, onboarding a new
starter, or a periodic capability check against the framework. It measures one
person against the framework's own description of this role level; it is one
input among several into a decision, never the whole of it — see the Advisory
section below for why.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

Because this instrument is unvalidated, using it inconsistently between
candidates — different questions, different evidence standards, a single
assessor working alone on a hiring or promotion decision — increases the risk
of unlawful adverse impact against candidates who share a protected
characteristic. Consistent, evidence-based administration, ideally by more
than one assessor, materially reduces that risk.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` in full before you score
anything, so you know what this specific level expects rather than a general
impression of "architect". Gather whatever evidence exists ahead of time: a
CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task, direct
observation of the person's day-to-day work, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating in the competency matrix
must trace to something the person did, said, produced or was observed doing.
If you cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess — a guess dressed up as a score is worse than an honest gap.

**Consistency.** Use the same method and the same evidence standard for every
candidate assessed against this role level. Where possible, especially for a
hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence. See the Advisory section above for why an
unvalidated instrument used inconsistently carries real legal risk, and treat
that risk as a reason to slow down and document your reasoning, not as small
print.

Enterprise architecture work regularly touches commercially sensitive
supplier information and, in some organisations, security-classified
material. Record evidence in general, job-relevant terms in the notes below —
never transcribe classified detail, live vulnerability information or
commercially confidential figures into this document.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale. It measures how well the person does the thing, on the
evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Strategy design.** A person rated 2 (Intermediate) can
challenge a requirement or assumption in a strategy discussion and support
implementation through a roadmap or plan, but does not yet own defining the
strategy itself. A person rated 3 (Advanced) — the baseline for this role
level — defines strategies or visions across teams that align with
organisational objectives, directs their implementation through roadmaps or
plans, defines architectural principles and patterns, and develops or
maintains the strategy in response to feedback, exactly as the framework
describes this level. A person rated 4 (Expert) does all of that across the
organisation or wider government, and is sought out to set the standard other
architects follow.

## About this role level

Enterprise architects are leaders working across different levels within an
organisation to translate business strategy into business change and
technical delivery. In the role generally, an enterprise architect identifies
priorities for change, owns the enterprise architecture vision, strategy and
roadmaps across business, technology and data, takes a strategic view across
architectural domains, portfolios and programmes, establishes architectural
principles, policies and standards, and develops the wider architecture
community.

This is the second of the role's four levels: enterprise architect, **senior
enterprise architect** (this level), lead enterprise architect, and principal
enterprise architect.

At this level, a senior enterprise architect supports lead architects in
ensuring the strategy is developed, agreed and followed. The framework
describes this level as networking and communicating with stakeholders across
domains or enterprises, identifying opportunities for improvement, supporting
a community or team, using best practice and emerging technologies and
approaches, playing an active part in the team with an understanding of how
to deliver its objectives to meet organisational goals, identifying influences
or risks — no longer only learning to — and playing a part in the successful
delivery of the long-term strategy rather than only the team's own objectives.

This level names eight skills: Architect for the whole context, Architecture
communication, Commercial perspective, Community collaboration, Enterprise
architecture, Making architectural decisions, Problem definition and shaping,
and Strategy design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What the framework expects at this level**

Support wider organisational objectives beyond your own immediate goals,
track emerging internal and external issues over time that could affect the
work of teams across the organisation, and take action — usually by
influencing colleagues — to solve or mitigate a problem that surfaces from
that tracking.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The skill's own wording is unchanged from the level
below, but the level's accountability has moved from "learn to identify
influences or risks" to identifying them outright, so this level is expected
to exercise the skill independently rather than under guidance.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Works only within their own immediate task or project and has not
  considered how it connects to wider organisational goals.
- 1 — Beginner: Notices an emerging issue only when a more senior colleague
  points it out.
- 2 — Intermediate: Notices an emerging issue that could affect other teams
  and raises it with a more senior architect, but relies on others to decide
  what to do about it.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently tracks internal and external issues over time,
  connects them to teams beyond their own, and influences colleagues to
  mitigate a problem without being asked.
- 4 — Expert: Is sought out by other teams for a view on emerging issues, and
  has changed how the organisation approaches a cross-cutting problem through
  their own influence.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an issue they tracked over time and how they
  used that tracking to influence a decision beyond their own team.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate acting on an emerging risk
  without being prompted.
- Review a document that shows the candidate connecting a wider trend to a
  specific piece of work.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

Lead the communication of complicated, complex or risky architecture topics
to technical and non-technical stakeholders, communicate with senior
stakeholders across the organisation, adapt your message and technique to
your audience, advocate on behalf of a team, and manage stakeholder
expectations effectively.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. A senior enterprise architect supporting lead
architects on strategy is expected to lead this kind of communication
themselves, not to be building towards it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Communicates only within their immediate team and has not
  presented architecture material to a non-technical audience.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a simple architecture topic to a non-technical
  stakeholder with support from a more senior colleague preparing the
  material.
- 2 — Intermediate: Adapts a message for a specific audience unaided for
  topics of moderate complexity.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads communication of a complicated or risky architecture
  topic to senior stakeholders, manages their expectations proactively, and
  advocates for their team's position.
- 4 — Expert: Is trusted to communicate the organisation's most contested or
  highest-risk architecture topics, and coaches others in how to do so.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of the candidate leading a briefing on a complicated or
  contested architecture topic, and how they handled disagreement.
- Observe the candidate presenting an architecture topic as part of a
  structured interview task, with a mix of technical and non-technical
  panellists.
- Ask a referee how the candidate manages a senior stakeholder's expectations
  on a topic with high delivery risk.

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What the framework expects at this level**

Understand commercial processes and the appropriate internal contacts within
a government department, and understand different sourcing strategies and
when to apply them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The skill's wording is unchanged from the level
below and remains a supporting capability, but a senior enterprise architect
supporting the team's long-term strategy is expected to apply this
understanding with less guidance than an entry-level colleague.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has no working knowledge of how commercial or sourcing decisions
  are made in a government department.
- 1 — Beginner: Knows who to approach internally for commercial advice and
  can describe, in general terms, more than one sourcing strategy.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains when a particular sourcing strategy would suit a
  given piece of work, with occasional guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises a project or programme on an appropriate sourcing
  approach without needing to check with a more senior colleague.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes an organisation's approach to sourcing strategy and is
  consulted by commercial colleagues on its architectural implications.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a sourcing decision they informed, and what
  drove their recommendation.
- Ask a structured interview question about which sourcing strategy suits a
  hypothetical piece of work relevant to the hiring organisation, and why.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level**

Work collaboratively in a group, actively networking with others, adapt
feedback so it is effective and lasting, use your own subject to identify
problems or issues in the team dynamic and rectify them, and identify issues
through Agile "health checks" with the team, helping to stimulate the right
response.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This wording is more active than the entry level's —
"rectify", not just "recognise" — matching a senior architect who supports a
community or team directly.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Works alone and has not contributed to another team's output.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to another team's work when asked, but does not
  yet notice team dynamic issues unprompted.
- 2 — Intermediate: Actively networks with others and names an issue in a
  team's dynamic when it arises, but relies on someone else to act on it.
- 3 — Advanced: Adapts feedback so it lands well and lasts, identifies an
  issue through an Agile health check or similar signal, and helps rectify
  it.
- 4 — Expert: Is sought out to advise on team composition and dynamics across
  the wider architecture community.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a team dynamic issue they identified and
  helped rectify, and what signal alerted them to it.
- Ask a referee for an example of feedback the candidate gave that visibly
  changed how a team worked afterwards.

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner

### Skill: Enterprise architecture

**What the framework expects at this level**

Design an architecture that defines the technology, people, process and other
elements needed to achieve organisational objectives, and work with others
across the organisation to ensure that design is used to achieve those
objectives.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The "with support" qualifier from the level below has
been dropped: this level designs the architecture itself, not just
contributes to one.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot design an architecture that connects technology, people
  and process to an organisational objective.
- 1 — Beginner: Designs a narrow part of an architecture with close direction
  from a senior architect.
- 2 — Intermediate: Designs a straightforward architecture element with some
  guidance, and needs support to connect it to the wider organisation.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs an architecture defining technology,
  people and process elements, and works with others across the organisation
  to see it used to achieve its objectives.
- 4 — Expert: Owns the design and adoption of enterprise architecture across
  multiple objectives at once, and is asked to review others' designs for
  organisational fit.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a work sample: an architecture the candidate designed, including how
  it connects technology, people and process.
- Ask the candidate to describe how they worked with colleagues elsewhere in
  the organisation to get a design actually used.
- Ask a referee whether a design the candidate produced achieved its intended
  objective.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

Make and guide architectural design decisions characterised by medium risk
and complexity, identify and address architectural risks that affect
multiple teams or domains, use architectural governance and assurance to make
design decisions and manage technical risks at the appropriate level, and
contribute to the development of that governance and assurance.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The scope has widened from a single team or domain to
multiple, and the verb has shifted from "work with others to make" to "make
and guide" — this level leads decisions rather than contributing to them.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not taken part in an architectural decision-making process.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to a decision made mainly by others.
- 2 — Intermediate: Makes a decision of managed risk affecting their own team
  or domain, with governance support.
- 3 — Advanced: Makes and guides architectural decisions of medium risk and
  complexity, identifies risks affecting multiple teams or domains, and uses
  governance and assurance to manage them.
- 4 — Expert: Is trusted to make decisions in situations of significant
  ambiguity across the organisation, and shapes how governance and assurance
  themselves work.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a decision the candidate made that affected more than
  one team or domain, and the risk it addressed.
- Ask how the candidate has contributed to developing an architectural
  governance or assurance process, rather than only following one.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What the framework expects at this level**

Frame a problem characterised by medium complexity, complication or risk so
that a solution can be created, produce architectural representations that
give different teams a shared understanding of the problem throughout its
life cycle, and describe options for solving problems so that appropriate
delivery methods can be decided.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The verb "help" has dropped from the level below —
this level frames the problem and produces the representations, not merely
contributes to them.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Takes a problem statement as given and does not question or
  reframe it.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to framing a simple problem with close support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Frames a problem of managed complexity with some
  guidance, and contributes to shared representations others have started.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently frames a problem of medium complexity,
  produces representations that give different teams a shared understanding
  of it, and describes options so delivery methods can be decided.
- 4 — Expert: Reframes problems that others have defined poorly, and is
  sought out to shape the organisation's most ambiguous problems.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a work sample: a problem-framing document or architectural
  representation the candidate produced.
- Ask the candidate to describe a time a shared representation they produced
  changed how different teams understood a problem.

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Strategy design

**What the framework expects at this level**

Define strategies or visions across teams that align with organisational
objectives, direct the implementation of a strategy or vision — for example,
by creating roadmaps or plans — define architectural principles and patterns,
and develop or maintain the strategy in response to feedback and findings.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This level defines and directs strategy rather than
supporting someone else's, a clear step up from the entry-level wording.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not contributed to defining a strategy or vision.
- 1 — Beginner: Supports someone else's strategy work with a small,
  well-defined contribution.
- 2 — Intermediate: Challenges an assumption in a strategy discussion and
  supports its implementation, but does not yet own defining it.
- 3 — Advanced: Defines a strategy or vision across teams, directs its
  implementation through a roadmap or plan, and develops the strategy further
  in response to feedback.
- 4 — Expert: Defines and connects strategies across the organisation or
  wider government, and is sought out to set the standard other architects
  follow.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a strategy, vision or roadmap document the candidate authored or
  co-authored.
- Ask the candidate how they revised a strategy in response to feedback or
  new findings, and what changed.
