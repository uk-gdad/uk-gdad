# Lead Network Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, an interview panel
member, or anyone else evaluating a candidate or an employee — who needs to
judge whether a person has the baseline abilities the Architecture
profession's lead network architect role level requires. It measures
demonstrated skill against the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own
description of this level, for a hiring decision, a promotion or progression
board, onboarding a new starter, or a periodic capability check. It is one
input into that decision, never the whole of it — see `## Advisory` before
you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what the
framework actually expects before you look at any evidence. Then gather what
exists for this person: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work
sample or task, direct observation of them at work, or references from people
who have worked with them. A lead network architect is accountable for a
business area, is a point of escalation, and represents network architects
internally and externally — look for evidence at that scale: a track record
of decisions made under pressure, influence on governance forums, and how
they have coached others, not only a single technical artefact.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess — an honest gap
teaches you more than a guess dressed up as a score.

**Stay consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
using the same evidence sources wherever you can. For a hiring or promotion
decision, have more than one assessor score independently, then compare
notes, rather than settling on a single view first.

**Fairness.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact even when nobody intends
it. Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

**Handling sensitive material.** A lead network architect is likely to have
been exposed to genuinely sensitive infrastructure and security detail,
including material that is classified or commercially confidential. When you
record evidence in `## Assessor notes and evidence log`, describe what the
person did and how they reasoned about it — never the classified material,
live vulnerability detail or specific security configuration itself.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale — how well the person does the thing, on the evidence
available, not how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example.** Take Skill: Governance and assurance. At 3 — Advanced, a
candidate runs governance and assurance processes for their own area
competently and improves them when they find a gap. At 4 — Expert, the
baseline for this level, they ensure governance and assurance across a
business area is proportionate and continuously improved, aligned to
organisational and government needs; they guide whole teams through
governance processes such as service assessments; and they have built enough
trust in how their team's governance works that stakeholders rely on it
without re-checking every decision. The difference is scale and trust, not
just technical accuracy — look for evidence the person has shaped governance
beyond their own immediate work. Calibrate against evidence like this before
you start scoring.

## About this role level

Architecture role: Network architect. A network architect is responsible for
network designs and specifications, including cloud networks.

The role has three levels, in order: associate network architect, network
architect, and lead network architect. This assessment covers the third and
most senior of those.

Role level: Lead network architect. A lead network architect is accountable
for designs and networks representing their business area.

At this role level, the framework expects someone to:

- be a point of escalation
- actively input into governance forums that determine IT strategy
- cascade messages, standards and best practice from other architecture teams
  and design authorities to the network architecture team
- act as a subject matter expert for network architects
- represent network architects internally and externally
- have an understanding of all network technology including LAN (local area
  network), WAN (wide area network), wifi, data centre LAN, SD
  (software-defined) networks, telephony, mobile solutions infrastructure,
  unified comms, network management solutions, and network-specific security
  and remote access technologies and approaches
- champion new technologies and solutions
- coach and mentor network architects and associate network architects
- be a strategic thinker who can communicate network concepts to technical
  and non-technical stakeholders

The framework names five skills for this level: Governance and assurance, IT
infrastructure, Systems design, Systems integration, and Troubleshooting and
problem resolution.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead network architect ensures appropriate and proportionate governance and
assurance is in place and continuously improved, aligned to organisational
and government needs; guides teams through governance and assurance
processes, such as service assessments, to ensure standards are met; and
shares the team's governance and assurance processes so stakeholders trust
them to deliver value in a sustainable way.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The scope has grown from developing and improving one's
own processes at network architect level to ensuring governance across a
business area, guiding whole teams, and building stakeholder trust in it —
mastery exercised at a scale that teaches and guides others, the definition
of Expert.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe what proportionate governance looks like for a
  business area.
- 1 — Beginner: follows governance processes someone else has set, with
  direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: applies governance processes correctly across their own
  work without prompting.
- 3 — Advanced: develops and improves governance processes for their own
  area, and supports individual colleagues through them.
- 4 — Expert: ensures governance across a business area is proportionate and
  kept current, guides whole teams through processes such as service
  assessments, and has built enough stakeholder trust that the team's
  governance is relied on rather than re-checked.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe how they have shaped governance or assurance
  for a business area, not just a single project, and what changed as a
  result.
- Ask about a service assessment or equivalent governance forum they guided a
  team through, including how they handled a disagreement in it.
- Ask a referee — ideally a stakeholder outside the immediate team — whether
  they trust the candidate's team's governance without checking every
  decision, and why.

### Skill: IT infrastructure

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead network architect leads teams and departments in the design,
implementation, administration and support of infrastructure solutions and
services.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's verb changes from designing and
implementing infrastructure, at network architect level, to leading teams and
departments in doing so — that is guiding others' mastery, not only
exercising your own, which is Expert by definition.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not led others in infrastructure work.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes to infrastructure work under someone else's lead.
- 2 — Intermediate: leads a small piece of infrastructure work with close
  support from a more senior colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: independently designs and implements infrastructure and
  occasionally directs a colleague's work on it.
- 4 — Expert: leads a team, or more than one team, through the design,
  implementation, administration and support of infrastructure, and is
  accountable for how it performs.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a piece of infrastructure work they led
  across a team or department, including how they divided the work and
  handled a setback.
- Ask a referee who reported to or worked under the candidate's lead on an
  infrastructure programme about how that leadership showed up day to day.
- Review a document showing the candidate's accountability for an
  infrastructure programme — a plan, a status report, a lessons-learned note.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead network architect designs systems characterised by high levels of
risk, impact, and business or technical complexity; controls system design
practice within an enterprise or industry architecture; influences
industry-based models for the development of new technology applications;
develops effective implementation and procurement strategies consistent with
business needs; and ensures adherence to relevant technical strategies,
policies, standards and practices.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This is the widest gap between adjacent levels in the
whole matrix — from reviewing colleagues' medium-risk designs at network
architect level to controlling design practice across an enterprise and
influencing industry-wide models — squarely Expert, the point where mastery
shapes practice beyond the person's own work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe how design practice is controlled across an
  enterprise or industry.
- 1 — Beginner: applies design standards someone else has set, for
  medium-complexity work.
- 2 — Intermediate: designs medium-risk systems competently and understands
  why enterprise-level design controls exist.
- 3 — Advanced: designs high-risk, high-complexity systems independently, but
  has not yet shaped design practice or procurement strategy beyond their own
  work.
- 4 — Expert: designs for high risk, impact and complexity; controls design
  practice within an enterprise or industry architecture; develops
  implementation and procurement strategies that suit the business; and
  ensures adherence to the relevant strategies, policies, standards and
  practices.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a high-risk or high-complexity system they
  designed, including how they managed the risk and who else's work their
  decisions constrained.
- Ask for an example of them influencing a design standard, an industry model
  or a procurement strategy beyond a single project.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate ensuring adherence to
  technical strategy or standards across more than one team.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead network architect defines the integration build, co-ordinates build
activities across systems, and understands how to undertake and support
integration testing activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework repeats this skill's wording verbatim
from the network architect level below — it does not ask for more here — so
the baseline stays at the same point the text supports rather than being
raised by habit. A lead is not expected to exceed this skill beyond what the
words say, even though their overall accountability is broader; expect the
same independent, define-and-coordinate capability as at network architect
level, exercised with the wider judgement this level's other skills require.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not taken responsibility for any part of an integration
  build.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes to one part of an integration build under
  direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: coordinates a small integration build with a senior
  colleague available to check progress.
- 3 — Advanced: defines an integration build and coordinates activities
  across systems independently, and supports the integration testing that
  follows, even where that means coordinating across teams the person does
  not directly manage.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted to define integration builds across complex,
  multi-system, multi-team programmes and to resolve disputes about how
  systems should connect.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an integration build they defined and
  coordinated across systems, particularly one involving teams outside their
  own.
- Review an integration plan or build schedule they produced or owned.
- Ask a referee about the candidate's role in a recent integration testing
  effort spanning more than one system.

### Skill: Troubleshooting and problem resolution

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead network architect breaks a problem down into its component parts to
identify and diagnose root causes, and troubleshoots and identifies problems
across different technology capabilities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. As with Systems integration, the framework repeats
this skill's wording verbatim from the level below, so the baseline holds at
the point the text itself supports. A lead's escalation-point accountability
means they will exercise this skill on harder, more visible problems, but the
framework does not ask for a different or higher standard of the skill itself.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe a structured approach to diagnosing a fault.
- 1 — Beginner: diagnoses a familiar fault type when guided through it.
- 2 — Intermediate: diagnoses faults in a familiar technology area
  unassisted, but needs help breaking down a fault that spans several
  capabilities.
- 3 — Advanced: independently breaks a cross-capability problem into its
  component parts, diagnoses the root cause, and resolves it or hands it on
  with a clear diagnosis — including when the problem has already defeated
  other people's attempts and has been escalated to them.
- 4 — Expert: is the final escalation point for the hardest faults across the
  business area, and teaches root-cause analysis technique to other network
  architects.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a problem escalated to the candidate after other
  people had already tried and failed to diagnose it, and how they approached
  it differently.
- Walk through a real or simulated multi-capability fault scenario and
  observe how the candidate decomposes it.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate being the final point of
  escalation for a difficult fault.

- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

The baseline for this role level is normally met when every skill above is

One or two skills below target is not automatically a fail. Weigh how central
the skill is to this level's accountabilities — Governance and assurance,
IT infrastructure and Systems design sit at Expert because they define what
"lead" means in this role, so a shortfall there deserves close attention;
whereas Systems integration or Troubleshooting sitting a point below Advanced
may be coachable quickly given a strong track record elsewhere. Record your
reasoning, not just the numbers: a panel or manager reading this later needs
to know why a below-target rating was, or was not, treated as a concern.
