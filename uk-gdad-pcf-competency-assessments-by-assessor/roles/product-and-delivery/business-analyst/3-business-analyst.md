# Business Analyst - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel, or a reviewer —
judging whether a candidate or employee meets the baseline abilities expected
of a **business analyst** in the product and delivery profession. It measures
demonstrated skill against the framework's own description of this role
level, for a hiring decision, a promotion from junior business analyst, or a
periodic capability check.

This is one input among several into that decision, never the whole of it. See
`## Advisory` below for why.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything.
This is the first level in the ladder where the framework expects independent
work, ownership of stakeholder relationships, and mentoring of others, so the
evidence you need is different in kind from a junior candidate's — look for a
track record, not a single supervised task.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. At this level, ask for
specific examples of projects led, decisions made, and stakeholders managed
directly — a candidate who can only describe what they would do, rather than
what they have done, has not yet demonstrated this level's independence. If
you cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way.
Where possible, have more than one assessor score independently before
comparing notes — this matters especially for a hiring or promotion decision.

**Stay fair.** See [Fair and lawful use](#fair-and-lawful-use) below. Never let
a rating be influenced by anything other than demonstrated, job-related
evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or
how they feel about it.

**Worked example — Stakeholder relationship management.** At 1, a candidate
can describe who their stakeholders are but has not managed a relationship
directly. At 3 — the baseline for this role level — a candidate has
independently identified important stakeholders on a project, communicated
with them clearly and regularly, tailored that communication to what each one
needed, and can describe a time they built or repaired consensus using
evidence. At 4, by contrast, a candidate directs stakeholder relationship
strategy across multiple teams and coaches other business analysts in
managing difficult relationships — evidence at that scale sits above this
role level and points towards a senior business analyst assessment instead.

## About this role level

The product and delivery profession's business analyst role exists to help
teams analyse and understand a business problem or opportunity: to research
how a business or business area works — its people, organisation, processes,
information, data and technology — identify areas for improvement, explore
feasible options, analyse the effects of change and define success measures.
Business analysts identify and elaborate user and business needs so that
services and business change can be effectively designed, developed and
tested; they make prioritisation and minimum viable product decisions using
analysis-led insights; they ensure new products and services meet business and
user needs and align with organisational goals; and they understand the
business and policy constraints a piece of work sits within, and assess the
implications.

The role has six levels: trainee business analyst, junior business analyst,
business analyst, senior business analyst, lead business analyst, and head of
business analysis. This assessment is for the third of those — the level the
role is named after, and the first at which the framework expects independent
delivery.

A **business analyst** leads small to medium size projects and supports
larger and more complex projects. At this role level, the framework says the
person will manage stakeholder relationships, work independently, have a good
understanding of their work area, and develop the business analysis community
by sharing best practice and mentoring others. This is a marked step up from
junior business analyst: ownership of stakeholder relationships replaces
support for someone else's, independent working replaces supervision, and
mentoring others appears for the first time.

The framework names nine skills for this level: Adapting to delivery
methodologies, Business modelling, Business process improvement, Context,
problem and option analysis, Defining and managing business needs, user needs
and requirements, Stakeholder relationship management, Digital and data
systems analysis, Testing (business analysis), and User experience analysis.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Adapting to delivery methodologies

**What the framework expects at this level**

A business analyst is expected to explain how products and services are
delivered through different methodologies, to follow an appropriate approach
to complete their work within the delivery methodology used by their team, to
adapt how they work and the work they produce based on their team's delivery
methodology to ensure they deliver value, and to adapt how they work with
other roles in a multidisciplinary team in line with the delivery methodology.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework now asks for adaptation of the
candidate's own working practice within the team's chosen methodology, which
is genuine applied skill, but stops short of setting or choosing the
methodology — that judgement belongs to a later level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how their work would differ under a different
  delivery methodology.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain the general differences between methodologies but
  has not adapted their own working practice to one.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has adjusted how they work — for example the size and
  pacing of their outputs — to fit their team's chosen methodology, and can
  give a concrete example.
- 3 — Advanced: Has adapted how they work with other roles in a
  multidisciplinary team, in line with the methodology, in more than one
  project context, and can explain the trade-offs involved.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level. Setting or evolving delivery
  methodology for others belongs to a senior business analyst or above.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe two projects run under different
  methodologies and how their own approach changed between them.
- Ask how they adjusted their working practice when a team's delivery
  methodology did not suit the way they had previously worked.
- Ask a referee how well the candidate fits into an established team's
  delivery rhythm without needing significant guidance.

### Skill: Business modelling

**What the framework expects at this level**

A business analyst is expected to model various elements of the business with
limited direction, and to understand the impact of potential changes and how
business processes, systems, data, roles and responsibilities interact.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "With limited direction" implies real independence
in producing a model, but the framework does not yet ask this level to lead
modelling across a project or programme, so the baseline sits below advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot produce a business model or explain what one is used for.
- 1 — Beginner: Can produce a simple model within a defined task, but needs
  close direction to do so.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has produced a business model with limited direction, and
  can explain how a proposed change would affect the process, system or roles
  it depicts.
- 3 — Advanced: Has independently modelled a moderately complex business
  situation and used it to inform a real decision or recommendation.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level. Leading the modelling of complex
  programmes across an organisation belongs to a senior business analyst or
  above.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask to see a business model, process diagram, or similar the candidate
  produced with limited direction, and ask them to walk through it.
- Ask a scenario: "if this step in the process changed, what else would be
  affected?" and see whether they can reason from the model.
- Ask a referee how much direction the candidate typically needs to produce a
  usable model.

### Skill: Business process improvement

**What the framework expects at this level**

A business analyst is expected to analyse and evaluate business processes to
identify problems and opportunities, to design, prioritise and facilitate the
implementation of business process improvements with some support, and to
validate improvements to business processes through testing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "With some support" signals real ownership of the
end-to-end improvement cycle, short of the fully independent leadership the
framework describes for senior levels.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never independently analysed or evaluated a business process.
- 1 — Beginner: Can identify a problem in a process when pointed at it, but
  has not led an improvement.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has analysed and evaluated a business process, proposed
  and prioritised an improvement, and helped facilitate its implementation
  with some support, then validated the result through testing.
- 3 — Advanced: Has done the above across more than one process improvement
  with minimal support, including handling resistance to the proposed change.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level. Leading large-scale improvement
  identification and setting direction for others belongs to a lead business
  analyst or above.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific example of a business process improvement the candidate
  drove from problem identification through to validated change.
- Ask what support they needed to get the improvement implemented, and from
  whom.
- Ask a referee to describe the candidate's role in a real process
  improvement effort.

### Skill: Context, problem and option analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

A business analyst is expected to plan and use the most appropriate analysis
techniques to understand the context, the problem and root causes, to
communicate how their work supports and aligns to strategic contexts and
goals, to define problem statements and develop and evaluate multiple options,
and to formulate recommendations, considering feasibility, benefits and
impact.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework now expects the candidate to plan
their own analysis approach and formulate recommendations independently —
real applied judgement, though not yet the level of influence over strategic
plans expected from a lead business analyst.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot define a problem statement or choose an analysis technique
  for it.
- 1 — Beginner: Can apply a named technique when told which one to use, but
  cannot yet select the right one independently.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has independently planned and used an appropriate
  technique to understand a problem, defined a problem statement, and
  formulated a recommendation weighing feasibility, benefits and impact.
- 3 — Advanced: Has done the above for a more complex, ambiguous problem, and
  can explain how the work connected to the organisation's strategic goals.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level. Advising on options analysis
  approach and guiding strategic decision making belongs to a lead business
  analyst or above.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a problem they analysed from first
  principles: how they chose their technique, what options they developed,
  and how they arrived at a recommendation.
- Ask how they would explain the value of a piece of analysis work to a senior
  stakeholder in terms of organisational goals.
- Ask a referee for an example of a recommendation the candidate made that was
  acted on.

### Skill: Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements

**What the framework expects at this level**

A business analyst is expected to identify, analyse, challenge and validate
business and user requirements, to work under limited supervision to
co-ordinate and review the prioritisation of requirements, and to use
appropriate requirements management life cycle methods to complete tasks and
outputs related to the project.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Challenge and validate" and "under limited
supervision" together describe someone who owns the requirements process for
their piece of work, including pushing back on requirements that do not hold
up — a strong grasp exercised with independence.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot identify or capture a requirement without close direction.
- 1 — Beginner: Can capture requirements accurately but does not yet challenge
  or validate them critically.
- 2 — Intermediate: Captures, analyses and validates requirements with some
  guidance, and can select an appropriate life cycle method with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies, analyses, challenges and validates
  requirements, co-ordinates their prioritisation with limited supervision,
  and applies the right life cycle method for the project.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level. Directing the approach to
  requirements management across a project or programme belongs to a senior
  business analyst or above.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a requirement the candidate challenged or rejected,
  and why.
- Ask how they decide which requirements management life cycle method to use
  for a given project.
- Ask a referee how much supervision the candidate needs to co-ordinate
  requirements prioritisation.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A business analyst is expected to identify important stakeholders and
communicate with them clearly and regularly, to tailor communication to
stakeholders' needs and work with them to build relationships while meeting
user needs, to build and reach consensus with stakeholders, and to work to
improve stakeholder relationships using evidence to explain decisions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The level description names owning stakeholder
relationships explicitly, and the skill's own wording — building consensus,
tailoring communication, using evidence to explain decisions — describes
confident, independent relationship management.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot identify stakeholders or explain why managing them matters.
- 1 — Beginner: Identifies stakeholders and has some direct but supported
  contact with them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Communicates with stakeholders directly and regularly, but
  needs support to handle disagreement or build consensus.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies stakeholders, tailors communication
  to their needs, builds and reaches consensus, and uses evidence to explain
  decisions when a stakeholder disagrees.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level. Directing stakeholder relationship
  strategy across teams belongs to a senior business analyst or above.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a stakeholder disagreement the candidate resolved, and
  how.
- Ask how they tailor communication differently for two contrasting
  stakeholders on the same piece of work.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate owns and manages stakeholder
  relationships without needing a manager to step in.

### Skill: Digital and data systems analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

A business analyst is expected to identify and analyse IT system
capabilities, and to develop models and system requirements for bespoke IT
systems or software packages, with minimal supervision.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "With minimal supervision" is real independence,
but the scope described — developing models and requirements for a system —
is narrower than the cross-system, cross-team work described at senior
levels.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot analyse an IT system's capabilities or describe a gap in
  it.
- 1 — Beginner: Can analyse a system's capabilities with guidance, but needs
  support to turn that into requirements.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently identifies and analyses IT system
  capabilities, and develops models and requirements for a bespoke system or
  package with minimal supervision.
- 3 — Advanced: Does the above for a more complex system, and can explain the
  trade-offs of different requirement or design choices to technical
  colleagues.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level. Setting direction for systems
  analysis across a programme belongs to a lead business analyst or above.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a system requirement they developed and how
  they arrived at it.
- Ask how they identify a gap between what a system does and what a business
  needs it to do.
- Ask a referee how much supervision the candidate needs for systems analysis
  work.

### Skill: Testing (business analysis)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A business analyst is expected to understand the stages and purpose of
testing and support the use of relevant tools and techniques, to identify
business scenarios and develop acceptance criteria to ensure requirements can
be traced to develop functionality, and to review prototypes, test plans and
test outcome reporting.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework now expects the candidate to
produce acceptance criteria and review testing artefacts themselves, a step
up from the supervised support expected of a junior business analyst.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe why acceptance criteria matter to a requirement.
- 1 — Beginner: Can write basic acceptance criteria with guidance.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently identifies business scenarios and develops
  acceptance criteria that trace requirements to functionality, and reviews
  test plans and outcomes.
- 3 — Advanced: Does the above for a more complex set of requirements, and can
  spot gaps in a test plan that would let a requirement go unverified.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask to see acceptance criteria the candidate has written, and how they trace
  to a requirement.
- A short task: give a requirement and ask the candidate to write acceptance
  criteria for it.
- Ask a referee how thorough the candidate's review of test plans and outcome
  reporting typically is.

### Skill: User experience analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

A business analyst is expected to apply basic techniques to analyse, validate
and prioritise user experience needs, and to present findings in an accessible
and easy to understand way to support data-informed decision making, based on
user research.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Apply basic techniques" independently, and
present findings clearly, describes solid working competence rather than
advisory-level expertise.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot analyse or prioritise a user experience need from user
  research.
- 1 — Beginner: Can identify a user experience need with guidance, but cannot
  yet prioritise it independently.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently applies a basic technique to analyse,
  validate and prioritise user experience needs, and presents findings clearly
  to both specialist and non-specialist audiences.
- 3 — Advanced: Does the above for a more complex or ambiguous set of needs,
  and can defend a prioritisation decision when challenged.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level. Recommending a range of techniques
  and advising on approach belongs to a senior business analyst or above.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of user research findings the candidate translated into
  a prioritised set of user experience needs.
- Ask how they present findings differently to a technical audience versus a
  non-technical one.
- Ask a referee whether stakeholders have found the candidate's presentations
  of user experience findings clear and useful.
