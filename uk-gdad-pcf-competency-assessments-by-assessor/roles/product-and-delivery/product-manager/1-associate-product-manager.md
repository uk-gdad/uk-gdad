# Product Manager (Associate Product Manager) - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a
reviewer — who needs to judge whether a candidate or an employee meets the
baseline abilities expected of an **Associate product manager** in the
**Product manager** role. Use it for a hiring decision, a promotion or
progression board, an onboarding check, or a periodic capability review
against the framework.

An associate product manager is an entry-level role — the framework describes
it explicitly as suited to civil servants moving into a digital role, or to
people on emerging talent schemes. This assessment measures demonstrated
skill against the framework's own description of that level. It is not a test
of potential or a prediction of future performance, and it does not compare
one candidate against another.

This is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of it. Read
`## Advisory` before you use any part of this document.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you are holding the same picture of the job as the matrix. Gather whatever
evidence exists ahead of the session: a CV or portfolio, a structured
interview, a work sample or task (for example, asking the candidate to sketch
a simple prioritisation of a small backlog), direct observation of the
candidate in a team setting, or references from someone who has worked with
them.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating you give must trace to
something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess — a gap in evidence is not the same as a gap in skill, and treating it
as one is how bias creeps in. This matters especially at entry level: someone
moving into a digital role from elsewhere in government, or coming from an
emerging talent scheme, may have transferable evidence that does not look
like a conventional product career, and it is still valid evidence.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this same role level the same
way, using the same evidence types wherever you can. Where possible, use more
than one assessor and score independently before comparing notes — this
matters most for hiring and promotion decisions, where the stakes for the
person being assessed are highest.

**Stay fair.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence — see `## Fair and lawful use` in this
project's specification. This instrument is unvalidated and AI-drafted: used
inconsistently, by a single assessor, without a shared evidence standard, it
can produce unlawful adverse impact against particular groups of candidates,
even unintentionally. Consistent, evidence-based administration, and having
more than one assessor score independently, materially reduces that risk. If
you are not confident you can administer it consistently, ask your
organisation's HR or occupational psychology colleagues for support before
you use it for a hiring or promotion decision.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
**proficiency** scale — it measures how well the person does the thing, on the
evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel about doing it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Applying user-centred insights.** At 0 (None), a person
cannot describe how they would find out what users need for a feature. At 1
(Beginner), they can sit in on user research someone else has organised, and
with support can say what one finding might mean for the feature they are
working on. At 2 (Intermediate), they help identify who to talk to for a
piece of research, and can weigh whether the team already has enough insight
to make a small decision, though a more experienced colleague checks their
judgement. At 3 (Advanced), they run this process independently across a
feature or small product, without needing anyone to check their judgement
first. Few or no associate product managers will show evidence at 3 or above;
that is expected at this entry level, not a concern in itself.

## About this role level

A **product manager** makes sure that their products provide value and
achieve the right outcomes by balancing user and business needs. In this
role, a product manager will:

- work with multidisciplinary teams, bringing together their views, user
  needs and business needs, to frame the problem
- work with multidisciplinary teams to provide the right outcomes for users
  and the organisation
- make decisions on priorities for their teams
- ensure teams comply with appropriate standards

The role has five levels, in order:

1. Associate product manager — **you are assessing this level**
2. Product manager
3. Senior product manager
4. Lead product manager
5. Head of product

An **associate product manager** is responsible for features, components, or
part of a product, contributing to the delivery of value. This is an
entry-level role for civil servants who are looking to move into a digital
role, or for those on emerging talent schemes. It is the first step on the
ladder, and the framework's own accountabilities for it are explicitly about
learning the discipline with support, not yet owning a whole product.

At this role level, an associate product manager will:

- work with product managers to advocate for user needs throughout the
  organisation, prioritising delivery of greatest value
- manage a smaller product with support, or a subset of features of a larger
  product
- learn product management approaches and methods

This level names nine skills, assessed in the competency matrix below:

- Agile and Lean practices
- Applying user-centred insights
- Creating value for money
- Life cycle management
- Managing product outcomes
- Product leadership
- Product management
- Stakeholder relationship management
- Strategic ownership

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile and Lean practices

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate product manager should demonstrate experience of applying Agile
principles in practice, provide a clear, open and transparent framework in
which teams can deliver, show an awareness of Agile tools and start to use
them intelligently, and visualise and make visible the work of the team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording — "show an awareness" and
"starting to use them intelligently" — describes someone who is still
learning to apply Agile tools with support, which is exactly what this entry
level expects.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what a backlog, a sprint, or a stand-up is for.
- 1 — Beginner: Takes part in the team's Agile ceremonies and, with support,
  can update a task board or backlog item correctly.
- 2 — Intermediate: Updates the team's visual tracking tools unprompted, and
  can explain to a new team member why the team works the way it does.
- 3 — Advanced: Adjusts how the team visualises and tracks its work when the
  current approach is not helping, without being asked to.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches other product people on applying Agile and Lean
  practices well.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through how they used a task board or backlog on
  a recent piece of work, and what they would change about it.
- Observe, or ask a referee about, the candidate taking part in a stand-up,
  sprint planning session, or retrospective.
- In a structured interview, ask what "making the work visible" means in
  practice, using an example from their own experience.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Applying user-centred insights

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate product manager should identify and engage users and
stakeholders to identify user needs and trends, decide when they need more
user insight and when they have enough to make a decision, work with others
to define the problem and research goals, and use quantitative and
qualitative user insights to improve product or service outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. This is a feature-level or supported role, and a
person newly learning to work with user insight typically needs a more
experienced colleague to sense-check when there is genuinely enough evidence
to act on.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not taken part in gathering or using user insight.
- 1 — Beginner: Sits in on user research sessions someone else has organised,
  and with support can say what one finding might mean for their feature.
- 2 — Intermediate: Helps identify who to research and drafts research goals
  with guidance, and can point to a piece of insight that shaped a decision.
- 3 — Advanced: Decides independently when a feature-level decision has
  enough user evidence behind it to proceed.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches others in the team on how to use user insight to
  improve outcomes.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a piece of user research or feedback they
  helped gather, and what it changed about the work.
- In a structured interview, present a small scenario with limited user
  evidence and ask whether they would proceed or gather more, and why.
- Review a research goal, discussion guide, or insight summary the candidate
  has contributed to.

### Skill: Creating value for money

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate product manager should clearly describe the value of their work,
determine or estimate the benefits their work is expected to deliver, and
identify opportunities for reuse, saving time and money.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. At feature or component level, describing value in
simple, concrete terms — rather than building a full benefits case — is a
reasonable starting point for someone learning the discipline.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain why the feature or task they are working on
  matters.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe, in plain terms and with support, why a piece of
  work is worth doing.
- 2 — Intermediate: Estimates the expected benefit of a small piece of work
  with some guidance, and spots an obvious opportunity for reuse.
- 3 — Advanced: Builds a value case for a feature or small product
  unsupported, and actively looks for reuse opportunities across the team.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches others in the team on how to describe and estimate
  value for their own work.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe, in plain language, why a recent piece of
  their work was worth doing.
- In a structured interview, ask for an example of reusing an existing tool,
  component, or piece of research instead of building something new.
- Review a short benefits statement, user story, or business case extract the
  candidate has contributed to.

### Skill: Life cycle management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate product manager should explain what each phase of the product or
service life cycle is for, and explain how the needs of the product or
service vary across phases of the life cycle, and how a team responds to
them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own bullets for this skill at this
level are entirely explanatory — describing the life cycle, not yet running a
phase of it — which matches close-supervision, knowledge-building work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name the phases of the product or service life cycle used
  by their team or organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Can name the phases and, with prompting, say roughly what
  happens in each one.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains unprompted why the team works differently in
  discovery than in live, using a real example from their own product.
- 3 — Advanced: Recognises unsupported when a feature or product is ready to
  move to the next phase, and can justify the call.
- 4 — Expert: Helps others in the team understand what changes as a product
  moves through its life cycle.

**Evidence to gather**

- In a structured interview, ask the candidate to explain, in their own
  words, why a team works differently during discovery than it does once a
  service is live.
- Ask the candidate to describe which phase a product or feature they worked
  on was in, and what that meant for how the team worked.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has correctly flagged that a piece of
  work was not yet ready to move phase.

### Skill: Managing product outcomes

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate product manager should explain the importance of identifying and
measuring product outcomes, explain the need for baseline data, and identify
and explain the priority outcomes for their product or feature.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. As with life cycle management, the framework's
bullets here describe explaining outcomes and baselines rather than setting
or measuring them independently, which fits a beginner still learning why
outcomes matter.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what outcome the feature or task they are working on
  is meant to achieve.
- 1 — Beginner: Can state, with support, why measuring an outcome matters and
  what "baseline data" means.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies the priority outcome for their own feature
  with some guidance, and can point to the data used to track it.
- 3 — Advanced: Sets baseline data and defines the priority outcomes for a
  small product independently.
- 4 — Expert: Helps others in the team understand why a particular outcome
  matters more than another, and coaches them on setting a baseline.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain what outcome a piece of work they contributed
  to was meant to achieve, and how anyone would know if it had worked.
- In a structured interview, ask why a team might collect baseline data
  before changing a feature.
- Review a metric, dashboard, or outcome statement the candidate has
  contributed to or referred to in their work.

### Skill: Product leadership

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate product manager should describe how product management works
with other roles in a multidisciplinary team, explain how user and business
needs should be balanced in product development, and attend product
management community activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Every bullet the framework gives for this skill at
this level is about describing or attending, not leading, which is a
beginner's engagement with the wider discipline rather than practising it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how a product manager's role differs from a
  delivery manager's or a designer's.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe, with prompting, how product management fits
  alongside other roles in a multidisciplinary team, and has attended at
  least one community activity such as a guild or show-and-tell.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains unprompted how user and business needs are
  balanced on their own product, using a real example.
- 3 — Advanced: Actively contributes to the wider product community, for
  example by presenting their own work or helping organise an activity.
- 4 — Expert: Is a recognised contributor to the product community beyond
  their own team, and helps others understand how the discipline works
  alongside others.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe how their day-to-day work depends on, or
  supports, a designer, a delivery manager, or a developer on their team.
- Ask about a product management community activity — a guild, a
  show-and-tell, a community of practice session — they have taken part in,
  and what they took from it.
- In a structured interview, ask for an example of balancing what users
  wanted against what the business needed on a real piece of work.

### Skill: Product management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate product manager should create and prioritise actionable goals
for their teams, balancing user needs and business perspectives, make or
recommend an evidence-based decision using insight and data, use a range of
product management methods — for example, prioritisation and roadmapping —
and work with others in multidisciplinary teams to plan and deliver
high-quality product increments.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is the core, doing-focused skill in the
block, and its bullets describe actively creating goals, recommending
decisions, and using named methods — more than the purely explanatory
bullets in some of this level's other skills, so a working, guided
proficiency is a fair minimum even at entry level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not helped prioritise or plan a piece of product work.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a backlog or roadmap someone else has set, with
  close supervision when priorities need to change.
- 2 — Intermediate: Helps prioritise a small set of goals for a feature with
  guidance, uses at least one named prioritisation method, and can point to
  the evidence behind a recommendation.
- 3 — Advanced: Creates and prioritises actionable goals for a smaller
  product unsupported, and makes evidence-based recommendations that a more
  senior product manager acts on.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches others on using product management methods well, and
  is trusted to make evidence-based recommendations at a wider product level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through how they helped prioritise a backlog or
  set of goals for a feature, and what evidence they used.
- In a structured interview, present a small scenario with competing user and
  business needs and ask how they would decide what to do first.
- Review a roadmap, prioritisation matrix, or backlog the candidate has
  contributed to.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate product manager should identify important stakeholders and
communicate with them clearly and regularly, tailor communication to
stakeholders' needs and work with them to build relationships while meeting
user needs, build and reach consensus with stakeholders, and work to improve
stakeholder relationships using evidence to explain decisions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The bullets describe actively identifying,
tailoring, and building consensus with stakeholders — more doing than the
purely explanatory skills elsewhere in this level's matrix — so a working,
guided proficiency is a reasonable minimum, even though full independence is
not expected yet.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name the stakeholders for the feature or product they are
  working on.
- 1 — Beginner: Attends stakeholder conversations but relies on a more
  experienced colleague to lead them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies the important stakeholders for their own
  feature with guidance, and communicates routine updates to them clearly.
- 3 — Advanced: Builds consensus with stakeholders on a smaller product
  unsupported, and uses evidence to explain a decision a stakeholder
  disagrees with.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches others on how to manage a difficult stakeholder
  relationship, and is trusted to resolve one when it has gone wrong.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a stakeholder they communicate with
  regularly, and how they tailor updates for that person.
- In a structured interview, present a scenario where a stakeholder disagrees
  with a decision and ask how they would respond.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate building a working
  relationship with a stakeholder over time.

### Skill: Strategic ownership

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate product manager should explain how they make decisions that are
aligned to the strategy of their organisation, and explain the importance of
having a strategy.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. As published, both bullets for this skill at this
level are explanatory rather than active — this is about understanding why
strategy matters, not yet setting or owning one, which is a beginner's grasp.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say how their day-to-day work connects to any wider
  organisational or team strategy.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain, with prompting, why having a strategy matters
  and roughly how their work connects to it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains unprompted how a specific decision they made
  aligned with the team's or product's strategy.
- 3 — Advanced: Weighs a real decision against the stated strategy
  unsupported, and can justify a choice that the strategy did not directly
  cover.
- 4 — Expert: Helps others in the team understand how their own decisions
  should align with strategy.

**Evidence to gather**

- In a structured interview, ask the candidate to explain, in their own
  words, why their team or product has a strategy and why it matters.
- Ask the candidate to describe a decision they made and how it connected to
  a stated goal or strategy.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has referred to team or organisational
  strategy when explaining a choice they made.
