# Digital Portfolio Manager - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the measuring: a line manager, an
interview panel, or a reviewer, judging whether a candidate or an employee
meets the baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for the
**Digital portfolio manager** role, at the **Digital portfolio manager** role
level — the entry level of this role. You might use it to inform a hiring
decision, to support a promotion board, to check a new starter's onboarding
progress, or simply to see where someone stands against the framework.

It is one input among several, never the whole of a decision. Read
`## Advisory` before you use it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you know what the framework actually expects at this level. Then gather
whatever evidence exists: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work
sample or task (for example, asking the candidate to talk through how they
would help set up governance for a small portfolio of projects), direct
observation of them at work, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating you give must trace back
to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess
— a gap you can name is more useful than a number you cannot justify.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
using the same evidence types where you can. Where possible, use more than
one assessor, scoring independently before comparing notes — this matters
most for a hiring or promotion decision, where the stakes for the person are
highest.

**Stay fair.** This is not a validated instrument, and used inconsistently it
can produce unlawful adverse impact — treating people differently in a way
that disadvantages a group sharing a protected characteristic, even
unintentionally. Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one
assessor materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by
anything other than demonstrated, job-related evidence — see
`## Fair and lawful use` principles reflected throughout this document: no
question or note field here should ever invite information about a protected
characteristic, health, or personal circumstances unrelated to the job.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale.
This is a **proficiency** scale: it rates how well the person does the thing,
on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Stakeholder relationship management.** At 0, a candidate
cannot describe having identified or communicated with a stakeholder on any
piece of work. At 1, they can describe having noted who the important
stakeholders were on a task, and having sent them an update when asked,
though someone more senior chose who to contact and when. At 2, they can
describe identifying stakeholders unprompted, communicating with them
regularly, and tailoring what they said to a specific audience — for example,
giving a different level of detail to a delivery team than to a sponsor. At 3
or 4 — beyond what this entry level expects — the person would describe
building consensus among stakeholders who disagreed, or coaching a colleague
on how to handle a difficult stakeholder relationship.

## About this role level

A digital portfolio manager co-ordinates and manages a group, or "portfolio",
of digital projects, using an iterative and flexible approach to help define
the governance processes for that portfolio.

This role has three levels: **2. Digital portfolio manager** — you are
assessing this level, the entry point into the role — **3. Senior digital
portfolio manager**, and **4. Head of portfolio**.

At this level, a digital portfolio manager helps to co-ordinate and manage a
portfolio of digital projects. In this role, you will:

- help to create environments that enable teams to deliver
- support the prioritisation of a group of projects and programmes
- help teams to follow governance and delivery processes
- help stakeholders understand the performance of work and any related risks
- support more senior portfolio managers and leadership in making
  improvements

This level names six skills: Agile working, Communicating analysis and
insight, Governance and assurance, Planning, Stakeholder relationship
management, and Strategic thinking. Each is assessed in the competency matrix
below.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an awareness of Agile methodology and of the ways to
apply its principles in practice. It expects an open-minded approach, the
ability to explain why iteration matters, and the ability to iterate quickly.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording stays at "show an
awareness" and "explain why", which is knowledge and explanation rather than
independent application — the hallmark of a beginner rating, appropriate for
the entry level of this role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what an iterative or Agile approach is, or has no
  experience working in a team that used one.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain in their own words why working iteratively helps
  a portfolio, and has taken part in Agile ceremonies (such as a stand-up or
  a retrospective) as a team member, under someone else's facilitation.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies Agile principles to their own small tasks without
  prompting, and can suggest a small process change based on what a
  retrospective surfaced.
- 3 — Advanced: Adapts an Agile approach to a portfolio's specific
  constraints, and helps a team move from a rigid plan to an iterative one
  when circumstances change.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches other portfolio staff on applying Agile principles, and
  is sought out when a team is stuck on how to work more iteratively.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they worked in an iterative way and
  what they learned from the first iteration that changed the second.
- In a work sample, give a short, static project plan and ask them to explain
  how they would break it into iterations.
- Ask a referee whether the person adapted their approach when circumstances
  on a project changed, or stuck rigidly to the original plan.

### Skill: Communicating analysis and insight

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to choose an appropriate medium to
communicate findings, to shape and share communications relevant to the
audience, to present analysis and visualisations clearly to both specialist
and non-specialist audiences (with support), and to give tactical
recommendations from their analysis to inform decision making.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording says "with support" for
presenting analysis, which is an explicit marker of close supervision rather
than independent practice at this level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not produced or presented any analysis of portfolio data to
  another person.
- 1 — Beginner: Has put together a short update on a project's status or risk
  for a stakeholder, with a more senior colleague checking it before it went
  out.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses the right format for the audience unprompted —
  for example, a one-page summary for a sponsor rather than a full data
  table — and can explain why they chose it.
- 3 — Advanced: Turns a messy set of portfolio data into a clear story that
  changes what a stakeholder decides to do next, without help.
- 4 — Expert: Is asked by colleagues across the organisation to review how
  they are presenting analysis, and improves the standard of communication
  more broadly than their own portfolio.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a work sample: a one-page summary of a fictional portfolio's
  status, written for a non-specialist stakeholder.
- In a structured interview, ask them to describe a piece of analysis they
  produced and how they decided who needed to see it and in what form.
- Ask a referee how much editing or coaching the person's written updates
  typically needed before they went to a stakeholder.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to be able to explain how governance and
assurance works, to follow the appropriate governance and assurance
processes, and to contribute to deciding what governance and assurance a
product or service needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Two of the three bullets are "explain" and "follow" —
understanding and compliance rather than ownership — which matches close
supervision; "contribute to deciding" is the one bullet that reaches slightly
further, but is not enough on its own to lift the baseline for the whole
skill at entry level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what a governance process such as a stage gate or
  a project board is for.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain, in their own words, what a specific governance
  process is checking for, and follows it correctly when told to.
- 2 — Intermediate: Notices when a governance step has been missed on a
  project and flags it, and can suggest which governance checkpoint a new
  small project should go through.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps design a governance process for a new portfolio, fitted
  to its size and risk, rather than applying a standard template by rote.
- 4 — Expert: Is asked to review or set governance standards across more than
  one portfolio, and other portfolio managers come to them with governance
  questions.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain, in plain terms, why a particular governance
  gate exists on a portfolio they have worked on.
- In a work sample, ask them to review a short project summary and say
  whether it is ready to pass a stage gate, and why.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate following, or failing to
  follow, an assurance process under time pressure.

### Skill: Planning

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an understanding of why planning and forecasting
matter, and an awareness of the different ways a plan can be developed.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Both bullets are about understanding and awareness
rather than doing — the thinnest description of any skill at this level —
which is a plain beginner baseline.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain why a portfolio needs a plan, or what forecasting
  is for.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain why planning matters for a portfolio, and can
  name more than one way a plan might be put together (for example, a
  roadmap compared with a detailed schedule).
- 2 — Intermediate: Has helped build part of a plan for a small piece of
  work, with someone more senior checking the result.
- 3 — Advanced: Builds a plan for a small portfolio or project group
  independently, choosing an appropriate planning approach for the work.
- 4 — Expert: Advises other portfolio managers on which planning approach
  suits a given situation, and is trusted with the most uncertain or
  high-stakes plans.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain, for a fictional group of projects, why a
  single fixed plan might not be the right approach.
- In a structured interview, ask them to describe two different ways they
  have seen a plan built, and when each was used.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has contributed to building a plan, and
  how much guidance that took.

- [ ] 0 — None

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to identify important stakeholders and
communicate with them clearly and regularly, to tailor communication to
stakeholders' needs while building relationships that meet user needs, to
build and reach consensus with stakeholders, and to work to improve
stakeholder relationships using evidence to explain decisions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Four substantive bullets, including "build and
reach consensus" and using evidence to explain decisions, describe more
active relationship work than the other skills at this level, so the
baseline sits a step higher even though this is still the entry level of the
role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name the stakeholders on a piece of work they have been
  part of.
- 1 — Beginner: Can list the stakeholders on a project and has sent them an
  update when told to, but has not chosen who to contact or when.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies stakeholders unprompted, communicates with
  them regularly, and tailors what they say to different audiences —
  supporting decisions with evidence rather than opinion.
- 3 — Advanced: Brings two stakeholders with different views to a shared
  position on a portfolio decision, without needing a more senior colleague
  to step in.
- 4 — Expert: Is the person other portfolio managers ask for advice on a
  difficult stakeholder relationship, and has repaired a relationship that
  had broken down.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they had to explain a difficult
  decision to a stakeholder who disagreed with it, and how they used
  evidence to do so.
- In a work sample, give a short scenario with two stakeholders wanting
  different things from the same piece of work, and ask how they would
  handle it.
- Ask a referee how the candidate is regarded by the stakeholders they have
  worked with most closely.

**Rate this skill**

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to explain the strategic context of their
work and why it matters, and to support strategic planning in an
administrative capacity.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. "Support... in an administrative capacity" is
explicitly a supporting rather than a leading role, which sits at beginner
for the entry level of this role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain how the portfolio they work on connects to any
  wider organisational goal.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain why the piece of work they are doing matters to
  the wider strategy, and has helped with administrative tasks that support
  strategic planning, such as collating input for a strategy document.
- 2 — Intermediate: Spots when a piece of work in the portfolio has drifted
  away from the strategic goal it was meant to serve, and raises it.
- 3 — Advanced: Contributes original thinking to a strategy or policy
  document, not just administrative support for one written by others.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes strategic direction for a portfolio and is asked to
  represent that thinking to senior leadership.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain how a project they have worked on served a
  wider organisational or departmental goal.
- In a structured interview, ask what administrative or supporting role they
  have played in a strategic planning exercise.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has shown curiosity about the "why"
  behind their work, beyond doing the task in front of them.
