# Chief Technology Officer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for you if you are judging whether a candidate or an
employee has the abilities needed to do the job of chief technology officer
in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — for a hiring panel, a
promotion conversation, an onboarding check, or a periodic capability review.

**One thing to know before you start.** The framework publishes this role and
describes what it involves, but it marks the role level itself as **not in
use**, and it publishes no expectation against any of the six skills it names
for it. That is the framework's own position, not a gap in this document. It
means there is no published baseline to measure a person against here. This
assessment therefore does something different from every other competency
assessment in this set: instead of comparing a person's rating to a target
figure, it gives you a structured way to record what a person's practice
actually looks like against the skill names the framework publishes, so that
the record is useful even though there is nothing to score it against.

This is one input to a much larger process. It is not the whole of a
decision, and it must never stand alone — see `## Advisory` below for why, and
read that section before you use anything else in this document.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
that the role's actual duties are fresh in your mind. Gather whatever evidence
exists ahead of time: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample
or task such as a technology strategy or an architecture paper, direct
observation of the person operating at this level, or references from people
who have worked with them.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every entry in this record must
trace to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If
you cannot point to evidence for an entry, record it as unknown rather than
guess — a chief technology officer's real work happens in board rooms,
architecture review boards and technology investment negotiations that an
assessor rarely sees directly, so gaps in your evidence are normal and should
be recorded as gaps.

**Consistency.** Record practice the same way for every candidate you assess
for this role, and, especially for a hiring or promotion decision, have more
than one assessor complete this record independently before comparing notes.

**This is an unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument.** Used inconsistently, or by
a single assessor working from impression rather than evidence, it can
produce unlawful adverse impact under the Equality Act 2010. Consistent,
evidence-based administration, ideally by more than one assessor, materially
reduces that risk — see [Fair and lawful
use](../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
for the constraints that follow from this.

**A note on sensitivity.** The framework's own description of this role
includes ensuring systems and services are continuously operational and
resistant to attacks and disasters, and working within appropriate levels of
risk. A candidate's answers on Technology architecture or Financial decision
making for technology may therefore touch how the organisation defends its
infrastructure. Do not record the detail of a live vulnerability, an
unresolved resilience gap, or anything that would itself create a disclosure
risk in the evidence log. Record that evidence exists and what it showed, not
the sensitive content itself.

## Rating scale

Every skill in this record is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.
Because no baseline is published for this role level, use the scale here to
describe the person's actual, observed practice rather than to compare that
practice against a target.

**A worked example**, using Technology architecture, one of the six skills
this role names. Evidence at 1 — Beginner might be a candidate who can
describe what a reliable technology architecture looks like, but has never
been accountable for putting one in place. Evidence at 3 — Advanced might be
a candidate who has independently put in place a technology architecture that
provided a reliable and permanent infrastructure for a mid-sized
organisation, and can describe how they made systems and services resistant
to attacks and disasters. Evidence at 4 — Expert might be a candidate whose
architecture judgement has demonstrably shaped how an organisation's — or
several organisations' — infrastructure evolved over several years, and who
other chief technology officers or specialist architects have looked to as a
reference.

## About this role level

**The role: chief technology officer.** A chief technology officer is the
organisation's technology strategist. They make decisions about the technical
direction of the organisation and work closely with other senior leaders.
The framework describes the role's duties as:

- create a strategy and vision for technology that aligns with the
  organisation's broader strategy and enables the organisation to achieve its
  objectives
- lead the organisation in implementing the technology strategy
- advocate for technology as a critical part of the organisation's broader
  strategy
- evaluate the organisation's range of technologies and which technologies it
  needs
- put in place a technology architecture that provides a reliable and
  permanent infrastructure
- ensure systems and services are continuously operational and resistant to
  attacks and disasters
- enable the organisation to be innovative and adopt new technologies that
  offer value
- work within appropriate levels of risk
- ensure people have the tools, technical skills and working environment
  they need
- create working practices and process that support effective delivery

**The role level is published as NOT IN USE.** The framework lists chief
technology officer in the chief digital and data profession, keeps the role
description above, and marks the level itself — and every skill expectation
beneath it — as not in use. There is no level above or below within this role
to compare a candidate's rating against, and no published wording for what
any of the six named skills should look like at this level.

**The six skills the framework names for this role**, with no expectation
published against any of them:

1. Capability building for digital, data and technology
2. Financial decision making for technology
3. Innovation in digital, data and technology
4. Strategic technology planning
5. Technology architecture
6. Technology evaluation

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Capability building for digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has ensured people have the
  tools, technical skills or working environment they need to do their jobs.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe why capability building matters, but
  the evidence shows only reactive fixes to individual complaints rather than
  a deliberate effort.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a capability activity — a
  tooling upgrade, a technical skills programme, an improvement to the
  working environment — with support from a technology or people function.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently ensured people across their
  organisation have the tools, technical skills and working environment they
  need, and can describe how they identified the gap and closed it.
- 4 — Expert: The person's capability building has demonstrably changed how
  effectively a whole organisation's technical people can work over several
  years, and other senior leaders look to them for judgement on this.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a specific gap in tools, skills or working
  environment they identified and closed, and how they knew it had worked.
- Ask a referee whether the technical working environment changed under the
  candidate's leadership, and how.
- Review a capability plan, tooling strategy or workforce survey the
  candidate authored or sponsored.

### Skill: Financial decision making for technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has made or influenced a
  financial decision about technology — no business case, no budget, no
  investment trade-off they were accountable for.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe how technology investment decisions
  are usually made, but has only worked within a budget set by others.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a technology business case
  or budget decision, with support from a finance function, and can describe
  the reasoning behind a specific choice.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently evaluated which technologies an
  organisation needs and made the financial case for them, working within
  appropriate levels of risk, and can describe a specific investment
  trade-off they made.
- 4 — Expert: The person's financial decision making for technology has
  demonstrably shaped how an organisation invests in its technology estate
  over several years, and they can show a track record of investment
  decisions whose value they can trace and quantify.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a technology investment decision where they
  had to choose between two competing, defensible priorities.
- Review a business case or technology budget strategy the candidate
  authored or sponsored.
- Ask a finance colleague how the candidate engaged with technology
  investment decisions.

### Skill: Innovation in digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has enabled an organisation to
  be innovative or adopt a new technology that offered real value.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe examples of technology innovation
  elsewhere, but has no example of having enabled one themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has supported the adoption of a new technology
  led by someone else, and can describe what made it work or fail.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently enabled the organisation to be
  innovative and adopted a new technology that offered value, working within
  appropriate levels of risk, and can describe how they judged that risk.
- 4 — Expert: The person's approach to technology innovation has
  demonstrably changed what an organisation is able to do, and they can
  describe how they built an environment — not just a single project — where
  adopting new technology that offers value is the norm.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a new technology they adopted, the value it
  delivered, and the risk they accepted to get there.
- Ask a referee whether the organisation's appetite for adopting new
  technology changed under the candidate's leadership.
- Review a business case or evaluation for a new technology the candidate
  sponsored.

### Skill: Strategic technology planning

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has created or led a technology
  strategy, and cannot describe how technology should align with an
  organisation's broader strategy.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can explain what a technology strategy is and why
  an organisation needs one, but has never created or led one themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a technology strategy, with
  support from a more senior sponsor, and can describe how it aligned with
  the organisation's broader strategy.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently created a strategy and vision
  for technology that aligns with the organisation's broader strategy, led
  its implementation, and can describe how they advocated for technology as a
  critical part of that broader strategy.
- 4 — Expert: The person's technology strategy has demonstrably shaped a
  whole organisation's direction over several years, has been cited by peers
  or a cross-government forum as a strong example, and they can show how
  they adapted it as circumstances changed rather than leaving it static.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe their technology strategy in two sentences,
  and name a senior leader outside the technology function who could repeat
  it.
- Review a technology strategy document the candidate authored or led.
- Ask a senior stakeholder how the candidate secured their support for the
  strategy, and what changed as a result of it.

### Skill: Technology architecture

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: The person cannot describe how their organisation's technology
  architecture works, and shows no evidence of having put one in place.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can talk about technology architecture principles
  in general terms, but has not been accountable for a real architecture
  decision.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a technology architecture
  decision, working alongside specialist architects, and can explain the
  reasoning behind at least one such decision.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently put in place a technology
  architecture that provides a reliable and permanent infrastructure, and can
  describe how they made systems and services continuously operational and
  resistant to attacks and disasters.
- 4 — Expert: The person's technology architecture judgement has demonstrably
  shaped how an organisation's — or several organisations' — infrastructure
  evolved over several years, and specialist architects or peer organisations
  look to them for judgement on this.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an architecture decision that improved the
  organisation's operational resilience, and what alternatives they rejected.
- Review an architecture decision record or infrastructure strategy the
  candidate authored or sponsored, without recording live resilience gaps in
  this log.
- Ask a specialist technology architect who has worked with the candidate how
  much of the architecture direction came from them.

### Skill: Technology evaluation

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has evaluated an organisation's
  range of technologies or judged which ones it needs.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe how technology evaluation should
  work in general terms, but has not personally evaluated a real technology
  choice for an organisation.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a technology evaluation,
  with support from specialists, and can describe the criteria that shaped a
  specific recommendation.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently evaluated the organisation's
  range of technologies and determined which ones it needs, and can describe
  a specific technology they recommended retiring or adopting, and why.
- 4 — Expert: The person's technology evaluation judgement has demonstrably
  shaped an organisation's — or several organisations' — technology
  portfolio over several years, and other senior leaders look to them for
  judgement on which technologies are worth adopting.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a technology they recommended retiring, and
  the evidence that led them to that recommendation.
- Review a technology evaluation report, options paper or vendor assessment
  the candidate authored or led.
- Ask a colleague from a delivery team how the candidate's technology
  evaluations affected their work.

process — which may set its own local expectations for this role — something
