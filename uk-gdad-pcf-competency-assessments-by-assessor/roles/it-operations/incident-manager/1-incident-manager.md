# Incident Manager - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, an interview panel
member, or anyone else evaluating a candidate or an employee — who needs to
judge whether a person has the baseline abilities the IT operations
profession's incident manager role level requires. It measures demonstrated
skill against the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description
of this level, for a hiring decision, a promotion or progression board,
onboarding a new starter, or a periodic capability check. It is one input
into that decision, never the whole of it — see `## Advisory` before you
begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what the
framework actually expects before you look at any evidence. Then gather what
exists for this person: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work
sample or task, direct observation of them at work, or references from people
who have worked with them. At this level the framework expects hands-on,
on-site fault fixing and first-time resolution, with escalation to major
incident managers and problem managers where needed, so look for evidence of
that hands-on work rather than only process knowledge.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess — an honest gap
teaches you more than a guess dressed up as a score.

**Stay consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
using the same evidence sources wherever you can. For a hiring or promotion
decision, have more than one assessor score independently, then compare notes,
rather than settling on a single view first.

**Fairness.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact even when nobody intends
it. Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale — how well the person does the thing, on the evidence
available, not how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example.** Take Skill: Incident management. At 1 — Beginner, a
candidate follows a runbook step by step to work through a familiar incident,
with a colleague checking each step. At 2 — Intermediate, the baseline for
this level, they diagnose and prioritise an incident unassisted, investigate
its cause and find a resolution — the framework's own wording for this level.
At 3 — Advanced, they take on incidents that span more than one technology
area and coach a colleague through the diagnosis. Calibrate against evidence
like this before you start scoring.

## About this role level

IT operations role: Incident manager. Incident managers aim to restore normal
service operation as quickly as possible and minimise any adverse effects on
business operations, so that the best possible levels of service quality and
availability are returned and maintained.

The role has two levels, in order: incident manager and major incident
manager. This assessment covers incident manager, the first of those.

Role level: Incident manager. An incident manager oversees incidents and
restores normal operations as quickly as possible, with the least impact on
the business or the user.

At this role level, the framework expects someone to:

- work on-site, maintaining the hardware and fixing technical problems as
  quickly as possible
- provide first-time resolution by troubleshooting and diagnosing or
  escalating faults, or both, to the major incident managers and problem
  managers to investigate and resolve
- coach apprentices and share knowledge with team colleagues

The framework notes that, in some departments, this role's responsibilities
may mirror those of an end user computing engineer.

The framework names eleven skills for this level: Asset and configuration
management, Availability and capacity management, Change management,
Community collaboration, Continuity management, Incident management,
Ownership and initiative, Problem management, Service focus, Service
management framework knowledge, and User focus.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Asset and configuration management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager tracks, logs and corrects information to protect assets
and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Tracking and correcting asset information
unassisted, without owning wider service compliance or risk, is
guided-but-independent record-keeping.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot say what asset or component information needs protecting.
- 1 — Beginner: logs asset information when told exactly what to record.
- 2 — Intermediate: tracks, logs and corrects asset and component information
  accurately without prompting.
- 3 — Advanced: spots a gap in asset tracking before it puts a component at
  risk, and improves how the team records it.
- 4 — Expert: owns asset and component protection across a whole service.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe how they track and correct asset information
  day to day, with an example.
- Review an asset or component log they maintained.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate catching an inaccurate asset
  record before it caused a problem.

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager can explain availability and capacity management
processes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework asks this level to explain the
processes, not yet manage service components against them — explaining a
process correctly is foundational, close-supervision-adjacent knowledge.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe what availability or capacity management covers.
- 1 — Beginner: explains the basic purpose of availability and capacity
  management processes when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: explains the processes accurately and links them to a
  real incident they have worked.
- 3 — Advanced: applies availability or capacity knowledge to judge whether an
  incident risks breaching a service level.
- 4 — Expert: teaches availability and capacity concepts to newer colleagues.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain, in their own words, why availability and
  capacity management matter to incident response.
- Ask for an example of an incident where availability or capacity was a
  factor, and what they understood about it.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's explanations of these processes are
  accurate.

### Skill: Change management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager implements change requests and applies change control
procedures under supervision.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording — "under supervision" —
sets the ceiling for this skill at this level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not implemented a change request.
- 1 — Beginner: implements a change request and applies change control
  procedure, with a colleague checking the work.
- 2 — Intermediate: implements routine change requests correctly without
  needing a check at every step.
- 3 — Advanced: implements complex changes unsupervised.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted to implement high-impact changes without supervision.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a change request they implemented and
  what the change control procedure required of them.
- Review a change record or ticket they closed.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's implemented changes have needed

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager contributes to the work of others, motivates and
empowers teams, creates the right environment for teams to work in and can
identify the best team makeup depending on the situation, and recognises and
deals with issues.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Contributing to others' work and recognising and
dealing with team issues, without yet running formal team-dynamic exercises,
is applied, guided collaboration rather than independent team leadership.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: works alone and does not engage with how the wider team is
  functioning.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes to a colleague's work when asked to.
- 2 — Intermediate: contributes to others' work unprompted, and notices and
  raises a team issue rather than ignoring it.
- 3 — Advanced: actively creates a better working environment for the team
  and judges what mix of people a task needs.
- 4 — Expert: is sought out for advice on team dynamics and empowering others.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of the candidate contributing to a colleague's work
  without being asked.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate noticing and dealing with a
  team issue.
- Ask the candidate to describe a time they helped create a better working
  environment for their team.

### Skill: Continuity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager can explain the basic principles of IT standard
continuity management processes and procedures.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Explaining basic principles, rather than managing
runbooks or testing continuity, is foundational knowledge appropriate to close
supervision.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe what IT continuity management is for.
- 1 — Beginner: explains the basic principles of continuity management when

- 3 — Advanced: follows a continuity procedure during a real or simulated
  event without needing to be walked through it.

  management in their own words.

### Skill: Incident management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager diagnoses and prioritises incidents, investigates their
causes and finds resolutions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Diagnosing, prioritising and resolving incidents
unassisted is the core, guided-but-independent work this level exists to do —
strong, but not yet the cross-team leadership of the level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot diagnose a fault beyond following a runbook.
- 1 — Beginner: diagnoses a familiar incident with a colleague checking the
  reasoning.
- 2 — Intermediate: diagnoses and resolves familiar incidents unassisted, and
  prioritises them correctly against other open work.
- 3 — Advanced: independently investigates incidents that span more than one
  technology area.
- 4 — Expert: is the escalation point for incidents that have resisted
  diagnosis by others.

**Evidence to gather**

- Walk through a real or simulated incident scenario and observe how the
  candidate prioritises and diagnoses it.
- Ask for an example of an incident they investigated from first report to
  resolution.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate correctly prioritising one
  incident over another under pressure.

### Skill: Ownership and initiative

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager owns an issue until a new owner has been found or the
problem has been mitigated or resolved.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Holding onto an issue through a handover rather
than dropping it once escalated is basic, guided-but-independent
professionalism expected of every working-level incident manager.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: drops an issue once it is out of their hands.
- 1 — Beginner: keeps an issue open on their own list but needs reminding to
  chase it.
- 2 — Intermediate: tracks an issue through to a confirmed handover or
  resolution without being chased.
- 3 — Advanced: proactively checks that a handed-over issue actually landed
  with its new owner and was picked up.
- 4 — Expert: is the person others rely on to never let an issue slip, across
  a whole shift or team.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of an issue the candidate followed through to resolution
  or a confirmed handover.
- Ask a referee whether issues assigned to the candidate have gone missing or
  stalled.
- Review a ticket history showing the candidate's ownership of an issue over
  time.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager initiates and monitors actions to investigate patterns
and trends to resolve problems, effectively consults specialists where
required, determines the appropriate resolution and assists with its
implementation, and determines preventative measures.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Initiating and monitoring investigations and
determining a resolution unassisted is guided-but-independent ownership of
problem management, matching Intermediate at this working level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot distinguish a problem from an incident.
- 1 — Beginner: investigates a problem when told exactly what to look at.
- 2 — Intermediate: initiates and monitors a problem investigation, consults
  specialists appropriately, and determines a resolution unassisted.
- 3 — Advanced: identifies a preventative measure that stops a problem
  recurring, not just a one-off resolution.
- 4 — Expert: spots a pattern across problems that others have missed.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a problem investigation they initiated, who
  they consulted, and what resolution they determined.
- Review a problem record or root-cause note they contributed to.
- Ask a referee for an example of a preventative measure the candidate
  determined that stopped a recurring issue.

### Skill: Service focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager takes inputs and establishes coherent frameworks that
work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Turning a set of inputs into a working framework
for their own area of work is applied, guided practice, short of the
bigger-picture, cross-service view the framework describes at the level
above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe how their daily tasks fit into the wider service.
- 1 — Beginner: follows a service framework someone else has set up.
- 2 — Intermediate: takes several inputs — incidents, escalations, requests —
  and organises their own work into a coherent, working routine.
- 3 — Advanced: improves a service framework so it works better for the
  wider team.
- 4 — Expert: designs a service framework that a whole team adopts.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe how they organise a busy mix of incidents and
  follow-ups into a coherent routine.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's way of working has helped or hindered
  the wider team.
- Review any process note or checklist the candidate has produced for their
  own work.

### Skill: Service management framework knowledge

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager shows they have a Level 3 service management framework
qualification.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. A named, held qualification demonstrates working
knowledge applied with some guidance; it is evidence of grounding rather than
of teaching others.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: holds no service management framework qualification and cannot
  describe the framework's basic concepts.
- 1 — Beginner: is working towards a Level 3 service management framework
  qualification.
- 2 — Intermediate: holds a Level 3 service management framework
  qualification and applies its concepts correctly in day-to-day work.
- 3 — Advanced: applies the framework's concepts to improve how their team
  works, beyond what the qualification alone requires.
- 4 — Expert: holds a higher-level qualification or is asked to explain the
  framework to others.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to show their Level 3 service management framework
  certificate or equivalent evidence.
- Ask the candidate to explain a framework concept, such as incident versus
  problem, in their own words.
- Ask a referee how the candidate applies the framework in practice.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

An incident manager identifies and engages with users or stakeholders to
collate user needs evidence, understands and defines research that fits user
needs, and uses quantitative and qualitative data about users to turn user
focus into outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Collating user needs evidence and turning data
into outcomes for a specific incident is applied, guided work — real, but
bounded to the case in front of them rather than the cross-channel judgement
of the level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: resolves the reported fault without asking about the user's
  wider impact.
- 1 — Beginner: asks the user basic clarifying questions when prompted to.
- 2 — Intermediate: engages the user or stakeholder directly to understand
  the business impact, and uses that to prioritise and resolve the incident.
- 3 — Advanced: spots a pattern in how several incidents affect users and
  feeds it back so the service improves.
- 4 — Expert: shapes how the wider team engages with users during incidents.

**Evidence to gather**

- Observe or ask the candidate to describe an incident where they found out
  more than the initial report about the business impact.
- Ask for an example where understanding a user's actual need changed how
  they prioritised an incident.
- Ask a referee how users or colleagues describe the candidate's manner
  during an incident.
