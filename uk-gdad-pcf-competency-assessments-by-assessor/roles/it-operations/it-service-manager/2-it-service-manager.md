# IT Service Manager - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee holds the baseline
abilities the IT operations profession's **IT service manager** role level
requires. It is built from the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own
description of this role level: its duties, its accountabilities and the nine
skills it names.

IT service managers are responsible for defining service level agreements
(SLAs) and operational level agreements (OLAs) across all relevant business
functions, and for managing a multi-sourced service support team. This
assessment turns that description into a structured matrix an assessor can
score against observed or reported evidence, for a hiring decision, a
promotion board, or a capability review against the framework.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of
one. Read `## Advisory` before using it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not
been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this form
must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything.
Then gather whatever evidence exists: a CV or portfolio, a structured
interview, a work sample or task (for example, drafting a sample SLA or
reviewing a service report), direct observation of the person leading a
service review, or references from a manager, peer or team member.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating in the competency matrix
must trace to something the person did, said, produced, or was reliably
reported to have done. If you cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it
as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, using
the same evidence sources where possible. For a hiring or promotion decision,
have more than one assessor score independently, then compare notes and
discuss any large gaps before agreeing a final rating.

**Fairness.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact against candidates who
share a protected characteristic — even unintentionally. Consistent,
evidence-based administration by more than one assessor materially reduces
that risk. See [Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
for what this means in practice, and never let a rating be influenced by
anything other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example — Service focus.** At 2 — Intermediate, a candidate might
describe managing a single service well, without much reference to how it
connects to others. At 3 — Advanced, a candidate for IT service manager should
be able to describe taking a group of related services — say, all the
services underpinning a department's case management — and explain a change
they made to how the underlying services worked together to improve the
overall outcome. That kind of "bigger picture" example, specific and with a
result, is what "see the bigger picture" evidence looks like at this level.

## About this role level

The IT operations profession's **IT service manager** role covers the people
who manage the delivery of information and communications technology (ICT)
services and work with IT service operations teams. It has four levels:

1. IT service analyst
2. **IT service manager** — this level
3. Senior IT service manager
4. Head of IT service management

The **IT service manager** sits above the IT service analyst and defines
service level agreements (SLAs) and operational level agreements (OLAs) across
all relevant business functions.

At this role level, you will:

- manage a fully functioning multi-sourced service support team with a
  customer-centric support approach across multiple locations, focused on
  service availability and performance
- ensure that all service and support functions remain responsive to customer
  needs
- ensure that the service catalogue and wider IT service operations knowledge
  library are maintained
- ensure that adequate reporting and service standards are met for your
  specific service
- ensure that all technical change is communicated and managed, with
  appropriate governance
- ensure that a programme of continual service improvement is put in place
  across the wider service delivery function

This role level names nine skills: Asset and configuration management,
Business analysis (IT operations), IT service reporting, Ownership and
initiative, Service focus, Service management framework knowledge, Stakeholder
relationship management (IT operations), Strategic thinking, and User focus.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Asset and configuration management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an IT service manager to maintain secure configuration
and accurate information, control IT assets in one or more areas, and verify
the location and state of IT assets.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The wording moves from the analyst's "track, log
and correct" to "maintain" and "control", which is a step up to owning
accuracy across an area rather than just entering data, but this level does
not yet describe the full configuration-risk accountability that appears
higher up the role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Relies entirely on the team to keep configuration data accurate.
- 1 — Beginner: Checks configuration accuracy occasionally, when prompted by
  an incident.
- 2 — Intermediate: Owns the accuracy of configuration data across their
  support area and can verify asset location and state on request.
- 3 — Advanced: Proactively audits configuration data, closes gaps before
  they cause an incident, and improves the team's logging discipline.
- 4 — Expert: Sets configuration standards other service managers adopt.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask how the candidate has previously assured configuration data accuracy
  across a team's area of responsibility.
- Ask for an example of an incident caused, or nearly caused, by inaccurate
  configuration data, and how it was addressed.

### Skill: Business analysis (IT operations)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an IT service manager to investigate problems and
opportunities in existing processes, contribute to recommending solutions, and
work with stakeholders to identify objectives and potential benefits.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The added bullet about working with stakeholders
to identify objectives and benefits marks a step beyond the analyst's
contributory role, without yet reaching the "lead investigative work"
independence expected of a senior IT service manager.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Does not connect process problems to business objectives.
- 1 — Beginner: Investigates a process problem when asked to, with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Works with stakeholders to frame what a process
  improvement should achieve, and puts forward a recommendation.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads a small investigation independently, weighing several
  options against stated objectives before recommending one.
- 4 — Expert: Their recommendations consistently deliver the benefits
  promised, and other teams ask them to review their processes too.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of working with a stakeholder to define what a process
  improvement needed to achieve, and what happened.
- Ask about a recommendation the candidate made that was, or was not, adopted,
  and why.

### Skill: IT service reporting

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an IT service manager to produce relevant reports in an
appropriate format and agreed timeframe, work with stakeholders to discuss any
changes in the reporting processes, and interpret a data set and communicate
this to others.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The wording is identical to the analyst level, but
the reports an IT service manager produces cover a wider support team and
carry more weight with business stakeholders, so evidence should reflect
reporting at that scale even though the baseline number is the same.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot produce a service report covering their team's full remit.
- 1 — Beginner: Produces reports to a template but needs help interpreting
  cross-team patterns.
- 2 — Intermediate: Produces accurate, timely reports across their service
  area and explains what they mean to business stakeholders.
- 3 — Advanced: Proactively changes what is reported when it stops serving
  stakeholders well, and justifies the change with evidence.
- 4 — Expert: Their reporting shapes how the wider service delivery function
  measures itself.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to talk through a service report they own, who reads it,
  and what decisions it has informed.
- Ask about a time reporting requirements changed and how they handled it.

### Skill: Ownership and initiative

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an IT service manager to own an issue until a new owner
has been found or the problem has been mitigated or resolved.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The wording is unchanged from the analyst level,
reflecting that this is a foundational behaviour expected consistently, but at
this level it should be demonstrated across a whole team's caseload, not just
the person's own tickets.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Lets issues drop once handed to another team, without following
  up.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows up personally, but does not ensure the wider team
  does the same.
- 2 — Intermediate: Follows through on issues personally and checks the team
  is doing likewise, most of the time.
- 3 — Advanced: Builds ownership into how the team works — clear handover
  points, visible tracking — so issues rarely drop.
- 4 — Expert: Is the person other managers escalate to when an issue has
  fallen between teams, because they reliably pick it up and see it through.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of an issue that risked falling between teams, and how
  the candidate ensured it did not.
- Ask a referee how reliably issues were followed through under the
  candidate's management.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Service focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an IT service manager to see the bigger picture by
taking groups of services and investigating how to get the best of underlying
services.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is a clear step up from the analyst's "show an
awareness of different products and services" — it asks for independent
investigation across grouped services, which fits the level's accountability
for the service catalogue and continual service improvement.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Manages each service in isolation, with no view of how they
  interact.
- 1 — Beginner: Recognises services are related but needs help investigating
  how to improve them together.
- 2 — Intermediate: Investigates related services together with guidance, and
  identifies some improvement opportunities.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently investigates a group of related services and
  identifies concrete ways to get better outcomes from the underlying
  services.
- 4 — Expert: Redesigns how a group of services is managed, delivering a
  measurable improvement, and is asked to advise other service managers.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of improving outcomes across a group of related
  services, not just one.
- Ask how the candidate would approach investigating underperformance across
  several interdependent services.

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

### Skill: Service management framework knowledge

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an IT service manager to show they have a Level 3
service management framework qualification, and to demonstrate knowledge of
the life cycle or capability elements of ITIL (Information Technology
Infrastructure Library).

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The qualification requirement is unchanged from
the analyst level, but the added expectation of ITIL life cycle or capability
knowledge is new here, so the baseline reflects a working grasp of that wider
framework rather than just holding a certificate.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Holds no relevant qualification and cannot describe ITIL's life
  cycle stages.
- 1 — Beginner: Holds the Level 3 qualification but describes ITIL concepts
  imprecisely.
- 2 — Intermediate: Holds the qualification and correctly applies ITIL life
  cycle or capability concepts to explain their own team's processes.
- 3 — Advanced: Uses ITIL concepts to diagnose why a process is underdelivering
  and to propose a fix aligned to the framework.
- 4 — Expert: Is a reference point colleagues turn to for how ITIL concepts
  should be applied locally.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe the ITIL life cycle and where their previous
  team's processes sat within it.
- Ask for an example of using framework knowledge to improve a process.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an IT service manager to identify important
stakeholders and communicate with them clearly and regularly, tailor
communication to stakeholders' needs and work with them to build
relationships while also meeting user needs, build and reach consensus, and
work to improve stakeholder relationships using evidence to explain decisions
made.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The wording matches the analyst level, but this
level's accountability for governance of technical change and for keeping
support functions responsive to customer needs means the stakeholders in
question sit at a business level, so the baseline reflects consistent,
independent practice rather than a developing habit.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Communicates the same way to every stakeholder regardless of role
  or need.
- 1 — Beginner: Identifies key stakeholders but needs coaching to tailor
  communication effectively.
- 2 — Intermediate: Communicates clearly and regularly with business
  stakeholders, tailoring the message and building consensus without
  prompting.
- 3 — Advanced: Manages several concurrent stakeholder relationships and uses
  evidence to bring sceptical stakeholders round to a decision.
- 4 — Expert: Is trusted by senior business stakeholders as the main point of
  contact for their service area.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of building consensus among stakeholders with competing
  priorities.
- Ask a referee how the candidate is regarded by business stakeholders they
  worked with.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an IT service manager to work within a strategic
context and communicate how activities meet strategic goals, and to
contribute to the development of strategy and policies.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The wording is unchanged from the analyst level,
but this level's remit for continual service improvement and governance means
"contribute to" should be demonstrated at a service level, not just an
individual one.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot connect their team's work to any wider service strategy.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe how their team's activities relate to strategic
  goals when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: Regularly explains to their team and stakeholders how
  day-to-day work connects to strategic goals, and contributes ideas to
  policy discussions.
- 3 — Advanced: Shapes a piece of service strategy or policy that is adopted.
- 4 — Expert: Is regularly consulted on strategic direction beyond their own
  service area.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of contributing to a strategy or policy discussion, and
  what came of it.
- Ask how the candidate would explain a specific piece of day-to-day work in
  terms of a wider strategic goal.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an IT service manager to identify and engage with users
or stakeholders to collate user needs evidence, understand and define
research that fits user needs, and use quantitative and qualitative data
about users to turn user focus into outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The wording matches the analyst level, but the
level's explicit accountability for a "customer-centric support approach"
across the whole team means this should be demonstrated as a working practice
for managing a team's focus, not just an individual's.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Manages the team to SLA targets alone, without reference to what
  users actually experience.
- 1 — Beginner: Collects some user feedback but does not systematically use
  it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Regularly collates user needs evidence and uses it to
  shape how the team prioritises work.
- 3 — Advanced: Uses combined quantitative and qualitative evidence to make a
  service change that measurably improves user outcomes.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds user-focused ways of working across the team as a
  standard practice, not a one-off.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of using user feedback data to change how a service or
  team operated.
- Ask how the candidate balances SLA-driven metrics against what users
  actually experience.
