# Data Ethicist (Data Ethics Lead) - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee holds the baseline
abilities the data profession's **data ethics lead** role level requires. It
is built from the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of
this role level: the data ethicist role it sits within, and the ten skills
this level names.

Data ethicists assess the societal effect of technology and data, and produce
recommendations for other data professionals — thinking about fairness,
accountability, the law, moral dilemmas and risks in the creation of
technology and data products and policies. The data ethics lead is the first
of this role's two-level ladder: they support the head of data ethics to
enable others across the organisation to understand data ethics and implement
best practice in their work. This assessment turns that description into a
structured matrix an assessor can score against observed or reported evidence,
for a hiring decision, a promotion board, or a capability review against the
framework.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of
one. Read `## Advisory` before using it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not
been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this form
must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you know what this role level is actually accountable for. Then gather
whatever evidence exists: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work
sample or task (for example, a redacted piece of data ethics advice or a
research summary the candidate has written), direct observation of the person
supporting a data science team or hosting a discussion with stakeholders, or
references from a colleague or stakeholder they have worked with.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating in the competency matrix
must trace to something the person did, said, produced, or was reliably
reported to have done. If you cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it
as unknown rather than guess — a guessed rating is worse than a gap, because it
looks like knowledge you do not have.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, using
the same evidence sources where possible. For a hiring or promotion decision,
have more than one assessor score independently, then compare notes and
discuss any large gaps before agreeing a final rating.

**Fairness.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact against candidates who
share a protected characteristic — even unintentionally. Consistent,
evidence-based administration by more than one assessor materially reduces
that risk. See [Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
for what this means in practice, and never let a rating be influenced by
anything other than demonstrated, job-related evidence. This is especially
important for this role level: the skills below include empathy and
inclusivity work, and the assessment must always evaluate the candidate's
demonstrated professional practice — how they design and run inclusive
consultation and outreach — and never the candidate's own protected
characteristics, identity, or personal circumstances.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example — Data ethics and privacy.** At 1 — Beginner, a candidate
might describe checking a single project's data handling against a checklist
someone else wrote, without being able to say why each check mattered. At 2 —
Intermediate, they might describe working with stakeholders on one project to
identify a privacy concern, but needing a more senior colleague to confirm how
serious it was. At 3 — Advanced, they would describe independently researching
a recent development in data ethics or privacy, and using it to constructively
challenge a proposed policy before it was adopted — evidence that would
justify a rating at this role level's baseline for that skill.

## About this role level

The data profession's **data ethicist** role covers people who assess the
societal effect of technology and data, and produce recommendations for other
data professionals. This involves thinking about fairness, accountability, the
law, moral dilemmas and risks in the creation of technology and data products
and policies. In this role, a data ethicist provides research and expertise on
data ethics, enables others to implement data ethics best practice in their
work — for instance by providing training, advising data science teams, or
demonstrating how to apply ethical principles through examples and case
studies — communicates effectively to explain and raise awareness of data
ethics issues, and listens to, convenes, advises and mediates between various
parts of the organisation, and helps people to ask questions, express concerns
and discuss ethical dilemmas.

The role has two levels:

1. **Data ethics lead** — this level
2. Head of data ethics

The **data ethics lead** is the first level of this role's two-level ladder.
They support the head of data ethics to enable others across the organisation
to understand data ethics and implement best practice in their work — the
person who does much of the hands-on advising, researching and mediating that
the head's organisation-wide policy work depends on. The framework does not
publish a separate list of accountabilities for this level beyond that one
description; what this level does day to day is expressed through its skills.

This role level names ten skills: Analysis and synthesis (data ethics),
Applied social sciences, Communicating between the technical and
non-technical, Communication (data ethics), Data ethics and privacy, Empathy
and inclusivity, Managing decisions and risks, Problem management, Product
ownership (data ethics), and Stakeholder relationship management.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Analysis and synthesis (data ethics)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to draw together, analyse and
evaluate qualitative and quantitative data and information; quickly read and
interpret complex documents from a range of sources and distil what is
relevant; turn research data into clear findings that inform decisions;
effectively involve colleagues in analysis and synthesis to increase consensus
and challenge assumptions; advise on the choice and application of techniques,
and critique colleagues' findings to assure best practice; and help teams
define their project outcomes and ethical considerations, and integrate
ethical diagnostics and assessment.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The bullets describe independent practice that already
supports other people's work — advising on technique choice and critiquing
colleagues' findings — which is a strong, working command of the skill, one
step below the "expertly" and organisation-wide language the framework uses
for this same skill at the head of data ethics level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot draw a clear finding from a small set of mixed evidence
  without help.
- 1 — Beginner: Summarises a single source accurately, but needs support
  combining several sources into one finding.
- 2 — Intermediate: Synthesises evidence from a few sources into a finding
  that supports a decision on one project, with some guidance on structure.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently synthesises complex, mixed evidence into a clear
  finding, advises a colleague on which technique to use for their own
  analysis, and constructively critiques a colleague's draft findings.
- 4 — Expert: Turns synthesis into a finding that shapes an
  organisation-wide data ethics decision, and has visibly built other
  people's capacity to do the same.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give the candidate a short, mixed bundle of evidence (a survey extract, a
  technical note, a stakeholder email) and ask them to produce a one-page
  finding from it.
- Ask for an example of advising a colleague on which analysis technique to
  use, and how they then critiqued the resulting findings.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's synthesis work has changed how a team
  approached a decision.

### Skill: Applied social sciences

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to explain social sciences, such as
anthropology, economics, sociology, philosophy, psychology or race theory, and
apply social science theories to inform data projects, products and policies;
evaluate and challenge assumptions made in data science projects; and work
with academics and external researchers, describing emerging theories and
concepts.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The bullets ask for independent evaluation and
challenge of live projects and direct engagement with academics and external
researchers, which is a strong, working command of the skill — but they stop
short of the "strategic oversight" and published research the framework
expects one level up.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot connect a social science concept to a specific data ethics
  question.
- 1 — Beginner: Names a relevant social science concept when prompted, but
  cannot yet apply it to a live project.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies a familiar social science framework to a data
  project, with some support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently applies theory from more than one discipline to
  evaluate and challenge an assumption in a live data science project, and
  engages directly with academics or external researchers on emerging
  thinking.
- 4 — Expert: Has used social science theory to shape strategic oversight of a
  data programme or policy, and has co-published or contributed to research
  with external academics.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a data science project where they used a
  specific social science theory to challenge an assumption.
- Ask about a recent piece of academic or external research they have engaged
  with, and how it changed their approach to a project.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's use of social science theory has
  visibly shifted a project's direction.

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to listen to and interpret the needs
of technical and non-technical stakeholders, and manage their expectations;
manage active and reactive communication; and support or host difficult
discussions within the team or with diverse senior stakeholders.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Hosting difficult discussions with senior stakeholders
without support is independent, working-level command of the skill; the scope
described stays internal to the organisation, which is what distinguishes it
from the external representation the head of data ethics level takes on.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Struggles to interpret what a stakeholder actually needs from a
  request.
- 1 — Beginner: Interprets a straightforward stakeholder need accurately, with
  support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Manages routine active and reactive communication with
  stakeholders, escalating anything contentious.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently hosts a difficult discussion with a senior
  stakeholder, manages their expectations, and reaches a workable outcome.
- 4 — Expert: Moderates high-risk, time-constrained discussions and represents
  the organisation's position to large audiences outside it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a difficult discussion they hosted with a
  senior stakeholder, and how they managed it.
- Give a short scenario with conflicting stakeholder expectations and ask how
  they would handle the communication.
- Ask a referee how the candidate is regarded by senior stakeholders they have
  worked with directly.

### Skill: Communication (data ethics)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to expertly translate technical
concepts to non-technical audiences so they are understood by all; demonstrate
a good understanding of how technology and data products and services are
built; understand technical jargon and have sufficient knowledge to hold
meaningful conversations with data science experts on issues such as
minimising bias in data, or gathering, collecting, cleansing, triangulating
and reusing data; and effectively support data scientists and engineers in
implementing data ethics.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework repeats this skill's wording unchanged
from the head of data ethics level above — this is one of the skills where the
two levels are not differentiated in the text. Because the data ethics lead
supports the head rather than owning data ethics organisation-wide, the
baseline here sits one point below the level above's full mastery, while still
recognising that this bridging work is core to the day-to-day job at this
level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot follow a data science team's discussion of a technical bias
  issue well enough to comment on it.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a technical explanation with help, but struggles to
  restate it for a non-technical audience.
- 2 — Intermediate: Translates a familiar technical concept accurately for a
  non-technical audience, with review.
- 3 — Advanced: Holds a substantive conversation with data scientists or
  engineers on a technical issue, such as bias in a dataset, translates it
  clearly for others, and gives the team practical support in implementing a
  data ethics change.
- 4 — Expert: Is sought out organisation-wide by data scientists and engineers
  to help them implement data ethics directly in their work.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give the candidate a short technical description of a data pipeline issue
  (for example, a biased sampling method) and ask them to explain it to a
  non-technical panel member.
- Ask for an example of supporting a data science or engineering team to
  implement a specific data ethics change in their work.
- Ask a referee from a technical team whether they found the candidate's
  input genuinely useful, or too high-level to act on.

### Skill: Data ethics and privacy

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to work with stakeholders to
identify and address ethical and privacy concerns; demonstrate and
communicate how data ethical issues fit into the wider organisational
context; research developments in data ethics and privacy to improve
compliance and processes; and assess and constructively challenge proposed
data ethics policies.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Independently researching developments and
constructively challenging a proposed policy is a strong, working command of
the skill; the bullets describe informing and challenging policy rather than
owning and continuously improving an organisation-wide ethical framework,
which is the head of data ethics level's own accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how a project was checked for ethical or privacy
  concerns.
- 1 — Beginner: Applies an existing checklist to one project, with close
  supervision.
- 2 — Intermediate: Works with stakeholders to identify an ethical or privacy
  concern on one project, with some guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently researches a recent development in data ethics
  or privacy, and uses it to constructively challenge a proposed policy or
  approach before it is adopted.
- 4 — Expert: Owns and continuously improves an organisation-wide ethical
  framework, and has visibly shaped the organisation's data ethics culture.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a proposed policy or approach they
  constructively challenged, and what changed as a result.
- Ask how they keep up with developments in data ethics and privacy, and for
  an example of applying something they learned.
- Review a redacted note, assessment, or piece of advice the candidate
  produced, if available.

### Skill: Empathy and inclusivity

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to incorporate a wide variety of
views from underrepresented groups into product and policy work, using
in-depth consulting and outreach strategies; be involved in the wider
organisational diversity and inclusion plan; draw on a multidisciplinary
background and personal experience to understand the consequences of data
systems on a diverse range of stakeholders; and demonstrate a thorough
understanding of social issues, and the types of bias and discrimination
different groups can face, using this knowledge to inform their data ethics
work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework repeats this skill's wording unchanged
from the head of data ethics level above. Because this level supports the
head's organisation-wide diversity and inclusion plan rather than shaping it,
the baseline sits one point below the level above's full mastery, while still
expecting confident, independent practice in running inclusive consultation
and outreach.

Assessors: rate this skill entirely on the candidate's demonstrated
professional practice — how they design outreach, run inclusive consultation,
and translate what they learn into data ethics work. Never ask about, infer,
or record the candidate's own protected characteristics, personal identity, or
lived experience; the skill measures a working method, not who the candidate
is.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe a specific method for hearing from groups a
  project would otherwise miss.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an existing outreach process to gather views from an
  underrepresented group, with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Draws on multidisciplinary knowledge to identify how a
  data system might affect a specific stakeholder group, with guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs and runs in-depth consultation or
  outreach that reaches beyond the usual stakeholders, and can explain how a
  piece of feedback changed a specific product or policy decision.
- 4 — Expert: Has shaped the organisation's own diversity and inclusion plan
  or a comparable strategic initiative, and can show, with a concrete
  example, how their outreach strategy improved outcomes for a range of
  stakeholders.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an outreach or consultation activity they
  designed to reach a group a project would otherwise have missed, and what
  changed as a result.
- Ask how they have contributed to a wider organisational diversity and
  inclusion initiative.
- Ask for an example of using knowledge of a specific type of bias or
  discrimination to change a data ethics recommendation.

### Skill: Managing decisions and risks

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to work with consequential or
complex risks; build consensus between services or independent stakeholders;
lead others to make good design decisions; and apply different risk
methodologies in proportion to the risk.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework repeats this skill's wording unchanged
from the head of data ethics level above. Working independently on a
consequential risk and building consensus between two stakeholders is a
strong, working command of the skill; leading a group through a
high-consequence decision at organisational scale, which the level above
takes on, sits one point higher.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain why one risk was treated more seriously than
  another on a project.
- 1 — Beginner: Applies a given risk methodology to a straightforward case,
  with supervision.
- 2 — Intermediate: Selects an appropriate risk methodology for a moderately
  complex case, with some guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Works independently on a consequential risk, and builds
  consensus between two services or stakeholders with different priorities.
- 4 — Expert: Leads a group of people through a complex, high-consequence risk
  decision to a good outcome, applying a proportionate methodology and
  explaining the reasoning so others can follow it next time.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe the most consequential risk decision they have
  worked on, including which methodology they chose and why.
- Give a short scenario involving two stakeholders with conflicting priorities
  and a data ethics risk, and ask how they would build consensus.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate leading colleagues towards a
  good design decision under real risk pressure.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to ensure that the right actions are
taken to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems, and to co-ordinate the
team to investigate problems, implement solutions and take preventive
measures.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework repeats this skill's wording
unchanged from the head of data ethics level above, and even at that level the
bullets are worded more operationally than the level's other skills. At this
level, resolving a problem independently and documenting it is the expected
standard; co-ordinating a wider team through investigation and prevention
without being asked is the step this skill takes one level up.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Reacts to problems individually as they arise, with no attempt to
  anticipate or prevent recurrence.
- 1 — Beginner: Investigates a reported problem when asked, with supervision.
- 2 — Intermediate: Investigates and resolves a straightforward data ethics
  problem independently, and documents what was done.
- 3 — Advanced: Co-ordinates colleagues through investigation, resolution and
  preventive action on a data ethics problem, without needing to be asked.
- 4 — Expert: Anticipates a class of problem before it occurs, and has put in
  place preventive measures that visibly reduced how often it recurred.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a data ethics problem they investigated and
  resolved on their own, and how they documented it.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate tends to anticipate problems, or mainly
  reacts to them once raised.
- Give a short scenario — a recurring data quality issue with ethical
  implications — and ask how they would investigate it.

### Skill: Product ownership (data ethics)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to develop data ethics tools and
translate theoretical principles into practice; use a range of product
management principles and approaches; consider new ways of working and adapt
to change; capture and translate user needs into deliverables; and demonstrate
familiarity with feedback gathering, evaluation mechanisms and product
promotion.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's own wording is more modest here
than for the head of data ethics level's version of the same skill — "develop"
rather than "define and create organisation-wide", and "demonstrate
familiarity with" rather than "demonstrate expertise in" — which describes
guided, working-level practice rather than independent ownership.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe a data ethics tool or resource they have
  contributed to.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to a tool someone else owns, with direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: Develops a data ethics tool or resource for one team,
  using a recognised product management approach and gathering feedback, with
  some support.
- 3 — Advanced: Owns a data ethics tool used across more than one team, and
  adapts the way of working when evidence shows it is needed.
- 4 — Expert: Defines and creates organisation-wide data ethics tools,
  translates a theoretical principle into something people actually use, and
  demonstrates expertise across the whole lifecycle from feedback gathering to
  promotion.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a data ethics tool or resource they
  developed, from principle to something people actually use.
- Ask how they gathered feedback on a tool they worked on, and what changed as
  a result.
- Ask a referee whether a tool or resource the candidate built is still in
  active use.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a data ethics lead to work with the team to develop and
maintain an understanding of stakeholders; work with the team to develop and
implement stakeholder communications strategies; identify and resolve issues,
influence stakeholders and manage relationships effectively; and build
long-term strategic relationships and communicate clearly and regularly with
stakeholders.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The bullets describe independently identifying and
resolving stakeholder issues and building long-term strategic relationships —
a strong, working command of the skill — but they are framed as working with
the team on strategy and understanding, rather than the level above's own
direction of the stakeholder relationship strategy and negotiation with senior
stakeholders on the organisation's behalf.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Manages individual stakeholder interactions with no wider plan
  behind them.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a stakeholder plan someone else set, with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Works with the team to maintain an understanding of a
  defined set of stakeholders, with some guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently implements a stakeholder communications
  strategy, identifies and resolves an issue without escalating it, and builds
  a long-term strategic relationship with at least one stakeholder.
- 4 — Expert: Directs the stakeholder relationship strategy across their teams
  and personally negotiates with senior stakeholders to resolve a stuck issue
  and enable progress.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a long-term stakeholder relationship they
  built, and how they maintained it.
- Ask for an example of identifying and resolving a stakeholder issue
  independently, without escalating it.
- Ask a referee, ideally a stakeholder the candidate has worked with, how they
  experienced the candidate's approach to the relationship.
