# Associate Test Engineer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is a competency assessment for the quality assurance testing role of test
engineer, at the associate test engineer level. It is written for an
assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — who is judging
whether a candidate or an employee possesses the baseline abilities this role
level requires. It is not the associate test engineer's own self-report; if
you are looking for that, see the sibling skills gap form for this role level.

The assessment sets out, skill by skill, what the UK Government Digital and
Data Profession Capability Framework expects at this level, a baseline rating
on a shared 0–4 scale, and the observable behaviour an assessor should look
for at each point on that scale. Use it to structure a hiring decision, a
probation review, an onboarding check, or a capability review against the
framework, alongside everything else you already know about the person.

This is one input among several, and never the whole of a decision. See
`## Advisory`, immediately below, for why.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you know what an associate test engineer is actually accountable for and how
this level differs from the levels above it. Then gather whatever evidence
exists: a CV or portfolio, notes from a structured interview, a work sample or
task, direct observation of the person working, or references. The more of
these you have, the more confidently you can rate.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating in the competency
matrix must trace back to something the person actually did, said, produced,
or was observed doing. If you cannot point to a specific piece of evidence for
a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess from a general impression of
the person. A confident manner is not evidence of skill, and a quiet manner is
not evidence of its absence.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
using the same evidence sources where you can. Where the decision matters — a
hiring or promotion decision in particular — have more than one assessor
score independently before comparing notes and discussing any difference.

**Staying fair.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently from one candidate to the next, can produce unlawful adverse
impact even when nobody intends it. Consistent, evidence-based administration,
ideally by more than one assessor, materially reduces that risk. Never let a
rating be influenced by anything other than demonstrated, job-related
evidence — see [Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
for what that rules out.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale.
This is a proficiency scale: it rates how well the person does the thing, on
the evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example: Designing and executing tests.** At 0, a candidate cannot
describe how they would go about checking whether a simple feature works, and
has no example to offer even when prompted. At 1 — the baseline for this
level — a candidate can walk you through a simple test they built from a
guide or a template, explain in general terms why they chose what they
checked, and describe executing it with a more experienced colleague nearby
to ask when they were unsure. At 2, the same candidate would be designing and
running that test with only occasional guidance, and would be able to say why
they chose one test type over another rather than simply following a
template. Look for the difference between "I followed the steps I was given"
(1) and "I decided what to check, and checked it myself, asking for a second
opinion on the harder parts" (2).

## About this role level

**The role: test engineer.** A test engineer undertakes test planning
activity — capturing what has been discovered, defining tests, and estimating
the effort testing will take — as part of a broader, risk-based approach to
quality.

**This level: associate test engineer.** An associate test engineer works
closely with test engineers and quality assurance test analysts to learn the
activities and techniques that establish the basis of testing. At this level,
the framework expects someone to:

- have domain and business knowledge
- develop test scripts under supervision
- raise awareness of and manage defects

**Where this level sits.** The test engineer role has four levels, and this
is the first:

1. **Associate test engineer** — this level
2. Test engineer
3. Senior test engineer
4. Lead test engineer

The step up from here is test engineer, where the person takes on
responsibility for writing, debugging and refactoring test code, works
closely with developers on unit-level coverage, collaborates with analysts on
acceptance scenarios, and starts to coach and mentor other testers.

**The skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating between the technical and non-technical
2. Designing and executing tests
3. Managing, reporting and resolving defects
4. Test analysis
5. Test and quality planning
6. Test engineering

Almost everything in the framework's own wording for this level is qualified
by supervision or support: "explain", "with support", "under supervision".
That is a description of the level, not a shortcoming in the person —
associate is the framework's learning level for this role, and a low,
closely-supervised rating across most of these skills is exactly what a
well-placed associate test engineer should show. The one accountability the
level names directly, rather than folding into general supervision, is
raising awareness of and managing defects — which is why that skill's
baseline sits a point higher than the rest.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an associate test engineer to be able to explain, not
yet necessarily to run at scale: why it matters to put technical concepts
into non-technical language, and what kinds of communication work with
internal and external stakeholders and what effect each has.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording for this skill at this
level is entirely about explaining and understanding, not about running
communication with stakeholders directly — that is squarely a
"just starting to learn" description.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why a non-technical colleague would need a test result
  explained differently from a technical one, and has no example of having
  tried.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain, in their own words, why translating a technical
  finding into plain language matters, and can name at least one difference
  between talking to a developer and talking to someone outside the team.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has actually rewritten a technical finding — a failed
  test, a defect — into plain language for someone outside the team, with a
  more experienced colleague checking it before it went out.
- 3 — Advanced: Regularly explains technical findings to non-technical
  stakeholders unsupported, and adjusts what they say depending on who is
  listening.
- 4 — Expert: Is the person others ask when a technical finding needs to
  reach a non-technical audience well, and coaches colleagues on how to do
  it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain, in plain language, what a specific failing
  test they have worked on would mean for someone using the service.
- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you had to explain a
  technical problem to someone who was not technical. What did you change
  about how you said it?"
- Review a written example — a defect report, a stand-up update, an
  email — the candidate wrote for a non-technical reader.

### Skill: Designing and executing tests

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate test engineer is expected to contribute to deciding which test
types and techniques are appropriate, to follow guidance when designing,
building and maintaining simple tests that match user needs and requirements,
to execute simple tests with support, and to explain why automation adds
value in testing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Every accountability here is qualified — "contribute
to", "follow guidance", "with support" — describing someone building real
tests, but still inside a structure someone else has set.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never built or run a test, and cannot describe what a simple
  test for a feature would check.
- 1 — Beginner: Can follow a template or a colleague's guidance to build and
  run a simple test that checks something real, and needs help when the test
  does not behave as expected.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses what a simple test should check with only
  occasional guidance, and can explain the choice.
- 3 — Advanced: Selects test types and techniques unsupported, builds and
  runs tests reliably, and starts to try approaches beyond what they were
  shown.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the approach other testers follow for designing and
  executing tests, and is sought out when a test type or technique choice is
  difficult.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: ask the candidate to design a simple test for a described
  feature, with a template or guide available, and talk through their
  choices.
- Structured interview question: "Talk me through the last test you built.
  What did it check, and why that?"
- Observed scenario: pair the candidate with a more experienced tester on a
  small task and see what they need help with.

### Skill: Managing, reporting and resolving defects

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an associate test engineer to explain how defects are
reported and tracked, to follow a defect management process to report,
communicate and maintain defects with the right information attached, and to
retest and escalate a defect when that is needed.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is the one skill the level's own
accountability text names directly — "raise awareness of and manage
defects" — so the framework expects an associate test engineer to already be
doing this with only some guidance, not merely explaining it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not reported a defect, and cannot describe what a defect
  report needs to contain.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain what a good defect report contains, but has only
  reported defects with close checking from someone else.
- 2 — Intermediate: Reports, tracks and retests defects using the team's
  process with occasional guidance, and knows when to escalate rather than
  sit on something.
- 3 — Advanced: Manages the full defect lifecycle unsupported for the
  defects they find, and helps colleagues write clearer reports.
- 4 — Expert: Sets or improves the team's defect management process, and is
  the person others check their escalation judgement against.

**Evidence to gather**

- Document review: read two or three defect reports the candidate has
  written; check they contain what happened, what was expected, and how to
  reproduce it.
- Structured interview question: "Describe a defect you reported and then
  retested. How did you know it was fixed?"
- Referee question: "How does the candidate handle a defect that needs
  escalating quickly?"

### Skill: Test analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate test engineer is expected to describe quality characteristics
and explain why they matter, to analyse information such as user stories,
prototypes, processes and designs with support, and to explain what might
put a quality goal at risk.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. "With support" is explicit for the one analytical
accountability, and the rest of the skill is description rather than
independent analysis.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a quality characteristic — such as reliability,
  accessibility or performance — or say why it matters.
- 1 — Beginner: Can name and describe a few quality characteristics, and can
  read a simple user story with a colleague to spot an obvious risk.
- 2 — Intermediate: Reads a story, a design or a process on their own and
  identifies at least one plausible risk to quality, checking their thinking
  with someone more experienced.
- 3 — Advanced: Analyses requirements and designs unsupported, and reliably
  spots risks a first read would miss.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches others in reading requirements for quality risk, and
  is asked to review analysis others are unsure about.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: give the candidate a short, real user story and ask them to
  identify what could go wrong with it from a quality point of view.
- Structured interview question: "What quality characteristics matter for a
  service you have tested, and why those?"
- Observed scenario: sit in while the candidate reviews a requirement with a
  colleague and note how much of the analysis is theirs.

### Skill: Test and quality planning

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an associate test engineer to explain why quality
testing approaches, plans and strategies matter, to explain how different
delivery methodologies change them, to follow them with support, and to
explain how to measure whether they are working and why that matters.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Three of the four accountabilities are "explain"; the
fourth is following a plan "with support" — knowledge and supervised
participation, not independent planning.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what a test plan or test approach is for.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain what the team's test approach covers and why it
  exists, and follows it with someone checking their work.
- 2 — Intermediate: Follows the team's test approach independently for
  routine work, and can say how it would differ in a different kind of
  project.
- 3 — Advanced: Adapts the team's approach to a new piece of work
  unsupported, based on the risk involved.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes the team's or organisation's test strategy and can
  justify it against alternatives.

**Evidence to gather**

- Structured interview question: "What does your team's test approach say
  about what gets tested and how, and can you tell me why it says that?"
- Document review: ask the candidate to point to where their team's test
  approach or plan lives and to summarise it.
- Structured interview question: "How would testing look different on a
  two-week sprint compared with a longer, plan-driven project?"

### Skill: Test engineering

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate test engineer is expected to explain why testing processes,
environments and tools matter, to follow test engineering practices and
standards with support, and to support the maintenance of the automated tests
and tools that testing needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Both concrete accountabilities are explicitly
supported — "with support", "support the maintenance" — describing a level
for learning the machinery, not yet running it alone.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not touched an automated test suite, a pipeline, or test
  environment configuration.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain why testing tools, environments and processes
  matter, and has helped fix or extend an automated test with someone more
  experienced guiding them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Adds to or fixes automated tests within the team's
  conventions with only occasional guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Maintains automated tests and the tooling around them
  unsupported, and diagnoses why a test is failing without help.
- 4 — Expert: Builds or substantially improves the team's test engineering
  tooling, and others come to them when the tooling itself is the problem.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: ask the candidate to fix a small failing automated test, or
  extend one, with support available if they ask for it.
- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a test you helped fix that
  was failing for its own reasons, not the service's."
- Direct observation: watch the candidate add a test to the pipeline and
  note how much support they need.

rated at or above its baseline target. One or two skills below target is not
automatically a fail: weigh how central the skill is to this level's
accountabilities — for an associate test engineer, domain knowledge and
defect awareness carry particular weight, per the framework's own level
description — and consider whether the shortfall is the kind of thing that is
quickly coachable once the person is in post and working alongside more
experienced colleagues. Record your reasoning, not just the numbers: a
shortfall you can explain and plan around is a different situation from one
nobody noticed.
