# Programme Delivery Manager - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for programme delivery manager. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. For a programme delivery manager, that means far more than abstract puzzles: the assessments described here are job-specific, using the kinds of artefacts the role genuinely handles — programme plans, dependency maps, RAID logs, burn-up charts, budget forecasts, supplier contracts, governance papers, and escalation emails — and the kinds of decisions it genuinely makes, such as sequencing delivery across multiple teams, resolving high-risk commercial issues, rebalancing people and resources as priorities shift, and moderating difficult conversations between technical specialists and senior non-technical stakeholders.

The role is accountable for the delivery of complex products and services delivered by multiple teams, often carrying high technical or political risk. That accountability demands sustained, high-quality reasoning under pressure: holding a web of dependencies in mind while a governance board asks pointed questions; reading a budget variance table and knowing within seconds whether the programme is genuinely in trouble; extracting the one material fact from a dense assurance report; and choosing a defensible course of action when two directors want incompatible things by Friday.

Each of the four sections follows the same pattern: an overview of what it measures and why employers use it for programme-level delivery roles; a mapping to the role's named skills; graduated practice questions with full worked explanations; then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score for calibrated reasoning — conclusions no stronger than the evidence, restraint over manufactured certainty — and for auditable working on numeric items, since at this level the discipline of showing working is also the discipline of making reporting auditable. Use each section's per-item timing as a guide for planning a session, and debrief against the explanations below.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract shapes and sequences: dependency maps, RAID logs, release plans, resource matrices, and governance schedules.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, containing 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a similar level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

For a programme delivery manager, cognitive assessment is particularly relevant because the core professional value of the role is structured thinking at scale: managing dependencies of varying complexity across multiple teams, predicting and mitigating risks at different phases of the life cycle, and being the initial escalation point when something goes wrong. Each of those duties is, cognitively, an exercise in holding many moving parts in mind at once, spotting the inconsistency or the critical path, and deducing what follows when one element changes.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum** and **Planning**: noticing recurring signals across sprint data, dependency maps and risk logs.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Planning** and **Life cycle management**: reasoning validly from constraints to their consequences, and predicting risks before they materialise at each phase.
- **Error checking** maps to **Making a process work** and **Financial management**: spotting the inconsistency — the process step that contradicts the policy, the forecast line that cannot be reconciled with the burn rate.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the duty to **balance objectives and redeploy people and resources as priorities change**, and to the role as **the initial escalation point for the programme**.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum** and **Community collaboration**: addressing complicated risks, issues and dependencies — including those without clear ownership — and solving issues between teams at the highest level.

Because this role also **supports and coaches delivery managers**, several questions place a candidate in a reviewing or coaching position — checking someone else's plan, or deciding what feedback will most improve another person's reasoning.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a RAID log**

A RAID log uses a fixed identifier convention: letter for type (R/A/I/D), then two-digit team number, then a sequential three-digit number — R-04-017 is the seventeenth risk from team 4. Entries added this week: (1) R-04-018, (2) I-11-003, (3) D-7-102, (4) A-09-001, (5) I-11-004. Ask which entry breaks the convention.

- A) Entry 2
- B) Entry 3
- C) Entry 4
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Entry 3 uses "7" rather than "07" for the team number, breaking the two-digit convention every other entry follows. This tests spotting a small structural inconsistency quickly — identifier conventions are what make a programme-level RAID log filterable and reportable, and a broken one silently undercounts a team's items in dependency reports.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a governance rule**

A programme board's terms of reference state: "Any change with a forecast cost impact above £100,000 must be approved by the programme board before implementation, unless the programme director has granted emergency authority in writing." A delivery manager reports a £150,000 change implemented last month that never went to the board. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The change breached the terms of reference.
- B) The programme director granted emergency authority in writing.
- C) Either the programme director granted written emergency authority, or the terms of reference were breached.
- D) The delivery manager should be formally disciplined.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one permitted exception, and the facts given cannot establish which world applies — so neither A nor B must be true alone, and D is an unsupported judgement about consequences. This tests deducing only what the evidence guarantees, and — as the initial escalation point — verifying whether an exemption exists before accusing a team of a governance breach.

**Question 3 (easy) — Prioritisation of incoming demands**

9:05am Monday, four items: (1) a critical third-party API used by two teams is down, both blocked; (2) the programme director wants talking points for a Thursday ministerial briefing; (3) a finance business partner needs a budget reforecast by end of week; (4) a delivery manager asks for review of a draft team health-check survey, no deadline. Ask what should be addressed first.

- A) Item 2 — the programme director is the most senior stakeholder
- B) Item 1 — an active blocker stopping two teams outranks everything with later deadlines
- C) Item 3 — financial deadlines are immovable
- D) Item 4 — quick reviews should be cleared first to empty the queue

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is the only item both urgent and actively costing delivery capacity every hour it remains unresolved. Option A confuses seniority with urgency; C treats a Friday deadline as if due today; D spends the highest-leverage hour on the lowest-leverage item. This tests ranking by impact-weighted urgency rather than seniority, deadline proximity alone, or ease.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Dependency deduction across teams**

Rules: (i) Team Alpha's payments service cannot reach public beta until Team Beta's fraud-check API is live; (ii) the API cannot go live until a data-sharing agreement is signed; (iii) security assurance of the payments service must be completed no earlier than two weeks before public beta, or repeated. The agreement signs end of week 6; the API needs two weeks from signature to go live; security assurance was completed in week 3. Ask, at the earliest possible public beta date, what follows.

- A) Public beta occurs in week 8 and the week 3 security assurance remains valid.
- B) Public beta occurs in week 8 and security assurance must be repeated.
- C) Public beta occurs in week 6 and security assurance must be repeated.
- D) Public beta cannot occur until security assurance is repeated, so its date cannot be determined.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Agreement signs end of week 6; API live end of week 8; beta earliest week 8. Assurance valid only if completed within two weeks of beta (week 6 or later for a week-8 beta); it was done in week 3, so it must be repeated — but the repeat can be scheduled inside the existing window without delaying the date, so D overreaches. This tests chaining hard dependencies forward, then testing conditional validity-window rules against the resulting dates.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Error checking a milestone report**

A report table: Private beta start, baseline 1 June, forecast 15 June, variance +2 weeks, RAG Amber. Security assessment, 1 July/1 July, 0, Green. Public beta start, 1 August/22 August, +3, Amber. Service assessment, 1 September/8 September, +1, Green. The narrative states: "All variances are consistent and no milestone slips more than three weeks." The programme standard: variance over two weeks must be Red. Ask how many distinct errors exist across the table and narrative.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rows 1, 2 and 4 are internally consistent with the standard. Row 3's +3-week variance is correctly dated but should be Red, not Amber — one error. The narrative's "no milestone slips more than three weeks" is technically true, but "all variances are consistent" is false given row 3's RAG breach — a second, distinct error. This tests finding the minimal set of genuine defects rather than double-counting one inconsistency described two ways, or flagging correct arithmetic as an error.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in team delivery data**

Six sprints of completed story points: Team A: 40, 42, 38, 41, 40, 39. Team B: 35, 36, 34, 28, 22, 15. Team C: 55, 30, 58, 27, 60, 29. Team D: 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40. Ask which team's pattern most warrants immediate investigation as a delivery risk.

- A) Team A
- B) Team B
- C) Team C
- D) Team D

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Team A is stable; D rises steadily (plausibly maturing); C oscillates regularly around a stable average (a process quirk, not danger); Team B shows a sustained, steepening decline (35 to 15, drops widening) — the signature of a compounding problem. This tests distinguishing a striking but benign pattern (oscillation) from a dangerous trajectory (accelerating monotonic decline) under time pressure.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Making a process work**

A change process has five gates: team review (catches 60% of defects), integration check (30%), security sign-off (9%), CAB (1%, adds 6 days' delay), release scheduling (0%, adds 2 days). Releases are fortnightly. Ask for the strongest candidate to improve flow without materially increasing risk.

- A) Remove team review, since teams should build quality in rather than inspect it
- B) Make CAB exception-based (only changes meeting defined risk criteria attend), retaining the other gates
- C) Remove security sign-off, since it catches under 10% of defects
- D) Add a sixth gate to catch the defects the process currently misses

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** CAB has the worst yield-to-delay ratio (1% for 6 days). Option A removes the highest-yield step; C confuses low frequency with low value for a high-severity gate; D adds process to a flow problem. This tests evaluating process steps on yield versus cost while weighting severity, not just defect count, and preferring proportionate redesign over deletion where risk is concentrated.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Resource redeployment logic**

An urgent two-sprint task needs two senior developers. Workstream X: 3 senior developers, 4 weeks from a legally mandated deadline. Workstream Y: 2 senior developers, mid-discovery, alpha not starting for 6 weeks. Workstream Z: 2 senior developers, one the only person who understands the legacy payments integration the urgent task concerns. No workstream may lose more than one person. Ask for the best combination.

- A) Two senior developers from X
- B) One from Y and one from Z (the payments specialist)
- C) Two from Y
- D) One from X and one from Y

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The one-person-per-workstream rule eliminates A and C. Between the remaining legal options, D draws from X — the worst place to remove capacity near a legal deadline — while B draws from Y (most absorbable, six weeks of slack) plus the specialist whose knowledge the task actually needs. This tests applying hard constraints as filters first, then optimising for knowledge fit and deadline risk.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

One event per day, Monday–Friday: programme increment planning (P), supplier contract negotiation (N), go/no-go review (G). Constraints: (i) P before G, with at least one clear day between; (ii) the commercial lead, required at N, is available only Tuesday and Friday; (iii) the SRO, required at both P and G, is unavailable Wednesday; (iv) N must not be the day immediately after P. Ask on which day G must take place.

- A) Wednesday
- B) Thursday
- C) Friday
- D) It cannot be determined

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** N must be Tuesday or Friday (ii); testing Tuesday forces P too late for G to fit the week at all, so N is Friday. With N Friday, G cannot be Friday (taken) or Wednesday (SRO), and testing Monday or Tuesday for G both fail to leave room for P beforehand with the required gap — only Thursday works, with P on Monday or Tuesday. This tests pinning the most restricted event first, eliminating candidates systematically, then explicitly verifying the surviving schedule satisfies every constraint.

**Question 10 (hard) — Deduction under uncertainty at escalation**

Three statements about a missed integration milestone, all true. Team One's delivery manager: "Our part was ready; Team Two's API changed without notice." Team Two's delivery manager: "We published the change four weeks ago in the change log; any team consuming it was notified via the standard feed." The technical architect: "The standard feed has been broken for six weeks; no notifications have been delivered to any team." Ask for the best-supported conclusion.

- A) Team Two failed to publish the change and is primarily at fault.
- B) Team One failed to read the change log and is primarily at fault.
- C) Both teams followed their local process, and the failure lies in a broken shared mechanism neither team owns.
- D) The technical architect is mistaken, because Team Two says notifications were sent.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The architect's statement reconciles both accounts: the change was published but never delivered, because the shared notification mechanism was silently broken. Option A is contradicted by publication having occurred; D wrongly treats a belief that notifications were sent as evidence against a direct statement about the feed's failure. This tests finding the reconciling reading across three true-but-apparently-conflicting accounts, rather than assigning fault to one party — the classic case of ownership sitting outside any team.

**Question 11 (hard) — Applied problem solving on the critical path**

Critical path: data migration (4 weeks) → integration testing (3 weeks) → security assessment (2 weeks) → launch, fixed 8 weeks away (sequential plan takes 9 weeks, 1 week over). Options: (a) start integration testing at 75% migration complete, saving 1 week, 20% chance of 2 weeks' rework; (b) run security assessment parallel with integration testing's final week, £40,000, saving 1 week, no quality impact; (c) compress migration to 3 weeks with contractors, £60,000, saving 1 week, no quality impact. Ask which single option gives the most reliable path to the date at lowest cost.

- A) Option (a), because it is free
- B) Option (b)
- C) Option (c)
- D) Options (b) and (c) combined

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Only 1 week of saving is needed. Option (a)'s "free" saving carries a 20% chance of landing a week late anyway once rework is priced. Options (b) and (c) both reliably deliver the date; (b) does so for £20,000 less. Option D buys 2 weeks of saving for £100,000 when only 1 is needed. This tests converting every option into common currencies (time, reliability, cost), meeting the constraint exactly, and not over-paying for unneeded slack.

**Question 12 (hard) — Coaching another's reasoning**

A delivery manager argues: "Every sprint where we've had the full team available, we've hit the sprint goal. Next sprint the full team is available. Therefore we will hit the sprint goal, so I've told stakeholders the release is certain." Ask for the most accurate assessment and the most useful coaching point.

- A) The reasoning is deductively valid; the only issue is stakeholder tone.
- B) The reasoning is a reasonable induction wrongly presented as a certainty; the coaching point is to communicate probabilistic confidence, not guarantees.
- C) The reasoning is worthless because past performance never indicates future results.
- D) The reasoning is flawed because sprint goals should never be shared with stakeholders.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The inference is sound induction (genuinely useful evidence, never a guarantee, since team availability is one of many conditions), not deduction (A) and not worthless (C overcorrects). The defect is the leap from "likely" to "certain," then transmitting false certainty upward. This tests separating the logic from the communication, and coaching the distinction — strong evidence supports stated high confidence, not manufactured certainty — rather than simply rewriting the message.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate deduces only what the evidence guarantees, choosing a disjunction ("either X or Y") over a satisfying-but-unsupported single conclusion.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a striking pattern (oscillation) from a dangerous trajectory (monotonic decline) when reading team data.
- Watch for whether the candidate verifies a surviving schedule against every constraint explicitly, rather than stopping at the first plausible fit.
- Score for whether the candidate finds the minimal, genuine set of errors in a report rather than over-flagging.
- Note whether the candidate separates sound induction from manufactured certainty when coaching another's reasoning.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that imports outside professional knowledge the stem does not supply.
- Missing when a candidate ranks a request by the requester's seniority rather than its actual urgency and impact.
- Rewarding double-counting — flagging one underlying defect as two separate errors.
- Accepting a scheduling or dependency answer that ignores a validity window or side condition ("no earlier than," "not immediately after").
- Missing when a candidate treats an eye-catching but stable pattern as the priority risk over a quieter, worsening one.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures the ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from quantitative information presented as it actually arrives in this role: budget tables, burn-up charts, resource forecasts, supplier invoices, velocity data, and risk exposure figures. The arithmetic itself rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, averages, rates and simple projections — the difficulty lies in reading the data accurately, choosing the right operation, and resisting plausible-looking wrong answers under time pressure.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions based on tables, charts, or short data extracts. For senior delivery roles the questions lean towards interpretation and decision relevance.

Numeric reasoning is load-bearing for this role: influencing complex budgets, managing the budget given, acting as escalation point for high-risk commercial issues, and reporting delivery status to boards who make funding decisions on the numbers presented.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and variances** map to **Financial management**: spend against profile, variance against baseline, and knowing which base a percentage is calculated on.
- **Rates and burn calculations** map to **Maintaining delivery momentum** and **Planning**: burn rate, velocity, cycle time, and run-rate projections.
- **Ratios and proportions** map to the duty to **balance objectives and redeploy people and resources**: rebalancing people across teams and comparing supplier rates.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**.
- **Trend interpretation and projection** maps to **Life cycle management** and **Commercial management**: projecting current data forward and testing a supplier's numbers rather than accepting them.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of budget spent**

Annual budget £4,800,000; actual spend at end of month 6 is £2,640,000. Ask for the percentage of budget spent.

- A) 52%
- B) 55%
- C) 58%
- D) 60%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 2,640,000÷4,800,000=55% — option B. This tests basic percentage calculation and, in a strong answer, noting that 55% at the halfway point is 5 points ahead of a straight-line profile — worth investigating before it reaches the board.

**Question 2 (easy) — Team velocity average**

Five sprints: 34, 38, 30, 42, 36 story points. Ask for the mean velocity.

- A) 34
- B) 35
- C) 36
- D) 38

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 180÷5=36 — option C. This tests basic averaging, and — in a strong answer — noting the range (30 to 42) alongside the mean, since quoting "36" without a range invites a commitment the team will miss roughly half the time.

**Question 3 (easy) — Supplier day-rate comparison**

Supplier A: £680/day. Supplier B: £600/day plus a fixed £2,400 monthly management fee. Need: one developer for one month of 20 working days. Ask which is cheaper and by how much.

- A) Supplier A, by £800
- B) Supplier B, by £800
- C) Supplier B, by £1,600
- D) They cost the same

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A: £680×20=£13,600. B: (£600×20)+£2,400=£14,400. A is cheaper by £800 — option A. This tests including fixed fees in a total-cost comparison rather than comparing day rates alone, and — in a strong answer — recognising that the fixed fee makes Supplier B's economics improve with volume.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Burn rate and runway**

£1,860,000 remaining, 31 weeks to year end. Current burn £72,000/week; a team expansion in 6 weeks raises it to £84,000/week. Ask whether the budget lasts, and the approximate surplus or shortfall.

- A) Yes, with roughly £128,000 to spare
- B) No, with a shortfall of roughly £672,000
- C) No, with a shortfall of roughly £228,000
- D) Yes, with roughly £28,000 to spare

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Phase 1 (6 weeks × £72,000) = £432,000; Phase 2 (25 weeks × £84,000) = £2,100,000; total £2,532,000 against £1,860,000 remaining = £672,000 shortfall — option B. This tests modelling a step change in phases rather than applying one rate across the whole period, since a burn-rate step change dominates the forecast when it applies to the longer phase.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Defect rate percentage change**

Production defects: January 40, February 46, March 58. Ask for the percentage increase from January to March.

- A) 18%
- B) 31%
- C) 45%
- D) 58%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** (58−40)÷40=45% — option C. Option A is the absolute increase misread as a percentage; B divides by the wrong base. This tests anchoring a percentage change to its stated old value, and, in a strong answer, recognising that "up 45%" and "18 more defects a month" will land very differently with the same audience.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Weighted programme RAG from team data**

Confidence by workstream, weighted by budget share: Alpha (40%) 90%; Beta (30%) 70%; Gamma (20%) 60%; Delta (10%) 40%. Ask for the budget-weighted average confidence.

- A) 65%
- B) 69%
- C) 73%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** (0.40×90)+(0.30×70)+(0.20×60)+(0.10×40)=73% — option C, not the unweighted 65% mean. This tests weighted averaging correctly, and — in a strong answer — flagging that Delta's 40% confidence barely dents the headline because it is only 10% of budget, which the weighted figure alone conceals.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Resource ratio rebalancing**

60 engineers split 3:2:1 (Casework:Payments:Reporting) must move to 2:2:1 with total headcount unchanged. Ask how many engineers move out of Casework.

- A) 6
- B) 10
- C) 12
- D) 24

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Current: 6 parts × 10 each = Casework 30. Target: 5 parts × 12 each = Casework 24. Move: 6 — option A. This tests recognising that changing a ratio changes the size of a "part" when the total is fixed, rather than assuming the part size stays constant.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a burn-up chart**

Total scope: 400 points (week 0) rising to 460 (week 6, scope addition week 4). Completed work: 0 to 300 points, linear, over the same 6 weeks. Ask in which week the team completes the current 460-point scope at the current rate.

- A) Week 8
- B) Week 9
- C) Week 9.2, so during week 10
- D) Week 12

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rate 300÷6=50/week; remaining 460−300=160; time 160÷50=3.2 weeks; week 6+3.2=week 9.2, landing during week 10. Option A uses the stale original 400-point scope. This tests projecting against current (not original) scope, and rounding a fractional week honestly upward when communicating to a non-technical stakeholder.

**Question 9 (hard) — Contingency draw-down analysis**

£900,000 contingency; draws Q1 £180,000, Q2 £275,000, Q3 £310,000. Ask, if Q4 follows the trend of the last three quarters, whether contingency runs out and by roughly how much.

- A) Yes — the average draw is £255,000 against £135,000 remaining: a gap of roughly £120,000
- B) Yes — the trend suggests a Q4 draw of roughly £375,000 against £135,000 remaining: a gap of roughly £240,000
- C) Yes — the gap is £765,000
- D) No — contingency exactly covers Q4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Remaining: £900,000−£765,000=£135,000. The draws are rising (up £95,000, then £35,000); continuing the trend gives roughly £375,000 for Q4 (any defensible reading lands £345,000–£405,000), a gap of roughly £240,000. Option A answers "average," not "trend," as the question asks. This tests distinguishing average from trend projection, and recognising that even the understated average method already shows contingency running out — a clear enough signal to escalate today with options attached.

**Question 10 (hard) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Draft board paper: (i) "Coverage improved from 60% to 75%, an increase of 15%." (ii) "The defect escape rate fell from 8% to 6%, a reduction of 25%." Ask which statement uses its terminology correctly.

- A) Both
- B) Only (i)
- C) Only (ii)
- D) Neither

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** (i) is a 15-point rise, which is a 25% relative increase — "an increase of 15%" is wrong as stated. (ii)'s 2-point fall is exactly (8−6)÷8=25% — correctly stated. This tests distinguishing percentage points from percent, a common error a candidate reviewing board papers is expected to be the last check against.

**Question 11 (hard) — Supplier invoice reconciliation**

Invoice: "3 senior developers × 19 days × £750 = £42,750; 2 developers × 20 days × £600 = £24,000; gross £66,750; less 5% volume discount = £63,412.50." The contract: the 5% discount applies only when gross monthly fees exceed £100,000, and only to the portion above that threshold. Ask what the invoice total should be.

- A) £63,412.50 — the invoice is correct as submitted
- B) £66,750 — no discount applies because gross fees are below £100,000
- C) £65,081.25 — the discount should be 2.5%
- D) £61,750 — the discount should also apply to expenses

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both line items verify correctly to £66,750 gross, which is below the £100,000 threshold, so no discount applies at all — option B, meaning the supplier has undercharged. Options C and D invent discount mechanics absent from the contract. This tests reproducing an invoice from its own components and testing every adjustment against the actual contract clause — an error in the customer's favour still needs correcting.

**Question 12 (hard) — Benefits versus run-cost projection**

Business case: £120,000/quarter staff-time saving once live, run costs £45,000/quarter, one-off build £600,000 spread evenly over Q1–Q2. Service goes live start of Q3. Ask, counting from start of Q1, at the end of which quarter cumulative break-even first occurs.

- A) Q5
- B) Q8
- C) Q10
- D) Q12

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Net benefit per live quarter: £120,000−£45,000=£75,000. Recovering £600,000 at £75,000/quarter takes 8 quarters, starting Q3, ending Q10 — option C. Option A divides £600,000 by the gross saving and forgets run costs and the build period; B counts 8 quarters from Q1 instead of from go-live. This tests netting run costs against gross savings before projecting break-even, and anchoring the recovery clock at go-live rather than first spend.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies the correct base before computing a percentage change or weighted average.
- Note whether the candidate models a step change (burn rate, headcount) in phases rather than applying one rate throughout a period.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes an average projection from a trend projection when the question specifies one.
- Score for whether the candidate reproduces a stated total (invoice, business case) from its own components before accepting it.
- Note whether the candidate rounds fractional results in the honest direction when communicating to a non-technical audience.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage change computed on the wrong base.
- Missing when a candidate averages a series the question asks to be projected as a trend.
- Rewarding a runway or burn calculation that ignores a mid-period step change.
- Accepting a stated total (invoice, business case break-even) without reproducing it from its components.
- Missing when a candidate rounds a fractional week or date in the flattering rather than the honest direction.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate extracts meaning from written material under time pressure, and how well they distinguish between what a text states, what it implies, and what it simply does not address. The materials mirror this role's working reading diet: governance papers, assurance reports, policy extracts, supplier correspondence, escalation emails, service standard guidance, and board minutes.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 15 to 30 questions, most commonly true / false / cannot say, plus inference strength, main-point identification, and clear-writing judgement items. The cardinal rule is answering from the passage alone — not from professional knowledge, however extensive.

For a programme delivery manager, verbal reasoning is core infrastructure: understanding, managing and communicating to complex stakeholder groups; mediating precisely between technical and non-technical people; moderating difficult discussions about high-risk topics; and reading contracts, assurance reports and ministerial correspondence where "must"/"should"/"may" and "has decided"/"is minded to" carry real delivery consequences.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Comprehension of dense passages** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: extracting a technical finding's meaning exactly, including its hedges and conditions.
- **True/False/Cannot Say discipline** maps to the role as **the initial escalation point** and to **Commercial management**.
- **Inference judgement** maps to **understanding, managing and communicating to complex stakeholder groups**.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **speaking on behalf of, and representing the community to, large audiences** and to **supporting and coaching delivery managers**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **having an awareness of the bigger picture** and **supporting the programme director by overseeing the delivery of their vision**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot Say on a policy extract**

Passage: "Departments must complete a service assessment before a service moves from private beta to public beta. Assessments are carried out by a panel of assessors who are independent of the service team. A service that does not meet the Service Standard at assessment may be reassessed once the panel's recommendations have been addressed."

Statement: "A service that fails its assessment can never move to public beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly provides a reassessment route, contradicting "can never." This tests matching an absolute statement's strength against the passage's actual strength — a delivery manager who believes a failed assessment is terminal will make worse decisions than one who knows the reassessment route exists.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading an escalation email precisely**

Passage: "The supplier has told us the API gateway upgrade is delayed by at least two weeks. This puts our integration test window at risk. We can hold the current go-live date if the upgrade lands within three weeks, but only by compressing regression testing. I haven't yet told the service owner, as I wanted your steer first."

Statement: "The go-live date has been missed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The date is at risk, explicitly recoverable within three weeks, not yet missed: False. This tests reading the exact ladder of certainty an email supports — delay is fact, risk is fact, the miss is conditional and not yet realised — and transmitting exactly that certainty upward, no more and no less.

**Question 3 (easy) — Identifying the main point**

Passage: "The programme demonstrates strong delivery discipline at team level, with mature agile practices and reliable reporting. However, dependencies between teams are tracked inconsistently, and no single view of cross-team dependencies exists. While individual teams are likely to meet their local milestones, the review team considers that the absence of a programme-level dependency view presents the most significant threat to the integrated go-live date."

Ask for the review's main message.

- A) The programme's agile practices are mature.
- B) Individual teams will miss their milestones.
- C) The biggest risk to the integrated go-live date is the lack of a programme-level dependency view.
- D) The programme's reporting is unreliable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The load-bearing sentence follows "however," explicitly signposted as the reviewer's considered verdict. Option A is context, not the message; B and D are directly contradicted. This tests finding the main point in a politely structured "praise, however, verdict" report rather than treating the opening praise as the headline.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Cannot Say discipline on a supplier letter**

Passage: "Following the change in scope requested on 14 May, we have re-baselined our delivery plan. The revised plan extends the delivery timeline by six weeks. We consider that the scope change constitutes a compensation event under clause 12.3, and we will submit a quotation for the additional costs within ten working days."

Statement: "The supplier's additional costs have been approved."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states neither that approval was given nor refused — approval is simply a future event not yet reached in the text: Cannot Say. This tests resisting the tempting False ("a quotation hasn't been submitted, so costs can't be approved") that would import outside commercial knowledge the passage does not state, and reading a supplier's assertion ("constitutes a compensation event") as a claim, not a fact.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from board minutes**

Passage: "The board noted the programme's amber-red status. The senior responsible owner reminded members that the spending review settlement remains provisional. The board agreed that recruitment to the three vacant delivery manager posts should proceed, but that offers above the standard pay range would require her explicit approval. The next deep-dive on the payments workstream was brought forward from November to September."

Ask for the best-supported inference.

- A) The board has lost confidence in the programme.
- B) The board has increased its scrutiny of the payments workstream.
- C) The three delivery manager posts will be filled by September.
- D) The spending review settlement has been cancelled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Bringing a deep-dive forward is a concrete, directly supported act of increased scrutiny. Option A overreaches (recruitment was also approved, an act of continued investment); C converts an approval to recruit into a delivery prediction; D distorts "provisional" into "cancelled." This tests calibrated inference — choosing the conclusion at exactly the strength the evidence supports.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot Say on standards guidance**

Passage: "Service teams should deploy to production frequently — many teams deploy multiple times a day. Teams must ensure that deployments do not cause downtime for users. Where a team cannot yet deploy without downtime, deployments should be scheduled during the service's lowest-usage periods, and the team should have a plan to reach zero-downtime deployments."

Statement: "Teams are required to deploy multiple times a day."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The only "must" attaches to avoiding downtime; frequent deployment is a "should," and "many teams" is an observation, not a universal requirement — the passage's own structure contradicts the claimed requirement: False. This tests tracking the must/should/may hierarchy that governs how standards, security policies and contracts should be read and written.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Judging the clearest rewrite**

A draft reads: "It should be noted that, in the event that the aforementioned dependency on the legacy data platform is not resolved in a timely manner by the responsible parties, there exists a possibility that the go-live date may potentially be subject to slippage." Ask which rewrite is clearest while preserving the meaning.

- A) "The go-live date will slip because the legacy data platform dependency is unresolved."
- B) "If the legacy data platform dependency is not resolved by [date], the go-live date is at risk."
- C) "Slippage to the go-live date may potentially occur due to unresolved dependency issues in a legacy context."
- D) "The legacy data platform team must resolve their dependency or they will be responsible for delay."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B strips the hedges and sharpens "timely manner" into a monitorable date, preserving the conditional. A converts a conditional risk into a settled fact; C keeps the fog; D converts a risk statement into blame. This tests coaching writing toward "boards act on conditions and dates, not hedges," naming the technique rather than simply rewriting the sentence.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Comprehension of a technical explanation for translation**

Passage: "Our current architecture routes all payment confirmations through a single message queue. Under normal load this is fine, but during peak periods the queue depth grows faster than consumers can drain it, adding up to 40 minutes' latency to confirmation emails. The fix — partitioning the queue by region — is straightforward but requires a coordinated change with the notifications team, who own the consumer service."

Ask which one-sentence director briefing is accurate.

- A) "Payment confirmations are failing during peak periods and the fix requires a full re-architecture."
- B) "During busy periods, confirmation emails can be delayed by up to 40 minutes; there is a known fix, but it needs joint work between two teams."
- C) "The notifications team's service is broken and they need to fix their consumer."
- D) "There is a minor technical issue with no user impact, being handled within the team."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B preserves the trigger, the user impact, the tractability, and the cross-team dependency at correct strength. A inflates (delays are not failures; "straightforward" is not re-architecture); C misassigns cause and ownership; D minimises a user-visible impact and hides the coordination need a director could actually help unblock. This tests translation as compression without distortion — keeping the facts the audience can act on.

**Question 9 (hard) — Cannot Say under diplomatic language**

Passage: "We remain committed to the data-sharing partnership and recognise the benefits it will bring to citizens. Our legal team continues to examine the draft agreement, and we anticipate being able to respond formally once that work concludes. We note the programme's proposed go-live date and will keep it in view as our internal processes proceed."

Statement: "The other department has agreed to the proposed go-live date."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Note" and "keep it in view" express neither agreement nor objection: Cannot Say. This tests distinguishing text from subtext — an experienced reader may correctly sense a cool temperature in diplomatic language, but the test asks what the text establishes, requiring both readings simultaneously: log the fact (no commitment given) while acting on the subtext (escalate the risk).

**Question 10 (hard) — Evaluating argument strength in a governance debate**

Passage: "At the design authority, the head of security argued: 'Every major incident this year has involved a change that skipped the full assurance path. Therefore the expedited change route should be closed.' The head of delivery responded: 'Ninety-four per cent of expedited changes this year completed without incident, and the expedited route exists precisely because the full path takes eleven days. Closing it would not have prevented the incidents — it would have driven more changes into emergency releases, which have the worst incident rate of all.'"

Ask for the most accurate analysis of the exchange.

- A) The head of security's argument is conclusive: the incident correlation proves the expedited route is unsafe.
- B) The head of delivery has shown that the expedited route is safe and should be expanded.
- C) The head of security infers causation from a correlation, without base rates; the head of delivery supplies a base rate and a displacement argument, but neither speaker has yet shown what would actually reduce incidents.
- D) Both arguments are equally weak because both use statistics.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The security argument lacks a base rate to support its causal leap; the delivery response is stronger (base rate plus displacement mechanism) but has shown closure is a bad remedy, not that the route is optimally safe — neither has identified what would reduce the 6% of expedited changes that do cause incidents. This tests crediting the strongest version of each side, naming the actual gap, and resisting both overreading (B) and lazy equivalence (D).

**Question 11 (hard) — Reading a contract clause against a scenario**

Passage: "The Supplier shall provide a monthly service report no later than the fifth working day of the following month. Where the Buyer identifies a material error in a service report, the Buyer may reject the report by written notice within ten working days of receipt. A rejected report shall be corrected and resubmitted within five working days of the notice. Service credits shall be calculated from accepted reports only."

Scenario: The March report arrived on the third working day of April. On the twelfth working day after receipt, a material error understating downtime was found. Ask for the accurate statement.

- A) The supplier breached the contract by submitting the report late.
- B) The rejection window has closed, and service credits for March will be calculated from a report believed to be materially wrong, unless the supplier agrees to correct it voluntarily.
- C) The report may still be rejected, because material errors extend the rejection window.
- D) Service credits for March are automatically forfeited.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report arrived within the deadline (no breach, ruling out A); the ten-day rejection window has no exception for late-discovered errors (ruling out C); credits are calculated from accepted reports, and this unrejected report stands as accepted, not forfeited (ruling out D). This tests reading a contract clause exactly against a scenario and recognising when the contractual remedy has lapsed, leaving only negotiation — a different register from entitlement.

**Question 12 (hard) — Synthesising conflicting written accounts**

Passage: Three documents about a slipped milestone. (1) Delivery team's retrospective: "The milestone slipped five days because the test environment was unavailable for a week; we flagged this to the environments team twice before the outage." (2) Environments team's report: "Environment maintenance was published in the annual schedule in January; teams were asked to plan around the March window. We received no change requests from the programme." (3) PMO log: "Two emails from the delivery team to the environments shared mailbox, dated 3 and 10 March, requesting the maintenance window be moved; no reply recorded. The maintenance window appears in the January schedule."

Ask for the conclusion the three documents, taken together, best support.

- A) The delivery team failed to plan around a schedule published two months in advance, and its account is false.
- B) The environments team ignored two direct requests, and its account is false.
- C) Both accounts are largely accurate from their own vantage points: the window was published in January, the delivery team did send two requests, and a channel failure (an unmonitored mailbox or unlogged requests) let each team believe the other was informed; the process gap, not either account's honesty, is the finding.
- D) The PMO log should be disregarded because it conflicts with both teams.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The PMO log corroborates part of each account, and "received no change requests" is ambiguous enough (an unmonitored mailbox) to reconcile with two dated emails actually being sent — neither team need be lying. Option D discards the one independent source that actually reconciles the other two. This tests finding the reading that explains all the evidence before assigning fault, and fixing the mechanism (a monitored channel with acknowledgement) rather than adjudicating blame.

### Administration tips

- Score for the three-way test applied mechanically — could the statement be false while the passage stays true?
- Watch for whether the candidate matches a statement's strength (absolute versus qualified) to the passage's actual strength.
- Note whether the candidate reads diplomatic or hedged correspondence for its literal text separately from its evident subtext.
- For argument-evaluation items, score on whether the candidate assesses the reasoning independent of the speaker's seniority or confidence.
- For multi-document synthesis items, score on whether the candidate reconciles apparently conflicting accounts before assigning fault to one.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that imports outside professional knowledge the passage does not state.
- Missing when a candidate reports subtext as established fact in a Cannot Say scenario.
- Rewarding a distorted paraphrase that reuses the passage's vocabulary but shifts its claim ("provisional" read as "cancelled").
- Accepting an absolute statement as true on partial support, or as false on one exception where the passage doesn't actually address the general case.
- Missing when a candidate lets a senior or confident speaker's argument pass unexamined in an argument-evaluation item.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Formats vary: most effective, most and least effective, full ranking, or independent rating. Scoring reflects the judgement of experienced practitioners and the employer's professional behaviour framework — for GDAD roles, behaviours such as user focus, collaboration, ownership, transparency, proportionate escalation, and inclusion.

The typical format is 12 to 25 scenarios in 25 to 40 minutes, usually untimed per item but timed overall.

For a programme delivery manager, the SJT is arguably the most role-relevant of the four assessments, because the work is a continuous stream of exactly these dilemmas: initial escalation point for ambiguous problems, balancing objectives and redeploying people weekly, moderating difficult discussions about high-risk topics, coaching delivery managers whose mistakes must be addressed without deflating them, and resolving issues between departments where no one owns the problem.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Escalation and ownership scenarios** map to being **the initial escalation point for the programme** and to **Maintaining delivery momentum**.
- **Stakeholder and communication scenarios** map to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**.
- **Coaching and people scenarios** map to **supporting and coaching delivery managers** and **Community collaboration**.
- **Commercial and financial scenarios** map to **Commercial management** and **Financial management**.
- **Process and methodology scenarios** map to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Making a process work**.
- **Priority and integrity scenarios** map to **balancing objectives and redeploying people and resources**, and to supporting **the programme director's vision** — including moments where support means private challenge.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A delivery manager brings a hidden slip to the candidate**

A delivery manager privately admits their team will miss a milestone by two weeks and hasn't put it in tomorrow's board report, hoping to catch up first. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Agree to keep it out of the report this once, since the delivery manager knows their team best.
- B) Insist the slip and the recovery plan both appear in tomorrow's report, and offer to work through the recovery plan together today so the report shows the problem and the response.
- C) Report the slip personally, directly to the board, and let the delivery manager explain themselves at the meeting.
- D) Tell the delivery manager to move people from another team to catch up before anyone notices.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B pairs transparency (reports must be true, or every green becomes suspect) with coaching (helping build the recovery plan turns concealment into a learning moment). Option A makes the assessor complicit in misreporting; C ambushes someone who came forward voluntarily; D compounds concealment with an unexamined resourcing decision. This tests protecting the truth-telling channel and making honesty the easier path.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit in front of the programme director**

At a showcase, the programme director praises "your turnaround of the payments workstream," when in fact the turnaround was designed and driven by a delivery manager who is in the room. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the praise gracefully and thank the delivery manager privately afterwards.
- B) Redirect the credit immediately and specifically: name the delivery manager as the person who designed and drove the plan, and let them speak to it.
- C) Say "it was a team effort" and move the agenda on.
- D) Accept the praise, since as programme delivery manager the candidate is accountable for the workstream's results.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Public, specific credit develops the delivery manager's visibility and signals to everyone watching that good work is seen and named — accountability and credit are different currencies. Option A captures the benefit privately; C launders a specific achievement into an anonymous one; D confuses accountability with credit exactly backwards. This tests specific public credit as cheap, high-value culture building.

**Question 3 (easy) — A process everyone tolerates**

Every release requires a form for an "architecture review board" disbanded eighteen months ago; a governance administrator files it, nobody reads it, teams spend an hour per release on it. Ask for the most effective first action.

- A) Tell teams to stop completing the form immediately — dead process is waste.
- B) Check briefly who, if anyone, still relies on the form or its data (audit, security, the administrator's records), and if genuinely orphaned, get its retirement confirmed with the governance lead and announce the change to all teams.
- C) Raise a paper for the next quarterly governance review proposing a working group on process simplification.
- D) Leave it — an hour per release is minor, and challenging governance processes creates friction.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A proportionate, quick check followed by proper retirement (confirmed with the governance lead, broadcast to all teams) avoids both breaking an invisible dependency (A's risk) and tolerating compounding waste indefinitely (D). Option C is disproportionate delay for a free win. This tests verifying cheaply and retiring decisively rather than either unilateral action or excessive process.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two directors, one team**

Two directors separately email implying a specialist data engineering team is committed to their priority for the next quarter; the team's delivery manager is caught in the middle and morale is dropping. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Allocate the team to the priority judged more valuable and inform both directors of the decision.
- B) Split the team 50/50 between both priorities so neither director loses face.
- C) Prepare a short options paper with the delivery impact of each allocation (including a split), get the two directors and the programme director into one conversation, and drive it to an explicit, recorded decision — then communicate it to the team the same day.
- D) Ask the team's delivery manager to balance the two demands as best they can while the situation is monitored.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C converts assertions into comparable evidence, forces the conflict into one room, involves the programme director appropriately for a call between directors' priorities, and closes the loop with the team same-day. Option A oversteps the assessor's authority; B is usually the anti-pattern the options paper would expose; D delegates the conflict to the person least able to resolve it. This tests resolving conflicts at the right level with the right machinery rather than unilaterally or by delegation downward.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Supplier performance and a friendly relationship**

A supplier's delivery has degraded for three months; the friendly account director asks to "keep this between us... formal escalation would trigger our internal red-account process and make everything slower." The contract requires performance issues in the monthly service review. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Agree to keep it informal for one more month in exchange for a written recovery plan.
- B) Record the performance facts in the service review as the contract requires, tell the account director this is being done and why, and offer to work the recovery plan jointly — being clear that the relationship and the record are separate tracks.
- C) Escalate immediately to contract termination discussions to signal seriousness.
- D) Record only the least serious issues to protect the relationship while creating some paper trail.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B keeps the record true and transparent (telling the account director openly) while staying collaborative on the fix. Option A trades the organisation's contractual position for an unenforceable promise; C is disproportionate before recovery is attempted; D falsifies the record while failing candour too. This tests holding governance and warmth simultaneously — the record is the record, the relationship is the relationship.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A blocked programme dependency nobody owns**

A central team outside the programme has deprioritised an API change needed for go-live in eleven weeks; two teams hit the blocker in about four weeks; the central head of unit says it's "not in our quarter's objectives." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Instruct one of the candidate's teams to build a workaround now, so the dependency disappears.
- B) Escalate the conflict to the programme director immediately and let seniors resolve it.
- C) Work the problem at this level first: quantify the impact with dates, explore with the central team's head what would make the change feasible (a smaller change, the candidate's engineers contributing under their review, a later integration date), and set a named date for joint escalation with facts if no path exists.
- D) Raise the risk in the RAID log with a high severity rating and review it weekly.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C quantifies, negotiates creatively (loaned engineers under review is a classic unlock), and pre-commits to a joint escalation date. Option D is necessary but not sufficient (a log entry is a memory, not an action); B spends political capital before establishing the problem can't be solved locally; A commits permanent duplicated infrastructure before cheaper options are explored. This tests the escalation ladder: work it locally with creativity and a declared date, before escalating.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The struggling delivery manager**

A delivery manager is visibly struggling — late/thin reports, cancelled sprint reviews, deteriorating team flow metrics. A peer suggests quietly taking over their team's planning. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Take over the planning temporarily as suggested — delivery comes first, development later.
- B) Have a direct, private conversation with the delivery manager: share the specific observations without accusation, ask what is going on, and agree a support plan — which might include pairing on planning, temporarily reducing their load, or addressing anything personal or systemic underneath.
- C) Raise the performance concerns with HR to start a formal process, since the pattern is well evidenced.
- D) Wait a month to gather more metrics before intervening, to be fair to them.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B finds out what is actually wrong (overload, team conflict, skills gap, personal circumstances, systemic problem) through specific observation and genuine inquiry before solutions. Option A humiliates and develops nothing despite good intent; C reaches for formal process before a single conversation; D confuses fairness with delay while the team continues deteriorating. This tests noticing early and asking well, treating coaching as the first tool.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Agile dogma versus delivery reality**

A newly arrived delivery manager, strongly certified in one framework, insists a team with excellent stable kanban metrics adopt their preferred framework's full ceremony set "because that's proper agile"; the resistant team asks the assessor to intervene. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Back the delivery manager publicly — undermining a new leader's first initiative would damage their authority.
- B) Instruct the team to keep kanban since its metrics are good, and tell the delivery manager to stand down.
- C) Coach the delivery manager privately: anchor on the principle that methods serve delivery outcomes, review the team's actual flow data together, and steer them towards evolving the team's approach through experiments the team co-designs — while making clear that imposing ceremonies on a well-functioning team is not what expertise in agile looks like.
- D) Have the team vote on which methodology to use.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C coaches rather than overrules, holding the principle that methods serve outcomes above any framework, and converts a brewing authority conflict into a development conversation. Option A backs a mistake publicly; B wins the argument but undermines the new leader's authority permanently; D treats methodology as majoritarian rather than evidence-based. This tests re-anchoring a dogmatic instinct on outcomes without publicly humiliating the person holding it.

**Question 9 (hard) — The programme director's optimistic reporting**

A draft quarterly report states delivery confidence "amber, improving," but the assessor's own dependency and burn analysis says "amber-red, deteriorating," and two delivery managers privately agree; the programme director wrote the assessment personally, and the report goes out tomorrow. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say nothing — the confidence assessment is the programme director's call, and the candidate's job is to support their vision.
- B) Raise the analysis with the programme director privately today: present the data, state plainly that the report is believed to overstate confidence, recommend the wording change, and if they still disagree, ask that the report at least include the specific risks and data so the board sees the evidence base.
- C) Contact the investment board secretariat directly to flag concerns before the report lands.
- D) Persuade the two delivery managers to raise it jointly at tomorrow's programme board, so the challenge has weight.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B challenges before the deadline (help, not blame), privately (avoiding forced defensiveness), with data, and with a fallback (include the evidence even if the wording doesn't change) that preserves both integrity and the director's authority. Option A is silent loyalty that leaves the record showing knowledge without action; C bypasses the hierarchy while a direct route is untried; D orchestrates an ambush at the delivery managers' expense. This tests candour upward through the person before around them.

**Question 10 (hard) — Redeployment with a human cost**

A confirmed, in-scope redeployment moves four engineers from a team that has become the highest-performing in the programme; the delivery manager warns it will "destroy the best team culture," and a specialist among the four may resign rather than move. Ask for the most effective approach.

- A) Delay implementation quietly for a month, hoping the political urgency subsides.
- B) Implement immediately by emailing the four engineers their new assignments, since prolonging uncertainty is worse than bad news.
- C) Implement without the specialist — swap in a different engineer — to remove the resignation risk.
- D) Move fast but through people: brief the delivery manager fully on the why, speak with the four engineers directly (the specialist first, exploring what would make the move workable for them), agree with both teams how knowledge transfer and the original team's rebuild will work, and set a review point to check both teams' health.
- E) Escalate back to the programme director asking for the decision to be reversed given the resignation risk.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Option D moves at pace while treating every person properly — the delivery manager briefed first to carry the message credibly, the specialist heard as an inquiry ("what would make this workable") rather than a fait accompli, and a rebuild plan protecting the team's culture as a consciously managed asset. Option A is quiet insubordination; B is right about pace and wrong about medium; C damages the mission the specialist is needed for and rewards resignation threats; E re-litigates a confirmed decision before testing whether the difficulty is real. This tests implementing a hard, confirmed decision with speed and humanity together, not as alternatives.

**Question 11 (hard) — Budget pressure and a quality corner**

Facing a 10% in-year budget cut, the least-damaging honest option pauses one workstream and slows another. A senior stakeholder proposes instead cutting automated testing and assurance across all teams (12% of spend) since "users never see it," and some anxious delivery managers are sympathetic. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Adopt the stakeholder's proposal — it keeps all commitments visible to users and stakeholders intact.
- B) Refuse in the meeting on principle: quality is non-negotiable and the proposal shows the stakeholder doesn't understand delivery.
- C) Bring evidence to the decision: show what testing and assurance spend actually buys (defect escape rates, incident costs, rework time — and what happened historically when it was cut), recommend the pause-and-slow package with the reasoning visible, and make the quality-cut option's real cost explicit in the options paper so the trade-off is decided honestly at the right level.
- D) Accept a compromise of cutting half the testing spend, since programme management is the art of the possible.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C shows, with evidence, that the false conclusion ("cutting is free") does not follow from the true observation ("assurance spend is invisible short-term"), and puts an honest options paper — including the tempting option's real price — to the right decision-maker. Option A trades short-term comfort for compounding delivery debt; B is relationally ruinous and evidence-free; D compromises reflexively without the analysis. This tests evidence over assertion in a budget dispute, and making trade-offs honest rather than pre-censoring or capitulating.

**Question 12 (hard) — An inclusion problem inside a high-performing team**

A junior engineer confidentially describes a pattern in the best-performing team: two senior engineers dismiss and interrupt newer members and route real decisions privately between themselves; the delivery manager "doesn't seem to see it"; the junior engineer asks not to be identified as the source. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Thank them, but explain that team dynamics are the delivery manager's responsibility and they should raise it there first.
- B) Attend the team's next several ceremonies personally to observe directly, then act on what is seen — telling no one why the candidate is there.
- C) Protect the confidence while acting on the pattern: coach the team's delivery manager onto the general territory (facilitation balance, decision transparency, retro health — observable things they can look for themselves), create structural changes that help regardless of individuals (round-robin facilitation, written decision logs, retro formats that equalise voice), and check back with the junior engineer; treat it as a formal matter only if the pattern persists or worsens.
- D) Raise the specific behaviours with the two senior engineers directly, without naming sources, so the problem is addressed at its origin.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C equips the delivery manager to discover the pattern themselves through observable, structural changes that improve things regardless of individual culpability, and cannot be traced back to the source — while checking back to honour the person who took a risk. Option A closes the door on the very blind spot the junior colleague identified; B changes the behaviour being observed and delays action; D barely protects a source in a small team and treats a possibly systemic issue as an individual matter. This tests protecting a source's confidence while genuinely acting on a real inclusion problem.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate routes decisions through people and structures rather than unilaterally, even when unilateral action reads as decisive.
- Note whether the candidate confuses loyalty with silence — supporting a senior person by not challenging them, versus candour delivered privately and early with evidence.
- Watch for the escalation ladder: working the problem locally with facts and a declared date, before escalating; neither jumping to escalation nor sitting on a risk indefinitely.
- Score for whether a candidate's response protects a truth-telling or disclosure channel, rather than kindly-motivated concealment.
- Note whether the candidate fixes the instance without damaging the underlying system (a report corrected without killing candour; a resignation risk resolved without rewarding threats).

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting decisive-looking unilateral action (taking over planning, overruling a new leader, building a workaround) that skips collaboration.
- Missing when a candidate's "support" for a senior person is actually silence rather than candid, evidenced challenge.
- Rewarding escalation that is either premature (spending capital before working the problem locally) or absent (pure risk-logging that watches a fuse burn).
- Accepting a kindly-motivated concealment — withholding a slip, keeping a supplier failure off the record, delaying redeployment news — as protective rather than penalising it as it should be.
- Missing the second-order effect of a response: fixing a report while killing a disclosure channel, or removing a resignation risk while teaching that threats work.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through cognitive questions exercising pattern recognition, deduction and prioritisation under dependency management and escalation; numeric questions rebuilding budgets, burn rates, weighted confidences and supplier invoices with every step shown; verbal questions sharpening precise reading of contracts, assurance reports and diplomatically worded letters; and situational judgement scenarios rehearsing the hardest part of the role — the moments when transparency, pace, people and politics pull in different directions.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
