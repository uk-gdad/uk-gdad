# Digital Portfolio Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for digital portfolio manager, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At this level a candidate helps to co-ordinate and manage a portfolio of digital projects: helping create environments that enable teams to deliver, supporting prioritisation of a group of projects and programmes, helping teams follow governance and delivery processes, helping stakeholders understand performance and risk, and supporting more senior portfolio managers and leadership in making improvements. The material is job-specific: it uses the artefacts a digital portfolio manager genuinely handles — portfolio dashboards, prioritisation frameworks, governance papers, delivery status reports, dependency maps, and stakeholder communications — rather than abstract puzzles.

This document covers four types of assessment, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving using portfolio artefacts.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — percentages, rates, trends, and budget arithmetic that fill portfolio dashboards.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — precision reading governance guidance, delivery updates, and stakeholder correspondence, and clear writing.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — transparency, collaboration, sensible escalation, and difficult conversations without damaging relationships.

Each section explains what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, maps its dimensions to the named skills in the role, gives a substantial set of practice questions with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, then gives administration tips and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Offer any unscored practice questions the platform provides. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract items. Instead of shape sequences and letter codes, a candidate reasons about the everyday artefacts of portfolio management: RAG-rated project lists, dependency maps, governance schedules, prioritisation matrices, and delivery data.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, with 20 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is typically norm-referenced and often adaptive. Most platforms offer unscored practice questions before the timer starts; encourage the candidate to take them.

This kind of assessment matters for a digital portfolio manager because professional value lies in structured thinking across many moving parts at once: reconciling conflicting status reports, tracing the knock-on effects of a slipped milestone through a dependency chain, spotting that a governance submission contradicts the tracker, and deciding which of several competing demands genuinely needs attention first.

### How this assessment maps to the role

Each cognitive dimension corresponds to named skills in the role:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: giving tactical recommendations starts with seeing which projects repeatedly slip, which risks recur across teams, and which figures break the trend.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance**: contributing to deciding what governance and assurance is required, and helping teams follow processes, means applying conditional rules correctly — including knowing what they do not require.
- **Error checking** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Governance and assurance**: reports, papers and trackers must agree with each other, and finding the inconsistency before a board does is a core service.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Planning** and the duty to support the prioritisation of a group of projects and programmes: ranking competing demands by impact and urgency, at both task level and portfolio level.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Agile working** and the duty to help create environments that enable teams to deliver: an open-minded, iterative approach to unpicking a blocker.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a RAG history**

Six months of RAG ratings for four projects: Avon G, G, A, G, G, G. Brent G, A, A, R, A, A. Calder G, G, G, G, G, G. Derwent A, G, A, G, A, G. Which project's history most suggests an unstable reporting or delivery pattern worth investigating, rather than a one-off issue or steady state?

- A) Avon
- B) Brent
- C) Calder
- D) Derwent

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Avon shows a single one-off blip; Brent shows a genuine deterioration and partial recovery, already a known trajectory; Calder is steady; Derwent oscillates amber-green-amber-green-amber-green with perfect regularity, which real delivery rarely does. This tests reading the shape of a data series as evidence in itself — a regular oscillation suggests something systematic (a reporting-cycle artefact, inconsistent rating criteria) worth investigating rather than a genuine delivery pattern.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an escalation rule**

An escalation rule states: "A risk must be escalated to the portfolio board if its impact is rated 'severe' and its mitigation is not yet funded." Risk 17 has severe impact and funded mitigation; Risk 22 has moderate impact and unfunded mitigation. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Neither risk meets the rule's threshold for mandatory escalation.
- B) Risk 17 must be escalated.
- C) Risk 22 must be escalated.
- D) Neither risk may be discussed at the board.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The rule requires both conditions together; Risk 17 fails the second and Risk 22 fails the first, so neither triggers mandatory escalation. Option D over-reads the rule — it mandates escalation in some cases but nowhere forbids discussing others. This tests the distinction between "the rule does not require it" and "the rule forbids it" — rules set floors, not ceilings, and answering accurately when a team asks "do we have to?" keeps governance proportionate.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a portfolio summary**

A draft summary reads: "The portfolio comprises 15 projects. Eight are in beta, four are in alpha, and two are in discovery. Total forecast spend this year is £4.2m against a budget of £4.0m, an underspend of £0.2m." How many distinct errors does this passage contain?

- A) None
- B) One
- C) Two
- D) Three

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The phase counts (8+4+2=14) do not match the stated 15 projects; and spending £4.2m against a £4.0m budget is an overspend, not an underspend — the arithmetic difference is right but the direction is wrong. This tests two classic portfolio-report defects: a breakdown silently disagreeing with its total, and a correctly calculated figure with an inverted interpretation — the direction check ("is this good news or bad news, and does my sentence say which?") is as important as the totals check.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across competing demands**

Monday 9:30: (1) the portfolio board meets Wednesday, the highlight report is due to the secretariat by 17:00 today, and two projects' sections still contain unverified figures; (2) a delivery manager wants an hour today to discuss a resourcing problem that "isn't urgent yet but will be"; (3) a senior portfolio manager has asked for comments on a draft governance improvement paper by Thursday; (4) a quarterly dependency-map refresh is due Friday. Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1 first with the figures verified, then 2 today as well; 3 Tuesday or Wednesday; 4 later in the week.
- B) 2 first — people problems always outrank paperwork; then 1, 3, 4.
- C) 1, 3, 4, and ask the delivery manager to come back next week when things are calmer.
- D) Delegate 1 to the two project teams and spend the day on 2 and 4.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is urgent and high-impact and leads; item 2, a delivery manager's early warning, deserves a same-day conversation after the report is submitted, since deferring it a week squanders the early warning and discourages future flagging. Option D miscasts delegation — verifying figures for the portfolio's own report is the quality gate, not the projects'. This tests sequencing by impact-weighted urgency while protecting an early-warning relationship, rather than deferring it wholesale (C) or reversing the priority of a hard deadline (B).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across delivery metrics**

Four teams' last four sprints' story points: Team 1 30, 31, 29, 30. Team 2 24, 30, 18, 32. Team 3 40, 36, 33, 29. Team 4 22, 24, 27, 29. Which team's data most warrants a supportive conversation about delivery predictability?

- A) Team 1, because its numbers never improve
- B) Team 2, because its velocity swings widely from sprint to sprint
- C) Team 3, because it has the highest velocity
- D) Team 4, because it started lowest

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Team 1 is stable, Team 4 is climbing steadily, Team 3 declines steadily but predictably; Team 2 swings from 18 to 32 — a range of 14 around a mean of 26 — making forecasting against it impossible. This tests examining variability and trend rather than level, since the question asks about predictability specifically, and treating story points as a cross-team league table (C, D) is itself a misread of what they measure.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction through a dependency chain**

A dependency map: (i) Project P cannot enter public beta until Project Q's API is live; (ii) Q's API cannot go live until the security assessment is complete; (iii) the security assessment requires the updated data-sharing agreement to be signed. The data-sharing agreement will now be signed six weeks later than planned; everything else proceeds as planned. What can validly be concluded?

- A) P's public beta will be exactly six weeks late.
- B) P's public beta cannot happen any earlier than six weeks later than currently planned, unless some activity in the chain is compressed or re-sequenced.
- C) Q's API will fail the security assessment.
- D) The delay will have no effect on P, because P and Q are different projects.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The delay pushes every downstream gate back by six weeks if nothing else changes, but this establishes an earliest possible date, not a certainty — the chain might be compressed by parallel working. This tests translating an upstream slip into its true downstream meaning without overstating precision (A) or ignoring the dependency chain entirely (D).

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking between two governance artefacts**

The portfolio risk register says Risk R-041 ("supplier exit") is owned by the Head of Commercial, rated Amber, mitigation due 30 September. The board highlight report says R-041 is owned by the Programme Director, rated Amber, mitigation complete. Today is 4 September. Which statement best describes the situation?

- A) The artefacts agree in substance; minor wording differences are acceptable.
- B) The artefacts disagree on two material facts — owner and mitigation status — and both discrepancies must be resolved with the risk owner before the report is submitted.
- C) The report is more recent, so its version should be assumed correct.
- D) The register is the formal record, so the report should be silently changed to match it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Owner and mitigation status differ materially, and while an early completion is plausible, it is unverified. This tests treating a discrepancy between two governance sources as a question to resolve with the actual owner, not a choice to make silently by assuming recency (C) or authority (D), either of which could easily be wrong.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving: a blocked team**

A delivery team has waited eleven days for a firewall change from a central infrastructure team, blocking their private beta, after chasing twice by email; other portfolio projects will need similar changes soon. What is the most effective first move?

- A) Tell the team to keep chasing weekly and log the delay in the risk register.
- B) Escalate immediately to the infrastructure team's director demanding priority treatment.
- C) Contact the infrastructure team to understand the queue and their prioritisation criteria, get the change appropriately prioritised, and assess whether a portfolio-level arrangement is needed for the similar changes coming behind it.
- D) Advise the team to find a technical workaround that avoids the firewall change.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Understanding before escalating may reveal a routine fix (a missing priority flag); resolving the instant case; and generalising given more similar changes are coming, prevents the next several blockages, not just this one. This tests converting a local blocker into both a resolution and a systemic improvement, rather than merely documenting it (A), escalating before understanding (B), or pushing a team to bypass a security control (D).

**Question 9 (hard) — Prioritisation using a scoring framework**

A prioritisation framework scores initiatives 1–5 on strategic alignment, user benefit, and deliverability. A mandatory compliance initiative scores 2, 1, 4 — total 7, the lowest in the portfolio — but legal advice confirms a statutory deadline. What should be recommended?

- A) Rank it last, as the framework dictates; frameworks only work if applied consistently.
- B) Quietly adjust its scores upward until it ranks first.
- C) Recommend it be treated as a mandatory item outside the scored ranking, note this transparently, and flag to senior portfolio managers that the framework needs an explicit mechanism for statutory obligations.
- D) Rescore the whole portfolio with compliance as a fourth criterion before deciding anything.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A statutory obligation is not discretionary at all, so scoring it against discretionary initiatives is a category error the framework was never designed to handle. This tests treating a scoring model as a decision aid, not a decision maker — handling the exception transparently and feeding the learning upward, rather than following a flawed model off a cliff (A), corrupting it silently (B), or over-engineering an immediate problem with a heavy intervention (D).

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

Three governance reviews — Alpha assessment (A), Spend approval (S), Risk deep-dive (D) — across Monday–Friday, at most one per day. Constraints: (i) S needs the finance pack, ready Wednesday morning; (ii) A's lead reviewer is available Tuesday only; (iii) D must follow A; (iv) D and S need one clear working day between them for write-up. Which schedule satisfies every constraint?

- A) A Tuesday, D Wednesday, S Thursday
- B) A Tuesday, D Wednesday, S Friday
- C) A Monday, D Tuesday, S Thursday
- D) A Tuesday, D Thursday, S Friday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A is fixed to Tuesday by reviewer availability (eliminating C); D must follow A, so Wednesday at the earliest; testing D on Thursday (D's option) leaves no room for S within the week. D on Wednesday, one clear day (Thursday), makes Friday the only feasible day for S, and A (option A's schedule) breaches the clear-day rule. This tests anchoring the most constrained item first and propagating dependencies forward, testing boundary cases rather than assuming a plausible schedule works.

**Question 11 (hard) — Interpreting an anomaly before the board**

Preparing a board pack, the portfolio's total benefits forecast has risen 18% since last quarter, driven almost entirely by one project doubling its forecast, with no mention of any change in its status narrative. The pack is due tomorrow morning. What is the strongest course of action?

- A) Include the new figure — the project supplied it, so it is their accountability, not yours.
- B) Revert to last quarter's figure for that project, since unexplained changes should not reach the board.
- C) Query the project today: is the doubling real (and if so, get the explanation into the narrative) or an error (and if so, correct it)? If unresolved by the deadline, include the figure with an explicit caveat that it is under verification.
- D) Exclude the project from the benefits total this quarter and mention nothing.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A large, silent movement is exactly the combination that demands investigation, and either explanation (genuine scope change or spreadsheet error) needs handling before publication, with honest labelling if time runs out. This tests treating a large unexplained movement as a query first, figures second, rather than passing it through uncritically (A), silently substituting a figure (B), or omitting it (D) — the portfolio office is the quality gate between project data and board decisions.

**Question 12 (hard) — Problem solving a capacity clash across the portfolio**

Five projects' next-quarter plans place demand on a shared specialist team (capacity 3 concurrent engagements/month): 2 engagements month 1, 5 in month 2, 2 in month 3. Two of month 2's engagements support a statutory deadline; the other three are discretionary. What should be recommended to the prioritisation forum?

- A) Approve all plans — total quarterly demand (9) is within total quarterly capacity (9).
- B) In month 2, protect the two statutory engagements, and work with the three discretionary projects to move at least two engagements into months 1 or 3, using the spare capacity (1 slot each) in those months.
- C) Ask the specialist team to increase capacity to 5 for month 2.
- D) Tell the three discretionary projects to defer to the next quarter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Quarterly totals balance, but monthly capacity is not poolable — month 2's demand of 5 exceeds its own ceiling of 3, regardless of month 1's spare slot. This tests spotting an aggregation fallacy that hides a period-level infeasibility, and proposing a proportionate, collaborative re-sequencing that protects the statutory work and uses exactly the spare capacity that exists elsewhere, rather than asking the constraint itself to move first (C) or over-correcting with wholesale deferral (D).

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads the shape of a data series** as evidence, not just its latest value.
- **Watch for whether a candidate distinguishes a rule's floor from a ceiling** — "not required" is not "forbidden."
- **Note whether a candidate treats a discrepancy between two sources as a question to verify**, rather than resolving it by assumed recency or authority.
- **Note whether a candidate checks period-level as well as total-level arithmetic** in capacity or budget questions.
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that concludes more than the evidence supports** — "cannot be earlier than X" read as "will be X."
- **Missing when a candidate treats rule-floors as ceilings.**
- **Crediting an answer that assumes the newer or more formal document is correct** without verifying at source.
- **Missing when a candidate league-tables incomparable, team-local metrics.**
- **Missing an aggregation fallacy** where a total balances but a specific period does not.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and confidently a candidate handles the quantitative material this role genuinely produces and consumes: spend against budget, RAG distributions, milestone completion rates, benefits forecasts, velocity trends, and capacity arithmetic. Questions typically present a table, chart, or short data narrative, followed by calculations and interpretations.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 35 minutes, with 15 to 20 questions, usually with a calculator permitted. Multiple-choice options are typically engineered so that common errors — dividing by the wrong base, averaging percentages, reading the wrong column — each produce a wrong answer.

This kind of assessment matters for a digital portfolio manager because the role is a quantitative communication hub: numbers flow up from projects and are validated, aggregated and interpreted before informing prioritisation, funding and escalation decisions. An error at this desk multiplies — one wrong percentage in a highlight report can misdirect a board.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages, rates and percentage change** map to **Communicating analysis and insight**: dashboards and highlight reports speak almost entirely in these forms.
- **Weighted averages and combined rates** map to **Governance and assurance**: portfolio-level figures aggregate unequal projects, and knowing when a simple average misleads keeps the assurance picture honest.
- **Budget and forecast arithmetic** maps to **Planning** and **Strategic thinking**: run-rate forecasts and burn analysis provide early warning of overspend.
- **Capacity and demand arithmetic** maps to **Planning** and the duty to support prioritisation: turning team capacities and project demands into feasible schedules.
- **Reading tables and charts precisely** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: only accurately extracted figures can be accurately explained.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio RAG percentage**

A portfolio of 24 projects has 15 green, 6 amber, 3 red. What percentage of projects are not green?

- A) 25.0%
- B) 37.5%
- C) 62.5%
- D) 9.0%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Not green = 6 + 3 = 9; 9 ÷ 24 = 37.5%. Option C is the green percentage — the complement of what was asked. This tests reading a negatively phrased question carefully and restating it in one's own words before calculating.

**Question 2 (easy) — Remaining budget with commitments**

A £480,000 annual budget has £310,000 spent to date, plus £95,000 contractually committed but not yet paid. How much budget remains uncommitted?

- A) £170,000
- B) £75,000
- C) £265,000
- D) £85,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** £480,000 − £310,000 − £95,000 = £75,000. Option A forgets the commitment. This tests distinguishing "remaining budget" from "remaining uncommitted budget" — the figure a board needs when deciding whether a project can absorb a new cost.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a delivery table**

Quarterly milestone data: Q1 planned 40, completed 32. Q2 planned 45, completed 36. Q3 planned 38, completed 34. Q4 planned 50, completed 38. In which quarter was the completion rate highest?

- A) Q1
- B) Q2
- C) Q3
- D) Q4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rates: Q1 80%, Q2 80%, Q3 ≈89.5%, Q4 76%. Q4 completed the most milestones in raw terms but had the worst rate. This tests distinguishing volume from rate, since they answer different questions and a stakeholder needs to be told which one is being reported.

**Question 4 (easy) — Percentage change in spend**

Monthly spend rose from £220,000 in May to £253,000 in June. What was the percentage increase?

- A) 13%
- B) 15%
- C) 33%
- D) 11.5%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Change £33,000 ÷ £220,000 (original) = 15%. Option A divides by the new value. This tests always dividing by the original figure, and, for a strong candidate's answer, estimating first (10% is £22,000, 20% is £44,000, so £33,000 points to 15%) before calculating precisely.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average delivery confidence**

Three programmes report confidence scores weighted by budget share: Alpha £6m, score 80. Beta £3m, score 60. Gamma £1m, score 40. What is the budget-weighted average confidence score?

- A) 60
- B) 70
- C) 72
- D) 66

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (6×80 + 3×60 + 1×40) ÷ 10 = 700 ÷ 10 = 70. The unweighted average (60, option A) treats all three programmes equally despite Alpha holding 60% of the budget. This tests weighting by budget rather than averaging scores, and stating clearly which was used when reporting to leadership.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Run-rate forecast against budget**

Seven months into the year, £2.38m of a £4.2m budget has been spent. If spend continues at the same average monthly rate, what is the forecast full-year spend and variance?

- A) £4.08m — £0.12m under budget
- B) £4.2m — on budget
- C) £4.42m — £0.22m over budget
- D) £3.9m — £0.3m under budget

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Monthly rate £0.34m × 12 = £4.08m, £0.12m under the £4.2m budget. This tests a run-rate forecast, sense-checked by proportion (7 months is 58.3% of the year; £2.38m is 56.7% of budget, consistent with a modest underspend) — and, for a strong candidate's answer, flagging early is what makes a forecast actionable rather than merely descriptive.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Ratio-based cost allocation**

A £270,000 shared platform is funded by three projects in the ratio 5:3:1 (Tay:Esk:Nairn). A fourth project, Spey, joins with volume equal to Nairn's; the cost is re-split by volume. How much does Tay pay after Spey joins?

- A) £150,000
- B) £135,000
- C) £120,000
- D) £96,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The ratio becomes 5:3:1:1 (10 parts); one part = £27,000; Tay's 5 parts = £135,000. Option A uses the old 9-part split, forgetting a new joiner changes everyone's denominator. This tests re-deriving a ratio split when membership changes rather than reusing an outdated part-value.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Interpreting percentage-point versus percentage change**

The portfolio's milestone completion rate rose from 72% in Q2 to 81% in Q3. Which statement is numerically correct?

- A) The completion rate rose by 9%.
- B) The completion rate rose by 9 percentage points, a relative increase of 12.5%.
- C) The completion rate rose by 12.5 percentage points.
- D) The completion rate rose by 81%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Absolute change 81−72=9 points; relative change 9÷72=12.5%. This tests distinguishing percentage points from relative percentage — the same true improvement described one way sounds modest and the other substantial, and choosing the correct framing (or catching a supplier's misleading one) is a core skill.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Combined completion rate across unequal projects**

Project Frome passed 180 of 200 test cases (90%); Project Kennet passed 45 of 100 (45%). A stakeholder asks: "So overall we're at about 67.5%?" What is the correct combined pass rate, and what error has the stakeholder made?

- A) 67.5% — no error; averaging the two rates is correct.
- B) 75% — the stakeholder averaged the rates instead of combining the totals.
- C) 72% — the stakeholder should have weighted by project budget.
- D) 62.5% — the stakeholder used the wrong denominators.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 225 total passed ÷ 300 total cases = 75%. Averaging the two rates (67.5%) understates the true position because it treats the larger, stronger project equally with the smaller one. This tests correcting a stakeholder's arithmetic gracefully and precisely — a genuine daily moment in the role.

**Question 10 (hard) — Benefits realisation trajectory**

A business case promises £1.2m annual benefits once fully rolled out. Roll-out is 40% complete, and measured benefits are running at £360,000/year, assumed to scale with roll-out. Which statement best describes the trajectory?

- A) On track — £360,000 is 30% of £1.2m, matching roll-out progress.
- B) Behind — benefits should be £480,000 at 40% roll-out; the programme is realising 75% of expected benefits at this stage, projecting to £0.9m rather than £1.2m if the shortfall persists.
- C) Ahead — £360,000 per year exceeds the £1.2m target's monthly equivalent.
- D) It is impossible to compare an annual figure with a percentage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Expected benefit at 40% roll-out is £480,000; actual £360,000 is 75% of that; projected forward at the same ratio gives £900,000 at full roll-out. This tests building the proportional expectation first, then comparing actual against it — converting "we'll see at the end" into a mid-course, actionable warning.

**Question 11 (hard) — Capacity arithmetic with a constraint**

A shared design team has 4 designers at 15 productive days/month each. Committed demand: Project A 22 days, Project B 18, Project C 12. A new discovery, Project D, requests 15 days in the same month. What is the maximum of D's requested days the team can absorb without cutting existing commitments?

- A) 15 — all of them
- B) 8
- C) 0 — the team is already over capacity
- D) 10

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total capacity 60 days; committed demand 52 days; headroom 8 days. This tests a straightforward capacity calculation, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting that planning shared teams to 100% theoretical capacity ignores leave and training, so real headroom is often smaller still.

**Question 12 (hard) — Untangling a two-baseline dashboard claim**

A dashboard states: "Spend to date: £1.44m, which is 96% of profiled spend. Profiled spend to date represents 50% of the full-year budget." A board member concludes: "So they've spent 96% of the annual budget — they're about to run out." What is the actual position?

- A) The board member is right: 96% of the annual budget is spent.
- B) Spend to date is 48% of the annual budget of £3.0m; the programme is marginally behind its spend profile at the halfway point, not close to running out.
- C) Spend to date is 50% of the annual budget; the programme is exactly on profile.
- D) The annual budget is £1.5m and the programme has £60,000 left.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Profiled spend = £1.44m ÷ 0.96 = £1.5m; annual budget = £1.5m ÷ 0.5 = £3.0m; spend to date as a share of budget = 1.44 ÷ 3.0 = 48%. This tests unpacking a two-baseline claim carefully — the board member chained "96%" to the wrong base — and, for a strong candidate's answer, restating the figure plainly to prevent the confusion recurring.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate names the base of every percentage** before quoting or comparing it.
- **Watch for whether a candidate computes combined or weighted rates from totals** rather than averaging separate percentages.
- **Note whether a candidate checks period-level arithmetic** in a capacity or budget question, not just the total.
- **Note whether a candidate reconciles an allocation split back to its total** after a ratio calculation.
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that divides by the wrong base** — change by the new value, spend by profile instead of budget.
- **Crediting an answer that averages rates across unequal groups** rather than using totals over totals.
- **Missing when a candidate forgets contractual commitments or other caveats** that change a headline figure's meaning.
- **Crediting an answer that conflates volume with rate**, or percentage points with percentage.
- **Missing when a candidate trusts a matching option** rather than their own derivation.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely a candidate reads, interprets and evaluates written material of the kind that crosses a portfolio manager's desk daily: governance guidance, spend control policy, delivery status narratives, board minutes, stakeholder correspondence, and standards extracts. The dominant format presents a passage followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot say. Supplementary formats test inference, assumption-spotting, and clear-writing judgement.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes, covering four to six passages with three to five statements each. The cardinal rule: judge statements against the passage alone, setting aside professional knowledge.

This kind of assessment matters for a digital portfolio manager because the role is textual as much as numerical: project narratives are digested and reproduced faithfully at portfolio level, governance rules are interpreted for delivery teams who will act on the interpretation, and papers are drafted whose wording determines what boards understand and decide.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Comprehension and detail extraction** map to **Governance and assurance**: helping teams follow processes means reading them with exactness — what is mandated, permitted, or silent.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: portfolio reports must distinguish what source updates actually say from what is inferred.
- **Inference and assumption-spotting** map to **Strategic thinking** and **Governance and assurance**: evaluating a team's case for a lighter process, or a paper's case for investment, means finding the unstated assumptions that make or break it.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: choosing between a jargon-heavy draft and a plain one.
- **Reading under time pressure** maps to **Agile working**: extracting a text's essence fast without sacrificing accuracy.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–3)*

"Portfolio governance framework, section 4: All projects must obtain spend approval before awarding any contract exceeding £100,000. Approval requests must be submitted at least ten working days before the planned award date. The portfolio office may fast-track requests where a delay would cause a statutory deadline to be missed; fast-tracking reduces the notice period to three working days but requires the finance director's personal sign-off. Contracts below the £100,000 threshold follow standard procurement rules and do not require portfolio-level approval. Splitting a requirement into multiple contracts to remain below the threshold is prohibited."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "A project awarding a £95,000 contract does not require portfolio-level spend approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only with the finance director's sign-off

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £95,000 is below the £100,000 threshold, so it does not require portfolio-level approval, exactly as stated. Option D transplants the finance director's sign-off from the fast-track clause, where it belongs to a different situation. This tests reading each clause with its own scope and not letting a detail migrate between clauses.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: "A fast-tracked approval request can be submitted three working days before the planned award date, provided the finance director personally signs it off and the delay would otherwise cause a statutory deadline to be missed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for contracts under £100,000 only

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** All three fast-track conditions are reproduced faithfully and in the right relationship. Option D inverts the framework's logic — fast-tracking concerns above-threshold contracts. This tests reconstructing every element of a compound rule individually, since a single altered condition would flip the answer.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "The portfolio office must fast-track a request whenever a statutory deadline is at risk."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, if the request also has finance director sign-off

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the office "may fast-track" — a discretion, not the "must... whenever" the statement claims. This tests the may/must distinction, arguably the single most consequential reading skill in governance: a team told "they must fast-track us" will plan on an approval it may not get.

*Passage 2 (for Questions 4–6)*

"Extract from a delivery status narrative: 'The payments migration remains amber. We completed the supplier integration two weeks ahead of schedule, and performance testing has begun early as a result. However, the third-party identity service released a breaking API change with thirty days' notice, and our remediation work will consume the buffer we had built for the release window. If a further breaking change lands before go-live, the December release date will not be achievable. We have asked the supplier for their change roadmap but have not yet received it. On current information we remain committed to December.'"

**Question 4 (easy) — Identifying the main point**

Which sentence best captures the update's overall message?

- A) "The project is ahead of schedule following early completion of supplier integration."
- B) "The December release has been cancelled due to third-party API changes."
- C) "The project remains committed to December, but its schedule buffer is now consumed, leaving no resilience against further third-party changes."
- D) "The project is waiting for a supplier roadmap before making any commitments."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The commitment stands, the buffer is gone, and the plan is fragile — a faithful summary must carry all three. Option A cherry-picks good news; B overshoots into a claim the update explicitly denies. This tests selecting what a summary needs to include for a decision, preserving the conditional structure the source uses.

**Question 5 (moderate)**

Statement: "The project's schedule buffer was consumed by the delay to supplier integration."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, according to the supplier's roadmap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Supplier integration was not delayed — it finished early; the buffer was consumed by remediation for the identity service's API change. This tests a causal-attribution question, catching a plausible-sounding recombination of real elements with the wrong cause reattached to the wrong effect.

**Question 6 (hard) — Evaluating the commitment's basis**

Which unstated assumption does the December commitment most critically depend on?

- A) That performance testing began early.
- B) That no further breaking change from the third-party service lands before go-live.
- C) That the portfolio office approves the remediation work.
- D) That the December release was in the original business case.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The update itself states the condition on which the date depends. This tests identifying an argument's load-bearing, unproven assumption — the sharp question a portfolio manager should ask rather than merely relaying "committed to December."

*Passage 3 (for Questions 7–9)*

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applying the guidance**

Two initiatives both require the portfolio's only machine-learning engineer next quarter. An analyst proposes quietly scheduling one first "to avoid bothering the board with a minor clash." Based only on the passage, what should be done?

- A) Accept the analyst's proposal — administrative efficiency is good practice.
- B) Present the trade-off explicitly to the prioritisation board, since the initiatives compete for a scarce capability, and ensure the decision and rationale are recorded and published.
- C) Reject both initiatives until a second engineer is hired.
- D) Ask the initiatives to negotiate directly with each other and report the outcome.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage speaks directly to this configuration and prohibits resolving it administratively. This tests recognising when an apparently minor operational choice is really a prioritisation decision in disguise — sequencing two initiatives against one scarce resource is a decision belonging to the accountable body, made in daylight.

**Question 9 (hard)**

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because government decisions must be transparent

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage specifies publication "to all affected teams" — internal publication — and is silent about any wider channel. This tests recognising that specifying one audience is not excluding others; the passage neither supports nor contradicts a wider publication claim.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

Which version of a highlight report summary serves a senior, mixed-specialism board best?

- A) "Payments migration: amber. Leveraging the accelerated supplier integration, the workstream has operationalised early performance testing; however, third-party API volatility has necessitated remediation activity absorbing schedule contingency, rendering the December milestone contingent on the non-recurrence of breaking changes."
- B) "Payments migration: amber. Integration finished two weeks early and testing has started. A third-party API change has used up the schedule buffer. December remains the plan, but another such change would make it unachievable. We are pressing the supplier for its change roadmap; board support in obtaining it would help."
- C) "Payments migration: amber. Some third-party issues have arisen but the team is confident and December is still on."
- D) "Payments migration: amber. The identity service provider issued a breaking change to their OAuth endpoints with a 30-day deprecation window, requiring re-work of the token-handling middleware and consuming the release contingency."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B gives the position, the conditional risk, and a specific ask in plain English. A is the same facts in officialese; C launders a fragile position into false comfort; D is accurate but written for engineers. This tests writing a highlight report line a non-specialist reader could repeat accurately to someone else.

*Passage 4 (for Questions 11–12)*

Incomplete submissions will be returned, and the clock starts only when a complete submission is received. Departments should note that the commitment applies to approval decisions, which include approval, conditional approval, and rejection.'"

**Question 11 (hard)**

Statement: "From October, departments will receive approval of their digital spend submissions within 15 working days."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, for complete submissions only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The commitment is to a decision within 15 days for complete submissions — and decisions include rejection, not only approval. This tests reading a service-level commitment as a promise of process and timescale, never of outcome — a department could be rejected on day 15 and the commitment fully honoured.

**Question 12 (hard) — Inference from the statistics**

Which one of the following can be properly inferred from the passage?

- A) Before the review, half of approval cases took longer than 18 working days.
- B) The 15-day commitment will reduce median approval time by 3 working days.
- C) Most departments submit incomplete submissions.
- D) After October, no case will take longer than 15 working days.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A median of 18 days means, by definition, half of cases exceeded it. Options B and D treat a future commitment as an already-achieved result; C has no frequency data. This tests knowing what a median statistic actually asserts, and distinguishing a stated intention from a reported outcome.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate distinguishes modal verbs precisely** — may versus must, should versus will.
- **Watch for whether a candidate keeps causes attached to the correct effects** when summarising.
- **Note whether a candidate converts a specified inclusion into an unwarranted exclusion** ("published to teams" read as "not published anywhere else").
- **Note whether a candidate reads a commitment as a process or timescale promise, not an outcome promise.**
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that conflates "False" with "not mentioned".**
- **Missing when a candidate lets a true detail migrate from one clause into the wrong context.**
- **Crediting a confident-sounding answer justified from outside the passage** ("because government decisions must be transparent").
- **Rewarding a summary that omits fragility to make the news pleasant.**
- **Missing an outcome/process switch** — "approval within 15 days" versus "an approval decision within 15 days."

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks a candidate to evaluate possible responses — selecting the most effective, the least effective, ranking all options, or rating each independently. It measures professional judgement: how a candidate balances honesty, delivery pressure, relationships, process and escalation when they pull in different directions.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed. There is rarely a flawless option; the test measures whether a candidate can distinguish good from slightly-flawed from harmful, consistent with behaviours like honesty and transparency, user focus, collaboration, ownership, appropriate escalation, inclusion, and proportionate use of process.

This kind of assessment matters for a digital portfolio manager because the role holds information that others act on, operating between teams and authority rather than over either — what to flag, what to soften, whom to involve, when to push, shapes whether governance is trustworthy or merely performed.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Transparency and reporting scenarios** map to **Communicating analysis and insight** and the duty to help stakeholders understand performance and risk: dilemmas about softening reports or sitting on emerging bad news.
- **Escalation scenarios** map to **Governance and assurance** and the duty to support senior portfolio managers and leadership: knowing what to resolve directly, what to escalate, and how.
- **Team-facing scenarios** map to **Agile working** and the duty to help create environments that enable teams to deliver: acting as enabler versus enforcer.
- **Stakeholder dilemmas** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing without authority, building consensus between conflicting interests.
- **Prioritisation dilemmas** map to **Planning** and the duty to support prioritisation of a group of projects and programmes.
- **Improvement scenarios** map to **Strategic thinking**: fixing the system as well as the instance.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Unverified good news before the board**

The day before the board, a delivery manager says his project's critical performance issue "is basically fixed — the overnight test run should confirm it," and asks for the draft rating to move from red to amber now so the board "sees the real picture." What is the most effective response?

- A) Change the rating to amber — he knows his project best, and boards dislike stale bad news.
- B) Keep red, but add a note that a fix is in final testing with results expected before the board; update the rating only if the overnight results confirm the fix.
- C) Keep red and say nothing about the fix, since unconfirmed progress is not reportable.
- D) Change the rating to amber but keep a private note to revert it if the test fails.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B keeps the rating evidence-based while giving the board genuinely useful new information and a clean trigger for updating on evidence. Option A rates on optimism; C omits material favourable context unfairly; D is quiet double-bookkeeping. This tests keeping ratings evidence-based while narratives carry momentum — boards handle "red, fix in final verification" well.

**Question 2 (easy) — A team struggling with governance paperwork**

A small team repeatedly submits late, incomplete governance returns; their delivery manager says the templates assume a programme office they don't have, and the returns genuinely are heavyweight for their size. What is the most effective response?

- A) Insist on full compliance — consistency across the portfolio is non-negotiable.
- B) Exempt the team from returns until they are bigger.
- C) Work with the team to agree a proportionate lightweight return that still provides the essential assurance data, and propose the tiered format to senior portfolio managers as a portfolio-wide improvement for small teams.
- D) Complete the returns for them each month personally.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Proportionality is a core assurance principle; option C fixes the instance and proposes fixing the system. Option A produces late, grudging, low-quality data; B creates a blind spot; D hides the process defect and drains capacity. This tests treating governance as a service to decision making, not a compliance ritual.

**Question 3 (easy) — Credit and visibility**

A director praises "the portfolio office's excellent new dependency dashboard," which was designed almost entirely by a portfolio analyst not present. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the praise on the office's behalf and move on — attribution details do not matter at senior level.
- B) Thank the director and name the analyst as the dashboard's designer, then let the analyst know their work was praised at the meeting.
- C) Say nothing in the meeting but tell the analyst privately that the dashboard went down well.
- D) Suggest the director email the analyst directly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Naming the analyst accurately allocates visibility, a career resource, and builds the trust that makes people bring their best work forward. Option A lets an inaccurate impression stand; C wastes the public benefit; D outsources what is the manager's to do. This tests passing credit down as a habit, not an afterthought.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Pressure to pre-announce a decision**

A programme director, frustrated by prioritisation delays, asks for a supplier to be told an expansion "is approved" since the board "will obviously wave it through next week" and two weeks of mobilisation would otherwise be lost. What is the most effective response?

- A) Confirm to the supplier — the probability is high and the delivery benefit is real.
- B) Refuse and report the director's request to the board as attempted interference.
- C) Decline to pre-announce a decision the board has not made, explain why, and offer what can be legitimately done: check whether any no-regrets mobilisation steps can proceed at the programme's own risk, and see whether the decision can be expedited — by an earlier paper, chair's action, or out-of-committee approval.
- D) Tell the director to contact the board chair himself and stay out of it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A likely decision is not an actual one, and pre-announcing replaces governance with prediction; option C refuses while pairing the refusal with genuine, legitimate help. Option A risks commercial exposure if the board attaches conditions or declines; B converts a first ask into an incident. This tests holding the line while helping within it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Discovering an error with money attached**

Reconciling figures, a candidate discovers they misallocated £150,000 of spend between two projects two months ago, making one look overspent and one underspent; the overspent project's team took awkward questions at the last board because of it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Correct the allocation in this quarter's figures without comment; the totals were always right.
- B) Correct it, tell the senior portfolio manager what happened, ensure the correction and its cause are noted to the board and the affected team, and check whether the reconciliation gap that let it through needs fixing.
- C) Correct it and apologise privately to the affected delivery manager, keeping it otherwise informal.
- D) Leave it — the totals are right, the error will wash out over the year, and reopening it invites blame.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The error caused real, visible harm to a team's standing, so correcting it must be as visible as the harm; a two-month-old undetected error also flags a reconciliation control worth fixing. Option A abandons the affected people; D compounds error with concealment. This tests owning an error at the visibility level of its impact and mining it for a systemic fix.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: a slipping flagship**

The portfolio's most visible, personally sponsored project is quietly slipping — two milestones missed, a third at risk, delivery manager still reporting green. Raised informally once, she said the team "will catch up." Which response is **least effective**?

- A) Review the milestone evidence with her, agree what the data supports, and explain that the portfolio's report must reflect the evidenced position, with her recovery narrative alongside.
- B) Rate the project amber in the portfolio-level view personally, clearly noting the office's assessment differs from the team's own rating, and tell her this is being done and why.
- C) Wait one more reporting cycle to see whether the catch-up materialises, keeping notes.
- D) Alert the senior portfolio manager that the flagship's self-reporting and the milestone evidence have diverged, before the gap widens further.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A, B, and D are all defensible responses to a known evidence-versus-reporting divergence; C converts that known divergence into deliberate delay on the most scrutinised project in the portfolio, with the candidate's own notes documenting the knowledge. This tests recognising that watchful waiting has its place for genuine early-stage wobbles, but not for a known evidence gap on a flagship.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Two stakeholders, one truth**

A finance business partner says spend forecasts must be "prudent — plan on the high side"; a delivery director says forecasts must be "ambitious — stretch targets drive pace." Delivery managers, caught between them, ask which instruction to follow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to follow finance's instruction — money outranks pace.
- B) Tell them to follow the delivery director's — delivery outcomes are what matter.
- C) Tell them to submit their honest central estimate this month, and get finance and the delivery director together to agree a single forecasting convention — likely a central estimate with explicit ranges or scenarios serving both needs — then have it documented in the return guidance.
- D) Tell them to submit prudent numbers to finance and ambitious ones to the delivery director.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a requirements conflict, not a seniority contest; C protects the data now with an honest interim estimate, convenes the principals to agree a standard serving both needs, and documents the outcome. Options A and B resolve by rank, leaving a real need unmet; D institutionalises two sets of books. This tests brokering a forecasting convention rather than adjudicating between senior stakeholders by rank.

**Question 8 (moderate) — An informal heads-up with strings attached**

A delivery manager privately shares that a data quality problem might mean re-running three months of migrated records, "as a friend, not reporting it," and asks for two weeks before anything goes in a report — while other projects consume this project's data downstream. What is the most effective response?

- A) Honour the confidence fully — informal trust channels are precious, and two weeks is reasonable.
- B) Thank him for the early warning, but explain this is material — especially with downstream consumers — so it cannot stay off the record: agree what gets said now (an emerging data quality issue, being sized, report in two weeks), ensure downstream projects are alerted to a possible impact, and support his team in the sizing.
- C) Refuse the conversation — "there is no off the record with the portfolio office" — and log it immediately as a red risk.
- D) Keep it informal but secretly warn the downstream projects personally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B honours the spirit of the confidence while being honest that materiality, not friendship, determines reportability — downstream teams get warned while mitigation is still cheap, and the delivery manager learns early disclosure is rewarded, keeping the channel open. Option A creates an information gap downstream teams will resent; C torches the relationship; D is dishonest in both directions. This tests holding both informal trust and formal duty as compatible, not opposed.

**Question 9 (hard) — Governance shortcut under deadline**

A statutory deadline means a contract must be awarded within three weeks, but spend approval normally takes four; a programme director proposes splitting it into two smaller awards, each below the approval threshold, "purely as a timing fix — we'll consolidate later." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — the statutory deadline creates genuine urgency, and the rules allow below-threshold awards.
- B) Refuse, and report the proposal to the finance director as attempted circumvention.
- C) Explain that threshold-splitting is explicitly prohibited regardless of motive, and immediately pursue the legitimate fast route: an expedited approval on statutory-deadline grounds — most spend control frameworks provide one — escalating today to the senior portfolio manager and the approvals team to compress the timeline.
- D) Suggest the project proceed at risk and regularise the approval retrospectively.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Threshold-splitting is motive-blind and expressly banned; C refuses the prohibited path and mobilises the legitimate one in the same breath. Option A exposes the project to procurement challenge; B escalates punitively over a first ask; D is approval theatre. This tests holding a rule absolutely while treating the underlying need as legitimate and knowing the compliant fast path.

**Question 10 (hard) — Inclusion under delivery pressure**

Facilitating the fortnightly prioritisation forum, a candidate notices two senior delivery managers dominate discussion and decisions consistently favour their projects, while two quieter, newer delivery managers — one of whom raised a concern last month that was talked over and later proved right — have stopped contributing. Decisions are formally sound and nobody has complained. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing — decisions are sound, attendance is full, and adults manage their own airtime.
- B) Redesign the forum's format to structurally rebalance contribution — pre-circulated written cases from every project, timed slots, round-robin views before open discussion, and active chairing to bring in quieter members — and speak to the two quieter managers beforehand to rebuild their confidence that contributions land.
- C) Publicly call out the two dominant managers at the next forum for excluding colleagues.
- D) Privately tell the quieter managers to be more assertive, as senior forums reward those who speak up.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The forum has already produced a concrete failure — a correct concern talked over — meaning the process suppressed information it needed. Structural changes rebalance participation without accusing anyone and improve decision quality. Option C humiliates people whose behaviour the old format invited; D locates the fault in the excluded. This tests owning a facilitator's dynamics and redesigning structure rather than refereeing personalities.

**Question 11 (hard) — Rank the responses: conflicting crises**

Thursday 15:00: (1) a delivery manager reports a supplier insolvency affecting two contracts; (2) a board pack due to the secretariat at 17:00 still needs final figures; (3) a director's office asks for a 15:30 call about a media query on a project's costs. Rank these responses from most to least effective: (1) take the 15:30 call briefly to triage the media query's factual needs, delegate the summary figures to a portfolio analyst with a final check at 16:30, and start the insolvency response — alert the senior portfolio manager and commercial colleagues today, get the two projects assessing exposure; (2) decline the call, finish the board pack personally first, then turn to the insolvency, briefing the senior portfolio manager tomorrow morning; (3) spend the afternoon entirely on the insolvency as the biggest risk, letting the pack slip to Friday with an apology and asking for questions by email; (4) join the call, then spend the rest of the day on the media query in case it escalates, fitting the other two in tomorrow.

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4
- D) 1, 3, 4, 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 parallelises through delegation, triages the media call, and starts the time-sensitive insolvency response today. Response 3 prioritises correctly but surrenders an achievable deadline; response 2 hand-finishes routine figures while a same-day-material event sits unbriefed overnight — the comfort-zone error. Response 4 devotes the whole afternoon to a possibility while two actual crises wait. This tests parallelising where possible, triaging where a fraction of time suffices, and giving irreplaceable hours to the irreversible item.

**Question 12 (hard) — Most and least effective: a candidate's improvement, their resistance**

An improved portfolio dashboard corrects a distortion that flattered projects re-baselining frequently, by also showing slippage against original baselines. Two delivery managers whose projects look worse under the new view lobby the senior portfolio manager to block it, calling it "unfair to teams that re-planned responsibly." Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses: (1) withdraw the original-baseline view — stakeholder acceptance matters more than analytical purity; (2) brief the senior portfolio manager proactively with the rationale and evidence, engage the two delivery managers directly on the legitimate kernel of their objection, and refine the view to distinguish approved re-baselines from drift, presenting both baselines with context; (3) implement unchanged and let the data speak for itself; (4) delay the dashboard indefinitely while a working group studies measurement methodology.

- A) Most effective: 2; least effective: 1
- B) Most effective: 3; least effective: 4
- C) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 3; least effective: 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 equips the manager before the lobbying lands, engages a legitimate kernel of the objection (an approved re-baseline is genuinely different from drift), and improves the artefact while keeping both baselines visible. Response 1 is least effective — it permanently restores a known distortion because the measured objected, teaching every team that lobbying beats evidence. This tests engaging resistance as free design review rather than either capitulating to it or ignoring it entirely.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate keeps a rating or figure evidence-based** while allowing the narrative to carry legitimate context.
- **Watch for whether a candidate refuses a prohibited or improper request while offering a legitimate alternative** rather than a bare refusal.
- **Note whether a candidate owns an error at the visibility level of its impact**, correcting both the record and the people affected.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes structural fixes from individual blame** in a team or forum dynamic.
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that trades accuracy for comfort** — softening ratings, sitting on a known divergence, pre-announcing a likely decision.
- **Crediting an answer that escalates as a first resort or a last one**, rather than calibrating to materiality and time-sensitivity.
- **Missing when a candidate refuses a request without offering a legitimate route.**
- **Rewarding an answer that resolves two senior stakeholders' conflicting instructions by rank** rather than designing a mechanism serving both needs.
- **Missing when a candidate treats a structural problem as an individual one**, or absorbs a systemic issue personally rather than fixing it.
