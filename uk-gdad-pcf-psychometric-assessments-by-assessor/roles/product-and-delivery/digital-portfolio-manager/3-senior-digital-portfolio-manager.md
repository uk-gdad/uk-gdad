# Senior Digital Portfolio Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for senior digital portfolio manager. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are standardised exercises measuring the cognitive capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict performance in a role. At this level they are rarely generic: assessment for a senior portfolio role should be contextualised to the actual substance of the work — portfolio-level delivery data, governance and assurance design, dependency and capacity management, delivery confidence assessments, and the stakeholder dynamics around them. The role co-ordinates and manages a portfolio of digital projects — tracking, analysing and helping deliver outcomes; giving guidance and dealing with issues so teams can deliver faster; managing governance and assurance processes and ensuring standards are met; and providing stakeholders with reliable information on performance and risk. The four assessments below are built from precisely those responsibilities.

Why does this matter at senior level? Because the failure modes change. Junior errors are usually computational; senior ones are interpretive and judgemental — the plausible inference that outruns the evidence, the aggregate that conceals the binding constraint, the governance rule applied to the letter while its purpose is defeated, the stakeholder accommodation that quietly compromises the record. The cognitive section tests multi-constraint reasoning, anomaly interpretation and error-checking across artefacts. The numeric section tests weighted aggregation, forecasting, capacity arithmetic and the honest presentation of delivery confidence. The verbal section tests precision with governance language — modal verbs, scope clauses, exceptions — and the clear-writing judgement a candidate's reports and board papers will depend on. The situational judgement section rehearses the dilemmas senior portfolio managers actually face: divergent self-reporting on visible programmes, pressure from principals, assurance findings with political weight, and the constant calibration of challenge and support.

Each of the four sections follows the same structure: what it measures and how it is typically run; how its dimensions map to the role's named skills — Agile working, communicating analysis and insight, governance and assurance, planning, stakeholder relationship management and strategic thinking; graduated practice questions with complete worked explanations; then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. At senior level, expect — and score for — a range of prior exposure to formal testing; judge reasoning and professional judgement, not polish or test-taking fluency. Several situational judgement items in this guide deliberately have more than one defensible response: scoring should reward recognition of what is actively harmful and the quality of the candidate's reasoning, not simple letter-matching against a single "correct" option. Use each section's per-item timing as a guide for planning a session, and debrief against the explanations below rather than against the answer letter alone.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental operations this role demands — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving — through scenarios built from the artefacts of portfolio management: delivery dashboards, dependency networks, governance schedules, risk registers, capacity models and assurance findings. At senior level the questions lean towards integration: holding several constraints simultaneously, detecting inconsistencies across documents rather than within them, and distinguishing what data proves from what it merely suggests.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 20 to 30 questions, frequently adaptive, scored against a professional comparison group and reported as a speed-accuracy profile rather than a single figure. Ungraded practice items are usually offered first; encourage the candidate to use them to calibrate the interface and pacing.

The role's core transactions — tracking and analysing outcomes, providing delivery confidence, removing blockers, ensuring standards are met — are all exercises in structured reasoning over messy, partial, sometimes contradictory information. The portfolio view exists because no individual project can see cross-cutting patterns: the shared dependency about to bind, the oscillating rating that signals a reporting problem, the aggregate that balances while every period fails. A well-built cognitive assessment reproduces those situations in miniature, and rewards the same disciplines the job does: verify before concluding, quantify before ranking, and separate the defensible inference from the attractive one. A senior candidate also guides others' analysis, so their reasoning habits propagate — the rigour this section drills is rigour the whole portfolio inherits.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: turning complex data into compelling, clear and actionable stories begins with detecting the structure in the data — trends, oscillations, outliers, and the cross-project recurrences only the portfolio altitude reveals.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance**: developing appropriate governance processes, and supporting others in applying them, requires exact reasoning about conditional rules — what a control mandates, permits and is silent on — and the downstream consequences when one element of a process changes.
- **Error checking** maps to **Governance and assurance** and **Planning**: ensuring standards are met means finding the inconsistency between register and report, plan and dependency map, narrative and milestone evidence, before a board does — and at senior level, designing the reconciliation controls that catch what individual checking misses.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Planning**: understanding the environment and prioritising the most important or high-value tasks, under capacity constraints and complex dependencies, is the daily substance of the skill.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Planning** and the duty to give guidance and deal with issues so teams deliver faster: removing blockers and developing plans for difficult situations is constraint-solving with human and organisational variables included.
- **Interpretive restraint** maps to **Strategic thinking** and **Communicating analysis and insight**: contributing to strategy, and communicating negative as well as positive information credibly, both depend on concluding exactly what the evidence supports — no more, no less — especially when the audience would prefer a stronger or softer message.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in portfolio-level data**

Present the candidate with this scenario: twelve projects report monthly. Reviewing six months of submission timestamps: seven projects submit one to three days early; three submit on the deadline day; two submit one to two days late roughly every other month, and both of those also show the portfolio's highest rates of post-submission corrections. Ask what the most defensible reading of this pattern is.

- A) The two late projects have weaker underlying reporting processes, and the correlation between lateness and corrections suggests a shared root cause worth investigating.
- B) The two late projects are hiding delivery problems.
- C) The deadline should be moved two days later to eliminate lateness.
- D) Ten projects are performing well, so no action is needed.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Co-occurring symptoms — periodic lateness and high correction rates — suggest a common cause: plausibly a manual, last-minute compilation process, a data source that arrives late in the month, or a single overloaded person doing the return. That is a hypothesis worth investigating, which is how option A frames it. Option B leaps from process symptoms to a motive accusation the data cannot support. Option C solves the visible symptom while ignoring the informative one: moving the deadline might eliminate lateness and leave the correction rate — the actual quality signal — untouched. Option D discards a genuine finding because the aggregate looks healthy. This tests whether the candidate treats portfolio metadata (timeliness, correction rates) as diagnostic data about reporting health, since reporting health determines whether everything else on the dashboard can be believed — investigate the process before judging the people.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an assurance rule**

The assurance framework states: "A service assessment is required before each phase transition. A project may not enter public beta without passing its beta assessment. A project that fails an assessment may be reassessed after addressing the report's recommendations." Project Tamar is in public beta. Ask which one statement must be true.

- A) Tamar passed its beta assessment at the first attempt.
- B) Tamar has passed a beta assessment.
- C) Tamar addressed recommendations from a failed assessment.
- D) Tamar's next assessment will be its live assessment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Tamar is in public beta; the framework forbids entering public beta without passing a beta assessment; therefore Tamar has, at some point, passed a beta assessment — option B, unconditionally. Option A adds "at the first attempt", which the rules cannot guarantee: the reassessment clause means Tamar might have failed once, addressed recommendations, and passed second time. Option D imports an assumption about sequencing the passage never states. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes must-be-true from could-be-true — the logical core of assurance work. A strong candidate's answer notes that certifying to a board "this project met the standard" asserts exactly the must-be-true, and that letting could-be-true statements harden into certified fact manufactures assurance the evidence does not contain.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Error checking across three artefacts**

Three artefacts describe Project Almond's schedule. The dependency map shows Almond's release gated by Project Blythe's API, due 30 June. The portfolio plan shows Almond releasing 15 June. Almond's own highlight report says: "Release on track for 15 June; no external dependencies outstanding." Blythe's report says the API is on track for 30 June. Assuming every artefact is honestly maintained, ask what single explanation would reconcile all four statements, and what the candidate should do first.

- A) Almond's team is concealing the dependency; escalate to their SRO.
- B) The dependency in the map may be stale — perhaps Almond removed its need for Blythe's API (for example, by building an interim capability) — so verify the dependency's current reality with both teams before touching any plan or report.
- C) Blythe's date is wrong; instruct Blythe to accelerate to 14 June.
- D) The portfolio plan is wrong; move Almond's release to 30 June.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The four statements conflict only if the dependency is real and current. But the stem stipulates honest maintenance, which invites reconciliation: Almond's "no external dependencies outstanding" is true if the dependency has been dissolved and the map simply has not caught up — dependency maps are notoriously the stalest artefact in a portfolio. Option B both identifies the reconciling hypothesis and prescribes the correct first move: verify with both teams, since the alternative — the dependency is real and Almond has forgotten it — is also live and very different in its implications. Options C and D each pick a world unverified and start issuing instructions in it. Option A converts a data discrepancy into an accusation on the least charitable reading, against the stem's own stipulation. This tests whether the candidate treats a discrepancy between artefacts as a question requiring a conversation with the teams, not an adjudication from the desk.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across the portfolio week**

Monday morning. Five items compete for the candidate's week: (1) Wednesday's investment committee needs a delivery confidence assessment for a £2m continuation decision — half a day's work, with two project inputs still unverified; (2) a delivery manager has asked for help unblocking a cross-department data-sharing agreement stuck for three weeks; (3) a quarterly governance process review, committed to leadership, is due Friday; (4) an analyst has asked for feedback on their first solo portfolio report before it goes out Thursday; (5) a director has invited a presentation on the portfolio's dependency approach to another department "sometime soon". Ask which allocation is most defensible.

- A) Item 1 Monday–Tuesday with the verification done; item 4 Tuesday; item 2 started Tuesday with unblocking conversations scheduled; item 3 Thursday–Friday; item 5 scheduled for a future week.
- B) Item 3 first since it is a personal commitment to leadership; then 1, 2, 4; decline 5.
- C) Item 1 Monday; item 5 Tuesday since director requests take precedence; items 2, 3, 4 compressed into Thursday–Friday.
- D) Item 2 all week — unblocking teams is the role's core purpose; fit the rest around it.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 dominates the early week: an investment committee will allocate £2m partly on this assessment, and unverified inputs are exactly what it must not contain — this is the "provide delivery confidence" duty at its most literal, and it is immovably dated. Item 4 is also deadline-coupled and a leverage activity: an hour of feedback develops the analyst and protects the portfolio's output quality. Item 2 is important and already three weeks old, but its resolution has lead times; the effective move is starting it Tuesday rather than blocking a full week (option D's error: heroic single-issue focus sacrificing four commitments to over-serve one). Item 3 has a Friday deadline and belongs to the week's back half. Item 5 is valuable but schedulable, not urgent; option C confuses seniority of requester with urgency of request. Option B front-loads the personal commitment ahead of the £2m decision. This tests whether the candidate sequences by real consequence and deadline coupling — and whether they place a junior colleague's deadline-coupled development need on a par with senior commitments, since at this level developing others on their deadlines is line-of-duty output protection, not discretionary kindness.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern interpretation: the too-stable metric**

For eleven consecutive months, Project Severn has reported exactly 85% milestone confidence. Every other project's confidence figure moves month to month, typically by three to ten points. Severn's delivery record over the period has been middling: some milestones hit, two missed. Ask for the most defensible interpretation.

- A) Severn has unusually stable delivery conditions.
- B) The 85% figure is likely not being genuinely reassessed each month — it has become a ritual number — so the reporting process behind it needs examination.
- C) Severn's confidence methodology should be adopted portfolio-wide, as stability aids planning.
- D) 85% is an acceptable confidence level, so there is no issue.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The signal is the absence of variance, not the level. Genuine confidence assessment responds to events; Severn's figure has not moved across a period containing two missed milestones — events that should have dented any honestly reassessed confidence. A metric that ignores its own evidence has stopped measuring. Option B names this and targets the process, not the person. Option A is contradicted by the middling delivery record. Option C rewards the pathology by adopting the least responsive methodology because its output is smooth. Option D confuses an acceptable level with an acceptable process. This tests whether the candidate recognises that healthy metrics breathe, and that suspicious flatness in confidence figures, risk counts or RAG distributions is itself a reporting-health finding — a diagnostic worth naming explicitly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction through a governance change**

The candidate is redesigning the assurance schedule. Current rules: (i) every project is health-checked every six months; (ii) projects rated red are additionally reviewed monthly until amber or better; (iii) health checks and reviews cannot occur in the same calendar month for the same project. Proposed change: health checks move to every four months. Project Derwent is red, and its last health check was in March. Ask in which month Derwent's next health check is due under the proposed rules, and what scheduling conflict the change creates for red projects generally.

- A) July; and none — reviews and health checks are different events.
- B) July; and a recurring conflict — red projects need a monthly review in every month, but rule (iii) forbids a review in any health-check month, so every health check now displaces a monthly review three times a year instead of twice.
- C) September; and no conflict, since rule (iii) resolves it automatically.
- D) July; but only if Derwent returns to amber first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The date: last check March, new cycle four months, so next check July — eliminating C (the old six-month cycle) and D (nothing conditions health checks on rating). The structural consequence: a red project requires a review every month; rule (iii) forbids review and health check sharing a month; so every health-check month for a red project is a month its mandatory review cannot occur. That conflict already existed twice a year; shortening the cycle to four months makes it three times a year — the proposed improvement mechanically increases how often the assurance regime contradicts itself for the projects needing the most scrutiny. Option A waves the conflict away by noting the events differ, but rule (iii) exists precisely because they compete. This tests whether the candidate propagates a rule change through the other rules' interactions before adoption, and whether they can identify that interaction effects concentrate on edge cases — here, red projects — which is exactly where assurance failures are most expensive.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a delivery confidence submission**

A programme submits this delivery confidence rationale: "Confidence: High. Rationale: The team has delivered every sprint goal for six sprints. Velocity is stable. The remaining scope fits within four sprints at current velocity, against six sprints of runway before the fixed go-live date. Key dependency: the payments provider's sandbox, promised by 1 October, which we need for the final two sprints of integration testing." Today is 20 September; go-live is 15 December; sprints are fortnightly. Ask what the flaw in this rationale is.

- A) Velocity is the wrong measure of progress.
- B) The arithmetic of sprints is wrong: four sprints of work do not fit in six sprints of runway.
- C) The confidence level ignores the rationale's own single point of failure — the sandbox — whose slippage would consume the schedule buffer sprint for sprint, and which sits outside the team's control with no stated fallback.
- D) Six sprints of delivered goals is too short a record to justify high confidence.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The internal arithmetic holds — twelve weeks is six fortnightly sprints, and four sprints of remaining scope inside six of runway gives a two-sprint buffer — eliminating B. The flaw is structural: everything favourable in the submission is within-team evidence, while the named threat — an external provider's sandbox, with no fallback described — is outside the team's control. If it slips, the buffer erodes sprint for sprint. "High confidence" is being carried by the controllable factors while the actual binding constraint goes unweighted. This tests whether the candidate spots the single most common defect in delivery confidence submissions: assessing what can be seen (execution) and under-assessing what cannot (external dependencies). A strong candidate's answer generalises the review question: what in this rationale is outside the team's control, and how is it weighted?

**Question 8 (hard) — Multi-constraint problem solving: the assurance calendar**

Four assessments must be scheduled in one five-day week (Monday–Friday), one per day maximum: Discovery peer review (P), Alpha assessment (A), Beta assessment (B), and a spend panel (S). Constraints: (i) the lead assessor for both A and B is available Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday only, and cannot do two assessments on consecutive days; (ii) S must occur after A, because the panel uses A's outcome; (iii) P must be on Monday or Friday, when the peer reviewers are free; (iv) Friday afternoon is a departmental all-staff event, so Friday can host only a half-day session, and only P is short enough to fit a half day. Ask which schedule satisfies all constraints.

- A) P Monday, A Tuesday, B Wednesday, S Thursday
- B) P Monday, A Tuesday, S Wednesday, B Thursday
- C) A Tuesday, B Thursday, S Friday, P Monday
- D) P Friday, A Tuesday, B Wednesday, S Thursday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Constraint (iv) means only P fits Friday, so S, A, B must all land Monday–Thursday. Constraint (i) puts A and B on non-consecutive days within Tuesday–Thursday, i.e. {Tuesday, Thursday}. Constraint (ii) puts S after A. Option A and D both put A Tuesday / B Wednesday — consecutive, violating (i). Option C puts S on Friday, violating (iv). Option B — P Monday, A Tuesday, S Wednesday, B Thursday — satisfies all four. This tests constrained scheduling under multiple simultaneous rules: whether the candidate anchors on the most restrictive constraint first, then works outward. It is also worth the candidate noting, unprompted, that the feasible schedule is unique — zero slack, so any single-day disruption cascades — since flagging that fragility and holding a contingency is itself part of providing delivery confidence.

**Question 9 (hard) — Interpreting a portfolio anomaly under pressure**

Preparing the quarterly leadership pack, the candidate finds the portfolio's aggregate benefits forecast has fallen 2%, but decomposition shows two large opposing movements: Programme X has cut its forecast by £1.8m (30% of its total) following a failed pilot, while Programme Y has raised its forecast by £1.6m (25% of its total) based on "revised adoption modelling" — a model revision made the same week X's cut became known, with no new adoption data cited. The pack is due in 48 hours. Ask for the strongest course of action.

- A) Report the net 2% — aggregation is standard practice and the movements roughly cancel.
- B) Report both gross movements prominently; treat X's evidenced cut as credible; challenge Y's uplift before the pack goes out — what new data supports the revised model? — and if unverified by the deadline, present Y's uplift as unvalidated, with the aggregate shown both with and without it.
- C) Exclude Y's uplift permanently as obviously gamed.
- D) Delay the pack until both movements are fully audited.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A net −2% built from ±30% gross swings conceals rather than summarises. X's cut follows a failed pilot — new, unfavourable, credible evidence. Y's uplift cites a model revision with no new data, timed suspiciously — not proof of gaming, but a verification burden unmet. Option B challenges now and, if unresolved, discloses honestly with the aggregate shown both ways, rather than laundering the figure or silently deleting it. Option A is the aggregation failure; C converts suspicion into verdict without the conversation; D sacrifices the leadership cadence to perfectionism when honest caveating would fully serve the decision need. This tests whether the candidate applies challenge intensity proportionate to a movement's size, direction and evidential backing — and whether negative and positive information get equal, honest prominence.

**Question 10 (hard) — Prioritisation with second-order effects**

Budget pressure requires a recommendation to pause one of four initiatives. Analysis: Initiative K — benefits £900k/yr, consumes the portfolio's only two data engineers, 60% complete; Initiative L — benefits £700k/yr, generic skills, 80% complete, contractually committed supplier ending in two months regardless; Initiative M — benefits £1.1m/yr, generic skills, 20% complete; Initiative N — benefits £400k/yr, generic skills, 30% complete, but it builds the data platform that K's remaining work and one committed future initiative both depend on. Ask which pause recommendation is most defensible, on these facts alone.

- A) N — lowest annual benefits.
- B) M — least sunk progress relative to benefits still distant.
- C) L — its supplier ends in two months anyway, so pausing it forfeits little.
- D) K — highest-cost scarce resource consumption.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** N is an enabler: pausing it strands K's £900k at 60% complete and blocks a committed future initiative, so its effective value exceeds its headline. L is 80% complete with a supplier ending regardless: pausing forfeits nearly-complete benefits and, since the supplier ends anyway, restarting later means re-procuring — the closing window is a reason to finish, not stop, inverting option C's logic. K is 60% sunk and precisely what N's platform serves — pausing it strands the most progress. M's benefits are furthest away, its sunk cost smallest, its skills generic, and nothing depends on it: pausing it loses the least realised-plus-enabled value. This tests whether the candidate reasons through dependency chains (enablers carry their dependents' value) and completion proximity, rather than ranking on headline annual benefit alone.

**Question 11 (hard) — Error checking the improvement metric**

Six months after streamlining the portfolio's stage-gate process, an analyst reports: "Average gate-approval time has fallen from 21 working days to 12. The improvement is working." The underlying data shows: gate volume fell from 40 approvals in the prior six months to 25; the new process introduced a 'pre-check' step that returns incomplete submissions before the clock starts; and returned submissions — 10 in the period — are excluded from the average. Ask for the soundest assessment.

- A) The improvement is confirmed: 12 days is unambiguously better than 21.
- B) The headline is unreliable as evidence of improvement: the clock-start redefinition and the exclusion of returned submissions mean the two averages measure different things, so end-to-end elapsed time (including pre-check and returns) must be compared like-for-like before claiming success.
- C) The improvement failed: lower volume proves teams are avoiding the new process.
- D) The metrics are irrelevant; only user satisfaction with the process matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The clock-start moved (waiting and rework time in pre-check now sits outside the measurement window), and the ten slowest-likely cases were excluded entirely — both changes bias the new figure downward independently of any real improvement. This is measurement-boundary gaming's honest cousin: a redesign that redefines a metric's boundaries silently breaks its comparability, without anyone needing to intend deception. Option B prescribes the sound test: end-to-end elapsed time, returns included, compared like-for-like. Option C over-reads the volume drop, which the data cannot attribute. This tests whether a candidate applies the same scepticism to a metric change when the improvement is their own initiative as they would to anyone else's — and whether they know to ask what a process change did to a metric's definition before accepting its trend.

**Question 12 (hard) — Applied problem solving: the cascading slip**

Wednesday: Project Foss confirms its data platform milestone will slip five weeks. The dependency map shows: Project Gelt needs Foss's platform for performance testing, currently scheduled to start in three weeks; Project Hodder needs Gelt's test results for its security case, due at Hodder's go/no-go board in nine weeks; Hodder's go-live is contractually committed with an external partner twelve weeks out. Gelt's performance testing takes three weeks; producing the security case from test results takes two weeks. Ask whether Hodder's go/no-go date holds if nothing changes, and what the highest-leverage intervention to examine first is.

- A) It holds — twelve weeks is enough; no intervention needed.
- B) It fails by four weeks against the board date; the highest-leverage first move is examining downstream compressibility — whether Gelt's testing can start on partial platform capability, run compressed or in parallel, and whether the security case can be drafted from interim results — since the Foss slip is upstream and fixed.
- C) It fails; Hodder should move its go-live by five weeks as the first response.
- D) It fails; Foss must be instructed to halve its slippage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Pre-slip, the chain completes at week 8 against a week-9 board — a one-week buffer. The five-week slip pushes Gelt's test start from week 3 to week 8; testing completes week 11; the security case lands week 13 — four weeks past the week-9 board, and one week past the week-12 contractual go-live. Option D demands an upstream fact improve, which is not a plan. Option C reaches for the most expensive lever — moving a contractual go-live — before cheaper options are exhausted. Option B correctly identifies that every compressible segment lies downstream of the fixed slip, and examines it first. This tests whether the candidate recomputes a dependency chain by hand rather than trusting a headline number, locates the buffer, and works interventions in cost order — downstream compression, then scope, then dates — before touching a contract or issuing an impossible instruction upstream.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate verifies before concluding — "consistent with the evidence" is not "established by the evidence" — rather than for confident, fluent wrong answers.
- Watch for whether the candidate adjudicates artefact conflicts from the desk versus recommending a conversation with the teams involved; the latter is the stronger senior response.
- Note whether the candidate reads variance, not just level — a suspiciously flat metric should draw comment even when its face value looks fine.
- Score against netted aggregates: does the candidate ask to see the gross movements behind a portfolio-level net figure before accepting it?
- Note whether the candidate weights controllable execution evidence against binding external constraints correctly, or is reassured by whichever evidence is most abundant.
- Watch for whether the candidate flags a uniquely feasible (zero-slack) schedule as brittle, not just feasible.
- Score for even-handed scepticism: does the candidate challenge evidence that flatters their own prior work as hard as evidence that damages it?

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a plausible-sounding inference as if it were established, when the candidate has not distinguished must-be-true from could-be-true.
- Rewarding an answer that resolves an artefact conflict by picking a "winning" document rather than recommending verification with the people involved.
- Missing when a candidate reads a metric's level but ignores its shape over time.
- Accepting a netted aggregate at face value because the candidate's final figure is arithmetically correct, without probing whether they asked for the decomposition.
- Overlooking a confidence assessment that is carried by comfortable, controllable evidence while the real external risk goes unweighted.
- Failing to note when a candidate treats a uniquely feasible schedule as good news rather than as a fragility to flag.
- Missing scepticism asymmetry — a candidate who probes an unfavourable figure hard but waves through a favourable or self-serving one.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures fluency and accuracy with the quantitative material a senior portfolio manager produces, validates and presents: multi-project spend and forecast tables, weighted portfolio metrics, capacity and demand models, benefits trajectories, trend decompositions, and the confidence arithmetic behind delivery assessments. Formats present tables, charts or short data narratives followed by calculation and interpretation items, with senior-level items emphasising interpretation — choosing the right measure, spotting the misleading aggregate, knowing what a figure can and cannot support.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 35 minutes with 15 to 20 questions, calculator permitted, distractors built from predictable errors — wrong bases, averaged percentages, boundary slips, conflated measures. At senior level, expect fewer pure-arithmetic items and more two-step questions where the calculation is easy but selecting the correct calculation is the test.

This role is where project numbers become organisational decisions: a candidate does not merely report figures, but certifies them to investment committees, boards and leadership, and translates them into recommendations with money and delivery consequences attached. Mistaking a points change for a percentage change, weighting a portfolio metric badly, or accepting a forecast whose base has quietly shifted is not a small error at this level — it miscalibrates a decision. The questions below drill the calculations a senior portfolio dashboard actually requires, each with complete step-by-step working.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Weighted aggregation and combined rates** map to **Communicating analysis and insight**: aggregating unequal projects honestly — budget-weighted confidence, totals-over-totals completion rates — and knowing when an unweighted view would mislead.
- **Forecasting and run-rate arithmetic** map to **Planning** and the duty to provide delivery confidence: burn analysis, straight-line and adjusted forecasts, and buffer arithmetic.
- **Capacity and dependency arithmetic** map to **Planning**: managing complex dependencies and ensuring teams plan appropriately for capacity are, at bottom, constrained-resource calculations.
- **Trend and variance interpretation** map to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Governance and assurance**: distinguishing signal from noise, percentage from percentage-point, and genuine improvement from measurement artefact.
- **Benefits and investment arithmetic** maps to **Strategic thinking**: fluency with benefits trajectories, realisation ratios and simple investment comparisons.
- **Presentation-of-numbers judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: showing the right figure, on the right base, with honest caveats.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio delivery rate**

Across the portfolio last quarter, 68 of 80 planned milestones were delivered. Ask for the portfolio delivery rate.

- A) 68%
- B) 80%
- C) 85%
- D) 88%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 68 ÷ 80 = 0.85 = 85% — option C. Option A quotes the numerator as a percentage; B quotes the denominator. A useful sense-check: three-quarters of 80 is 60, seven-eighths is 70 — 68 sits just below seven-eighths, so 85% is plausible and 68% is impossible on inspection. This tests basic rate calculation and sense-checking against simple fractions.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change in portfolio spend**

Portfolio monthly spend was £640,000 in January and £560,000 in February. Ask for the percentage change.

- A) A fall of 14.3%
- B) A fall of 12.5%
- C) A fall of 8.0%
- D) A rise of 12.5%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Change = £560,000 − £640,000 = −£80,000; 80,000 ÷ 640,000 (the original, January) = 0.125 = a fall of 12.5% — option B. Option A divides by the new month instead of the original — the commonest wrong-base error, which is why it is the first option offered. Direction dispatches D immediately. This tests whether the candidate divides by the correct base and gets the direction right.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a capacity table**

The shared-capability tracker shows next month's committed designer-days by project:

| Project | Committed designer-days |
|---|---|
| Aire | 18 |
| Calder | 12 |
| Douglas | 9 |
| Eden | 15 |

The design team's capacity is 60 designer-days per month. Ask what percentage of capacity is committed.

- A) 80%
- B) 85%
- C) 90%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total demand: 18 + 12 + 9 + 15 = 54; 54 ÷ 60 = 0.9 = 90% — option C. The distractors are near-misses from single addition slips. This tests table reading, arithmetic accuracy and — in a strong candidate's answer — recognition that 90% committed leaves little absorption margin, which is why mature portfolio offices treat 80–85% as a healthier planning ceiling.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Budget-weighted portfolio confidence**

Four programmes report delivery confidence, to be aggregated budget-weighted for the leadership dashboard:

| Programme | Budget (£m) | Confidence (%) |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | 10 | 90 |
| P2 | 5 | 70 |
| P3 | 3 | 50 |
| P4 | 2 | 80 |

Ask for the budget-weighted portfolio confidence.

- A) 72.5%
- B) 75.0%
- C) 78.0%
- D) 80.0%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Sum of (budget × confidence): (10×90) + (5×70) + (3×50) + (2×80) = 900 + 350 + 150 + 160 = 1,560; divided by total budget 20: 1,560 ÷ 20 = 78.0% — option C. Option A is the unweighted mean (72.5%); the 5.5-point gap between the two views is the substantive story, since P1 holds half the money at the highest confidence. This tests whether the candidate can compute a weighted average correctly and, ideally, comments that the aggregation basis should be stated explicitly on any dashboard, since a reader assuming the wrong basis mis-sizes portfolio risk.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Points versus percent in an assurance trend**

The portfolio's standards-compliance rate rose from 64% to 80% following assurance process improvements. Ask which statement describes this change correctly.

- A) Compliance rose 16%, from 64% to 80%.
- B) Compliance rose 16 percentage points — a relative improvement of 25%.
- C) Compliance rose 25 percentage points.
- D) Compliance rose 20%, since 16 of 80 is 20%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Absolute change: 80 − 64 = 16 percentage points. Relative change: 16 ÷ 64 = 0.25 = 25%. Option B states both correctly. Option A conflates points with percent; D divides by the new value instead of the original. This tests the points-versus-percent distinction, which matters because the gap between "16 points" and "25%" is exactly the space in which improvement claims get oversold or undersold — including a candidate's own.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Run-rate with a step change**

Eight months into the year, Programme Quill has spent £1.92m of its £3.3m annual budget. From month nine, a new supplier contract adds a fixed £90,000 per month. Assuming the underlying run rate continues and the contract cost is additional, ask for the forecast full-year spend.

- A) £2.88m
- B) £3.24m
- C) £3.30m
- D) £3.42m

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Underlying run rate: £1.92m ÷ 8 = £0.24m/month. Remaining four months: £0.24m × 4 = £0.96m. Contract addition for four months: £90,000 × 4 = £0.36m. Total: £1.92m + £0.96m + £0.36m = £3.24m — option B. Option A ignores the step change (naive straight line); D misapplies the contract cost across six months instead of four. This tests decomposed forecasting — continuing baseline plus known step changes — over naive straight-lining or guesswork.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Combined completion across unequal quarters**

The portfolio's milestone record: Q1 delivered 45 of 60 planned; Q2 delivered 66 of 80. A draft leadership summary states: "Completion improved from 75% to 82.5%, averaging 78.75% for the half-year." Ask which figure is wrong and what the correct value is.

- A) The Q2 rate; it should be 80%.
- B) The half-year average; combining totals gives 111 ÷ 140 = 79.3%.
- C) The Q1 rate; it should be 72%.
- D) Nothing is wrong; averaging the two rates is valid here.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Q1 (45÷60=75%) and Q2 (66÷80=82.5%) are both correct individually. The half-year figure should combine totals, not average rates: (45+66) ÷ (60+80) = 111 ÷ 140 = 79.3% — option B, half a point below the drafted 78.75%. The error survives review here because the quarters are only moderately unequal; it would be far larger with more divergent quarter sizes. This tests whether the candidate applies the combined-totals rule without exception, since averaged rates look right when group sizes are close and mislead badly when they diverge.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Benefits realisation ratio**

A programme's business case forecast £2.4m annual benefits at full rollout. Rollout is 62.5% complete; measured annualised benefits to date are £1.2m. Assuming benefits scale linearly with rollout, ask what percentage of expected benefits at this stage the programme is realising.

- A) 50%
- B) 62.5%
- C) 80%
- D) 100%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Expected benefits at this stage: 62.5% × £2.4m = £1.5m. Realisation ratio: £1.2m ÷ £1.5m = 0.8 = 80% — option C. Option A divides measured benefits by the full-rollout target instead of the expected-at-stage figure, answering a different question. This tests expected-at-stage reasoning versus share-of-eventual-target, and — in a strong candidate's answer — the projection this implies: if the pattern persists, full-rollout benefits land around £1.92m, a material shortfall worth surfacing now.

**Question 9 (hard) — Critical path through a dependency network**

Four workstreams feed a portfolio go-live. W1 (data migration) takes 6 weeks, starts now. W2 (integration build) takes 5 weeks, starts now. W3 (assurance testing) takes 4 weeks, cannot start until both W1 and W2 complete. W4 (training rollout) takes 7 weeks, cannot start until W2 completes, and can run in parallel with W3. Go-live requires W3 and W4 both complete. Ask for the earliest go-live and which workstream is on the critical path's final segment.

- A) Week 10, via W3
- B) Week 12, via W4
- C) Week 13, via W3
- D) Week 12, via W3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** W3 can start at week 6 (the later of W1's and W2's completion) and finishes week 10. W4 starts at week 5 (W2's completion) and finishes week 12. Go-live needs both, so the later governs: week 12, via W4 — option B. The critical path is W2→W4, not the intuitively "heavier" W1→W3 chain, which carries two weeks of slack. This tests whether the candidate computes the critical path correctly rather than assuming the longest single duration or the most conspicuous workstream governs — and, in a strong answer, that monitoring and compression effort belongs on W2→W4, not on the biggest-looking block.

**Question 10 (hard) — Interpreting a variance decomposition**

Portfolio spend this quarter was £5.75m against a plan of £5.0m — a £750k overspend. Decomposition: Programme A overspent £900k (unplanned remediation after a security finding); Programme B underspent £400k (recruitment delays on a critical team); Programme C overspent £250k (accelerated delivery, pulling next quarter's work forward). Ask which reading should anchor the leadership narrative.

- A) The portfolio overspent 15%; spending discipline needs tightening across programmes.
- B) The three variances have three different meanings — A's is a genuine cost increase to fund or offset, B's is delay masquerading as saving, and C's is timing neutral over two quarters — so the honest position is roughly £900k of true cost pressure, partially hidden by £400k of worrying slippage, not a uniform 15% discipline problem.
- C) The net £750k rounds to A's £900k, so report Programme A and omit the others.
- D) B's underspend offsets most of A's overspend, so the portfolio is broadly fine.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic nets correctly (900 − 400 + 250 = 750), but variances carry meanings, not just magnitudes. A's overspend is a real, permanent cost increase. B's "underspend" is money unspent because a critical team is missing — delivery slipping in financial disguise, not savings, so treating it as an offset (option D) double-counts. C's variance is timing, netting to zero across quarters. Option A's uniform "discipline" framing would prompt squeezing all programmes, punishing C's acceleration while doing nothing for A's funding gap or B's recruitment blocker. This tests whether the candidate classifies every material variance as cost, timing, or delivery-in-disguise before netting anything.

**Question 11 (hard) — Confidence interval reasoning for delivery forecasting**

Delivery analytics show a team completes between 18 and 26 story points per sprint (observed range over 12 sprints), mean 22. Remaining backlog for a fixed-date release is 130 points across 5 remaining sprints. Ask which statement best characterises delivery confidence.

- A) Confident: mean throughput gives 5 × 22 = 110... so the release will miss; report certain failure.
- B) At mean throughput the team delivers 110 of 130 points — a 20-point shortfall — and even at best observed throughput (5 × 26 = 130) success requires every sprint at the historical maximum; so on current scope the release is high-risk-to-implausible, and the conversation should move to scope reduction of roughly 20 points or more.
- C) The range means anything from 90 to 130 points is possible, so the outcome is unknowable; make no forecast.
- D) Confident of success: 130 points is within the observed 5-sprint maximum.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Mean case: 5 × 22 = 110, a 20-point shortfall. Worst observed: 5 × 18 = 90. Best observed: 5 × 26 = 130 — exactly enough, but only if the team hits its historical maximum five consecutive times, which the 12-sprint record makes deeply improbable. Option B assembles the correct posture: quantified shortfall at the central estimate, explicit implausibility of the success path, and the pivot to a scope conversation. Option D plans on the maximum; option C mistakes a range for total ignorance; option A overstates as badly as D understates and forecloses the scope conversation. This tests whether the candidate can summarise a distribution honestly — central estimate, plausible range, and the decision the numbers point to — rather than picking a single anchor.

**Question 12 (hard) — The re-baselining illusion**

Project Kelvin has re-baselined twice. Original plan: 40 milestones by December. First re-baseline (April): scope cut to 32 milestones, December unchanged. Second re-baseline (August): 28 milestones, December unchanged. Kelvin's latest report: "26 of 28 milestones on track — 93% delivery confidence." Ask what the on-track proportion is measured against the original baseline, and what the gap between the two figures represents.

- A) 93% either way; re-baselining does not affect the calculation.
- B) 65% against the original baseline (26 of 40); the 28-point gap between 93% and 65% is the cumulative scope reduction that two re-baselines have moved outside the measure — both figures are true, but they answer different questions and a board should see both.
- C) 70% against the original baseline (28 of 40); the gap is a data error.
- D) 81.25% against the original baseline; the gap is normal delivery variance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Current-baseline view: 26 ÷ 28 = 93%, honest as far as it goes. Original-baseline view: 26 ÷ 40 = 65%. The 28-point gap is the 12 milestones removed across two re-baselines — commitments that have exited the denominator and the measure with them. Both figures are true answers to different questions, and a governance view showing only the first records two quiet scope cuts as rising confidence. This tests whether the candidate recognises that any metric whose denominator shrinks with each re-plan will show improvement while delivery erodes, and that dual-baseline reporting, with the approved change history, is the honest presentation.

### Administration tips

- Drill the senior toolkit: weighted averages, combined rates, change on the original base, expected-at-stage benefits, critical-path maxima, run-rate-plus-step forecasts.
- Score for whether the candidate names the base of a percentage aloud — "percent of what?" — before dividing.
- Note whether the candidate computes both weighted and unweighted, or current- and original-baseline, views of a contested figure, and comments on the gap.
- Score for whether the candidate classifies a variance as cost, timing or delivery-in-disguise before netting it.
- Watch for forecast language that states a central estimate and a plausible range, rather than a single confident number.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting wrong-base division — change over the new value rather than the original, or a similar substitution — even when the final figure looks plausible.
- Missing an averaged-rate answer that should have combined totals instead, especially where the quarters or groups are of similar size and the error is small enough to look right.
- Rewarding a candidate who banks an underspend as good news without asking why it arose.
- Accepting a forecast built on the best observed case rather than a central estimate.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a re-baselined metric at face value without asking what it looked like against the original plan.
- Crediting arithmetic presented without its assumption — a straight-line forecast, a benefits ratio — as if the figure alone were the analysis.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures precision in reading, inference and written judgement, using material of the kind a senior portfolio manager handles daily: governance frameworks, assurance reports, ministerial and directorate correspondence, spend control policy, delivery narratives, and extracts from standards. The core format presents a passage followed by statements to classify as **True** (supported by the passage), **False** (contradicted), or **Cannot say** (not determinable from the passage). Senior-level variants add inference items, assumption items, argument-evaluation items, and clear-writing judgement.

The typical format is 15 to 25 minutes online, four to six passages, twenty-odd statements, and distractors engineered around modal verbs, scope boundaries, dropped conditions and plausible-but-unstated claims. The governing rule is strict textual discipline: the passage is the whole universe; professional knowledge stays outside it.

Language is the medium in which this role's authority operates: interpreting governance frameworks that teams act on, certifying positions to boards in sentences whose modal verbs carry weight, drafting the papers through which leadership perceives the portfolio, and correcting the misreadings of others. Precision failures at this altitude propagate — a misread "may" becomes a planning assumption becomes a missed deadline; an over-strong summary sentence becomes a board's recorded position becomes an accountability dispute. The passages below are built from exactly these materials.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Textual precision with rules** maps to **Governance and assurance**: exact command of what framework language mandates, permits, excepts and omits.
- **Inference discipline** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and the duty to provide performance and risk information: separating what an update establishes from what it suggests.
- **Assumption detection** maps to **Planning** and **Strategic thinking**: finding the load-bearing unstated premises in a delivery commitment or strategy document.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: engaging with a stakeholder's reasoning rather than trading conclusions.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: turning complex data into compelling, clear and actionable stories is finally a drafting skill.

### Practice questions

**Passage for Questions 1–3:**

"Portfolio assurance framework, section 7: Each programme must undergo an independent assurance review at least once every twelve months. The portfolio office may commission additional reviews where delivery confidence falls below 60% or where spend variance exceeds 15% of profile. Review findings are graded: 'critical' findings require a response plan within ten working days; 'significant' findings require a response plan within twenty working days; 'advisory' findings require no formal response but should be considered at the next planning cycle. A programme's SRO may challenge a finding's grading within five working days of the report; challenges are determined by the head of portfolio, whose decision is final. Programmes may not be granted more than one grading challenge per review."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "A programme whose delivery confidence is 55% must be given an additional assurance review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, if spend variance also exceeds 15%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The portfolio office "may commission additional reviews where delivery confidence falls below 60%" — a discretionary power, not a duty. Confidence of 55% satisfies the trigger, but the statement's "must be given" asserts an obligation the passage does not contain: False, not Cannot say, since the text actively contradicts an asserted obligation. Option D compounds the error: the two triggers are alternatives, and neither converts "may" into "must". This tests the may/must distinction that governs how governance triggers should be read — and misread ones generate real friction, from programmes claiming an entitlement to programmes resenting a review they think they "shouldn't" get.

**Question 2 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "An SRO who misses the five-day challenge window can appeal a critical finding's grading to the head of portfolio."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the head of portfolio's jurisdiction is unlimited

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage defines an in-window challenge route and simply does not address a late request — no bar, no residual discretion clause. With the case unregulated by the text, the answer is Cannot say. Reading a stated window as an implied bar is a natural inference, but an inference nonetheless, resting on convention rather than the text. This tests whether the candidate can distinguish a case the passage regulates from one it is silent on — senior interpretive craft lies largely in seeing where the rules end and discretion begins, and saying so rather than manufacturing certainty in either direction.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Applying the framework**

A review of Programme Wye grades one finding 'critical' and two 'advisory'. Wye's SRO believes the critical finding overstates the issue and wants it re-graded; she also wants one advisory finding re-graded upward to 'significant' so it attracts a formal response from a supplier. Ask, based only on the passage, which is correct.

- A) She may challenge both gradings, as they concern different findings.
- B) She faces a constraint: the framework allows her at most one grading challenge for this review, so she must choose which grading matters more — and any challenge must be lodged within five working days.
- C) She may not challenge at all, because critical findings are exempt from challenge.
- D) She may challenge the critical finding now and the advisory finding at the next review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Nothing exempts critical findings, eliminating C. The binding rule is the one-challenge-per-review ceiling: two desired challenges, one review, so she must choose — option B, which correctly carries the five-day deadline too. Note that the challenge right is not limited by direction — "challenge a finding's grading" covers re-grading upward as well as downward. Option D invents a deferral mechanism the passage does not provide. This tests whether the candidate applies a numeric ceiling correctly alongside a directional reading of the challenge right.

**Passage for Questions 4–6:**

"Extract from an assurance review report: 'The programme's delivery of its core case-management capability is impressive: the team demonstrates strong engineering discipline and the beta service performs well against its published metrics. However, the review team was unable to verify the programme's benefits model. The model's adoption assumptions derive from a 2023 pilot involving 200 users in one region; the business case extrapolates these to a national user base of 1.4 million with materially different demographics. No sensitivity analysis was provided. The programme director advised that a refreshed adoption study is planned. Until its results are available, the review team regards the stated benefits figure of £8.2m per annum as unevidenced, though not necessarily wrong. This concern does not affect our positive assessment of technical delivery.'"

**Question 4 (easy) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The review team found the programme's benefits figure to be wrong."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the model was unverifiable

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly regards the figure as "unevidenced, though not necessarily wrong". A statement claiming the team found it wrong is contradicted: False. Option D conflates unverifiable with false — the evidence chain fails, not the conclusion. This tests the distinction between "not proven" and "disproven", among the most consequential in assurance language: "unevidenced" tends to harden into "wrong" as it travels through summaries and corridor conversations, and a strong candidate names both failure directions — hardening and its opposite, softening.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Identifying the core weakness**

Ask, according to the passage, what the central defect in the programme's benefits model is.

- A) The 2023 pilot was too long ago to be relevant.
- B) An extrapolation problem: assumptions from 200 users in one region are scaled to 1.4 million nationally across materially different demographics, with no sensitivity analysis to show how outcomes vary if the assumptions fail to transfer.
- C) The programme has no benefits model at all.
- D) The engineering team lacks discipline in benefits tracking.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The model exists (eliminating C); its adoption assumptions rest on a small, unrepresentative sample extrapolated to a demographically different national population, with the missing sensitivity analysis the aggravating factor. Option A mislocates the defect in recency rather than representativeness and scale. Option D transplants a compliment into an unrelated criticism. This tests whether the candidate can locate a passage's precise defect rather than a nearby but distinct one — extrapolation-without-sensitivity is a common defect in government business cases, so the pattern recognition transfers directly to investment committee reading.

**Question 6 (hard) — Evaluating a summary's fidelity**

A directorate summary of the review states: "Assurance found the programme technically excellent but its £8.2m benefits claim unsupported; benefits should be treated as at risk pending the refreshed adoption study." Ask for the most accurate assessment of this summary.

- A) Faithful: it preserves the technical praise, the evidential status of the benefits figure, and the pending remediation.
- B) Unfaithful: the review never called the programme technically excellent.
- C) Unfaithful: "unsupported" is materially harsher than "unevidenced, though not necessarily wrong".
- D) Unfaithful: the summary omits the sensitivity analysis, so it fails.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "Technically excellent" fairly compresses the review's emphatic technical praise (option B's word-matching objection fails, since summaries are licensed to compress meaning). "Unsupported" is a near-synonym for "unevidenced", and "at risk pending" preserves the not-necessarily-wrong nuance rather than hardening it (against option C). Omitting supporting detail like the sensitivity analysis is legitimate compression, not infidelity (against D). This tests whether the candidate can audit a summary for the two failure modes — hardening and softening — and correctly conclude that this one is faithful: a useful test is whether the original author would say "yes, that is what I found".

**Passage for Questions 7–9:**

"Email from a programme director to the senior portfolio manager: 'Following Friday's prioritisation forum, I want to register my concern formally. The forum deferred our scaling investment for the second consecutive quarter, on the basis of the portfolio's capacity model. I understand the model shows the platform team fully committed. But the model counts the platform team at its funded establishment of twelve engineers, when we have been running at nine for five months due to recruitment freezes — so the "full commitment" it reports is calculated on three engineers who do not exist. Meanwhile our service's waiting list has grown 40% in six months, a cost the capacity model does not see at all. I am not asking the portfolio office to bypass prioritisation. I am asking whether decisions of this consequence should rest on a model with known stale inputs and no representation of demand-side cost. If the model cannot be corrected before next quarter's forum, I request that our case be considered alongside the model rather than through it.'"

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The programme director is asking the portfolio office to bypass the prioritisation process."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True in effect, whatever his words say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email states directly: "I am not asking the portfolio office to bypass prioritisation." Both his actual requests — correct the model, or consider the case alongside it — operate within the process. Option D embodies "true in effect" reasoning, attributing to a correspondent the position suspected behind their words. This tests whether the candidate answers the email as written rather than the email suspected — the test discipline and the relationship discipline are the same: if a stated position seems to mask another, the professional route is to test that directly in conversation, not to encode the suspicion into a summary.

**Question 8 (hard) — Locating the argument's strongest point**

Ask which element of the programme director's argument, if verified, most seriously undermines the forum's deferral decisions as made.

- A) The waiting list has grown 40% in six months.
- B) The capacity model counts twelve platform engineers while nine exist, so the "fully committed" finding that drove both deferrals was computed on 25% phantom capacity.
- C) The deferral has now happened twice.
- D) The programme director feels strongly enough to write formally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option C is procedural history, not invalidation; D carries no evidential weight. Option A is real cost but argues for a better instrument, rather than showing the capacity finding itself was wrong. Option B is different in kind: if the establishment figure is false, the model's core output was computed on false inputs, contaminating the specific finding relied on for both decisions. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes a fatal factual claim from a design critique from mere context — the triage a senior reader needs to apply to any stakeholder challenge.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the response**

Ask, as the senior portfolio manager receiving this email, which reply best serves the portfolio, judged by the passage and sound stakeholder practice.

- A) "The forum's decision followed due process and stands; the next cycle is the appropriate route for re-submission."
- B) "Thank you for the specific challenge. You are right that the model must be accurate to be decisive: we will verify the establishment discrepancy with the platform team this week and correct the model if confirmed. Demand-side cost is a real gap; I will propose it for the model's next iteration. If the correction is not landed before next quarter's forum, your case will be heard alongside the model, as you request — and I will report back on all three points by Friday."
- C) "The model is imperfect, as all models are; the forum weighs many factors and the deferral reflected the whole picture."
- D) "Given the error you have found, I will ask the forum to reverse the deferral and approve your scaling investment."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email contains one verifiable factual challenge, one design critique, and one procedural request, from a stakeholder who has stayed inside the process. Option B answers all three at the correct speed, with a dated report-back. Option A defends process while ignoring that the process's evidence is impugned. Option C is vague, unresponsive absolution that teaches every director that specific challenge earns no real answer. Option D overcorrects: a confirmed error invalidates the deferral's basis, but does not itself validate the investment — the remedy is a re-decision on corrected inputs, not a reflex reversal. This tests stakeholder relationship management under a legitimate, evidenced challenge.

**Passage for Questions 10–12:**

"Extract from draft portfolio strategy: 'Over the next three years the portfolio will shift investment towards services with the highest citizen impact, while maintaining the resilience of critical legacy systems. New investment proposals will be assessed against a citizen-impact weighting from 2027–28. Legacy remediation will be funded at not less than 20% of annual portfolio spend until the legacy risk register shows no systems rated critical for two consecutive years, after which the floor may be reviewed. Nothing in this strategy overrides statutory, security or safety obligations, which take precedence over the citizen-impact weighting in all cases.'"

**Question 10 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "If no critical-rated legacy systems appear on the register for two consecutive years, the 20% funding floor will be removed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True from 2027–28 onwards

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The floor holds "until" the two-clean-years condition, "after which the floor may be reviewed" — a discretionary reconsideration with an unspecified outcome. The statement asserts automatic removal, converting may-be-reviewed into will-be-removed. Option D attaches a date from an unrelated clause (the citizen-impact weighting's application date). This tests trigger-versus-outcome parsing — a compound conditional (condition, then discretion, then unspecified outcome) that is the native grammar of strategy documents, and a common source of misreading when a team plans against a removal that was only ever a promised review.

**Question 11 (hard) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "Under the strategy, a security-critical remediation with low citizen impact could be funded ahead of a high-citizen-impact service improvement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because citizen impact is the strategy's paramount criterion

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The exception clause states that statutory, security and safety obligations "take precedence over the citizen-impact weighting in all cases" — precisely the priority relationship the statement describes, so "could be" is well supported: True. Option D reads the headline commitment and stops before the exception clause — the classic first-paragraph answer to a last-paragraph question. This tests whether the candidate reads to the end of a strategy document, since headline rules and their exceptions typically arrive far apart, and the exception is usually where the hard cases live.

**Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement at board level**

The portfolio board needs one paragraph on the strategy's legacy funding position for a mixed audience of executives. Ask which drafting is best.

- A) "Pursuant to the strategic framework, legacy remediation expenditure shall be sustained at a quantum not inferior to one-fifth of aggregate annual portfolio disbursement, subject to potential recalibration contingent upon the bi-annual absence of criticality-rated register entries."
- B) "Legacy funding is protected: at least 20% of annual portfolio spend goes to legacy remediation. That floor stays until the risk register shows no critical-rated systems for two consecutive years — at which point it becomes reviewable, not abolished. Security, safety, and statutory obligations sit above all other priorities throughout."
- C) "We are spending a lot on legacy, as we must, but the strategy sensibly allows this to change as things improve."
- D) "The 20% legacy floor may be reviewed after two clean years on the register."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B carries the number, the exact trigger condition, the correctly sized consequence ("reviewable, not abolished"), and the precedence hierarchy — everything a board deciding trade-offs needs. Option A is the same content in officialese that forces translation and, worse, actively corrupts meaning ("bi-annual" suggests twice-yearly rather than two consecutive years). Option C is confident vagueness with no figure, condition or hierarchy. Option D is accurate but radically incomplete, framing a protection as a sunset by omitting the floor's size and the precedence rule. This tests clear-writing judgement: every number that governs a decision, every condition that gates a change, every hierarchy that resolves a conflict, and nothing else.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes must/will/shall from should/may — the modal ladder — accurately across statements.
- Note whether the candidate separates what a passage's silence leaves undetermined from what it actively contradicts.
- Watch for whether the candidate reads to a document's exceptions and precedence clauses before concluding, rather than stopping at the headline.
- Score summary-fidelity items on whether the candidate can tell compression (legitimate) from hardening or softening (not).
- For the clear-writing item, score on completeness of governing detail carried in plain language, not on tone or brevity alone.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that reads "may" as "must", or vice versa.
- Missing when a candidate treats a passage's silence on a case as if it settled that case.
- Rewarding hardened or softened summaries as "close enough" to the source's evidential status.
- Accepting a first-paragraph answer to a question actually resolved by a later exception or precedence clause.
- Crediting "true in effect" reasoning that attributes an unstated position to a correspondent.
- Rewarding officialese or vague reassurance over a plain paragraph that carries every governing number and condition.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to evaluate response options — most effective, least effective, full rankings, or independent ratings. It measures professional judgement against behavioural benchmarks rather than knowledge. For senior digital portfolio roles, scenarios concentrate where the role's tensions live: assurance findings with political consequences, divergence between self-reported and evidenced delivery, pressure from principals and programmes, the calibration of challenge and support, the stewardship of standards under deadline, and the development of the people and processes around the candidate.

Typical format: 15 to 25 scenarios, untimed or generously timed, scored against experienced-practitioner consensus and the organisation's behaviour framework — for government digital roles, expect honesty and transparency, ownership, collaboration, user and citizen focus, inclusion, proportionate escalation, and improvement orientation. Senior-level instruments discriminate less on avoiding obviously bad options and more on ordering the plausible ones: several responses will be defensible, and scoring should reward candidates who consistently choose the response that serves the institution — its decisions, its information quality, its people — over the response that merely resolves the moment.

Senior judgement is the product this role delivers: certifying information boards act on, arbitrating between programmes and process, and setting professional norms by example. Each explanation below identifies the behaviours at stake, works the options against each other comparatively, and names the transferable principle.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Information-integrity dilemmas** map to **Communicating analysis and insight** and the duty to provide performance and risk information: scenarios about softening, delaying or selectively presenting findings test whether negative and positive information reach decision makers with equal fidelity and speed.
- **Standards-under-pressure dilemmas** map to **Governance and assurance**: compliance tested precisely where it is costly.
- **Blocker and issue dilemmas** map to **Planning**: whether the candidate removes impediments at the right level.
- **Influence and conflict dilemmas** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: evidence-led, relationship-preserving challenge.
- **Development and inclusion dilemmas** map to the leadership dimension of the role: how a struggling analyst, a dominated forum, or credit for others' work is handled.
- **Improvement dilemmas** map to **Strategic thinking** and **Governance and assurance**: converting incidents into process improvements rather than absorbing recurring problems case by case.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The pre-board rating request**

Sunday evening, before Monday's portfolio board. A programme director texts the candidate: her programme's rating in the portfolio report is amber; she believes it should be green, says the two issues driving amber were "resolved on Friday", and asks for the rating to be updated before the board sees the pack. The candidate cannot verify the resolutions tonight. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Update to green — she is accountable for the programme and says the issues are resolved.
- B) Keep amber, and reply that the resolutions will gladly be reflected once verified: propose she brings the resolution evidence to the board verbally, note in the meeting that verification is underway, and update the rating in-cycle when confirmed.
- C) Keep amber and do not reply until after the board.
- D) Split the difference: present the programme as "amber improving, green by next cycle".

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rating is the portfolio office's own verified assessment — the reason boards trust it over self-assessment. Option A converts it into an unverifiable Sunday-night claim; option B holds the evidential line while leaving the door explicitly open, gets the freshest information to the board through the honest channel, and models the norm that ratings move on verification, not advocacy. Option C needlessly loses the relationship through silence. Option D invents a hybrid status committing to a future rating no evidence supports. This tests whether the candidate spends their signature only on what has been checked, while still treating the stakeholder generously.

**Question 2 (easy) — The analyst's error, found after publication**

A portfolio analyst discovers he transposed two programmes' forecast figures in the quarterly pack published to leadership last week, making one look £600k better and the other £600k worse than reality. He comes to the candidate visibly anxious, correction in hand, and says: "I can slip the fix into next quarter's pack — nobody has queried it." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Agree — the error nets to zero at portfolio level, and a correction notice would dent the office's credibility.
- B) Issue the correction now to the pack's recipients, thank him for surfacing his own error quickly, involve him in drafting the correction so he learns the repair process, and separately review how the transposition escaped the pack's quality checks.
- C) Issue the correction personally without naming him, and quietly tighten personal review of his work.
- D) Issue the correction and formally note the error in his performance record as a data-quality incident.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two programmes' forecasts are materially wrong in leadership's hands; "nets to zero" repeats the aggregation fallacy, since leadership acts on programme-level figures. Thanking him fast is the incentive structure that keeps errors self-reported (option D's punishment teaches concealment instead — though it would be the right call for concealment or recklessness, not a self-surfaced first error). Involving him in the fix builds the skill; the quality-check review targets the system, since a transposition reaching publication is a control failure, not just a person failure. Option C substitutes surveillance for a system fix. This tests whether the candidate can correct fast, protect psychological safety, and fix the control, all at once.

**Question 3 (moderate) — The forum's quiet capture**

The candidate chairs the portfolio's monthly dependency forum. Over recent months, one programme's delivery director — articulate, senior, well-prepared — has increasingly dominated: dependencies affecting his programme get resolved first and favourably; two smaller services' items are routinely squeezed into the final minutes or deferred; their representatives have begun sending apologies. Minutes show every decision individually defensible. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Nothing — decisions are defensible, attendance is voluntary, and strong preparation deserves its wins.
- B) Restructure the forum's mechanics: time-boxed agenda ordered by portfolio priority rather than by voice, dependencies triaged on evidence before the meeting, written positions from all affected parties circulated in advance — and personally re-recruit the two smaller services, telling them what has changed and why their items need their presence.
- C) Take the delivery director aside and ask him to contribute less.
- D) Rotate the chair role to the delivery director, giving him responsibility for balance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Every decision defensible, yet the pattern skewed, is capture by process rather than misconduct — and the smaller services' withdrawal converts soft dominance into hard absence. Option B fixes the machine: priority-ordered agendas, pre-circulated evidence, and active re-recruitment of the services that stopped attending. Option A mistakes the absence of rule-breaking for the absence of a problem. Option C attacks the person rather than the structure. Option D hands the captured instrument to its principal beneficiary. This tests whether the candidate redesigns mechanics rather than managing personalities when a governance forum drifts.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The improvement that implicates the candidate**

Reviewing assurance data, a new analyst finds that response plans for critical findings — required within ten working days — have averaged nineteen days over the past year, and that the tracking spreadsheet's owner field for a third of overdue plans reads simply "portfolio office". The process design, including the vague owner field, is the candidate's own, from two years ago. She asks how the candidate would like her to present the analysis. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell her to present it fully and factually, including the process-design cause and its authorship, and add a personal commitment to a redesign with named owners and escalating reminders — then take both finding and fix to leadership together.
- B) Tell her to present the averages but attribute the delays to programme teams' slow responses, which is partly true.
- C) Thank her, take the spreadsheet, and handle the matter from here.
- D) Tell her to hold the analysis until the redesign is complete, so the finding and fix land together in a better light.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Nineteen against a ten-day standard is a genuine compliance breach in the office's own machinery, and the vague owner field is the diagnostic heart of it. Option A lets the evidence speak whole, names authorship without theatre, pairs the finding with a fix, and protects the analyst's integrity at the exact moment she is testing whether truth here is negotiable. Option B corrupts her analysis with a partial truth. Option C removes her from her own work and looks like suppression however kindly intended. Option D delays disclosure of a live compliance finding for presentational advantage. This tests whether the candidate's improvement orientation survives self-implication.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The director's summary**

At the investment committee, a director summarises the candidate's carefully caveated assessment — "the programme can achieve March if the supplier's November release lands on time and the migration dress-rehearsal succeeds" — as: "Portfolio office confirms March is achievable. Next item." The minutes will record it. The committee is moving on. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it stand — interrupting a director over nuance is disproportionate, and the caveats are in the written paper anyway.
- B) Intervene briefly now: "One precision for the minutes — March is achievable subject to two conditions, the November release and the dress-rehearsal; we will report against both." Then let the meeting proceed.
- C) Say nothing in the meeting but email the secretariat afterwards asking for the minutes to reflect the written paper.
- D) Interrupt to restate the full assessment, taking the committee back through the analysis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Minutes are institutional memory, and the gap between "confirms achievable" and "achievable subject to two named conditions" is exactly where accountability disputes are later fought. Option B corrects the record at the moment it forms, in one sentence, with a forward commitment, at negligible cost to the meeting. Option A trades the record for comfort. Option C repairs downstream, after the committee has already heard and acted on the unconditional version. Option D spends the committee's patience unnecessarily. This tests whether the candidate defends the exact size of their own assessment, at the cost of one graceful sentence, every time.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: the recurring blocker**

For the third time in four months, a programme's release has been delayed because the central security team's review queue exceeded its published ten-day turnaround — this time by four weeks. The programme's delivery manager is furious; the security team lead cites two vacancies and a major incident. Ask which response is LEAST effective.

- A) Convene the security lead and the portfolio's delivery managers to build a shared forward view of review demand, so programmes can book slots early — and take the structural capacity gap to leadership with the data on all three delays.
- B) Work with the security lead on interim triage criteria — which review types genuinely need the full process, which can be expedited on defined conditions — and publish the criteria to all programmes.
- C) Escalate the pattern to leadership with quantified delivery impact, recommending they either fund the security team's vacancies or formally accept the delivery consequences.
- D) Advise the programme to schedule future releases assuming a six-week security review, and share that guidance with other programmes.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Options A, B and C each attack the mismatch between review demand and capacity from a legitimate angle. Option D quietly institutionalises the failure: it accepts a breach of a published standard as the new planning parameter and, by sharing it portfolio-wide, converts one team's capacity crisis into everyone's permanent tax — realistic-sounding and immediately useful, which is exactly why passive normalisation is a common senior failure. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes buffering as an explicit interim measure (fine, inside A–C) from buffering as the whole response (surrender dressed as pragmatism).

**Question 7 (moderate) — The confidential heads-up**

A trusted delivery manager tells the candidate privately: his programme director — who reports the programme's status upward — has for two cycles reported "minor slippage, recovering" while the internal burn-down shows the programme will miss its statutory deadline by six to eight weeks unless descoped. He shows the internal data and asks the candidate not to reveal how they know, fearing for his position. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Promise full confidentiality and act as if nothing is known until official reporting catches up.
- B) Tell him materiality means action is required, but it can happen through means that protect him: the portfolio office has standing authority to verify any programme's reporting — commission an evidence-based review of the programme's milestone and burn-down data through the normal assurance route, which will surface the same facts officially, keeping his name out of it entirely.
- C) Confront the programme director immediately with what the delivery manager showed.
- D) Report both the discrepancy and its source to the programme director's line manager the same day.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A statutory deadline at risk behind two cycles of misreporting cannot be ignored, ruling out option A. Options C and D each destroy the informant's protection in a different way, and with it the informal intelligence network the assurance function depends on. Option B resolves the dilemma through the mechanism built for it: standing verification authority surfaces the facts officially, attributed to evidence rather than the informant, weeks earlier than official reporting would allow. This tests whether the candidate can hold both duties — acting on material risk and protecting a source — through an institutional route rather than a private promise.

**Question 8 (hard) — The strategic misalignment nobody owns**

Preparing the annual portfolio review, the candidate finds three separate programmes each building case-working capability — different suppliers, different technology, overlapping user groups — combined spend £7m. Each was approved individually across three years; each is individually green; no governance body ever saw them side by side, because the investment committee reviews proposals, not the portfolio's accumulated shape. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Note the duplication in the annual review's appendix; all three programmes passed governance properly, so no action is warranted.
- B) Present the duplication prominently in the review with options analysis — consolidation candidates, interoperability minimums, or accepted divergence with rationale — and propose the systemic fix: a portfolio-shape review at the investment committee, so accumulated capability overlap becomes visible before the next approval, with the portfolio office maintaining the capability map that enables it.
- C) Halt the two later programmes' case-working workstreams pending a consolidation study.
- D) Raise the duplication privately with the three SROs and let them agree a way forward among themselves.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** £7m of overlapping capability, every pound properly approved, is a governance design gap rather than a compliance failure. Option B treats both layers: honest options analysis for the instance, and a systemic fix — a portfolio-shape view fed by a capability map — that prevents recurrence. Option A files a £7m finding without addressing value. Option C substitutes unilateral delivery intervention for a governance decision not yet made. Option D delegates a portfolio-level trade-off to the parties least able to resolve it. This tests whether the candidate can surface the instance and fix the structural blindness at the same time.

**Question 9 (hard) — Rank the responses: the deadline and the data protection question**

Thursday. A programme launching Monday — ministerially announced, media expected — asks the candidate to sign the portfolio's go-live readiness confirmation. Everything is green except one item: the data protection impact assessment (DPIA) was updated for a late design change (a new analytics module), but the data protection officer's (DPO) review of that update — normally five working days — has not happened. The programme director says the module is "plainly low-risk" and the DPO's office has confirmed they cannot review before Monday. Ask for a ranking of these responses from most to least effective:

1. Sign the confirmation with the DPIA item explicitly excepted: readiness confirmed except DPO review of the analytics update, with a recommendation the module launch is disabled until reviewed — and put the residual decision to the SRO and DPO's office today, escalating to the head of portfolio if unresolved by Friday noon.
2. Refuse to sign until the DPO review completes, whatever happens to Monday.
3. Sign the confirmation clean, noting the DPIA point verbally to the programme director — the module is probably low-risk and the announcement is immovable.
4. Sign clean, but email the DPO asking them to review urgently next week and confirm retrospectively.

- A) 1, 2, 4, 3
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 1, 4, 2, 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 refuses to launder the gap, engineers the risk down by disabling only the unreviewed module, and routes the residual decision to the accountable parties on a clock — using the confirmation instrument precisely. Response 2 never certifies what has not been verified, but sacrifices a possibly salvageable launch through blunt refusal with no attempt to optimise. Responses 3 and 4 both sign a confirmation stating something false; 4 at least creates a written trail and review commitment, while 3 buries the gap in a verbal aside. This tests whether the candidate identifies that, when a control cannot complete before a deadline, the honest instruments are exception, descope or delay — never a clean signature with private reservations.

**Question 10 (hard) — The talented analyst and the visible programme**

The candidate's strongest portfolio analyst — eighteen months in role, clearly ready for more — has been asked for by name by a high-profile programme, on secondment for six months. Losing her would set back a reporting automation project by a quarter and leave the office short for the annual review cycle. She wants to go but says she will stay if needed. The head of portfolio has left the decision with the candidate. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Keep her — the portfolio's needs are real, the request is only a secondment, and she has said she is willing to stay.
- B) Release her with visible support: negotiate the start date around the annual review's peak if possible, use the gap to stretch a second analyst into her responsibilities with coaching, and agree with her what capability she will bring back — treating the secondment as the portfolio community's development pipeline working as designed.
- C) Propose a compromise: she splits her weeks between the secondment and the portfolio office for the six months.
- D) Release her only if the programme funds a backfill contractor for the full period.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Her offer to stay is deference, not indifference — she will read the answer as evidence of what this profession does with talented people. Option A converts her loyalty into a cost she pays. Option B manages the real costs honestly: the start date negotiated, the gap converted into a second analyst's development, an agreed return-capability making the secondment an investment with a specified yield. Option C fails both sides — a divided analyst serves neither well. Option D holds her opportunity hostage to a funding negotiation. This tests whether the candidate treats developing others as measured by where their alumni go, not by how long they are kept.

**Question 11 (hard) — Most and least effective: the benchmark a candidate commissioned**

The candidate commissioned an external benchmark of the portfolio's delivery performance, expecting it to evidence improvement. Draft findings: delivery predictability genuinely improved 20%; but portfolio overhead costs run 35% above comparator organisations, and the benchmark attributes this partly to reporting processes the candidate personally designed and defended. The consultancy asks whether the candidate would like to "contextualise the overhead finding" before the final report goes to leadership. Ask which response is MOST effective, and which LEAST:

1. Ask the consultancy to move the overhead finding to an annex and lead the report with predictability.
2. Let the report stand as drafted; add a management response accepting the overhead finding, committing to a costed simplification of the reporting processes — including the ones designed by the candidate — with dates, and present both finding and response to leadership personally.
3. Challenge the benchmark's comparator set as not like-for-like, delaying the report until the methodology is re-examined.
4. Accept the report but present it to leadership through the head of portfolio, distancing from the overhead finding.

- A) Most effective: 2; least effective: 1
- B) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- C) Most effective: 3; least effective: 1
- D) Most effective: 1; least effective: 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 lets an uncomfortable, commissioned finding stand and converts it into committed, costed, dated action, presented personally — buying the credibility of everything presented after. Response 3 may sometimes be legitimate but here follows the finding's unfavourable direction, and even so leaves the finding intact rather than reshaping it. Response 4 games the messenger but leaves the information intact. Response 1 alone reshapes the report itself — annexing the unfavourable finding while leading with the favourable one manipulates what leadership reads, which is the worst failure because it is undetectable to the reader. This tests whether the candidate's self-implication survives contact with their own commissioned evidence.

**Question 12 (hard) — The minister's office and the portfolio's truth**

Wednesday afternoon. The minister's office calls, reaching the candidate directly since the head of portfolio is abroad: the minister will be asked in the House tomorrow about Programme Solway's much-reported delays, and the office wants "a line confirming the programme is back on track". Solway's verified position: recovery plan agreed and on schedule for six weeks; two of nine recovery milestones delivered, both on time; the critical third milestone — supplier performance fix — due in twelve days; delivery confidence 60% and rising. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Provide the line requested — the recovery plan is on schedule, so "back on track" is defensible, and ministers' offices need usable lines.
- B) Provide a line that is both usable and exact: "A recovery plan is in place and on schedule; the first milestones have been delivered on time; the critical supplier fix completes within a fortnight, and delivery confidence is improving" — and offer a short factual briefing note that evening so the minister can handle follow-up questions accurately.
- C) Decline to provide any line until the head of portfolio returns — parliamentary lines are above delegated authority.
- D) Refer the minister's office directly to Programme Solway's director for the line.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Back on track" asserts a completed recovery the evidence does not support — if the supplier fix slips, the minister will have told the House something the data never confirmed, sourced to this office's line, with Hansard as the record. Option C leaves the minister's office to improvise from press coverage rather than the dashboard. Option B threads both risks: every clause verifiable against the data, usable as drafted, reinforced by a briefing note for follow-ups, with the absent head of portfolio copied immediately rather than used as an excuse for paralysis. Option D deletes the portfolio's verification role at the moment it matters most. This tests whether the candidate can produce a line that still reads true after the twelve days run.

### Administration tips

- Score against the organisation's own behaviour framework — honesty and transparency, ownership, proportionate escalation, improvement orientation — rather than a single fixed key, since several options are often defensible.
- For ranking items, credit the candidate's reasoning for the ordering, not only the final sequence.
- Watch for the recurring senior temptation patterns: normalising a broken standard, absorbing what should be surfaced, spinning what should be reported, keeping who should be released, signing what needs excepting.
- Note whether the candidate proposes an honest middle instrument — an exception, a caveat, a dual-baseline report — rather than treating every dilemma as a binary between refusal and capitulation.
- Debrief on whether a candidate's chosen response would still read as true if things go wrong on their natural timescale.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that certifies or signs off something unverified, however diplomatically worded.
- Missing when a response normalises a broken standard by presenting it as pragmatic.
- Rewarding suppression dressed as kindness — a quietly handled correction, an absorbed finding, an unnamed fix.
- Failing to note when a response burns an informant's confidentiality, or promises silence the candidate cannot institutionally guarantee.
- Crediting a personality-level fix (asking someone to behave differently) where the scenario calls for a structural one.
- Missing self-implication failures — a candidate who holds others to a standard they exempt themselves from.
- Confusing rigour with refusal — failing to credit a candidate who finds the honest middle instrument between capitulation and blanket refusal.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through a demanding, role-specific set of assessment material: dependency chains and critical paths; ritual metrics, boundary-broken measures and the re-baselining illusion; weighted portfolio confidence and variance decomposition; governance language read to its modal verbs and exceptions; and the dilemmas that define senior portfolio practice — the Sunday-night rating request, the finding that implicates the assessor's own design, the informant who needs protecting, the minister's office that needs a line that will still be true in twelve days.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level. Where a candidate's answer is defensible but not the strongest option, use the debrief to name what the strongest option does differently, since that is where most of the developmental value of the exercise sits.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
