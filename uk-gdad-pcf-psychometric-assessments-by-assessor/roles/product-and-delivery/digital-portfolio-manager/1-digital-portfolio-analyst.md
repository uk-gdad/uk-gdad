## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking skills this role actually uses, through scenarios drawn from real work rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of shapes and sequences, a candidate sees the everyday materials of portfolio support work: project trackers, RAG status tables, milestone lists, risk logs, and meeting actions, and is asked to spot patterns, follow rules to their logical conclusions, find errors, and solve small practical problems.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions. Scoring is typically norm-referenced and often adaptive. Most platforms offer unscored practice questions before the timer starts; encourage the candidate to take them.

This kind of assessment matters for a digital portfolio analyst because the core value of the role is reliable, accurate information handling. When a candidate updates a tracker, checks a status report, or prepares figures for a senior portfolio manager, a small unnoticed error can travel a long way — into governance boards, stakeholder updates, and ministerial briefings.

### How this assessment maps to the role

Each dimension of the cognitive assessment lines up with a named skill in the role:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: presenting analysis and visualisations clearly starts with noticing patterns in the data — which projects keep slipping, which risks keep recurring, which figures do not fit the trend.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance**: following governance and assurance processes under supervision means applying rules correctly to real cases.
- **Error checking** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Governance and assurance**: finding the row that contradicts the summary, or the RAG rating that does not match the narrative, is one of the most valuable things an analyst does.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Planning**: understanding the importance of planning and forecasting includes deciding what matters most when several tasks compete for time.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Agile working** and **Strategic thinking**: an open-minded approach and quick iteration require thinking a problem through step by step and choosing a sensible next move.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a project tracker**

A portfolio tracker lists projects using a fixed code format: two letters for the directorate, a hyphen, then three digits (for example, "DS-104"). While tidying the tracker, a candidate sees: (1) DS-104; (2) CT-092; (3) DS-17; (4) HR-233; (5) CT-310. Which entry breaks the coding pattern?

- A) Entry 2
- B) Entry 3
- C) Entry 4
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Entry 3, "DS-17", has only two digits after the hyphen, breaking the two-letters-hyphen-three-digits rule. This tests spotting a malformed code before it spreads into reports and dashboards — consistent project codes are what let a tracker sort, filter and match records reliably.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a governance rule**

A governance process states: "Any project rated red at the monthly review must be discussed at the next portfolio board." At this month's review, Project Kestrel was rated amber and Project Heron was rated red. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Project Kestrel will not be discussed at the portfolio board.
- B) Project Heron must be discussed at the next portfolio board.
- C) Project Heron will be re-rated amber before the board.
- D) Both projects must be discussed at the next portfolio board.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Heron is red, so the rule requires it be discussed. Option A over-reads the rule — it says nothing about what cannot happen to amber projects; C and D invent information the rule does not give. This tests applying a governance rule exactly as written without adding assumptions, and knowing that uncertainty about an amber project is a question for a senior portfolio manager, not a guess.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a status summary**

A monthly portfolio summary says: "Of our 12 projects, 7 are green, 4 are amber and 2 are red." What is wrong with this sentence?

- A) Nothing — the sentence is correct.
- B) The RAG categories are listed in the wrong order.
- C) The numbers add up to 13, not 12.
- D) Red projects should always be listed first.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 7 + 4 + 2 = 13, not the stated 12. This tests the totals-and-breakdown check — whenever a summary gives both a total and a breakdown, adding the breakdown catches a surprising number of real mistakes before they reach stakeholders.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritising a morning**

At 9:00 Tuesday, four tasks compete: (1) a senior portfolio manager needs the updated milestone tracker for an 11:00 meeting with a director; (2) a project manager has emailed asking for an explanation of a figure in last month's report, no deadline given; (3) the weekly team stand-up is at 9:30 (15 minutes); (4) tidying the shared folder structure was planned for this week. Which order of attention is most sensible?

- A) 4, 3, 1, 2 — finish planned work first
- B) 3, 1, 2, 4 — attend the stand-up, then complete the tracker for 11:00, then reply to the email, then tidy
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4 — always answer emails first
- D) 1, 3, 2, 4 — skip the stand-up until the tracker is done

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stand-up is a short fixed commitment leaving ample time before the urgent, high-impact tracker deadline; the email has no deadline; the folder tidy is background work. This tests sequencing by deadline and impact together while protecting a short fixed team commitment, rather than prioritising routine work (A), treating all email as urgent (C), or sacrificing team alignment unnecessarily (D).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across monthly reports**

Milestones completed per month over three months: Project A 4, 4, 4; Project B 6, 5, 3; Project C 2, 3, 4; Project D 5, 5, 4. Which project's trend most needs a conversation with the team?

- A) Project A, because it never improves
- B) Project B, because its completions are falling month on month, and the drop is accelerating
- C) Project C, because it started with the lowest number
- D) Project D, because any drop is a concern

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Project A is stable, C is improving, D shows a minor one-month dip, but B falls from 6 to 5 to 3 — an accelerating decline most likely to signal a real blocker. This tests comparing the direction and speed of a trend rather than judging by starting point (C) or treating stability as failure (A).

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction with two conditions**

An assurance checklist states: (i) a project may move to "in delivery" only if its business case is approved; (ii) a project with an approved business case must have a named senior responsible owner (SRO). Project Osprey is in the "in delivery" stage. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Project Osprey has a named SRO.
- B) Project Osprey's business case was approved late.
- C) Project Osprey is rated green.
- D) Project Osprey has no open risks.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Being in delivery requires an approved business case (rule i), which in turn requires a named SRO (rule ii) — a two-step chain of deduction. Options B, C and D introduce information the rules never mention. This tests chaining rules correctly and concluding only what they guarantee — a genuine governance gap (an in-delivery project with no recorded SRO) is exactly what this reasoning surfaces.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking dates in a milestone plan**

A plan lists: Discovery complete 12 May; Alpha assessment 3 July; Alpha starts 19 May; Beta starts 28 June. Stages must run in order: discovery, then alpha (ending with the alpha assessment), then beta. Which two dates are inconsistent with the required order?

- A) Discovery complete and Alpha starts
- B) Alpha starts and Alpha assessment
- C) Alpha assessment and Beta starts
- D) Discovery complete and Beta starts

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Beta is planned to start (28 June) five days before alpha's own assessment concludes it (3 July) — beta cannot start before alpha finishes. This tests re-sorting a jumbled table into logical order before checking for contradictions, since disorder is exactly what hides an error, and raising the finding as a query rather than a silent fix.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a data gap**

A monthly portfolio report is due by 16:00 today. Eleven of twelve project teams have sent updates; the twelfth, Project Linnet, has not responded to two reminders, and its project manager is on leave until tomorrow. What is the most sensible course of action?

- A) Delay the whole report until tomorrow so it is complete.
- B) Copy last month's Linnet update into this month's report without comment.
- C) Fill in Linnet's section based on what seems probably happening.
- D) Send the report on time with Linnet's section clearly marked "update not received — carried over from last month, to be confirmed", and tell the senior portfolio manager.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Option D keeps the deadline, preserves accuracy by labelling the gap, and keeps the manager informed. Option B presents stale data as current, misleading every reader; C invents status information. This tests handling imperfect information honestly, delivering on time with known gaps clearly flagged rather than sacrificing eleven good updates for one missing one.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in risk log entries**

Four recently logged risks: A — "Key developer leaving in June; knowledge transfer not planned." B — "Supplier contract expires in August; renewal not started." C — "Test environment shared with another programme; availability uncertain." D — "Lead user researcher role vacant for eight weeks; recruitment ongoing." Is there a common theme worth raising at portfolio level?

- A) All four risks are about software quality.
- B) Three of the four risks (A, B and D) concern people or supplier resourcing gaps, suggesting a portfolio-wide resourcing theme.
- C) All four risks are the fault of poor project management.
- D) There is no meaningful pattern in only four entries.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A, B and D are variations of "the people or partners we rely on may not be there"; C is a different, infrastructure-based risk. This tests grouping risks by underlying cause to spot a cross-project pattern individual project teams cannot see — a candidate reservation ("worth a portfolio-level look?") is exactly the kind of tactical recommendation the role produces, rather than leaping to blame (C) or dismissing a small but real signal (D).

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-step deduction about a reporting cycle**

Reporting rules: (i) updates are due by the last Friday of the month; (ii) the portfolio report is issued three working days after; (iii) the board meets the first Wednesday of the month and must receive the report at least one working day before. In one month, the last Friday is the 27th, and the first Wednesday of the next month is the 1st. Assuming no bank holidays, can the standard cycle deliver on time?

- A) Yes — the report is issued on the 30th, one working day before the board.
- B) Yes — the report is issued on the 1st, the day of the board.
- C) No — the report would be issued on Wednesday the 1st, the day the board meets, leaving no working day's notice.
- D) No — updates are due after the board meets.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Counting three working days from Friday 27th (Mon 30th, Tue 31st, Wed 1st) lands the report's issue date on the same day the board meets, breaching the one-day notice requirement. This tests careful calendar reasoning through a multi-step rule chain, spotting a cycle that fails in a particular month rather than assuming it always works.

**Question 11 (hard) — Prioritisation with a governance conflict**

At 14:00, four requests arrive: (1) a senior portfolio manager asks for figures in tomorrow's board paper to be checked; (2) a project manager asks for a new risk to be added "sometime this week"; (3) a director's assistant asks for portfolio spend figures "as soon as possible" for an unspecified purpose; (4) a teammate asks for help formatting a slide deck for next Monday. Checking the board paper reveals a figure contradicting the tracker, requiring most of the afternoon to resolve. What is the best course of action?

- A) Do the director's request first because directors are the most senior.
- B) Focus on the board paper correction, tell the senior portfolio manager about the contradiction immediately, and ask the director's assistant what deadline and purpose the spend figures are for; schedule the other two tasks for later in the week.
- C) Split the afternoon evenly across all four tasks so nobody is kept waiting.
- D) Correct the board paper figure personally without mentioning the contradiction, to save the manager worry.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The board paper is both urgent and high-impact and takes priority; clarifying the vague "as soon as possible" request is professional rather than deferential; and the contradiction found is escalated rather than silently corrected. This tests distinguishing urgency from seniority (A) and fairness from effectiveness (C), and recognising that silently editing a governance paper (D) bypasses the supervision this role is built around.

**Question 12 (hard) — Applied problem solving across the portfolio**

Three projects each depend on a central design team that can support at most two projects per month. Project X needs design support in May and June; Project Y in June only; Project Z in May, June and July. Nobody has cross-checked these plans. What should be reported?

- A) There is no problem, because each project's own plan is achievable.
- B) In June all three projects need the design team at once, exceeding its capacity of two; the portfolio needs to re-sequence at least one project's design work.
- C) The design team should be told to work faster in June.
- D) Project Y should be cancelled because it only needs one month of support.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Building a month-by-month picture shows May and July are within capacity, but June has all three projects competing for two slots — a clash invisible to any single project's own plan. This tests the essence of portfolio-level analysis: seeing across individual plans to find a conflict none of them shows alone, and proposing a proportionate fix (re-sequencing) rather than wishing the constraint away (C) or a wildly disproportionate response (D).

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate applies a governance rule exactly as written** without adding unstated assumptions.
- **Watch for whether a candidate compares trends by direction and pace**, not by a single number or starting point.
- **Note whether a candidate escalates a discrepancy or gap rather than silently fixing or inventing it.**
- **Note whether a candidate re-sorts jumbled information into logical order** before checking it for errors.
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that adds assumptions to a governance rule** beyond what it guarantees.
- **Missing when a candidate judges a trend by its latest or starting figure** rather than its direction and pace.
- **Crediting an answer that silently fixes a discrepancy** rather than flagging it.
- **Missing when a candidate confuses "no deadline" with "not urgent"**, or the reverse.
- **Penalising a candidate for a defensible answer to a hard question** — this is an entry-level role, so look for improving reasoning across the section, not a flawless score.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately a candidate works with the numbers this role genuinely handles: percentages of projects at each RAG status, spend against budget, milestone completion rates, simple averages across projects, and month-on-month trends. A candidate is typically shown a small table or chart and asked several questions about it.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 30 minutes, with 15 to 20 questions. A calculator is often permitted; offer rough paper.

This kind of assessment matters for a digital portfolio analyst because so much of the role's output is numerical — turning raw figures into the percentages and trends that appear in reports. A misplaced decimal or a percentage calculated against the wrong base can misinform a governance board.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Communicating analysis and insight**: portfolio reporting speaks in percentages, and presenting analysis clearly starts with calculating them correctly.
- **Averages and rates** map to **Planning**: understanding forecasting begins with simple averages.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Governance and assurance**: extracting the right number from the right row is a daily task.
- **Budget and spend arithmetic** maps to **Strategic thinking**: supporting strategic planning in an administrative capacity includes knowing whether the portfolio is over or under budget.
- **Trend comparison** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: using evidence to explain decisions is very often a trend, calculated correctly.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of projects at green**

A portfolio has 20 projects; 13 are rated green. What percentage of the portfolio is green?

- A) 13%
- B) 55%
- C) 65%
- D) 70%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 13 ÷ 20 = 65%. Option A quotes the raw count as a percentage. This tests a basic percentage calculation, and, for a strong candidate's answer, sense-checking against a half and three-quarters before committing.

**Question 2 (easy) — Spend against budget**

A project has an annual budget of £250,000; spend to date is £180,000. How much budget remains?

- A) £60,000
- B) £70,000
- C) £80,000
- D) £430,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** £250,000 − £180,000 = £70,000. Option D results from adding instead of subtracting. This tests a basic subtraction with a sense-check that remaining budget can never exceed the whole budget.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a RAG table**

| Directorate | Green | Amber | Red |
|---|---|---|---|
| Digital Services | 5 | 2 | 1 |
| Corporate | 3 | 3 | 0 |
| Operations | 4 | 1 | 2 |

How many amber projects are there across the whole portfolio?

- A) 5
- B) 6
- C) 7
- D) 12

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 2 + 3 + 1 = 6. Option D sums the Green column instead. This tests careful column tracing in a table — a common real-work error is drifting into the wrong column, especially side by side on screen.

**Question 4 (easy) — Milestone completion rate**

In April, 25 milestones were planned and 19 completed. What was the completion rate, to the nearest whole percent?

- A) 19%
- B) 74%
- C) 76%
- D) 79%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 19 ÷ 25 = 76%. This tests a basic completion-rate calculation, and, for a strong candidate's answer, using a shortcut such as multiplying both sides by 4 to make the denominator 100.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Average milestones per project**

Five projects completed 6, 3, 8, 5, and 3 milestones this quarter. What is the mean number of milestones per project?

- A) 4
- B) 5
- C) 5.5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum = 25; ÷5 = 5. This tests a basic average, and, for a strong candidate's answer, sense-checking that a mean must fall inside the range of its values (3 to 8), eliminating options outside that range instantly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change in delivery**

40 milestones were completed in Q1 and 50 in Q2. What is the percentage increase?

- A) 10%
- B) 20%
- C) 25%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Change = 10; 10 ÷ 40 (the original) = 25%. Option B divides by the new value instead. This tests always dividing by the starting value — dividing by the wrong base would overstate or understate a trend reported to stakeholders.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Ratio of spend across projects**

Two projects share a combined budget of £360,000 in the ratio 5:4 (Project Swift : Project Martin). How much is Project Martin's share?

- A) £144,000
- B) £160,000
- C) £180,000
- D) £200,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 9 parts total; one part = £40,000; Martin's 4 parts = £160,000. Option D is Swift's share. This tests the total-the-parts, find-one-part, multiply method for ratios, and matching the correct side of a named ratio to the correct project.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a spend trend table**

Monthly spend (£000s): Jan 110, Feb 120, Mar 135, Apr 155. Between which two consecutive months was the largest increase, and how big was it?

- A) Jan to Feb: £10,000
- B) Feb to Mar: £15,000
- C) Mar to Apr: £20,000
- D) Jan to Apr: £45,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Increases are 10, 15, 20 — accelerating, with the largest between March and April. Option D wrongly treats a non-consecutive span as the answer to a question asking about consecutive months. This tests reading a question's exact wording precisely, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting the accelerating pattern as a worthwhile observation.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Percentage of budget spent, portfolio-wide**

| Project | Budget (£) | Spend to date (£) |
|---|---|---|
| Swift | 200,000 | 150,000 |
| Martin | 160,000 | 120,000 |
| Wren | 240,000 | 90,000 |

What percentage of the combined budget has been spent, to the nearest whole percent?

- A) 55%
- B) 60%
- C) 63%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total budget £600,000; total spend £360,000; 360,000 ÷ 600,000 = 60%. Option C comes from averaging the three individual percentages, which misweights a small project equally with a large one. This tests computing a combined percentage from totals over totals, never from an average of separate percentages.

**Question 10 (hard) — Forecasting spend to year end**

By the end of month 4, £96,000 of a £300,000 annual budget has been spent. If spending continues at the same average monthly rate, what will total spend be at year end, and will the project be over or under budget?

- A) £288,000 — under budget
- B) £288,000 — over budget
- C) £312,000 — over budget
- D) £192,000 — under budget

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Average £24,000/month × 12 = £288,000, under the £300,000 budget. This tests the straight-line forecast method (spend so far ÷ months so far × total months), and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting the assumption of a stable rate as a caveat worth stating.

**Question 11 (hard) — Combined completion rate across two quarters**

Q1: 36 of 48 planned milestones completed. Q2: 44 of 52. What was the overall completion rate across both quarters, to the nearest whole percent?

- A) 75%
- B) 80%
- C) 82%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total completed 80 ÷ total planned 100 = 80%. Options A and D are the individual quarterly rates. This tests combining rates from totals rather than averaging the two separate percentages — the safe habit that stays correct even when quarters differ greatly in size.

**Question 12 (hard) — Interpreting a stacked dashboard figure**

A dashboard reports: "Portfolio spend to date: £1.08 million, which is 90% of profiled spend for this point in the year. Full-year budget: £1.8 million." One board member says the portfolio has spent 90% of its annual budget; another says the portfolio is 10% behind its planned spend profile. Who is right, and what fraction of the annual budget has actually been spent?

- A) Member 1 is right; 90% of the annual budget is spent.
- B) Member 2 is right; 60% of the annual budget is spent.
- C) Both are right.
- D) Member 2 is right; 54% of the annual budget is spent.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Profiled spend" is the plan's expected spend by now, not the annual budget; £1.08m being 90% of profile means the portfolio is 10% behind profile. Separately, £1.08m ÷ £1.8m = 60% of annual budget. This tests naming the base of every percentage before quoting it — "90% of what?" is one of the most valuable questions a portfolio analyst can ask.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate checks the direction of a division** — completed over planned, change over original — before calculating.
- **Watch for whether a candidate computes combined rates from totals** rather than averaging separate percentages.
- **Note whether a candidate names the base of a percentage** before quoting or comparing it.
- **Note whether a candidate reads a question's exact wording** (such as "consecutive months") before answering.
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that divides by the wrong base** in a percentage-change or profile calculation.
- **Crediting an answer that averages percentages** rather than using totals over totals.
- **Missing when a candidate drifts into the wrong column or row** of a table.
- **Crediting an answer that answers a non-consecutive or differently scoped question** than the one actually asked.
- **Missing when a candidate skips a sense-check** that would have caught an implausible answer.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads, understands and draws conclusions from written material of the kind this role handles daily: governance guidance, status report narratives, emails from project managers and stakeholders, and short policy passages. The classic format presents a passage followed by statements to judge as **True**, **False**, or **Cannot say**. Other question types test inference and clear-writing judgement.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes, with 20 to 30 statements across several passages. The governing rule: judge every statement using only the passage, not outside knowledge.

This kind of assessment matters for a digital portfolio analyst because so much of the role is reading and relaying written information faithfully — any gap between what was written and what was understood becomes an error in the portfolio's records.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading comprehension** maps to **Governance and assurance**: following governance and assurance processes under supervision starts with reading process documents accurately.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: relaying project information means separating what the source actually says from what is assumed.
- **Inference** maps to **Strategic thinking**: explaining the strategic context of work means drawing sound conclusions from documents without over-reaching.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: choosing plain language for the audience.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Agile working**: iterating quickly requires grasping the essence of feedback and source material fast.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–3)*

"All projects in the digital portfolio must submit a monthly status report by the last working day of the month. Reports must include a RAG rating, progress against milestones, and an updated risk summary. Projects rated red must additionally provide a recovery plan. The portfolio office reviews all reports within three working days of submission and may request clarification from project teams. Projects that miss two consecutive submission deadlines will be raised at the portfolio board."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Projects rated red must provide a recovery plan."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only for large projects

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states this directly. Option D adds a condition the passage never states. This tests matching a clean statement to the passage carefully, without adding unstated qualifications.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "The portfolio office reviews reports within one working day of submission."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for red projects only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states three working days, directly contradicting the claimed one day. This tests distinguishing False (the passage contradicts a statement) from Cannot say (the passage is silent) — here the information is present and the statement gets it wrong.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A project that misses one submission deadline will be raised at the portfolio board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, if it is also rated red

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage's trigger is two consecutive missed deadlines; it says nothing about a single miss. This tests avoiding the over-inference that a rule stating what happens at two misses implies nothing happens at one — rules state what must happen, not everything that may.

*Passage 2 (for Questions 4–6)*

"The department's digital strategy commits to reducing legacy technology risk across the portfolio. From April, business cases for new projects must include an assessment of their impact on legacy systems. The portfolio office will pilot a quarterly legacy-risk dashboard for six months, initially covering the ten highest-spend projects. If the pilot proves useful to decision makers, coverage will extend to the whole portfolio. Project teams remain responsible for the accuracy of the data they submit."

**Question 4 (easy) — Locating a detail**

Which projects will the pilot dashboard initially cover?

- A) All projects in the portfolio
- B) The ten highest-spend projects
- C) Projects with the most legacy risk
- D) Projects that started after April

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states selection is by spend, not risk. This tests reading what the document actually specifies rather than what would seem logical (C) — plausible reasoning is not what the passage says.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference**

Which one of the following can be properly inferred from the passage?

- A) The legacy-risk dashboard will definitely extend to the whole portfolio.
- B) Extension of the dashboard beyond the pilot depends on whether decision makers find it useful.
- C) Legacy technology risk is the department's biggest problem.
- D) Project teams have been submitting inaccurate data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage makes extension explicitly conditional. Option A drops the "if"; C inflates a commitment into a ranking; D reverses a responsibility statement into an accusation. This tests spotting three common inference errors — dropping conditions, inflating strength, and reversing implications.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Business cases for projects that started before April must be resubmitted with a legacy impact assessment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only for the ten pilot projects

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage addresses new projects from April only; it is silent about existing projects. This tests recognising that silence is not exemption — a rule applying only to new projects establishes nothing about old ones either way.

*Passage 3 (for Questions 7–9)*

"Email from a project manager to the portfolio office: 'Hi — quick update ahead of the monthly report. We've completed the supplier selection milestone two weeks early, which is great news. However, the data migration workstream has hit a problem: the legacy extract took longer than expected, and we now expect to complete migration four weeks later than planned. We think we can recover two of those weeks in testing, as the test environment is now available earlier than assumed. On that basis we're proposing to keep our RAG rating at amber rather than moving to red. Happy to discuss.'"

**Question 7 (moderate) — Identifying the main point**

Which sentence best summarises the email for the portfolio log?

- A) "Project reports supplier selection completed two weeks early."
- B) "Project reports data migration will be four weeks late."
- C) "Project reports migration delay of four weeks, expects to recover two weeks in testing, and proposes to remain amber."
- D) "Project requests to be re-rated red."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A good summary preserves everything a reader would act on — the delay, the recovery, and the rating proposal. Options A and B are each true but dangerously partial. This tests selecting what a summary needs to include to support a decision, not merely shortening a source.

**Question 8 (moderate)**

Statement: "The project's net expected delay to migration, after recovery, is two weeks."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True only if the portfolio office agrees

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Four weeks expected delay minus two weeks expected recovery equals two weeks net — matching the email's own careful language of expectation. This tests following an email's internal arithmetic precisely, and noticing that the statement claims only expectation, matching the source's hedged wording.

**Question 9 (hard) — Evaluating the reasoning**

The project manager argues the rating should stay amber "on that basis" — the expected two-week recovery. Which unstated assumption does this argument most depend on?

- A) The supplier selection milestone was completed early.
- B) The recovery of two weeks in testing will actually be achieved.
- C) The portfolio office reads emails promptly.
- D) Other projects in the portfolio are also amber.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rating argument depends on the hoped-for recovery actually happening, which the email itself signals is uncertain ("we think we can recover"). This tests identifying an argument's load-bearing, unproven assumption — the sharp question a senior portfolio manager would ask when reviewing this proposal.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

Which version of a report sentence about the email is clearest for a non-specialist senior audience?

- A) "Utilisation of the earlier-than-anticipated availability of the test environment will facilitate partial mitigation of the migration timeline slippage."
- B) "Migration is expected four weeks late; the team expects to recover two weeks during testing and proposes remaining amber."
- C) "There are some timing issues on the project but the team is on top of it."
- D) "The legacy extract ETL overran, impacting the E2E migration critical path, though early NFT environment availability derisks the glide path."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B is plain, specific, and complete — the delay, recovery, and proposal in one sentence a non-specialist can act on. Option A is officialese; D is unexplained jargon; C is friendly but empty and hides the size of the delay. This tests choosing concrete facts and everyday words over padding or vagueness.

*Passage 4 (for Questions 11–12)*

"Extract from portfolio governance guidance: 'Portfolio reporting exists to support decision making, not to create work. Reports should be proportionate: the effort of producing them must be justified by the decisions they inform. Where information is already collected through delivery tools, it should be reused rather than re-keyed. Portfolio staff should challenge any reporting requirement that no longer serves a clear purpose, and should retire metrics that are no longer used in decisions. However, statutory and audit reporting obligations must always be met in full, regardless of local views on their usefulness.'"

**Question 11 (hard)**

Statement: "Portfolio staff may retire a statutory report if they judge that it no longer serves a clear purpose."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, with the portfolio board's approval

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage's final sentence draws a hard boundary excluding statutory and audit obligations from the general encouragement to retire unused metrics. This tests reading to the end of a passage, since a "however" clause near the end often reverses the answer the first sentences would suggest.

**Question 12 (hard) — Applying the passage's principles**

A project manager complains that a monthly report field duplicates data already held in a delivery tool, and asks whether they can stop filling it in. Based only on the passage, what is the best response?

- A) "Yes — the guidance says duplicated reporting should stop, so leave the field blank from now on."
- B) "No — every existing reporting requirement must be met in full."
- C) "The guidance supports reusing data rather than re-keying it, so I'll raise this with the portfolio office to check whether the field can be sourced from the tool or retired — unless it feeds a statutory obligation."
- D) "Fill it in for now and I'm sure it will be reviewed eventually."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The guidance authorises challenge, not unilateral abandonment, and the statutory exception must be checked. This tests applying multiple principles from a passage together — acknowledging the legitimate point, routing it through the correct process, and checking the exception — rather than converting "challenge" into "just stop" (A) or ignoring the guidance's explicit support for challenge (B).

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate treats "Cannot say" as a genuine, confident answer** rather than avoiding it.
- **Watch for whether a candidate distinguishes False (contradicted) from Cannot say (silent)** precisely.
- **Note whether a candidate reads to the end of a passage** before answering, catching a reversing "however" clause.
- **Note whether a candidate answers from the passage alone**, not from outside knowledge of how things usually work.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that treats silence as exemption** rather than as missing information.
- **Missing when a candidate imports outside job knowledge** rather than reading only the passage.
- **Crediting a plausible-but-unstated answer** over what the passage actually says.
- **Missing when a candidate answers from a passage's first half** without reading to a reversing final sentence.
- **Rewarding an impressive-sounding but jargon-laden rewrite** over a clear, concrete one in a clear-writing item.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks which response is most or least effective. Unlike the other assessments, it has no arithmetic or passage to parse — it measures professional judgement: how a candidate balances honesty, helpfulness, process, relationships and escalation when situations get awkward.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed because they measure judgement rather than speed. Formats vary — most effective, least effective, ranking, or rating each option. Scoring compares judgements with those of experienced professionals and a behavioural framework — for UK government digital roles, behaviours like user focus, collaboration, ownership, honesty and transparency, appropriate escalation, and inclusion.

This kind of assessment matters for a digital portfolio analyst because judgement, unlike technical skill, is hard to teach quickly. At this level, assessors are especially interested in whether a candidate knows the boundaries of a supervised role: when to act, when to ask, and when to escalate.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Escalation and openness scenarios** map to **Governance and assurance**: following processes under supervision means knowing what can be decided alone and what needs a senior portfolio manager.
- **Stakeholder dilemma scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: communicating clearly, tailoring communication, and using evidence to explain decisions.
- **Team and delivery scenarios** map to **Agile working**: an open-minded approach, valuing iteration, and honesty about work in progress.
- **Information-handling scenarios** map to **Communicating analysis and insight**: several dilemmas turn on whether a candidate softens bad news, oversells certainty, or shares draft data prematurely.
- **Workload and priority scenarios** map to **Planning**: sequencing by impact and urgency, protecting commitments, and renegotiating openly.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — A mistake in a sent report**

Two hours after a monthly portfolio report went out, a candidate realises one project's spend figure is wrong — £129,000 instead of £192,000. Nobody has commented yet. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — it may go unnoticed, and it can be quietly corrected next month.
- B) Tell the senior portfolio manager immediately, propose sending a short correction to recipients, and check how the error slipped through so it does not recur.
- C) Email the correction to recipients directly, without telling the senior portfolio manager, to fix it before they find out.
- D) Wait to see if anyone notices, and correct it only if someone asks.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B surfaces the error promptly, proposes the fix, involves the person accountable for the report, and learns from the miss. Options A and D gamble the portfolio's credibility on nobody noticing; C hides the event from the manager, who may need to know for assurance reasons. This tests honesty and ownership — errors are recoverable, concealment is what does lasting damage.

**Question 2 (easy) — Asked for data of uncertain sensitivity**

A friendly project manager from another directorate asks for spend figures across the portfolio "for benchmarking for my director." The candidate is unsure whether the data can be shared outside the portfolio team. What is the most effective response?

- A) Send the figures — being helpful builds relationships.
- B) Refuse — data should never leave the portfolio team.
- C) Explain the sharing rules need checking first, confirm with the senior portfolio manager, and respond as soon as known — taking the requester's details and purpose.
- D) Send the figures but ask the project manager to keep them confidential.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C balances helpfulness with proper care, captures the purpose, and commits to a prompt answer. Option A shares data of unknown sensitivity on the strength of friendliness; D acknowledges sensitivity while sharing anyway. This tests careful information-handling under social pressure — "let me check and come back to you today" is a strong answer to uncertain requests.

**Question 3 (easy) — Feedback on a first analysis**

After three days building a first portfolio trend analysis, a senior portfolio manager suggests significant changes in a team review — different groupings, a simpler chart, removal of a section the candidate was proud of. What is the most effective response?

- A) Defend each original choice in detail so the effort is not wasted.
- B) Make the changes silently but feel the analysis is no longer really yours.
- C) Ask questions to understand the reasoning behind each suggestion, make the changes, and note the principles for next time.
- D) Ask a teammate afterwards whether they think the feedback was fair.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C treats the review as a learning opportunity, converting each change into a transferable principle. Option A confuses effort with quality; B complies without learning. This tests openness to feedback and a learning orientation — the first version of any analysis is a draft, and review is the process working, not the author failing.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Conflicting instructions**

A senior portfolio manager asked for data prepared for Thursday's board; mid-morning, a senior programme manager (not the candidate's line management) asks for the day to be spent on a slide pack for tomorrow's programme review instead. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do what the programme manager says — she is more senior.
- B) Politely explain the current priority, and say the senior portfolio manager will be checked with straight away about whether and how to fit the slide pack in.
- C) Refuse, because only the line manager can give work.
- D) Try to do both by working late, telling no one.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is transparent with the programme manager, protects the board preparation until told otherwise, and puts the prioritisation decision where it belongs. Option A lets seniority override an agreed priority without the accountable person knowing; D hides a resource conflict managers need to see. This tests understanding tasking lines in a supervised role while staying helpful.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A project manager asks for a report to be softened**

A project manager reviewing a draft portfolio entry asks for a line about a real, confirmed testing delay to be removed: "We'll catch up, and I don't want the board worrying unnecessarily." What is the most effective response?

- A) Remove the line — the project manager knows their project best.
- B) Keep the line but promise not to mention it verbally at the board.
- C) Explain the report needs to reflect the known position, offer to include the project's recovery plan and context alongside the delay, and if the project manager still objects, refer the disagreement to the senior portfolio manager.
- D) Remove the line but keep a private note of it in case it becomes a problem later.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C holds the line on accuracy while offering fair context, and refers an unresolved disagreement upward. Option A hands editorial control of governance information to the people being reported on; D creates a hidden record while publishing a sanitised one. This tests integrity under pressure — bad news plus context and a plan is good reporting; bad news deleted is a governance failure.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A possible risk nobody has raised**

Updating trackers, a candidate notices three separate projects list the same small supplier as critical to their autumn milestones; nobody has mentioned the overlap, and the candidate is unsure it is a real problem. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing — supplier risk is outside the candidate's role.
- B) Raise it with the senior portfolio manager as an observation: three projects depend on one supplier in the same period, and it may be worth checking capacity.
- C) Email all three project managers telling them their plans are at risk.
- D) Add a red risk to the portfolio risk log personally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is the model analyst move — noticing a cross-project pattern, framing it honestly as an observation, and routing it to the person who can judge and act. Option A wastes the observation; C broadcasts an unverified alarm; D overreaches the supervised governance level with a formal act made on uncertain grounds. This tests the pattern of notice boldly, verify humbly, escalate promptly, act within one's level.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Least effective: handling a quiet week**

A senior portfolio manager is away for three days with no specific tasks left, and routine work fills perhaps half the time. Which response is **least effective**?

- A) Work through the backlog of tracker tidy-ups and data quality checks.
- B) Draft improvements to a report template stakeholders have found confusing, for the manager to review on return.
- C) Wait to be given work, keeping time free in case something urgent arrives.
- D) Ask teammates whether they need help with anything this week.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A, B, and D are good uses of unsupervised time — data quality investment, initiative correctly left for approval, and team support. Option C's idleness, justified by a hypothetical urgent task, is neither planning nor responsiveness, since an actual urgent task could be reprioritised for when it arrives. This tests proactive usefulness within one's authority rather than a passive default.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A stakeholder challenges the candidate's figures in a meeting**

Presenting portfolio milestone data — one of the candidate's first solo presentations — a senior stakeholder interrupts: "Those numbers are wrong. My team completed six milestones last month, not four." The candidate believes the data is right but is not certain. What is the most effective response?

- A) Insist the figures are correct — backing down undermines the portfolio office.
- B) Immediately accept their number and promise to change the report.
- C) Thank them, say the figures will be verified against the source submissions after the meeting and confirmed to them and the group, then continue with the presentation.
- D) Ask them to prove their claim in front of the group.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C neither capitulates nor digs in, commits to a verifiable check, and keeps the meeting on track. Option A defends an uncertain position; B corrupts the report on one person's say-so; D turns a data question into a public confrontation. This tests composure and honesty about uncertainty under public pressure.

**Question 9 (hard) — Discovering a team's process gap**

Preparing evidence for an internal audit, a candidate discovers the portfolio office has not carried out a required monthly data quality check for three months, predating the candidate's joining, with nobody aware. The audit meeting is next week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Start doing the check from this month and say nothing about the previous gap.
- B) Tell the senior portfolio manager what was found, with the evidence, so the team can decide how to address it and be transparent with the audit.
- C) Quietly perform the three missed checks now and backdate the records so the audit finds no gap.
- D) Mention it informally to the auditor first, since audits value candour.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is honest, properly sequenced (the manager owns the audit response), and enables a team that finds and discloses its own gap to typically earn audit credibility rather than lose it. Option C is falsification, a serious integrity breach; D goes to the auditor before the manager, breaking trust inside the team. This tests routing a serious finding up the line first, never concealing or falsifying.

**Question 10 (hard) — Balancing inclusion in a rushed team task**

A team must produce a data pack in two days; a senior analyst suggests splitting the work between herself and the candidate, "since there's no time to bring the new starter up to speed" — the new starter joined three weeks ago and looks uncomfortable nearby. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — deadlines come first, and the new starter can learn next time.
- B) Suggest the new starter take a well-defined, manageable slice of the work with a short handover, so the pack still lands on time and they build capability and belonging.
- C) Insist the work be split three ways equally, whatever the deadline.
- D) Say nothing in the meeting but reassure the new starter privately afterwards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B finds the genuine balance — a bounded slice with a brief handover costs little time and builds belonging while protecting the deadline. Option A treats inclusion as a luxury; C ignores that experience genuinely differs; D comforts without changing the exclusion. This tests serving both the delivery goal and the people, not one at the other's expense.

**Question 11 (hard) — Rank the responses: an impossible Friday**

Friday 13:30: (1) a senior portfolio manager, offline until Monday, left a note asking for the risk log updated by end of day; (2) a director's office calls asking for a portfolio summary slide by 16:00 for Monday; (3) a project manager reports their Wednesday status update contained a wrong milestone date now in the draft board pack. Rank these responses from most to least effective: (1) correct the board pack date (with a note of the change), build the 16:00 slide from existing verified data, then update the risk log, flagging by email anything unfinished; (2) update the risk log first because the manager asked first, then the slide if time remains; (3) tell the director's office a slide is impossible today and suggest Monday; (4) build the slide first, skip the correction until Monday since the board pack is only a draft.

- A) 1, 2, 4, 3
- B) 1, 4, 2, 3
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Response 1 sequences by impact and urgency — a quick correction that prevents wrong data reaching a board, a hard external deadline met with verified data, then the risk log with honest flagging of anything unfinished. Response 2 sequences by who asked first rather than impact; response 3 communicates honestly but surrenders an achievable deliverable; response 4 leaves known-wrong data in a governance pack on a weak rationale. This tests comparing options against each other on impact, honesty and feasibility, not judging each in isolation.

**Question 12 (hard) — Most and least effective: a teammate's shortcut**

A teammate copies last month's figures into report sections when projects submit late, without marking them as carried over: "Everyone knows late projects rarely change much — it keeps the report complete and nobody has ever complained." Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses: (1) say nothing — he is more experienced, and no harm has come of it; (2) tell him unmarked carry-overs could mislead readers, suggest the report adopt a clear "carried over — to be confirmed" label, and if he disagrees, raise the practice (not the person) with the senior portfolio manager; (3) report him to the senior portfolio manager immediately without speaking to him first; (4) start doing the same personally for consistency.

- A) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 3; least effective: 1
- C) Most effective: 2; least effective: 3
- D) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 addresses the practice directly and collegially, proposes a constructive labelling fix, and has a principled escalation path framed around the practice rather than the person. Response 4 is least effective, converting one person's bad habit into team practice and actively spreading the problem. This tests challenging constructively, preferring fixing systems over blaming people, and never adopting a shortcut just because it is established.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate acts within their authority, is transparent, and involves a senior portfolio manager for decisions above their level.**
- **Watch for whether a candidate reads the question stem carefully** — "most effective", "least effective", "rank all four" need different responses.
- **Note whether a candidate compares options against each other** in a ranking item rather than judging each in isolation.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes protecting image from protecting accuracy** — softened reports, hidden gaps, or quiet fixes should score poorly however kind their motive.
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding deference over transparency** — "they're senior, so do what they say" fails whenever it hides a conflict or corrupts information.
- **Crediting an answer that confuses kindness with concealment** — removing worrying lines, covering for teammates, sparing "unnecessary worry."
- **Missing silent heroics** — working late secretly, absorbing impossible workloads, fixing others' errors without a word.
- **Rewarding overreach disguised as initiative** — logging formal risks or emailing alarms on one's own authority skips the supervision this level is designed around.
- **Missing when a candidate escalates a colleague's behaviour before a direct conversation**, except in cases of serious misconduct such as falsifying records.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a digital portfolio analyst: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested checking project codes and milestone dates the way they are checked in a tracker, applying governance rules exactly, and spotting cross-project patterns invisible to any single project. The numeric section tested the arithmetic that fills portfolio dashboards — completion percentages, spend against budget, combined rates, and honest forecasts. The verbal section tested reading exactly what a passage says, holding the line at "Cannot say," and choosing the clearest way to relay findings. The situational judgement section tested workplace dilemmas a portfolio analyst genuinely faces — correcting a sent report, holding the line when asked to soften bad news, and knowing when to ask rather than guess.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on a different answer, particularly on harder items, is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score, particularly valuable at this level, where the goal is as much development as selection.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can think carefully with portfolio artefacts, work accurately with the numbers that fill dashboards, read precisely, and exercise sound judgement while learning under supervision — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
