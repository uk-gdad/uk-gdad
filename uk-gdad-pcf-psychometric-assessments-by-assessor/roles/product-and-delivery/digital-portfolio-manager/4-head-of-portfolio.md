# Head of Portfolio - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of portfolio. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments at this level are a distinctive proposition. They are less about whether a candidate can compute, read or decide — a career at this altitude has evidenced all three — and more about the reliability and calibration of judgement at organisational scale: whether reasoning holds when data is aggregated and adversarial, whether reading of strategy, policy and assurance language is exact enough to be quoted, whether decisions correctly locate authority and accountability, and whether the standards a candidate applies to their portfolio survive being applied to themselves. Selection panels and development centres for head-level roles increasingly deploy contextualised cognitive, numerical, verbal and situational instruments precisely because these qualities predict what interviews struggle to observe: how a leader thinks under load.

The role frames everything in this guide. It leads the tracking and governing of digital projects and ensures the right controls are used; addresses risks, and deals with or escalates issues; provides leaders with insights into the portfolio's future so they can make informed decisions; and builds and leads a diverse, skilled and motivated team of digital portfolio managers, ensuring the portfolio team meets the organisation's needs and supporting the professional development of the portfolio community through line management and coaching. The four assessments here are constructed from those duties: the cognitive section tests multi-constraint reasoning over investment, capacity and governance design; the numeric section tests the portfolio-level arithmetic behind funding decisions, benefits trajectories and organisational metrics; the verbal section tests precision with strategy, policy and ministerial-adjacent language; and the situational judgement section rehearses the dilemmas that reach a head of portfolio's desk precisely because they cannot be resolved below it.

Each of the four sections follows the same structure: what it measures and how it typically runs; how its dimensions map to the role's named skills — Agile working, communicating analysis and insight, governance and assurance, planning, stakeholder relationship management and strategic thinking; graduated practice questions with complete worked explanations; then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. At head level, several situational and verbal reasoning items in this guide deliberately have more than one defensible response, and some explicitly test whether a candidate applies their own standards to themselves as rigorously as to others — score for the quality and symmetry of the reasoning, not simple letter-matching. Use each section's per-item timing as a guide for planning a session, and debrief against the explanations below, since much of the value in a head-level debrief lies in naming exactly what the strongest option does differently from a defensible-but-weaker one.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures reasoning — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, applied problem solving — through scenarios built from the material this role actually governs: investment portfolios, control frameworks, capacity models, escalation chains, organisational metrics, and assurance regimes. At head level, the questions concentrate on integration and design: reasoning across several artefacts and constraints simultaneously, detecting the flaw in a system rather than a datum, and distinguishing what evidence establishes from what it merely permits, under time pressure calibrated to executive workloads.

Typical format: online, timed, 15 to 30 minutes, 20 to 30 questions, frequently adaptive, normed against senior professional comparison groups, reported as a speed-accuracy profile. Ungraded warm-ups are usually offered; encourage the candidate to use them for interface calibration.

The relevance is structural. A head of portfolio is an organisation's instrument for seeing its change portfolio whole: aggregating what programmes report, correcting for the biases aggregation introduces, and providing leaders with insights into the portfolio's future — which is to say, this role's reasoning is itself a governance control. Loose deduction produces a loose organisational picture; missed error-checking lets boards decide on contradictions; prioritisation captured by urgency or seniority misdirects the portfolio's resources. There is also a leadership multiplier: the role builds and leads the team whose collective reasoning produces the portfolio's evidence base, and the disciplines this section drills are ones a portfolio community learns largely by watching its head exercise them.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and the duty to provide leaders with insights into the portfolio's future: foresight is pattern-reading — cross-programme recurrences, leading indicators, a metric whose shape signals a reporting problem — surfaced early enough for leaders to act.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance**: ensuring the right controls are used means reasoning exactly about what each control mandates, permits and omits, and how controls interact, including the edge cases where well-intended rules contradict each other.
- **Error checking** maps to **Governance and assurance** and the duty to lead the tracking of digital projects: at head level the errors that matter are systemic — the aggregation that conceals, the measure that broke when the process changed, the register that disagrees with the pack a board will see tomorrow.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Planning** and **Strategic thinking**: prioritising the most important or high-value work across a portfolio, under capacity constraints and against strategic criteria, is the role's core resource decision, repeated at every scale.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Planning** and the duty to address risks and deal with or escalate issues: the issues that reach a head of portfolio are the ones without clean answers, and solving them means constraint-reasoning with organisational, commercial and human variables included.
- **Team-system reasoning** maps to the duty to build and lead a diverse, skilled and motivated team: workload allocation, capability coverage and development sequencing across a team of portfolio managers are themselves constraint problems.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern in escalation flows**

Reviewing a year of escalations reaching the candidate's desk, counted by category: supplier/commercial 14; inter-programme dependency 11; funding/approval timing 9; data or reporting quality 3; team capability 2. Portfolio managers handle escalations below the candidate; anything they cannot resolve rises. Ask what the distribution most plausibly indicates.

- A) Supplier management is the portfolio's weakest discipline.
- B) The categories reaching the candidate in volume — commercial, dependency, funding — are those whose resolution needs authority or cross-boundary reach the portfolio managers lack, which is the escalation system working; the near-absence of data-quality and capability escalations, however, deserves a check: genuinely rare, or being absorbed below?
- C) The portfolio managers are escalating too much.
- D) The distribution is random and carries no information.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Escalation volume measures the boundary between what a team can resolve and what needs the head, not problem frequency. Commercial, dependency and funding issues require negotiating authority or cross-boundary reach that sits at head level — their dominance is the system routing correctly, not a discipline failing. The interesting signal is the near-zero categories: data-quality and capability issues certainly occur, so their absence means either they are resolved well below, or absorbed and hidden — invisible by construction either way. Option B reads the volumes correctly and targets the check where the information isn't. This tests whether the candidate treats an inbox as a sensor with known biases, auditing the silences as carefully as triaging the arrivals.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction across control rules**

A control framework states: (i) any programme exceeding £5m lifetime cost requires a gate review at each phase transition; (ii) gate reviews must be chaired by someone independent of the programme; (iii) heads of portfolio may chair gate reviews except for programmes within their own portfolio. Programme Tay, in the candidate's portfolio, costs £8m and is approaching a phase transition. Ask which one statement must be true.

- A) A chair must be found from outside the organisation.
- B) Tay requires a gate review, and the candidate cannot chair it.
- C) Tay's review can be waived since the candidate cannot chair it.
- D) Rule (iii) conflicts with rule (ii) and one must be repealed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rule (i) makes the review mandatory (Tay exceeds £5m, approaching a transition); rule (iii) bars this head of portfolio from chairing it, since Tay is in their own portfolio. Both follow necessarily: option B. Option A overreaches — the rules require independence from the programme, not from the organisation; another qualified independent chair internally satisfies the text. Option C inverts the logic: ineligibility to chair is a staffing constraint, not grounds to waive a mandatory control — exactly how controls erode in practice. Option D misreads harmony (iii is a specific application of ii's principle) as conflict. This tests whether the candidate ensures the controls that constrain them personally are honoured exactly as any other control would be — a distinction between "this rule is inconvenient for me" and "this rule is defective" that is closely watched at this level.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Error checking an aggregate before the executive committee**

A quarterly executive pack drafts: "Portfolio delivery confidence: 78% (up from 74%). All ten programmes reported. Method: unweighted average of programme confidence scores." The underlying data: nine programmes cluster between 70% and 85%; the tenth — the largest, holding 40% of portfolio spend — reports 45%, down from 60% last quarter. Ask for the most important defect in the draft.

- A) The arithmetic is wrong; the average of the ten scores cannot be 78%.
- B) The headline conceals a materially deteriorating largest programme: unweighted averaging lets nine small improvements mask a 15-point fall in the programme holding 40% of the money — the pack's "up from 74%" tells leaders the opposite of what the spend-weighted picture shows.
- C) Ten programmes is too small a sample for averaging.
- D) Confidence percentages should never be averaged by any method.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic can be internally correct (nine scores in the 70–85 band with one 45 can average to 78), so A is beside the point. The defect is representational: an unweighted mean gives the largest programme — deteriorating from 60 to 45 — one-tenth of the headline's attention while nine smaller improvements pull the average up. A spend-weighted view would show confidence falling, driven by the programme that matters most; "up from 74%" is directionally wrong for the question leaders actually ask — how safe is the money? This tests whether the candidate refuses to sign an aggregate that inverts the story it should tell, and prescribes the fix: report spend-weighted alongside unweighted, and decompose the movement rather than letting one average carry a mixed story. Options C and D retreat into methodological nihilism rather than diagnosing the actual defect.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritising the head's week**

Monday, 8:30. Competing for the candidate's week: (1) Thursday's investment committee — presenting the annual prioritisation recommendation allocating £30m across fourteen bids; the analysis is complete but the top three rankings have not been stress-tested; (2) a portfolio manager the candidate line-manages has requested an urgent conversation — she is visibly struggling and may resign; (3) the director general's office wants a view by Wednesday on a machinery-of-government change moving two programmes out of the portfolio; (4) a supplier's account director has requested a meeting about "a commercial matter best discussed early", unspecified; (5) a monthly portfolio community session, coaching twelve portfolio managers, is Friday and currently unprepared. Ask which allocation is most defensible.

- A) Item 2 Monday morning; item 1 stress-tested Monday–Tuesday; item 3 drafted Tuesday, submitted Wednesday; item 4 scheduled Wednesday with an agenda requested first; item 5 prepared Thursday afternoon after the committee.
- B) Item 1 all day Monday and Tuesday; item 3 Wednesday; item 2 Thursday after the committee; items 4 and 5 next week.
- C) Item 3 first — the director general outranks all else; then item 1; item 2 Friday; delegate item 5; decline item 4.
- D) Item 4 first, since unspecified commercial matters are usually serious; then items 1, 3; items 2 and 5 as time allows.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 2 goes first: a trusted portfolio manager signalling crisis is time-critical and high-stakes for the duty to build and lead a motivated team, and an hour Monday morning is the cheapest, highest-return slot available (option B's Thursday slot for her is the classic failure — people problems deferred behind content problems). Item 1 then dominates Monday–Tuesday: a £30m allocation with untested top rankings is not ready for a committee that will probe exactly there. Item 3 needs a considered view by Wednesday, drafted with overnight distance before submission. Item 4 gets scheduled with an agenda requested first, rather than walked into unprepared (option D's urgency-by-vagueness lets the supplier set the agenda). Item 5 is prepared late but genuinely, rather than delegated or dropped. This tests whether the candidate puts people first when people are at risk, then the decision with the largest irreversible consequence, then positions that become commitments — without letting vagueness or seniority jump the queue.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern in the investment pipeline**

Three years of investment decisions show: bids sponsored by directorates with a seat on the investment committee were approved at 71%; bids from directorates without a seat, 34% — with independently assessed business-case quality statistically similar across both groups. Committee members, shown the figures, respond: "We fund the strongest cases; seated directorates simply understand our standards better." Ask for the most defensible reading.

- A) The members' explanation is confirmed: familiarity with standards explains the gap.
- B) With case quality controlled and a 37-point approval gap remaining, the evidence indicates a structural advantage for represented directorates — advocacy in the room, informal pre-socialisation — that quality differences cannot explain; the members' account, offered without evidence, is a hypothesis the data already undermines.
- C) Unseated directorates should be told to write better cases.
- D) The gap is acceptable if the funded work delivers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The members' explanation requires case quality to differ between the groups; the independent assessment says it does not — so the explanation is not merely unevidenced but contradicted by the one measurement designed to test it. What remains to explain the gap is presence: advocacy in the room and the relational capital of committee membership — a structural bias in the allocation mechanism. Option C prescribes for a disproven diagnosis; option D lets downstream delivery validate an upstream mechanism that never let unfunded bids demonstrate anything. This tests whether the candidate hears "we fund the strongest cases" for what it is when the controls say otherwise — a system explaining itself to itself — and moves towards redesign options such as blind first-round scoring or standing advocacy for unseated bids.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction through a delegation design**

Draft delegation rules: (i) portfolio managers may approve programme-level changes with cost impact up to £250k; (ii) changes above £250k, or any change affecting a statutory deadline regardless of cost, require head of portfolio approval; (iii) any single programme may receive at most three delegated approvals per quarter — the fourth and subsequent changes in a quarter, regardless of size, come to the head. A programme submits a £180k change, its fourth that quarter, no statutory impact. Ask who approves, and what behavioural risk rule (iii) creates that the drafting should anticipate.

- A) The portfolio manager approves — £180k is within delegation; rule (iii) is advisory.
- B) The head approves — rule (iii) routes any fourth-in-quarter change to the head regardless of size; and the rule predictably incentivises change-bundling: teams packaging several small changes into one submission to preserve delegation headroom, so the design needs a companion rule about bundled changes or aggregate quarterly cost.
- C) The head approves, and the behavioural risk is that portfolio managers will stop approving anything.
- D) Nobody can approve it this quarter; the change must wait.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rule (iii) is drafted as mandatory routing, not guidance — the fourth-in-quarter change escalates regardless of the £250k limit, ruling out A (demoting the rule) and D (inventing a prohibition). The design question is how the rule will reshape behaviour: a frequency cap on approvals makes approval slots scarce, and scarce slots get economised — teams will bundle several genuine changes into one submission, degrading the audit trail exactly where the rule sought scrutiny, echoing invoice-splitting under spend thresholds. This tests whether the candidate asks the designer's second question — not just "what does this rule require?" but "what will this rule teach people to do?" — and proposes a companion provision (bundled submissions counted per constituent change, or an aggregate-cost trigger) rather than predicting paralysis, which frequency caps do not typically cause.

**Question 7 (hard) — Error checking a benefits claim across artefacts**

Three artefacts describe Programme Lune's benefits. Business case (approved two years ago): £6m annual benefits from month 30, comprising £4.2m staff-time savings and £1.8m reduced error costs. Latest programme report: "On track for full £6m benefits; staff-time savings validated by time-and-motion study." Finance's benefits ledger: £1.9m annualised benefits banked to date, all from error-cost reduction; staff-time savings "recognised in principle; not yet cash-releasing — headcount unchanged." The programme is at month 27. Ask which reading should govern the report to leadership.

- A) The programme's "on track for £6m" stands: the study validated the savings.
- B) The artefacts agree £1.9m is real; the contested £4.2m is time saved but not released — staff are doing other work, headcount and budgets unchanged — so the honest position is: £1.9m cash-releasing benefits banked (error costs, exceeding their £1.8m target), £4.2m of efficiency claims validated as time but not yet converted to cash or capacity value, and the conversion mechanism is the open question leadership should press.
- C) Finance's ledger is authoritative; report benefits at £1.9m and the £4.2m as failed.
- D) Split the difference and report £4m as a prudent midpoint.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The three artefacts measure different things, not contradictory facts: the time-and-motion study can be sound (staff genuinely save hours), and Finance's ledger can be simultaneously right (no budget line shrank, because saved hours diffused into other work rather than converting to headcount reduction or output increase). Option A over-reports (the study validates time, not money); C over-corrects ("failed" is false, since the time is real); D — the midpoint — corresponds to no state of the world. This tests whether the candidate decomposes a benefits claim by evidential status (banked and cash-releasing; validated-in-principle; unevidenced) and surfaces the conversion mechanism as the actual decision leadership must own, rather than collapsing three distinct claims into one comforting number.

**Question 8 (hard) — Multi-constraint reasoning: the team design problem**

Four portfolio managers (PMs) are being assigned to four programme clusters for the coming year. Constraints: (i) the Justice cluster requires current security clearance — only PM Asha and PM Dan hold it; (ii) PM Bella led Health for the past two years, and rotation policy bars a third consecutive year on the same cluster; (iii) the Borders cluster's programme director has a documented unresolved conflict with PM Dan — pairing them is excluded; (iv) PM Carl is six months into the role and, by development design, must be paired with the cluster whose governance is most mature — Health. Ask which assignment satisfies all constraints.

- A) Asha–Justice, Bella–Borders, Carl–Health, Dan–Corporate
- B) Dan–Justice, Bella–Corporate, Carl–Health, Asha–Borders
- C) Asha–Justice, Bella–Health, Carl–Corporate, Dan–Borders
- D) Both A and B satisfy all constraints; the choice between them is a judgement call on development and succession grounds.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Constraint (iv) fixes Carl–Health, which also satisfies (ii) by displacement. Constraint (i) restricts Justice to Asha or Dan. Testing Asha–Justice forces Dan–Corporate and Bella–Borders (satisfying iii) — option A, fully feasible. Testing Dan–Justice leaves Asha and Bella for Borders and Corporate, either arrangement working since Dan's conflict is moot once he is placed — option B, also fully feasible. Option C fails on inspection (Bella–Health breaches rotation; Dan–Borders breaches the conflict exclusion). This tests whether the candidate recognises when a constraint set under-determines the answer, and — the head-level content — names the unmodelled criteria that should decide between A and B (for instance, whether to keep the most experienced cleared PM on the sensitive cluster, or invest Dan's clearance elsewhere while buying time to resolve his conflict properly), rather than either declaring a false unique solution or shrugging at the tie.

**Question 9 (hard) — The leading indicator**

A basket of portfolio health indicators, tracked over four months: milestone delivery holds steady at 82–85%; spend tracks profile within 3%; but three quieter series move together — voluntary attrition among delivery-team staff rises from 8% to 14% annualised; average time-to-fill vacancies rises from 6 to 11 weeks; and the proportion of programme risks flagged as "resource-related" doubles from 12% to 24%. Programme RAG ratings remain unchanged. Ask for the strongest interpretation for the leadership insight report.

- A) Delivery is fine — the primary indicators (milestones, spend) are stable, and the people metrics belong to HR.
- B) The portfolio is consuming its resilience: three independent people-capacity series deteriorating in concert are a leading indicator, and stable milestones alongside rising resource risk flags suggest teams are currently absorbing the strain — so delivery metrics will follow with a lag, and the insight for leaders is a forecast ("expect delivery deterioration in six to nine months absent intervention on retention and recruitment"), not a reassurance.
- C) The attrition rise is within normal market variation; revisit next quarter.
- D) RAG ratings are unchanged, so programmes themselves see no problem; defer to their assessment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Milestones and spend are lagging indicators, recording what capacity already accomplished; the three moving series are leading — attrition raises load on remaining staff, lengthening time-to-fill converts departures into gaps, and doubled resource-risk flags are programmes reporting strain in a channel that doesn't yet trigger scrutiny. Their concert matters: three independent measures agreeing is how weak signals earn strong conclusions, and stable output alongside rising strain flags specifically indicates absorption, which has a horizon. Option A confuses lagging with primary and outsources a core delivery dependency to HR; C treats each series alone and discards the correlation that is the actual signal; D defers to a rating the strain data itself explains. This tests whether the candidate produces a dated forecast with named intervention levers — the distinctive "insight into the portfolio's future" this role is meant to supply.

**Question 10 (hard) — Prioritisation under a funding cut**

Mid-year, the portfolio's budget is cut 15% (£4.5m of £30m), effective in one quarter. Four discretionary candidates for reduction: Programme W — £2m remaining-year cost, delivers statutory compliance capability eight weeks before its legal deadline; Programme X — £2.5m remaining-year cost, 75% complete, benefits £3m/yr starting in five months; Programme Y — £2.5m remaining-year cost, 15% complete, benefits £5m/yr starting in two years, no dependencies; Programme Z — £2m remaining-year cost, delivers the shared data platform that X's final integration (worth £0.8m of X's benefits) and two next-year initiatives depend on. Ask which reduction package is most defensible.

- A) Cut W and Z (£4m) plus trim £0.5m of Y — the two cheapest programmes take the burden.
- B) Cut Y (£2.5m) and find £2m through a negotiated descope of Z's platform to the minimum X and the committed next-year initiatives require — protecting the statutory programme absolutely, the near-complete benefits engine, and the dependency spine, while the least-committed, furthest-horizon investment pauses.
- C) Cut X and W (£4.5m exactly) — a clean match to the target.
- D) Spread 15% cuts evenly across all four programmes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** W is near-untouchable — statutory compliance with eight weeks of margin, so cutting it converts a funding decision into a legal breach. C cuts W anyway, plus X, the portfolio's best near-term value at 75% complete. D — the even spread — abdicates prioritisation and imposes pain on a statutory deadline and a near-complete programme alike. Option B ranks correctly: Y pauses because it is the least irreversible choice (15% sunk, no dependents, benefits two years out), and the residual £2m comes from Z via descope rather than cancellation, since killing Z strands X's benefits and two committed initiatives. This tests whether the candidate ranks cut candidates by irreversibility and dependency rather than by headline value or arithmetic convenience, and treats statutory obligations as constraints rather than candidates.

**Question 11 (hard) — The metric that improved**

A year after delivery-confidence targets were introduced into portfolio managers' performance objectives ("portfolio average confidence ≥75%"), the average stands at 81%, up from 69%. Independent evidence: milestone delivery is flat year-on-year; two programmes had late-stage surprises their high confidence scores never foreshadowed; and confidence submissions now cluster tightly at 78–84%, where they used to range 45–90%. Ask for the soundest conclusion.

- A) The target worked: confidence is up 12 points.
- B) The evidence pattern — improved scores, unimproved delivery, vanished variance, and failed predictions — indicates the metric was captured by the incentive: once scored against a target, confidence stopped measuring delivery risk and started measuring compliance; the fix is to remove confidence from individual performance objectives, restore it as a forecasting tool scored on calibration, and find delivery incentives that reward honest early warning.
- C) The two late surprises were unforeseeable; otherwise the system works.
- D) Raise the target to 85% to drive further improvement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Scores rose while delivery — the thing scores claim to predict — didn't move; genuine risk varies enormously across programmes, so the collapsed range just above the target line is target-seeking, not convergent reality; and the two late surprises are the system failing at its only job. This is Goodhart's law at portfolio scale, and it doubles as a self-implication test since the candidate built the incentive. Option B carries diagnosis, mechanism and redesign together. Option A reads a corrupted instrument at face value; C excuses failed predictions individually; D escalates the error, buying more capture with a higher target. This tests whether the candidate retires their own control when evidence shows it now manufactures the signal it was meant to detect.

**Question 12 (hard) — The cascading commitment**

At the executive board, the chief operating officer proposes committing the organisation publicly to "all twelve citizen-facing services meeting the top accessibility standard within eighteen months". Portfolio data: five services already comply; four can comply within 12–15 months inside existing plans; three run on a legacy platform whose accessibility remediation is technically unbounded — the platform's own replacement completes in 24 months, after which compliance is straightforward. The COO turns to the candidate: "Portfolio can deliver that, yes?" Ask for the most effective response.

- A) "Yes" — the ambition is right, the majority is achievable, and the details can be managed later.
- B) "No" — three services cannot make eighteen months, so the commitment is impossible.
- C) "Nine of twelve, yes, within the window. The remaining three sit on the legacy platform that replatforms at month 24 — for those, remediation before replacement would be spent twice. I'd support committing publicly to nine by eighteen months and all twelve by twenty-six, or alternatively fund interim assisted-digital provision for the three — I can bring the costed options within a week."
- D) "I'll need to take that away and come back in due course."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option A mortgages the organisation's public word against three services known not to comply in-window. Option B is accurate but useless, handing the board a wall where the data contains a bridge. Option D defers what the data can answer now, and the commitment may be drafted before a return visit. Option C leads with verified fact, names the constraint with its reason as a value-for-money argument rather than a technical mystification, and offers two committable alternatives with a dated offer of costed detail. This tests whether the candidate can compress a loaded position into fact, constraint, options and a dated offer — the executive-grade structure that turns a yes/no trap into a decision the board can actually use.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate audits the silences in their own information flow (an inbox, a reporting channel), not just its arrivals.
- Note whether the candidate applies a control that constrains them personally with the same rigour as any other control, rather than treating self-inconvenience as evidence the rule is wrong.
- Watch for whether the candidate refuses to sign an aggregate that conceals its largest mover.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes forced outcomes from genuinely open judgement calls, and names the criteria that decide the latter.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes lagging from leading indicators, and produces a dated forecast rather than a status report.
- Watch for symmetry: does the candidate apply the same scepticism to a metric or system they built as to one they inherited?

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that reads volume or activity as a rate without a baseline to compare it against.
- Missing when a candidate treats their own inconvenience under a rule as grounds to waive or discount it.
- Accepting an aggregate at face value because the final number is arithmetically defensible, without probing whether the candidate asked what it might conceal.
- Rewarding a candidate who declares a unique forced answer when the constraints in fact leave a genuine judgement call.
- Missing a candidate who ranks a lagging indicator above a leading one, or defers to a rating the leading evidence already explains.
- Overlooking asymmetric rigour — a candidate who applies harder scrutiny to others' figures or systems than to their own.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures accuracy and fluency with the quantitative material a head of portfolio certifies and decides on: multi-programme funding tables, weighted portfolio metrics, benefits trajectories, capacity economics, unit-cost comparisons, and the forecast arithmetic behind the insights provided to leaders. Formats present tables, charts or data narratives followed by calculation and interpretation items, with head-level instruments weighting interpretation heavily — selecting the correct measure, detecting the misleading aggregate, sizing what a figure can honestly support.

Typical format: online, timed, 20 to 35 minutes, 15 to 20 questions, calculator permitted, distractors built from predictable errors — wrong bases, averaged rates, conflated measures, boundary slips. Expect two-step questions where the arithmetic is modest but choosing the right arithmetic is the test.

At head level a candidate rarely computes figures from source — a team does that — but is the last check before numbers become decisions: signing the pack, presenting the allocation, answering "so can we afford it?" in the room. That demands rapid verification, aggregation judgement, and forecast discipline — and the coaching version of all three, since a head of portfolio sets the analytical standards a community works to. Every question below shows complete working; the explanations are written to double as teaching material.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Weighted aggregation** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and the duty to provide leaders with insights: choosing and disclosing a weighting basis is the difference between a lens and a distortion.
- **Funding and allocation arithmetic** maps to **Planning** and **Strategic thinking**: cut packages, prioritisation scores and affordability envelopes are the numbers strategy is executed through.
- **Forecasting and trajectory analysis** map to **Planning** and the duty to provide insights into the portfolio's future: run-rates with step changes, realisation curves and strain projections convert present data into decision-grade foresight.
- **Unit-cost and benchmark reasoning** maps to **Governance and assurance**: challenging a benchmark, a supplier rate or an overhead figure requires knowing exactly what its denominator contains.
- **Verification at speed** maps to **Governance and assurance** and leadership of tracking: recomputing a pack's load-bearing figures quickly is the control of last resort.
- **Presentation-of-numbers judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders runs on the right figure, on the declared base, with its assumption attached, at conversational speed.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio spend share**

The portfolio's annual budget is £36m across three clusters: Citizen Services £16.2m, Case Working £12.6m, Corporate £7.2m. Ask what percentage of the portfolio budget Case Working holds.

- A) 30%
- B) 35%
- C) 36%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 12.6 ÷ 36 = 0.35 = 35% — option B. Cross-checking the full distribution (45% + 35% + 20% = 100%) confirms it. This tests basic share calculation together with a reconciliation habit — checking a set of shares sums to 100% — worth crediting even when not explicitly prompted.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change with direction**

Portfolio headcount charged to programmes fell from 480 FTE to 432 FTE over the year. Ask for the percentage change.

- A) A fall of 10%
- B) A fall of 11.1%
- C) A fall of 4.8%
- D) A rise of 10%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 480 − 432 = 48; 48 ÷ 480 (the original) = 10% fall — option A. Option B divides by the new value instead. This tests correct-base percentage change and, in a strong candidate's answer, awareness that a 10% reduction needs a cause before it means anything — efficiency, delivery contraction, or unfilled vacancies driving strain.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a funding table under time pressure**

The investment committee table shows approved funding by year:

| Programme | 2025–26 (£m) | 2026–27 (£m) | 2027–28 (£m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alder | 3.2 | 4.1 | 2.2 |
| Birch | 2.8 | 3.0 | 3.4 |
| Cedar | 1.5 | 2.4 | 4.0 |
| Rowan | 2.5 | 1.5 | 0.9 |

Ask which programme's funding profile falls in every successive year.

- A) Alder
- B) Birch
- C) Cedar
- D) Rowan

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Testing each row for a strict year-on-year decline: only Rowan falls in both transitions — option D. Alder's endpoints also fall (3.2 to 2.2), the classic trap for readers who scan endpoints rather than transitions. This tests table-reading discipline and, ideally, the candidate narrating what each profile shape means — Rowan closing down, Cedar ramping to a 2027–28 peak — since shape communicates more to a board than isolated cell values.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Spend-weighted versus unweighted confidence**

Five programmes report delivery confidence:

| Programme | Annual spend (£m) | Confidence (%) |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | 12 | 55 |
| P2 | 6 | 80 |
| P3 | 4 | 85 |
| P4 | 5 | 70 |
| P5 | 3 | 90 |

Ask for the unweighted and spend-weighted average confidence, and which should lead the executive pack.

- A) Unweighted 76%, weighted 70%; lead with the weighted figure, showing both — the money is markedly less safe than the programme count suggests.
- B) Unweighted 76%, weighted 76%; either may lead.
- C) Unweighted 70%, weighted 76%; lead with unweighted.
- D) Unweighted 76%, weighted 72%; lead with unweighted, as weighting is a presentational choice.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Unweighted: 380 ÷ 5 = 76%. Weighted: sum of products (660+480+340+350+270=2,100) ÷ sum of weights (30) = 70%. Option A. P1 holds 40% of spend at the lowest confidence, so the money-weighted view must sit well below the count-weighted view; the six-point gap is the insight, and both bases should be shown with the driver named. This tests whether the candidate treats basis selection as analytical rather than cosmetic — option D's framing is exactly the error to watch for.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Marginal value in an allocation decision**

£1.2m of unallocated year-end funding; two competing bids. Bid One: £1.2m to accelerate Programme K, bringing £2.4m/yr of benefits forward by four months. Bid Two: £1.2m to extend Programme L's rollout to three additional regions, adding £0.5m/yr of recurring benefits from next year, indefinitely. Using a three-year value horizon, ask which bid delivers more value, and by how much.

- A) Bid One, by £0.3m — acceleration always beats extension.
- B) Bid Two: £1.5m versus £0.8m over three years — a £0.7m advantage.
- C) They are equal at £1.5m each.
- D) Bid One: £2.4m versus £1.5m — a £0.9m advantage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Bid One's value is one-off: 4/12 × £2.4m = £0.8m — the benefit was arriving anyway; only the earlier arrival is new value. Bid Two adds £0.5m/yr recurring: 3 × £0.5m = £1.5m over the horizon. £1.5m − £0.8m = £0.7m in Bid Two's favour — option B. Option D mistakes the accelerated stream's full annual rate for the acceleration's actual value, the commonest error in such cases. This tests whether the candidate values the increment a bid creates, not the stream it merely touches, and notes that horizon choice can flip such comparisons (a one-year horizon favours Bid One).

**Question 6 (moderate) — Run-rate, step change and the affordability question**

Staff-cost run rate is £1.5m/month through month 6 (£9.0m spent). From month 7: a contractor conversion saves £150k/month; a newly approved programme adds £250k/month from month 9. Annual staff budget: £18.5m. Ask for the forecast full-year position.

- A) £18.0m — £0.5m under budget
- B) £18.1m — £0.4m under budget
- C) £18.5m — exactly on budget
- D) £18.7m — £0.2m over budget

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Months 1–6: £9.0m. Months 7–12 baseline: 6 × £1.5m = £9.0m. Saving, months 7–12 (6 months): £0.9m. Addition, months 9–12 (4 months): £1.0m. Forecast: 9.0 + 9.0 − 0.9 + 1.0 = £18.1m; £0.4m under budget — option B. This tests segmented forecasting with correctly counted month-windows for each step change, and — in a strong answer — flags that £0.4m of headroom (2.2% of budget) is thin cover against two behavioural assumptions that could each slip.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Unit cost with an honest denominator**

Office annual cost £1.8m; portfolio annual spend under management £36m; the office also directly runs the assurance regime for 5 programmes in another portfolio, spend £9m, recharged at cost £0.3m (already netted out of the £1.8m). A benchmark quotes comparable portfolio offices at 4.5–5.5% of spend under management. Ask for the honest comparable figure.

- A) 5.0% — £1.8m over £36m.
- B) 4.0% — £1.8m over £45m, counting all spend the office serves.
- C) 4.7% — £2.1m gross cost over £45m total spend served, aligning both numerator and denominator to the full service scope.
- D) 4.2% — £1.5m over £36m, netting the recharge again.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Full service scope: £36m + £9m = £45m served, at gross cost £1.8m + £0.3m = £2.1m (the recharge is real cost of serving the second portfolio, netted from the budget line, not from resources consumed). 2.1 ÷ 45 ≈ 4.7% — option C. Option B is the flattering mismatch: full denominator, netted numerator, understating the ratio. This tests whether the candidate aligns numerator and denominator to the same scope — the same check that matters when challenging a supplier's rate or a comparator's overhead claim.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Benefits trajectory versus business case**

A flagship programme's business case promised cumulative benefits of £10m by end of year 3, profiled £1m / £3m / £6m across years 1–3. Actuals: year 1 delivered £0.8m; year 2 delivered £2.1m. Ask what year 3 must deliver to hit the cumulative £10m, and what that requirement implies.

- A) £6.0m — exactly the original year-3 profile; the programme remains on plan.
- B) £7.1m — 18% above the original year-3 profile; the shortfall to date (£1.1m) has been pushed into the final year, so the recovery assumption needs explicit evidence before leadership treats £10m as still credible.
- C) £4.9m — the profile adjusts downward automatically.
- D) £7.1m — but cumulative targets are met by definition if each year is "broadly close".

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Delivered to date: £2.9m against profiled £4.0m — a £1.1m shortfall. Year 3 requirement: £10m − £2.9m = £7.1m, an 18% uplift on the year already expected to be the most demanding. Option B carries both the figure and its meaning — the "hockey stick" pattern, where shortfalls migrate into the final period and compound the required rate there. This tests whether the candidate recomputes the implied remaining-period requirement whenever a cumulative target is reaffirmed, and asks what specifically would deliver above the plan's own best year, rather than accepting the headline target unexamined.

**Question 9 (hard) — Capacity economics of the portfolio team**

A portfolio office employs 9 portfolio managers/analysts at a loaded cost of £80k each. 30% of their combined time goes to manually assembling reports that a £250k one-off plus £40k/yr automation investment would reduce to 10%. Released time is redeployed to assurance and forward analysis, not headcount reduction. Ask, for year 1 and steady state, what value is released, and whether the investment pays back within two years on time-value alone.

- A) Year 1: £144k value against £290k cost — no; two-year total £288k against £330k — payback just missed.
- B) Year 1: £144k against £290k — no; but two-year value £288k against £330k means payback arrives early in year 3, so the investment fails a strict two-year test while remaining sound on any longer horizon.
- C) Both A and B state the same arithmetic; the difference is the recommendation — and B's is the defensible one.
- D) Year 1: £216k against £290k; payback inside two years.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Combined staff cost £720k/yr; time released 20 percentage points (30% to 10%), worth £144k/yr. Costs: year 1 £290k, year 2 £40k. Two-year totals: value £288k, cost £330k — payback misses by £42k, arriving early in year 3. D misreads the 30-to-10 reduction as a flat 30% saving. A's arithmetic is correct but stops at "payback just missed"; B completes the analysis by noting a strict two-year test fails while any longer horizon passes comfortably. This tests whether the candidate, after verifying the arithmetic, checks what question it was allowed to answer — an appraisal that halts at an arbitrary payback line when the asset's life obviously exceeds it is arithmetic without judgement.

**Question 10 (hard) — The two-baseline funding claim**

A programme director tells the investment committee: "We've delivered 15% under budget this year." Portfolio data: original annual budget £8.0m; in-year re-baseline (approved, after descoping a workstream) reduced the budget to £7.2m; actual spend £6.8m. Ask for an evaluation of the claim.

- A) The claim is correct: £6.8m against £8.0m is 15% under.
- B) The claim is correct against the original budget (£1.2m ÷ £8.0m = 15%) but conflates two effects: £0.8m (10 points) came from an approved scope reduction — less was bought — and only £0.4m against the re-baselined £7.2m (5.6%) is genuine spend efficiency; the committee should hear both figures with the descope made explicit.
- C) The claim is false: the only valid comparison is £6.8m against £7.2m, so the underspend is 5.6%.
- D) The claim is false: re-baselining eliminates any right to quote the original budget.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both frames are arithmetically true (15% against original, 5.6% against re-baseline); the decomposition is the point — £0.8m of the headline is a scope decision (spent less because less was bought), and only £0.4m evidences actual cost efficiency. This tests whether the candidate refuses to let scope reduction masquerade as efficiency, without over-correcting into declaring the original-baseline comparison illegitimate — the house standard is both baselines, always labelled, with scope changes stated.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reading the distribution, not the mean**

Fourteen programmes' schedule variances (days late per completed milestone): 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 6, 28, 41. A draft pack states: "Mean schedule variance: 7 days (median 2.5), portfolio delivery broadly timely." Ask what the pack should actually say.

- A) The draft is fine: both mean and median are quoted.
- B) Twelve of fourteen programmes run 0–6 days late — genuinely timely — while two extreme outliers (28 and 41 days) both inflate the mean nearly threefold and are themselves the finding: the pack should report the timely core and name the two outliers for individual attention, because averaging a bimodal reality into one number hides both truths.
- C) Remove the outliers and report a mean of 2.4 days.
- D) The median alone should be reported, as it is robust to outliers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic checks out (mean 7, median 2.5), but neither figure alone tells the true story: this is a timely portfolio of twelve plus two programmes in genuine schedule distress. Option C deletes the finding by excluding outliers from a report rather than an estimate; option D's "robust to outliers" logic is exactly backwards for governance purposes, since the outliers are what governance exists to catch. This tests whether the candidate reads a distribution's shape rather than defaulting to a single summary statistic, and names material outliers explicitly instead of averaging them away.

**Question 12 (hard) — The in-room affordability check**

A bid team states: "Our programme needs £5.4m over three years — £2.6m, £1.6m, £1.2m — but delivers £2.8m annual savings from year 2, so it's self-funding within the period." Ask what quick arithmetic shows, and what the response should be.

- A) "Confirmed — cumulative savings of £5.6m by end of year 3 exceed the £5.4m cost."
- B) "Broadly right on the period total — £5.6m savings versus £5.4m cost — but not self-funding as it runs: cumulative cash breaks even only late in year 3, after a peak funding requirement of about £1.4m net exposure at end of year 2 — so the committee is being asked to carry roughly £1.4m of peak exposure, and the honest framing is 'repays within the period', not 'self-funding'."
- C) "The claim fails: £2.8m of savings is less than £5.4m of cost."
- D) "Savings claims from year 2 are never credible; discount them and assess the bid at full £5.4m cost."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Year-by-year cash: year 1 net −£2.6m; year 2 net +£1.2m, cumulative −£1.4m; year 3 net +£1.6m, cumulative +£0.2m. Period totals favour the bid (£5.6m versus £5.4m), but the programme is cash-negative until late in year 3, with £1.4m of peak exposure the organisation must actually fund. This tests the distinction between "is it worth it?" (period totals) and "can we carry it?" (cash profile) — bid teams reliably present only the first, and a strong candidate corrects the load-bearing word ("self-funding" to "repays") while quantifying the exposure, rather than either confirming the claim as stated or dismissing it wholesale.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate runs verification methods (part-over-whole, change-over-original, products-over-weights, segmented forecasts, cash profiles) fast enough to catch an error, not just correctly on paper.
- Note whether the candidate reconciles a distribution's parts to its total as a matter of habit.
- Watch for whether the candidate interrogates a denominator's scope before accepting a benchmark or unit-cost ratio.
- Score for whether the candidate decomposes a headline claim (underspend, benefits, savings) into its components rather than accepting it whole.
- Note whether the candidate reads a distribution's shape before reaching for a single summary statistic.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting wrong-base division even when the resulting figure looks plausible.
- Missing an averaged-rate or unweighted-mean answer that should have used totals or weights instead.
- Rewarding a candidate who values a benefit stream instead of the increment a bid actually creates.
- Accepting a boundary or window slip in a segmented forecast without checking the month count.
- Missing when a candidate lets a period-total claim stand in for a cash-profile answer.
- Crediting an unlabelled baseline claim (underspend, delivery percentage) without probing whether a scope change sits behind it.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures exactness in reading, inference and written judgement over material a head of portfolio lives in: strategy documents, spending frameworks, assurance reports, board minutes, ministerial correspondence, machinery-of-government notices, and the policy language a team inherits its interpretation of. The core format presents passages followed by statements to classify as **True** (supported), **False** (contradicted) or **Cannot say** (indeterminable); head-level variants weight inference, assumption-detection, argument evaluation, and drafting judgement.

Typical format: 15 to 25 minutes, four to six passages, twenty-plus items, distractors built on modal shifts, scope migration, dropped conditions, and outcome/process conflation. The governing discipline is strict textualism: the passage is the universe; professional knowledge waits outside it.

At head level this measures something institutionally significant: this role is where authoritative interpretation happens, and it propagates — interpretations become a community's precedents. The drafting dimension carries equal weight: insight reports, committee papers and single-sentence positions are the interface between the portfolio and the leaders deciding about it, and imprecision there is imprecision with organisational consequences. The passages below are drawn from these registers.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Exactness with frameworks and controls** maps to **Governance and assurance**: reading controls rightly — mandate, permission, silence, the exception that governs the hard case.
- **Inference discipline** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and the duty to provide insights: the boundary between what evidence establishes and what it suggests is the boundary between reporting and speculation.
- **Assumption and argument evaluation** map to **Strategic thinking**: finding what a strategy's promise silently depends on, and which clause fails first under pressure.
- **Drafting judgement** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: sentences carrying every governing condition in plain words.
- **Interpretive leadership** maps to the duty to support the professional development of the portfolio community: readings train a community's readings.

### Practice questions

**Passage for Questions 1–3:**

"Extract from the digital investment framework: 'Proposals exceeding £2m lifetime cost require full business cases; proposals at or below £2m may proceed on a light-touch case unless they process special category data, in which case a full case is required regardless of value. The investment committee must consider full cases within two committee cycles of submission. The committee may approve, approve with conditions, reject, or return a case for development; a returned case is not a rejected case, and its resubmission does not count as a new submission for the purposes of the two-cycle requirement. Heads of portfolio must certify that each submitted case's delivery assumptions are consistent with portfolio capacity before submission; certification does not constitute endorsement of the case's merits.'"

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "A £1.6m proposal that processes special category data may proceed on a light-touch business case."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, provided the head of portfolio certifies it

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The value threshold carries an explicit exception with a trumping clause — "regardless of value" — for special category data. £1.6m is inside the value threshold but inside the exception too: a full case is required, contradicting the statement. Option D imports certification as a cure, but certification (per the final sentence) addresses capacity consistency, not case-type eligibility. This tests whether the candidate reads past a threshold to its exception — the reading error most likely to arrive at a head of portfolio's desk as "we're under £2m so we're fine".

**Question 2 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "A case returned for development must be reconsidered by the committee within two cycles of its resubmission."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because returned cases have no right to reconsideration

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes that resubmission does not count as a new submission for the two-cycle requirement, but does not say what timing rule, if any, governs reconsideration of a resubmitted case. Three readings survive and the text cannot arbitrate among them: Cannot say. Option A asserts a fresh right the text withholds; option D swings to no right at all, which the text neither states nor implies. This tests whether the candidate can identify a genuine gap in a framework and state that it needs a ruling or amendment, rather than manufacturing confidence in either direction.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "By certifying a case, a head of portfolio signals support for its approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True by convention, though not by rule

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly forecloses this: "certification does not constitute endorsement of the case's merits." The statement claims the severed connection: False. Option D smuggles extra-textual convention into a strict-text exercise. This tests whether the candidate recognises a deliberately severed pair of signals — capacity consistency from the head of portfolio, merits judgement from the committee — and can explain why the severance matters (protecting the capacity check from becoming a disguised merits veto).

**Passage for Questions 4–6:**

"Extract from a machinery-of-government readiness note: 'Under the announced transfer, responsibility for the two border-services programmes moves to the receiving department on 1 April. Programme staff transfer under standard arrangements; however, the sending department's portfolio office retains assurance responsibility until the receiving department's portfolio function passes a readiness assessment, expected in the second quarter. During this interim, the sending head of portfolio reports assurance findings to both departments' accounting officers. Contractual novation for the programmes' supplier agreements proceeds on a separate track and is not expected to complete before September. Nothing in these arrangements alters the statutory duties of either accounting officer.'"

**Question 4 (easy) — Locating the governing detail**

Ask, according to the passage, when the sending department's portfolio office ceases to hold assurance responsibility for the transferring programmes.

- A) On 1 April, when responsibility for the programmes transfers.
- B) When the receiving department's portfolio function passes its readiness assessment.
- C) In September, when contractual novation completes.
- D) In the second quarter, automatically.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three separate tracks run on three separate clocks: programme responsibility (1 April), assurance responsibility (event-gated, on readiness-assessment passage), and contractual novation (September, its own track). Option D mistakes "expected in the second quarter" — a forecast — for a deadline; if the assessment slips or fails, assurance responsibility stays with the sender regardless of the calendar. This tests whether the candidate keeps separately-clocked tracks apart rather than assuming a single date moved everything — a common source of governance accidents in machinery-of-government interims.

**Question 5 (hard) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "If the readiness assessment has not been passed by September, the sending head of portfolio will hold assurance responsibility for programmes whose supplier contracts have already novated to the receiving department."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because assurance and contracts must transfer together

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario the statement describes is consistent with the passage, but the statement is a conditional prediction about a future point, and the passage describes current arrangements without warranting their persistence against amendment. It supports the scenario's possibility but cannot guarantee the prediction: Cannot say. Option D invents a transfer-together doctrine the passage explicitly refutes by keeping the tracks separate. This tests the hardest verbal-reasoning distinction in the guide: "the rules as written produce this outcome in that scenario" (checkable) versus "this outcome will obtain" (unknowable) — the same distinction that separates describing a governance machine from predicting the future when briefing leaders.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The statutory anchor**

Ask what function the final sentence — "Nothing in these arrangements alters the statutory duties of either accounting officer" — performs in the note.

- A) It is boilerplate with no operative effect.
- B) It subordinates the transfer arrangements to a fixed accountability layer: however responsibility, assurance and contracts move between departments, each accounting officer's statutory duties persist unaltered — so no interim arrangement can be read as diluting either officer's personal accountability, and any conflict resolves in the duties' favour.
- C) It transfers statutory duties to the receiving accounting officer on 1 April.
- D) It makes the accounting officers responsible for completing novation by September.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A "nothing in X alters Y" clause is a hierarchy statement, declaring the instrument being read cannot touch the preserved layer, and instructing the reader to resolve any apparent conflict in its favour. Here it protects each accounting officer's personal statutory accountability from being diluted by the interim's complexity. Options C and D read the clause as doing things its grammar is built to deny. This tests whether the candidate locates savings and precedence clauses first in a governance document, since they show which parts of the text can bend and which cannot.

**Passage for Questions 7–9:**

"Letter from the chair of the public accounts scrutiny session to the department: 'The committee noted that the department's flagship programme has re-baselined three times in two years, and that each re-baseline was individually approved through the department's governance. We do not question the propriety of those approvals. Our concern is cumulative: taken together, the re-baselines have moved the delivery date by nineteen months and reduced scope by an estimated thirty per cent, while the department's public reporting has described the programme throughout as "on track". The committee requests: first, an account of how the department's portfolio governance considers cumulative change, as distinct from approving each change singly; and second, the department's view on whether "on track" remains an accurate public description of a programme whose track has materially moved. We ask for a response within six weeks.'"

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The committee alleges that the department's re-baseline approvals were improper."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True implicitly, given the letter's overall tone

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The letter states its position without ambiguity: "We do not question the propriety of those approvals." Option D tone-mines an allegation the letter expressly disclaims — a serious error, since the letter has deliberately separated propriety (conceded) from the adequacy of governance that only sees decisions singly (challenged). This tests whether the candidate answers correspondence at its exact word rather than an imagined implication, since a defensive response to the wrong allegation both annoys the committee and leaves the real question unaddressed.

**Question 8 (hard) — The letter's central argument**

Ask which best characterises the letter's core analytical point.

- A) Programmes should never re-baseline more than once.
- B) Governance that approves each change singly can be blind to cumulative drift: three proper approvals summed to nineteen months and thirty per cent scope loss without any decision point ever seeing the total movement — and public reporting anchored to the ever-refreshed baseline ("on track") can thereby become progressively less informative while remaining technically accurate at each step.
- C) The department's governance processes were not followed.
- D) Public reporting should always use the original baseline only.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The letter's two requests decode its argument: how governance considers cumulative change "as distinct from" single approvals identifies the structural blindness, and whether "on track" remains accurate identifies the reporting consequence of a baseline that keeps refreshing. Options A and D propose crude fixes the letter notably does not demand; C repeats the refuted allegation from Question 7. This tests whether the candidate recognises the re-baselining illusion arriving as parliamentary scrutiny — the same defect this guide's numeric sections test as arithmetic, now escalated to an institutional level.

**Question 9 (hard) — Drafting the response's key sentence**

Ask which sentence best serves accuracy and the department's credibility, drafting the response on the "on track" question.

- A) "The programme has been, and remains, on track against its approved baseline."
- B) "The department acknowledges that describing the programme as 'on track' without reference to its re-baselining history understated the cumulative movement of nineteen months and thirty per cent of scope; future public reporting will state progress against both the current and the original baseline, with approved changes summarised, and the portfolio's governance now includes a cumulative-change review triggered by aggregate drift."
- C) "The description 'on track' was technically accurate at each point of use and the department stands by it."
- D) "The department regrets any confusion caused by its reporting."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Options A and C restate the defence the letter has already dismantled, confirming the committee's diagnosis while contesting it. Option D concedes and commits to nothing. Option B concedes the specific, evidenced point, quantifies the concession, commits to a structural fix in verifiable terms, and thereby converts the exchange from dispute to demonstrated improvement. This tests drafting judgement under scrutiny: concede exactly what is true, commit exactly what will change, and make both auditable — without conceding impropriety, which was never alleged.

**Passage for Questions 10–12:**

"Extract from the draft portfolio people strategy: 'The portfolio profession in this department will grow its own leadership. Within three years, at least half of portfolio manager appointments should be filled by internal candidates who have completed the development scheme. To that end, every portfolio team member will have a documented development plan, and heads of portfolio are accountable for the scheme's completion rates within their teams. Line managers should release scheme participants for up to fifteen days of development activity annually; release may be deferred where delivery need demands, but deferral of more than five days in a year requires the head of portfolio's approval. External recruitment remains available at all levels and is expected where internal pipelines cannot meet a role's requirements; nothing in this strategy establishes a preference for internal candidates in any individual appointment decision.'"

**Question 10 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "Under the strategy, internal candidates who have completed the development scheme are preferred in individual appointment decisions."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the fifty per cent target requires it

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly severs this: "nothing in this strategy establishes a preference for internal candidates in any individual appointment decision." Option D's logic feels compelling but misreads a two-layered document: the target is an aggregate aspiration achieved through supply-side means (the scheme, release entitlements, development plans), and the final clause protects each individual decision from being tilted to serve it — appointment-by-merit being a live constitutional principle, not drafting decoration. This tests whether the candidate keeps an aggregate target and an individual-decision disclaimer both operative, pursuing the target only through the supply side.

**Question 11 (hard) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "A line manager may, on their own authority, defer four days of a participant's development release in a year."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only with the participant's consent

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Deferral of more than five days requires head-of-portfolio approval; four days sits below that threshold, so authority rests with the line manager, conditioned only on delivery need demanding it. True — option A. Option D invents a consent requirement the passage does not contain. This tests precise threshold reading, and — in a strong candidate's answer — recognition of the accountability design the passage creates: the head of portfolio is accountable for completion rates while line managers hold day-level deferral discretion below the threshold, meaning sub-threshold deferrals in aggregate need active monitoring since the escalation trigger will not surface them individually.

**Question 12 (moderate) — Drafting the community briefing**

Ask which summary should open a briefing to portfolio managers on this strategy.

- A) "From now on, we grow our own: internal candidates get priority for portfolio manager roles, so get your teams through the scheme."
- B) "The strategy commits us to building internal leadership capability — every team member gets a documented development plan, participants are entitled to up to fifteen days' development time with deferrals over five days needing my approval, and within three years the aim is that half of appointments can be won by scheme graduates on merit. Two boundaries: individual appointments remain strictly merit-based with no internal preference, and external recruitment stays open at every level."
- C) "There's a new people strategy; the main point for you is more paperwork around development plans."
- D) "Development release is now capped at fifteen days and I must approve any deferrals."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B carries the supply-side commitments with correct thresholds, frames the aggregate target correctly ("won... on merit"), and states the two boundary clauses that prevent likely operational errors. Option A opens with precisely the unlawful misreading the strategy's final clause exists to prevent — and, briefed by a leader, would propagate as policy. Option D inverts an entitlement into a cap and misstates the deferral threshold. This tests whether a candidate's summary of a governing document carries the numbers, thresholds and boundaries a community will actually act on, since a leader's summary functions as the document for most of its audience.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies a document's architecture — thresholds, exceptions, severance and savings clauses, escalation triggers — before answering statements about it.
- Note whether the candidate tracks separately-clocked events (transfers, novations, assessments) separately rather than conflating them.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes a machine-description claim ("the rules produce X in scenario Y") from a prediction ("X will happen").
- Score correspondence-reading items on whether the candidate answers what is written and expressly not written, rather than an imagined tone or implication.
- For drafting items, score on whether every governing number, threshold and boundary survives into the candidate's plain-language version.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that stops at a threshold and misses the exception that overrides it.
- Missing when a candidate treats a forecast ("expected in") as if it were a deadline that gates something.
- Rewarding an answer that merges two signals a document keeps deliberately separate — certification and endorsement, a target and a preference.
- Accepting a "true implicitly, given the tone" answer to correspondence that states its position explicitly.
- Missing when a candidate defends the technically accurate over the substantively honest, in a summary or a drafted response.
- Crediting a drafted briefing or summary that drops a governing threshold, boundary or condition while keeping the general gist.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic dilemmas and asks the candidate to evaluate responses — most effective, least effective, full rankings, or independent ratings. At head level it measures leadership judgement against behavioural benchmarks: how a candidate weighs information integrity, delegation, accountability, people development, political pressure and institutional standards when they collide — which, at this altitude, is the normal condition. The scenarios that reach a head of portfolio's desk are definitionally the ones that could not be resolved below it.

Typical format: 15 to 25 scenarios, untimed or generously timed, scored against senior-practitioner consensus and the organisation's leadership behaviours — for UK government digital roles, expect honesty and transparency, ownership, empowering and developing others, inclusion, proportionate escalation, and stewardship of public money and standards. Head-level instruments discriminate almost entirely among plausible options: scoring should reward candidates who consistently choose responses that strengthen systems and people rather than merely resolving incidents, who locate decisions at the right level of authority — including above themselves — and who apply their standards reflexively, self included.

A further reason employers use SJTs for head-level selection is institutional: a leader's judgements become precedents, so the consistency the instrument measures is what an organisation will inherit. Each explanation below identifies the behaviours at stake, works the options comparatively, and states the transferable principle.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Information-integrity dilemmas** map to **Communicating analysis and insight** and the duty to provide insights: pressure to soften, delay or over-firm the portfolio's picture tests whether the insight function stays trustworthy under load.
- **Delegation and development dilemmas** map to building and leading a diverse, skilled and motivated team and supporting the community's professional development.
- **Standards-under-pressure dilemmas** map to **Governance and assurance**: controls tested precisely where they are most inconvenient.
- **Escalation and accountability dilemmas** map to the duty to address risks and deal with, or escalate, issues: at head level, escalation runs into accounting-officer and ministerial territory.
- **Senior-stakeholder dilemmas** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: holding the portfolio's evidence steady against rank, urgency and charm.
- **Organisational-design dilemmas** map to **Strategic thinking** and **Planning**: the strongest responses repair the structure that produced an incident.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The struggling portfolio manager's first big error**

A portfolio manager appointed eight months ago — promising, hardworking, still growing — presented a flawed capacity analysis to a programme board yesterday: she double-counted a shared team's availability, and the board approved a schedule the portfolio cannot actually staff. She has not yet realised the error. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct the analysis and quietly re-route the revised version to the board secretary, sparing her the embarrassment.
- B) Meet her today: walk through the analysis until she finds the error herself, then support her to own the correction — she notifies the board, presents the corrected position, and receives visible backing; separately, add a peer-check step for board-bound analyses, since a single-author error reached an approval unchecked.
- C) Take the analysis off her, reassign board-facing work to a more experienced manager, and revisit her portfolio in six months.
- D) Wait for the board or the programme to notice, so the lesson lands naturally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The plan needs fast correction; the manager needs to grow through the failure, not around it; and the system that let a single-author error reach a board needs a control. Option B serves all three: guiding her to find the error herself builds the checking discipline itself, having her own the notification with visible backing teaches everyone watching that errors are surfaced by their authors and authors survive surfacing, and the peer-check addition treats her error as a process finding too. Option A amputates her growth; C converts a recoverable error into a demotion; D gambles the portfolio's schedule integrity to engineer a harsher lesson. This tests whether the candidate builds a motivated team through, not around, a developing appointee's first big error.

**Question 2 (easy) — The director general wants a friendlier dashboard**

Reviewing a quarterly portfolio dashboard before it goes to the departmental board, the director general (DG) says: "Three reds out of ten looks terrible for us in front of the board. Two of those are basically ambers — the teams are on it. Can we reflect the direction of travel?" The two programmes' recovery plans are real but unproven; the rating criteria rate them red on current evidence. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Re-rate the two programmes amber — the DG knows the board's context, and recovery plans exist.
- B) Keep the ratings as the criteria produce them; add for each red a dated recovery trajectory and the evidence that would move it to amber, so direction of travel is visible without moving the rating; and tell the DG that consistent criteria are what make the greens credible — if the criteria themselves are miscalibrated, offer to review them openly for next quarter, prospectively not retrospectively.
- C) Remove RAG ratings from the dashboard this quarter in favour of narrative reporting.
- D) Rate them amber but keep a private note that they are really red.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B refuses the rating change on the ground that survives repetition — criteria bent once are bent — while serving the legitimate kernel of the request honestly through dated recovery trajectories, and distinguishes rating integrity (non-negotiable retrospectively) from criteria calibration (legitimately reviewable prospectively, in the open). Option A trades the dashboard's entire evidentiary value for one comfortable meeting; D is A with a written confession attached; C abolishes the instrument rather than defend it. This tests whether the candidate resists a reasonably worded, senior-sourced request to bend a rating, while still giving the requester something real.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Credit, visibility and the diverse team**

Preparing departmental awards nominations, the candidate reviews the year's standout work: a new dependency-mapping approach — now adopted by two other departments — was substantially designed by a junior analyst, building on a rough concept a portfolio manager sketched. The portfolio manager has drafted the nomination naming himself as lead with the analyst "supporting". The analyst, early-career and from a group under-represented at senior digital levels, has said nothing. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Submit the nomination as drafted — the portfolio manager originated the concept, and rank conventionally carries the credit.
- B) Correct the attribution before submission: speak with the portfolio manager, acknowledging his concept while establishing that design leadership was the analyst's, resubmit with roles accurately stated, ensure the analyst presents the work at the next cross-government portfolio community session, and treat the episode as a prompt to check how attribution flows generally.
- C) Withdraw the nomination to avoid an awkward conversation.
- D) Submit as drafted but privately assure the analyst that the truth is known.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Attribution accuracy is an integrity behaviour; its distribution is an inclusion behaviour; both are systems a head of portfolio owns. Option B corrects the record before it becomes official, has a fair conversation with the portfolio manager that keeps his genuine contribution credited, and adds the visibility move — platform access, not just an accurate document — that many leaders miss. Option A launders inflation through convention; D lets a false record stand while privately compensating its victim; C destroys deserved recognition to avoid a manageable conversation. This tests whether the candidate treats credit distribution as a system to actively manage, not an incident to witness.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The minister's office and the pre-announcement**

A special adviser calls: the minister wants to announce a new digital service's launch date at a conference in three weeks. Portfolio data: private beta going well; public beta gate review in five weeks; the delivery team's own forecast for a launchable service is four to six months, contingent on the beta findings. The adviser says: "Just give us a date we can say. It can be soft — 'expected by spring'." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Provide "expected by spring" — it is soft, plausible, and relations with the minister's office matter.
- B) Explain that any announced date lands ahead of the evidence — the gate review in five weeks is the point at which a date becomes forecastable — and offer what is announceable now: the beta's progress, its user evidence, and a commitment to announce the launch date "following the beta assessment this autumn"; offer to brief the adviser on why gate-sequenced announcements protect the minister from a slipped-date correction story.
- C) Refuse: "The portfolio office does not provide launch dates for political events."
- D) Give the team's forecast range — "four to six months" — and let the office round it as they wish.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A soft date is remembered and reported as firm, and its slippage becomes a story sourced to this office's acquiescence; a pre-announced date, however soft, also forecloses the gate review as a genuine decision point. Option B supplies real announceable substance now, commits to a dated announcement event with better material, and reframes the refusal as service by explaining why gate-sequenced dates protect the minister. Option D looks transparent but functions like A once a communications office strips its contingency clause in transit. Option C is correct in principle and teaches the office to route around the portfolio's data next time. This tests whether the candidate gives power something better to say than the thing it cannot be given.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The assurance finding on a candidate's own initiative**

An internal audit of portfolio governance — commissioned by the candidate — reports a significant finding: a prioritisation framework the candidate designed and championed two years ago is being gamed; programmes have learned to draft bids that score well against the criteria while delivered scope diverges from what was scored, with three documented examples. The audit lands two weeks before the annual investment round, which runs on the same framework. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the finding for action after the investment round — changing the rules two weeks out would be destabilising.
- B) Accept the finding now and apply an immediate compensating control to this round — a delivered-scope-versus-scored-bid reconciliation for the three documented gamers and spot-checks portfolio-wide, announced to all bidders — while committing a framework redesign for completion before next year's round; report the finding, its examples, personal authorship of the framework, and the compensating measures to the investment committee before it convenes.
- C) Challenge the audit's sample of three as too small to establish a pattern, and request further evidence before acting.
- D) Quietly rescore the three documented programmes' current bids downward and proceed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** What actually destabilises an investment round is discovering afterwards that it ran on known-gamed rules; a compensating control announced beforehand stabilises the round by making gaming unprofitable within it. Option B pairs an immediate control with a realistic structural fix and full upward disclosure, including personal authorship — the disclosure that makes everything else credible. Option C attacks the messenger's sample size in the candidate's own convenience; D is unauthorised, invisible score-tampering, indistinguishable from the gaming it punishes once it surfaces. This tests whether a candidate's governance duty survives discovering that the failed control is their own.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: the failing supplier on the flagship**

The flagship programme's prime supplier has missed three consecutive delivery milestones; the programme director believes the supplier's key team has been quietly redeployed to a bigger client. Commercial colleagues confirm contractual remedies exist but warn that invoking them adversarially could stall delivery for months. The programme is politically visible; the accounting officer has asked to be kept informed. Ask which response is LEAST effective.

- A) Convene a supplier executive session with the programme director and commercial lead: present the milestone evidence, require a remediation plan with named resourcing within ten working days, and state — without yet invoking — the contractual position, while briefing the accounting officer on the strategy and triggers.
- B) Commission an immediate independent capability assessment of the supplier's actual team deployment, so any subsequent step runs on verified facts rather than belief.
- C) Begin contingency planning in parallel: map what insourcing, re-procurement, or descoping would require, so the portfolio negotiates with options rather than dependence.
- D) Hold the current course for one more milestone cycle: three misses may reflect teething, invoking anything now risks the relationship, and the supplier's account director has promised improvement.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** A, B and C are a sequence, not alternatives: verify facts, confront with structure, build the alternative — together the standard response to supplier failure on a visible programme. Option D fails on every axis, and each of its clauses is a recognisable rationalisation: "teething" ignores the pattern that three consecutive misses establishes; "risks the relationship" protects a relationship where the supplier's best people already serve someone else; and an unverified promise from the person whose job is promising is weighted against three verified misses. This tests whether the candidate recognises that, with failing suppliers, patience without structure is not relationship management but unilateral disarmament, and that the accounting officer's request to be kept informed goes unmet if nothing is actually done.

**Question 7 (hard) — The portfolio manager who wants to leave — or lead**

The candidate's strongest portfolio manager reveals privately that she has been offered a deputy director role elsewhere, starting in eight weeks. She is torn: she loves the portfolio, but "there's no obvious next step here." The candidate's own succession thinking had her leading the office's planned expansion next year — mentioned to no one, since funding is unconfirmed. Losing her now would leave two major programmes' assurance without senior cover through the winter. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell her about the expansion role now, making clear its funding is unconfirmed, so she can weigh a real (if uncertain) internal future against the certain external offer — and congratulate her genuinely either way; separately, fix the succession opacity her "no obvious next step" reveals.
- B) Counter-offer immediately: promise her the expansion leadership, and seek the funding confirmation afterwards.
- C) Accept her likely departure gracefully and begin recruiting; sharing unconfirmed plans would be unfair to her decision-making.
- D) Remind her of the two programmes needing winter cover and ask her, as a professional courtesy, to defer any move until spring.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "No obvious next step here" reveals that a real succession plan exists but is invisible to her — an information failure that belongs to the leader, not to her. Option A discloses honest uncertainty, giving her a true comparison without weaponising it, and separately treats succession opacity as a systemic finding to fix regardless of her choice. Option B converts an unconfirmed plan into a promise that may not be keepable; C's fairness logic is inverted, since withholding decision-relevant information decides for her by omission; D spends the relationship to solve a coverage problem. This tests whether the candidate treats visible pathways for their strongest people as a leadership duty distinct from the immediate staffing pressure.

**Question 8 (hard) — The accounting officer's direction**

The accounting officer (AO) tells the candidate the department will bid to host a cross-government shared platform and asks for confirmation to the Cabinet Office that the portfolio can absorb the hosting programme "within existing capacity". Analysis: absorbing it would consume the portfolio's entire contingency capacity for eighteen months and delay two mid-tier programmes by a quarter each; it is absorbable, but only by spending resilience that would then not exist. The AO's framing suggests the answer is expected to be yes. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Confirm as asked — the AO owns the department's risk appetite, and the bid is strategically valuable.
- B) Decline to confirm, and advise the AO that the portfolio cannot support the bid.
- C) Tell the AO the true position and put the real decision to them: "Absorbable — by consuming our full contingency for eighteen months and delaying two named programmes a quarter each. If we accept that exposure, I can confirm; I recommend we say so internally in those terms, so the risk acceptance is explicit and the two programmes' stakeholders hear it from us. If the exposure is unacceptable, the honest bid says 'with two additional posts' — which may still win. Your call, and I'll make either version true."
- D) Confirm to the Cabinet Office as asked, but record capacity concerns in the portfolio risk register for the file.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Risk appetite can only be exercised over risks that have been stated; a "yes" without the exposure attached disables the AO's authority rather than respecting it. Option C states the price, frames the decision as the risk acceptance it actually is and places it with its owner, offers a constructive alternative, and commits to making either version true. Option B substitutes the candidate's own risk appetite for the AO's; option D documents the risk privately well enough to prove it was known, which is not the same as raising it. This tests whether the candidate can advise power with the price tag visible, neither paying it silently nor refusing it on the decision-maker's behalf.

**Question 9 (hard) — Rank the responses: the whistleblower's email**

Monday, 07:40. An email from a mid-level analyst inside Programme Osprey — not in the candidate's line — sent directly, marked "in confidence": the programme's reported user-volume figures, cited in board papers and the department's public annual report, are "calculated in a way everyone inside knows is wrong", inflating active users roughly threefold; he has raised it twice with programme leadership and "been told to focus on my own work"; he asks to be protected. Ask for a ranking of these responses from most to least effective:

1. Acknowledge to him today — confirming receipt, that raising it was right, and that protection is an explicit priority; commission an independent verification of the counting methodology through the portfolio's standing assurance authority, without naming any source; on its findings, correct the record wherever it is wrong, and address the "told to focus on your own work" management response as its own serious finding.
2. Forward his email to Programme Osprey's director, asking for their account of the methodology, copying him for transparency.
3. Verify the methodology first without acknowledging his email, so no link between him and the review can exist; contact him only once it concludes.
4. Advise him to raise it through the department's formal whistleblowing channel, and take no portfolio action while that process runs.

- A) 1, 3, 4, 2
- B) 1, 4, 3, 2
- C) 3, 1, 4, 2
- D) 1, 3, 2, 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 leads by holding all four duties at once — the discloser acknowledged, the facts independently verified, the record corrected wherever wrong, and the management culture finding addressed. Response 3 gets verification right but the human wrong, sacrificing his certainty that anyone acted, when unlinkability is achievable with acknowledgement too. Response 4 outsources a standing duty to a formal channel while taking no action on figures already in board papers. Response 2 is catastrophic: forwarding a confidential disclosure to the leadership it accuses, with the discloser copied, identifies him to the people who already silenced him. This tests whether the candidate protects the person, verifies the facts, corrects the record and fixes the culture — with protection due the same day.

**Question 10 (hard) — The inclusion finding in a candidate's own leadership team**

Preparing evidence for a department's inclusion review, three years of data on the candidate's own leadership team: the five portfolio managers appointed are demographically near-identical to their predecessors; development-scheme nominations skew heavily towards headquarters-based staff; and exit interviews from the office's leavers include two — both from under-represented groups — citing "limited visibility of progression". The candidate genuinely believed the office was inclusive. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Contextualise the data for the review: small numbers, strong appointees each individually justified, and two exit interviews are anecdote, not pattern.
- B) Present the data to the review unvarnished, with the reading that individually defensible decisions have summed to a systemically narrow pattern, and a concrete plan announced to the office: structured interviews and diverse panels for every appointment, development nominations balanced across locations, progression pathways published, and the data reviewed with the leadership team quarterly, with named personal accountability for movement.
- C) Commission a further year of data collection before drawing conclusions, to ensure the pattern is statistically robust.
- D) Delegate the response to the business manager as the data owner, endorsing whatever plan they produce.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Individually defensible decisions summing to an indefensible pattern is the cumulative-change blindness this guide tests elsewhere, now applied to people rather than baselines, and in the candidate's own office. Option A contextualises adverse data in exactly the way the candidate would never accept from a programme; C's caution fails the same symmetry test, since another year of data means another appointment round under an unexamined pattern; D delegates what cannot be delegated, since the pattern is in the candidate's own decisions. Option B applies the candidate's own standards reflexively, with structural fixes and named accountability. This tests whether a leader audits themselves in public with the same rigour applied everywhere else.

**Question 11 (hard) — Most and least effective: the peer head of portfolio**

At a cross-government portfolio leaders' network, a peer head of portfolio — a friend — asks to speak privately. Her department's flagship programme is in serious trouble; her leadership "doesn't want it red yet"; she has kept it amber for two quarters and the gap is now unbridgeable. "You've faced this," she says. "How did you handle it? And — between us — would you have a quiet look at my evidence pack and tell me if the red case is as unanswerable as I think?" Ask which response is MOST effective, and which LEAST:

1. Review her evidence pack informally and give an honest reading; peer review between heads is how the profession self-corrects, and her department's governance is not the candidate's jurisdiction.
2. Decline any private review of another department's assurance evidence, but give what peer support legitimately offers: how the equivalent pressure was handled, rehearsing the conversation she must have with her leadership this week, and naming clearly that the two-quarter gap itself now needs disclosing, not just the rating; offer to be called after she has done it.
3. Tell her that sustained knowing misrating under leadership pressure obliges an alert to her department's audit function if she does not act, and set her a deadline.
4. Sympathise, share war stories in general terms, and leave the specifics alone — her department, her problem.

- A) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 1; least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 2; least effective: 3
- D) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Her evidence question is already answered by her own words — she is not seeking analysis but company in certainty. Response 2 aims at what she actually needs: transferable craft, rehearsal of the hard conversation, and naming that two quarters of knowing misrating is now itself part of what must be disclosed. It declines the pack review on the right ground: an informal cross-departmental read creates accountability without authority. Response 3 is heavy-handed and premature but takes the misreporting seriously; response 4 protects nothing, offering warmth while walking away from a colleague who needs help. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes clumsy integrity from polished indifference — response 3 outranks response 4 even though neither is the strongest option.

**Question 12 (hard) — The last question: what is left behind**

The candidate is eighteen months from a planned move, successor unknown. An organisational effectiveness review asks each senior leader to name their single highest-priority institutional investment for their remaining tenure. Four candidates: (1) personally leading the recovery of the portfolio's largest troubled programme; (2) codifying the portfolio's operating model — criteria, controls, escalation routes, reporting standards — currently carried substantially in the candidate's head and habits, into a maintained, owned framework; (3) accelerating the leadership development of the two most senior portfolio managers, including deputising rotations, committee exposure and external assessment; (4) a portfolio-wide data platform consolidating the reporting pipeline, fully specified and fundable now. Ask which choice is most defensible as the single priority.

- A) 1 — nothing matters more than the flagship's recovery, and the odds-raising contribution is unique and time-limited.
- B) 2 and 3 pursued as one investment — codify the operating model through the two senior managers who must inherit it: they lead the codification with the candidate as reviewer, deputise through the committees the framework governs, and by departure the function's judgement exists outside one person's head and inside both a document and two people.
- C) 4 — platforms outlast people; automate the pipeline and the rest follows.
- D) 3 alone — people are always the answer; frameworks and platforms date.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A function whose judgement lives "substantially in your head and habits" is a key-person risk wearing an org chart. Option B recognises that 2 and 3 are each other's method: a framework codified without formed successors is documentation nobody can operate with judgement, while successors developed without codification inherit authority but rebuild the operating model from fragments. Having the two managers lead the codification with the candidate as reviewer fuses both. Option A invests scarce time in an instance rather than an institution; C buys pipes for a function that hasn't secured the judgement flowing through them; D bets everything on two careers with nothing written down. This tests whether the candidate builds something that works after they leave the room, rather than a legacy that depreciates from the day they depart.

### Administration tips

- Score against the organisation's own leadership behaviours — information integrity, decisions priced and placed with their owners, people developed through failure, standards applied reflexively — rather than a single fixed key.
- For ranking items, credit the reasoning behind an ordering, not only the final sequence.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes an instance that needs rescuing from an institution that needs repairing, and rewards the response that improves the system.
- Watch for symmetry: does the candidate apply the same rigour to their own decisions, appointments, or frameworks as to a programme's or a peer's?
- Score for whether a candidate's chosen response equips someone with agency, rather than quietly doing the work around them.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting deference dressed as respect — confirming or endorsing something untrue or unpriced because a senior figure preferred it.
- Missing when a response protects someone by removing their agency rather than by equipping them.
- Rewarding a private record of the truth (a register note, a private caveat) kept alongside a public statement that says otherwise.
- Failing to note asymmetric rigour — a candidate who pattern-reads others' evidence sharply but contextualises their own.
- Crediting an instance-level rescue that depends on the candidate's continued personal presence, over a structural repair.
- Missing when a candidate answers the pressure of the moment rather than the precedent their response will set.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through four assessments pitched at head of portfolio: reading a portfolio as a system, the arithmetic that governs portfolio decisions, governing documents read at their exact word, and the particular exposure of a head-level role, where the hardest items put a candidate's own framework, appointments and judgement under the same scrutiny applied elsewhere.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — many head-level items reward the quality and symmetry of reasoning as much as the final choice, and several situational items have more than one defensible response. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level. Where a candidate's answer is defensible but not the strongest, use the debrief to name what the strongest option does differently.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
