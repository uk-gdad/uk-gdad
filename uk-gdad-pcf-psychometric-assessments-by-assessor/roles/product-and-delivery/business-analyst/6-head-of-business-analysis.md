# Head of Business Analysis - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of business analysis, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At this level a candidate leads the business analysis function across an enterprise area and builds business analysis capability for the whole organisation. The material is job-specific: it is not about whether a candidate can write a good requirement — that is assumed, along with the ability to teach it — but about reasoning clearly at the level where decisions operate on function strategy and workforce plans, standards and their governance, budgets and their trade-offs, organisational design, and the credibility of the profession itself.

This document covers four types of assessment, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — percentages, ratios, weighted averages, rates, and interpreting tables and charts.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — precise comprehension, True/False/Cannot-say discipline, inference, and clear-writing judgement.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — realistic dilemmas involving strategic influence, evidence integrity as organisational infrastructure, capability stewardship, and proportionate governance.

Each section explains what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, maps its dimensions to the named skills in the role, gives a substantial set of practice questions with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, then gives administration tips and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Offer any unscored practice questions the platform provides. At this level errors compound through systems the candidate designed, and nobody downstream re-checks the reasoning, so the situational judgement items reward responses that fix the immediate instance and treat every decision as a precedent the wider function will learn from — score for that combination, not only whether the letter matches the key. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. For a head of function, that means reasoning about systems of work rather than single artefacts: standards and their exception paths, assurance regimes and their incentives, workforce models and their dependencies, portfolios and their contradictions.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is typically norm-referenced and often adaptive. At senior levels, accuracy is often weighted above speed: a head of function who is slowly right is more valuable than one who is quickly wrong, because nobody downstream re-checks the reasoning.

This kind of assessment matters for a head of business analysis in a distinctive way. This function exists to bring structured thinking to the organisation's hardest problems, and this role is its exemplar and final quality gate — determining how complex programmes are represented across the organisation, spotting when models, plans or policy intent quietly contradict each other, designing the standards through which dozens of analysts reason on their behalf, and making resourcing decisions whose logic must survive challenge at board level.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in the role profile:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business modelling** and **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: determining the scope and methodology for representing complex programmes across an organisation, and helping define capabilities and operating models, both mean recognising structural patterns — and structural contradictions — across many models, plans, and directorates at once.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Consultancy**: advising on options analysis, feasibility and impact assessment, and leading the definition of guidance for the whole organisation, require valid reasoning from constraints to consequences — with the discipline to state assumptions and deduce nothing more.
- **Error checking** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements** and **Testing (business analysis)**: directing the approach to requirements management and reviewing test and assurance reporting across a function means detecting the defect that survived everyone else's review — because after this role, there is no one.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: setting standards for how teams deliver, directing stakeholder strategy, and allocating a function's scarce capacity across an enterprise portfolio are all ranking problems under competing, legitimate pressures.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Business process improvement** and **Digital and data systems analysis**: leading the identification of large-scale improvement opportunities, and understanding how processes, systems and measurements interact across an organisation, both mean decomposing tangled enterprise problems into parts that can be reasoned about, evidenced and recombined into decisions.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a standards library**

A function's standards library contains four documents. Standard A: "Requirements informing decisions above £1m must be independently reviewed." Standard B: "All discovery outputs must be peer-reviewed within the producing team." Standard C: "Business cases above £5m must use the approved benefits model." Standard D: "All models informing decisions above £1m must be independently reviewed, except models produced under emergency protocols." A new analyst asks which single structural feature most distinguishes Standard B from the other three. What is the best answer?

- A) Standard B is the only one without a financial threshold — it applies to all discovery outputs regardless of decision value.
- B) Standard B is about discovery, and discovery matters less.
- C) Standard B is the shortest.
- D) Standard B is the only one that cannot be breached.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Standards A, C and D all scope their obligations by financial threshold; B alone applies universally to a class of artefact, with internal rather than independent review. This tests spotting that a standards library mixes two scoping philosophies — value-thresholded independent review versus universal peer review — since mixed philosophies generate boundary confusion that is where assurance fails. Option B smuggles in an unstated value judgement; C is trivial; D is simply false.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a delegation scheme**

A delegation scheme states: "The head of business analysis may approve methodology exemptions lasting up to one quarter. Exemptions exceeding one quarter require the digital director's approval." A programme requests a two-quarter exemption; the digital director is on leave for three weeks and the work "cannot wait". Which conclusion must be true under the scheme as written?

- A) The exemption can be approved because the director is unavailable.
- B) An exemption of up to one quarter can be approved now; the two-quarter request as such requires the director's approval, which the scheme does not transfer in her absence.
- C) The programme must halt until the director returns.
- D) The scheme permits approval if the director is informed afterwards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scheme grants authority up to one quarter and reserves anything longer to the director; nothing creates an urgency exception or a deputising rule, so A and D read permissions into silence. C overreaches the other way — the scheme constrains exemption approval, not delivery, so a compliant one-quarter approval can keep work flowing. This tests the discipline that a governance document's silence means "the document does not provide for that", not "so presumably it can be done" — while still finding the compliant move available within the authority actually held.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a capability matrix**

A draft workforce paper states: "Function total: 24 analysts. By level: 3 leads, 8 seniors, 10 analysts, 4 associates. By specialism: 12 service analysis, 7 data-focused analysis, 6 process analysis." What is wrong?

- A) Nothing — both breakdowns are internally consistent.
- B) The level breakdown sums to 25, not 24.
- C) The specialism breakdown sums to 25, not 24; one person is double-counted or the total is wrong.
- D) Both breakdowns sum incorrectly.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Levels sum to 25 (3+8+10+4); specialisms also sum to 25 (12+7+6), both against a stated total of 24. This tests finishing a systematic check rather than stopping at the first discrepancy found — since both breakdowns independently reach 25, the most economical explanation is that the stated total itself is wrong, a diagnosis that changes the correction made and the question asked of the paper's author, and that a workforce paper failing this basic self-consistency check will be destroyed at the first board question.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a head of function's week**

Monday morning presents: (1) Wednesday's executive committee will decide next year's budget from a paper whose workforce numbers came from the flawed matrix in the previous question and need re-verification; (2) a lead analyst has resigned, and her programme director wants an urgent conversation about cover for a gate review in three weeks; (3) the cross-government heads-of-profession network meets Friday to hear a presentation of the capability framework two departments plan to adopt; (4) an analyst has raised, via an open-door route, a concern that a supplier-run discovery is presenting fabricated user research quotes, reporting to its programme board in two weeks. Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1, 4, 2, 3 — budget paper verification, then triage the fabrication concern, then cover planning, then the presentation
- B) 4, 1, 2, 3 — fabrication first because integrity issues always come first
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — the order the demands arrived
- D) 3, 1, 4, 2 — external reputation first

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two items carry this-week consequence: the Wednesday budget decision and the fabrication allegation, which has a two-week runway. The right Monday action on item 4 is a same-day short triage — hear the analyst, secure the evidence, commission discreet verification — not a full investigation launched ahead of making the executive paper safe. Option B's "integrity always first" ignores that a same-day triage achieves the urgent protection without displacing the paper. This tests designing a week so every hard-deadline item gets its minimum effective action in time, rather than sequencing by arrival order or by a slogan.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction about an assurance regime's incentives**

An assurance regime scores programmes red, amber or green on analysis quality. Observations: (i) programmes self-assess before the function's review; (ii) a programme whose self-assessment matches the review outcome faces no follow-up, while mismatches trigger a deep-dive; (iii) over the year, self-assessments have converged almost perfectly with the reviews — and both have drifted greener. Which conclusion does the evidence best support?

- A) Analysis quality has genuinely improved across the portfolio.
- B) The regime rewards agreement rather than accuracy: programmes minimise deep-dive risk by predicting the review rather than assessing themselves honestly, and reviewers face no counterweight to grade inflation — so convergence-plus-greening is exactly what gaming would produce, and independent evidence is needed to distinguish it from genuine improvement.
- C) The reviewers are colluding with programmes.
- D) Self-assessment should be abolished.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Mismatch costs a deep-dive; match is free, so programmes optimise for predicting the reviewer, and nothing penalises drift toward green if both sides drift together. This tests deducing the limits of what a measurement system can establish — not claiming gaming is occurring, merely that the regime cannot distinguish it from genuine improvement — rather than leaping to misconduct (C) or discarding a genuinely useful mechanism (D).

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in escaped defects**

Escaped defects this year: ambiguous requirement wording 8, missed stakeholder impact 6, incorrect policy interpretation 22, missing non-functional requirement 4. Three years ago policy-interpretation escapes were 15% of the total; they are now 55%. In the same period the organisation's legislative programme has doubled and the two policy-specialist senior analysts have both been promoted into generalist lead roles. What is the strongest reading?

- A) Analysts have become careless with policy work and need reminding of the standard.
- B) The rise in policy-interpretation escapes tracks rising policy complexity and the loss of specialist capacity to promotion — a capability-supply problem the function's own structure created; the response is structural: rebuild policy-analysis specialism, not exhortation.
- C) The other categories have improved, which is what matters.
- D) The classification scheme is probably wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** One category has quadrupled in share while its known drivers moved in the same direction: demand doubled while specialist supply fell to zero. This tests reading defect data against workforce data to reach a diagnosis neither yields alone, and recognising that promotion decisions are capability decisions — exhortation (A) addresses motivation with no evidence of a motivation problem, and the other categories' stability (C) does not offset the category driving the total.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a board paper's logic**

A paper for the investment board argues: "Our user research shows 78% of surveyed users want feature X. Feature X costs £400k. Our competitor department delivered a similar feature for £250k, proving our supplier is overcharging. Therefore we should insource the build." Which is the most serious logical defect?

- A) The 78% figure is from surveyed users, who may not represent all users.
- B) The conclusion (insource) does not follow from the premises: even granting the survey and the price gap, the £250k comparison at most supports renegotiating or re-procuring — the paper never assesses insourcing's own cost, capacity or feasibility, and "similar feature" elsewhere does not establish comparability.
- C) User wants are not user needs.
- D) The paper does not state the survey sample size.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A, C and D are all genuine weaknesses, but B identifies the argument's structural failure: insourcing appears in the conclusion having never itself been assessed — no capacity check, no cost estimate, no delivery risk — while the evidence at most supports renegotiation. This tests catching the invalid inference from acceptable premises, the conclusion-swap where evidence against one option is treated as evidence for an unexamined alternative, rather than only the weaker premises reviewers at every level would catch.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving: designing a triage rule**

Demand for a function exceeds supply by roughly 40%, arriving as statutory work (15%), executive-sponsored strategic initiatives (25%), programme delivery support (45%), and speculative "come and help us think" requests (15%). A triage rule is needed that leads can apply without the head of function. Which rule is best designed?

- A) First come, first served — it is objective and cannot be accused of favouritism.
- B) Statutory work is always accepted; strategic and delivery work is accepted against published criteria (decision value, user impact, analytical leverage) with scores visible to requesters; speculative requests get a time-boxed one-day scoping conversation rather than an engagement; and the rule itself is reviewed quarterly against what was declined and what the declines cost.
- C) Executive-sponsored work is always accepted, since executives set organisational priority.
- D) All requests are accepted with effort scaled down proportionately — everyone gets 60% of what they asked for.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A decouples allocation from value entirely; C teaches the organisation to escalate sponsorship rather than improve cases and would crowd out statutory work; D spreads capacity so thin that analysis below a critical mass produces confident-looking but unsafe work. This tests designing a triage rule as a system under load: a genuine bright line only where the law puts one, published contestable criteria, a cheap product for speculative demand, and a rule that audits its own declines — publishing criteria is what actually defends against favouritism, not queuing.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint workforce planning**

Two seniors (S1, S2) and two analysts (A1, A2) must be placed into four teams (Casework, Payments, Data, Policy), one hire per team. Constraints: (i) Policy must receive a senior, given the policy-defect problem; (ii) A1, a former caseworker, cannot join Casework this year under the conflict-of-interest rule, but is the only candidate with payments-platform experience; (iii) S2 is the only candidate qualified to lead Data's regression testing work, a senior-level duty; (iv) each team gets exactly one hire. Which placement satisfies all constraints?

- A) S1→Policy, S2→Data, A1→Payments, A2→Casework
- B) S1→Data, S2→Policy, A1→Payments, A2→Casework
- C) S1→Policy, S2→Casework, A1→Payments, A2→Data
- D) S1→Payments, S2→Data, A1→Policy, A2→Casework

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** From (iii), S2 must go to Data — the unique qualification eliminates B and C. From (i), with S2 fixed at Data, Policy's senior must be S1 — eliminating D, which sends an analyst to Policy. A satisfies every constraint: A1 avoids Casework and covers Payments; A2's Casework placement breaches nothing. This tests deriving forced placements from the tightest constraints first, then propagating — the discipline behind a defensible audit trail for real workforce decisions.

**Question 10 (hard) — Deduction about a target operating model's arithmetic of accountability**

A proposed operating model states: (i) every programme has a named analysis lead accountable for analysis quality; (ii) analysis leads must be at senior analyst level or above; (iii) no individual may be analysis lead for more than two programmes; (iv) the portfolio runs 11 programmes next year. A function has 3 leads and 4 senior analysts; one senior analyst is on parental leave for the full year. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The model is workable as stated.
- B) Available senior-or-above headcount is 6, giving a maximum of 12 programme-lead slots against 11 needed — arithmetically feasible only if every available lead and senior takes the maximum two programmes with a single exception, leaving no slack for attrition, leave or the duties those roles otherwise perform.
- C) The model requires hiring at least two more seniors.
- D) Constraint (iii) should be relaxed to three programmes each.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eligible individuals = 6; maximum slots = 12; required = 11 — feasible only at near-total saturation, with zero capacity for a resignation, a new programme, further leave, or the day jobs leads and seniors otherwise perform. This tests computing the arithmetic boundary and then asking what the slots assume about everything else those people do — options C and D are possible responses, not deductions the facts force, and A ignores the fragility the arithmetic exposes.

**Question 11 (hard) — Prioritising the function's improvement portfolio**

Two of four internal improvement initiatives can be funded this year: (1) a requirements-quality toolchain, high cost, strong payback but dependent on adoption by tool-fatigued teams; (2) a policy-analysis capability programme (recruitment, training, review support), directly addressing the dominant escaped-defect category, benefits over 18–24 months; (3) a function-wide dashboard of analysis metrics, cheap, fast, popular with the director, measuring mostly what is easy to count; (4) an early-engagement protocol with the policy directorate, embedding analysts before positions harden, cheap, addressing the source of the policy-defect category, requiring the policy director's probable-but-uncertain cooperation. Which pair is the strongest investment?

- A) 1 and 3 — tooling plus visibility
- B) 2 and 4 — attack the dominant defect category from both the supply side (capability) and the demand side (upstream engagement)
- C) 3 and 4 — the two cheap ones
- D) 1 and 2 — the two big ones

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Policy interpretation is 55% of escaped defects and rising, driven by demand surge and specialist-supply collapse; initiatives 2 and 4 attack that single dominant problem from both ends and hedge each other if one falters. Initiative 1 targets a stable, smaller category with adoption risk; initiative 3 is a distractor that is cheap, fast and popular but measures the wrong thing. This tests allocating an improvement budget against a function's own defect data rather than by price, size, or optics.

**Question 12 (hard) — Applied problem solving: contradictory strategic mandates**

A digital strategy commits to "90% of transactions digital by 2028"; a customer strategy commits to "assisted routes maintained at current service levels throughout the strategy period"; a finance settlement assumes £6m savings from contact-centre reduction, achievable only by reducing the protected assisted routes. All three documents are board-approved. A programme asks which mandate governs their business case. What is the strongest analytical response?

- A) Advise the programme to follow the most recently approved document.
- B) Advise the programme to follow the finance settlement, since funding constraints are the binding reality.
- C) Surface the three-way contradiction explicitly to the board level that owns all three documents — with the arithmetic that proves the conflict, scenario options showing what each resolution implies for the other two commitments, and an interim analytical position for the programme so it is not paralysed while the board resolves it.
- D) Advise the programme to draft its case ambiguously enough to be defensible under any of the three readings.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A's recency rule and B's funding-first rule each silently repeal a board commitment without the board's knowledge; D institutionalises the contradiction. C converts a felt tension into a demonstrated arithmetic conflict, brings scenario options since boards resolve trade-offs rather than complaints, and gives the programme an interim position so delivery continues while governance catches up. This tests recognising that ensuring streams of work align to strategic context sometimes means proving the strategic context does not align with itself, and saying so where the owners of all three documents can hear it together.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate names governance silences explicitly** — "the document does not provide for that" — rather than inferring permission from silence.
- **Watch for whether a candidate interrogates a measurement regime's incentives** rather than reading convergence as automatic evidence of improvement.
- **Note whether a candidate insists on positive assessment of a recommended option**, catching the conclusion-swap where a rejected alternative is treated as support for what remains.
- **Note whether a candidate states an arithmetic finding's fragility explicitly** rather than presenting a saturated plan as reassuring.
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level — accuracy over speed is the standard for this level's scoring emphasis.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that infers authority from a governance document's silence.**
- **Missing when a candidate treats rising agreement between self-assessment and review as evidence of improvement**, without checking the underlying incentives.
- **Crediting an answer that fixes a capability-supply problem with exhortation** rather than a structural response.
- **Missing when a candidate accepts a conclusion that swaps in an unexamined alternative** after evidence eliminates other options.
- **Rewarding a workforce or improvement-portfolio answer that ignores the fragility of a saturated plan** or picks by price or optics rather than evidence.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures a candidate's ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from quantitative information of the kind this role genuinely handles. The arithmetic stays within percentages, ratios, averages, rates, and projections; the difficulty lives in judgement — extracting the right figures, choosing the right operation under time pressure, and recognising precisely what a number does and does not establish.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 40 minutes, with 15 to 25 questions anchored to tables, charts and short data-rich passages. Calculators and rough paper are normally permitted.

This matters for a head of business analysis in two ways. First, the numbers are function-scale: budgets, pay bills, day rates, utilisation, capability investment cases, benefits portfolios. Second, and more importantly, they arrive pre-packaged: finance summaries, supplier reports, programme dashboards and workforce returns land already aggregated, already averaged, already framed — and this role is the last analytical checkpoint before they become decisions. The errors that survive to this level are structural rather than arithmetical: wrong denominators, unweighted averages, mix effects, annualisation games, survivorship, false comparability.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Budget and unit-cost arithmetic** map to **Consultancy** and **Context, problem and option analysis**: leading the definition of guidance on how the organisation uses digital and data skills effectively includes making — and defending — the numeric case for the function itself.
- **Weighted averages, distributions and mix effects** map to **Business modelling** and **Digital and data systems analysis**: models supporting strategic planning must survive compositional questions or they misdirect the decisions built on them.
- **Rates, capacity and workforce arithmetic** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: setting standards for how teams deliver, and shaping operating models, both require converting demand, headcount, utilisation and ramp-up assumptions into plans that hold.
- **Benefits and investment appraisal** map to **Business process improvement**: leading the identification of large-scale improvement opportunities means owning benefit-cost logic for a portfolio, including improvement investments in the function itself.
- **Reading packaged data critically** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: influencing senior stakeholders and reviewing assurance reporting both mean re-deriving, sometimes live in the meeting, the aggregates someone else prepared.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Function budget shares**

A function's annual budget is £2.4 million: £1.68 million on permanent staff pay, £480,000 on contractors, and the remainder on training, tools and travel. What percentage of the budget is the non-pay remainder?

- A) 8%
- B) 10%
- C) 12%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Remainder = £240,000; 240,000 ÷ 2,400,000 = 10%. Cross-check: 70% permanent pay, 20% contractors, 10% remainder sums to 100%. This tests budget-share fluency, since these three numbers open most conversations with finance about a function's shape, and the contractor share is the figure most often challenged.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cost of a vacancy gap**

A senior analyst post (£68,000 total employment cost per year) has been vacant for 4 months, covered by a contractor at £520 per day, who has worked 82 days. How much more has the cover cost than the vacant post would have?

- A) £19,973
- B) £22,667
- C) £42,640
- D) £15,400

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Contractor cost = £42,640; the post's 4-month cost = £22,667; premium = £19,973. This tests the vacancy-premium calculation that converts "we have vacancies" into a concrete monthly cost over plan — the form of words that unlocks recruitment decisions.

**Question 3 (easy) — Utilisation rate**

A function of 20 analysts recorded 3,540 billable-equivalent days last year against a theoretical maximum of 4,400 available days. The target utilisation is 78%. Did the function meet the target, and what was actual utilisation?

- A) No — 76.4%
- B) Yes — 80.5%
- C) Yes — 78.0% exactly
- D) No — 74.2%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 3,540 ÷ 4,400 ≈ 80.5%, exceeding the 78% target. This tests basic rate calculation, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting that the maximum's exclusions must be confirmed for comparability, and that over-target utilisation is not unambiguously good news since the non-billable remainder funds training, community and standards work.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Weighted cost per engagement**

A function completed 30 engagements last year: 18 small (20 analyst-days each), 9 medium (60 analyst-days), 3 large (180 analyst-days). At a blended £450 per analyst-day, what was the average cost per engagement?

- A) £13,500
- B) £21,600
- C) £39,000
- D) £27,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total days = 1,440; total cost = £648,000; average per engagement = £21,600. Option C is the unweighted mean of the three size-averages, ignoring that small engagements are six times as numerous as large ones. This tests weighting by count rather than averaging size categories, and, for a strong candidate's answer, volunteering the concentration finding (3 large engagements consume 37.5% of capacity) alongside the headline figure.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Benchmarking with unlike bases**

A benchmarking report states: "Department X's analysis function costs £96,000 per analyst per year; yours costs £120,000 — you are 25% more expensive." Investigation shows X's figure covers salary and employer costs only, while the other function's figure additionally includes training, tools, travel and overheads totalling £21,000 per analyst. What is the like-for-like comparison?

- A) £99,000 to X's £96,000 on X's basis — about 3.1% more, not 25%.
- B) The report is right: 25% more expensive.
- C) Cheaper than X by £3,000.
- D) No comparison is possible.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £120,000 − £21,000 = £99,000 on X's basis; gap = £3,000, about 3.1%, not 25%. This tests asking what each figure includes before comparing sizes — the reflex that defends a function's cost base honestly against a pre-framed benchmark.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capability investment payback**

A policy-analysis capability programme costs £180,000 in year 1. Escaped policy-interpretation defects currently cost £600,000 per year (20 at £30,000 average remediation each). The programme is forecast to reduce annual defect costs by 30% in year 1 and 60% from year 2 onward. What is the net position by the end of year 2?

- A) £540,000 net benefit
- B) £360,000 net benefit
- C) £180,000 net benefit
- D) £0 — break-even

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Year 1 saving £180,000; year 2 saving £360,000; total £540,000; net of the £180,000 cost = £360,000. Each distractor drops one component — A forgets the cost, C drops year 1's contribution, D stops after year 1's saving offsets the cost. This tests phasing a benefits calculation honestly and netting off spend rather than quoting a gross figure.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Demand forecast for workforce planning**

Analysis demand: 1,150 analyst-weeks two years ago, 1,265 last year, 1,392 this year. If the growth rate continues, what is a sound forecast for next year, and roughly how many additional analysts does it imply at 44 productive weeks per analyst?

- A) About 1,460 analyst-weeks; 1 additional analyst
- B) About 1,531 analyst-weeks; 3 additional analysts
- C) About 1,620 analyst-weeks; 5 additional analysts
- D) About 1,392 analyst-weeks; 0 additional analysts — forecasting growth is speculative

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Consistent 10% year-on-year growth (1,265÷1,150 and 1,392÷1,265 both ≈1.10) gives 1,392 × 1.10 ≈ 1,531; increment 139 ÷ 44 ≈ 3 analysts. This tests verifying a growth pattern is genuinely proportional before compounding it, and converting capacity to people explicitly rather than leaving a statistic unconverted into a workforce plan.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The contractor conversion case**

Six contractors cost £520/day, working 210 days per year. Converting all six to permanent posts at £72,000 total employment cost each incurs a one-off £90,000 conversion cost; recruitment realistically fills only 4 of 6 posts in year 1, the remaining 2 covered by contractors for the full year. What is the year 1 net saving (or cost)?

- A) £223,200 saving
- B) £58,800 saving
- C) £58,800 cost
- D) £148,800 saving

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Baseline £655,200; conversion scenario £596,400 (4 permanent posts + 2 contractor-covered + one-off cost); net saving £58,800. Option A is the steady-state annual saving presented as year 1's; D omits the one-off cost. This tests building both scenarios in full and phasing the case honestly — conversion cases are won on an honest, thin year 1 and a strong year 2, not a claimed steady state immediately.

**Question 9 (hard) — Portfolio benefits attribution**

Three programmes claim benefits from the same process improvement: P £400,000, Q £350,000, R £250,000, totalling £1,000,000. Finance's bottom-up measurement finds £640,000 realised. Claims share a baseline, scopes do not overlap, each applied its own uplift assumptions. If realised benefit is allocated pro-rata, what does each programme book, and what is the more important observation?

- A) P: £256,000; Q: £224,000; R: £160,000 — and the claims exceeded reality by 56%, meaning the uplift assumptions systematically inflated, which needs a common benefits standard more than it needs an allocation formula.
- B) P: £400,000; Q: £240,000; R: £0 — first come, first served.
- C) Each books £213,333 — equal shares.
- D) P: £256,000; Q: £224,000; R: £160,000 — and the allocation formula is the important thing.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Realisation ratio = 0.64; pro-rata figures as stated (checking: 256+224+160=640). Options A and D share correct arithmetic but differ in judgement: the important signal is that three non-overlapping claims on a common baseline totalled 156% of measured reality — one missing control, not three separate errors. This tests answering an allocation question and then asking what the need for allocation reveals about the controls upstream.

**Question 10 (hard) — Survey evidence for a board paper**

An annual stakeholder survey reports: "87% of stakeholders rate the function good or excellent (up from 79%)." This year: 240 sent, 98 responded (41%). Last year: 180 sent, 122 responded (68%). Response is voluntary; this year's survey followed a well-publicised flagship success. Which statement should govern how this headline is used in a board paper?

- A) Report the improvement as evidence of rising function performance.
- B) The 87% describes this year's 98 self-selected respondents; with response down from 68% to 41% and a salient recent success likely influencing both who responded and how, the year-on-year comparison is not like-for-like — report the figure with response rates alongside, note the non-response risk, and avoid claiming a measured improvement.
- C) Suppress the figure entirely as statistically meaningless.
- D) Weight the responses to correct for non-response and report the adjusted figure as definitive.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Respondents fell while the population grew, and salience effects cloud the comparison, so the data cannot separate genuine improvement from response-composition shift. This tests applying the same evidential standard to a function's own good-news figure as to any supplier's claim — option A is the flattering over-claim; D promises more precision than the method can deliver, since non-response weighting requires knowing how non-respondents differ.

**Question 11 (hard) — Mix effect in function performance**

Average engagement duration: Year 1, 40 engagements averaging 45 days; Year 2, 50 engagements averaging 39 days, celebrated as "13% faster". Segmented: Year 2 has 35 standard engagements averaging 30 days (Year 1: 28 standard at 32 days) and 15 complex averaging 60 days (Year 1: 12 complex at 75 days). What does the segmentation show?

- A) The celebration is legitimate: both segments genuinely improved — standard by 6.3%, complex by 20% — and with the complex share of the portfolio unchanged at 30%, the aggregate's 13% gain reflects real segment-level improvement rather than mix shift.
- B) The improvement is entirely mix shift; both segments actually slowed.
- C) The aggregate and the segments tell the same story, so segmentation added nothing.
- D) Complex engagements should be excluded from the metric.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Both aggregates verify correctly, both segments genuinely improved, and the complex share held steady at 30% in both years, so the aggregate faithfully reflects real gains. This tests the inverse of the usual trap: having learned aggregates can mislead, the sophisticated error is assuming they always do — segmentation vindicates an honest aggregate exactly as readily as it exposes a dishonest one, and C is wrong because trustworthiness is only known after segmenting.

**Question 12 (hard) — The savings target arithmetic**

A spending review requires modelling a 15% reduction on a £2.4m base (£360,000). Fixed commitments: £140,000 contractually committed tooling (reducible only at contract break in 18 months); £120,000 training; £40,000 travel; £480,000 contractor spend; the rest is permanent pay (£1.62m across 24 posts, averaging £67,500 each). Eliminating all training and travel saves £160,000. Which options honestly reach £360,000 in-year?

- A) Cut training and travel entirely (£160,000) and cut contractor spend by £200,000 (42% of it) — total £360,000 — presenting the delivery impact of losing roughly 385 contractor days and all capability development.
- B) Cut tooling (£140,000), training (£120,000) and travel (£40,000), plus £60,000 of contractor spend.
- C) Declare the target unachievable and refuse to model it.
- D) Reduce permanent headcount by 5.3 posts to save £360,000.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** B counts the contractually committed tooling, which cannot move in-year; D's fractional post ignores consultation and notice periods that make annualised savings unreachable within a year; C forfeits a seat in the decision. A reaches £360,000 through genuinely stoppable spend and states its consequences explicitly. This tests testing every option's arithmetic against real constraints (contracts, HR process, timing) and pricing the consequences of the reachable option rather than the arithmetic alone.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate aligns bases before comparing figures** — the reflex that turns a "25% more expensive" claim into "3% more expensive" once inclusions match.
- **Watch for whether a candidate verifies, segments, then concludes**, rather than pattern-matching that every aggregate must be misleading.
- **Note whether a candidate phases a calculation through time** — in-year versus annualised, ramp-up, contract breaks — before trusting a total.
- **Note whether a candidate asks who is missing from a sample** (response rate, survivorship, self-selection) before quoting packaged evidence.
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that quotes gross where net is the question** — contractor cost against vacancy savings, savings targets against committed spend.
- **Crediting an answer that averages engagement sizes** instead of weighting by count.
- **Missing when a candidate accepts a benchmark's frame** without re-basing it first.
- **Rewarding an answer that presents a steady-state figure as year 1's** in a conversion or capability case.
- **Missing when a candidate applies weaker evidential standards to the function's own good news** than to a supplier's claim.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate extracts meaning from written material of the kind this role handles daily — spending review guidance, machinery-of-government announcements, cross-departmental agreements, professional standards, ministerial correspondence, consultation papers — and how soundly they reason from what a text actually says rather than what experience predicts it should say.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes. Each short passage carries a cluster of questions: True / False / Cannot say; best-supported inference; and clear-writing judgement. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone — hardest for the most senior candidates, since seniority supplies confident context the passage may deliberately not share.

This matters for a head of business analysis because verbal precision is an instrument of governance at this level: standards authored here bind analysts never met; agreements negotiated here are read in their least generous construction at the worst possible moment; briefings to executives are quoted onward without caveats unless the caveats are structurally impossible to detach.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)** and **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements**: translating business drivers, goals and constraints into objectives starts with reading strategies, settlements and mandates exactly — including their qualifiers, defined terms, silences, and scope boundaries.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis**: separating what evidence states from what it suggests — and holding "not established" as a finding — is the verbal form of the options and impact rigour required of every analysis a function signs.
- **Inference judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Consultancy**: reading senior correspondence for its commitments, hedges and deliberate silences — without converting an informed guess into a stated position — is the daily craft of directing stakeholder strategy at executive level.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to standards-authorship and **Business modelling** duties: guidance a head of function leads the definition of, and models supporting strategic planning, must be unambiguous to readers who cannot ask what was meant.
- **Reading binding documents** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Testing (business analysis)**: methodology standards, assurance frameworks and acceptance regimes bind exactly what their words bind.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–5)*

"The settlement provides £42 million over three years for the digital transformation portfolio, of which no more than £6 million may be spent on external consultancy and contractor resource. Release of funding beyond year one is conditional on the portfolio achieving the milestones set out in Annex B and on the department demonstrating, at the year-one review point, a credible plan to grow internal digital and data capability. The Treasury notes the department's intention to expand its business analysis and architecture functions and expects this to be reflected in the capability plan. Funding may not be transferred between the transformation portfolio and other departmental programmes without prior Treasury consent. Nothing in this letter constitutes approval of individual programme business cases, which remain subject to existing approval processes."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "The department may spend £8 million of the settlement on contractors if consultancy spend is zero."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The cap is a single combined limit on "consultancy and contractor resource" together; £8 million on contractors alone breaches it regardless of consultancy spend. This tests reading combined-versus-separate limits precisely — a candidate planning workforce mix against this letter needs the combined reading, since a contractor-heavy plan assuming separate caps would fail at the first Treasury checkpoint.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "If the portfolio misses an Annex B milestone, year-two funding will be withheld."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Milestone achievement is a condition of release, but the letter never states the consequence of missing it — automatic withholding is one possibility among several. This tests distinguishing "a condition exists" from "a specified consequence follows" — the accurate briefing says release is conditional and the consequence of a miss is unspecified and would be negotiated.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "The Treasury has approved the expansion of the department's business analysis function."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The Treasury "notes the department's intention" and "expects this to be reflected in the capability plan" — noting and expecting are not approving, and the letter's closing sentence explicitly withholds approving force. This tests matching a briefing's verbs to the document's verbs exactly — noted is not approved, and expected is not funded.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following is best supported by the passage?

- A) The Treasury regards growing internal capability as a means of reducing dependence on the capped external resource.
- B) The Treasury believes the department's business cases are weak.
- C) The department's other programmes are forbidden from using consultants.
- D) The year-one review will be conducted by the Treasury itself.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The letter caps external resource while conditioning funding on growing internal capability and explicitly links this to the same functions — a coherent policy whose connecting purpose is a modest, supported inference. B converts a routine jurisdictional clause into an unstated quality judgement; C misapplies the cap's scope; D invents an actor. This tests inferring purpose from a document's deliberate structure while never inferring facts it does not contain.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

Which one-paragraph briefing for directorate leadership is best?

- A) "Good news: £42m secured over three years, and the Treasury has backed our function's expansion."
- B) "We have £42m over three years, but with a £6m combined cap on consultancy and contractors, milestone-conditional release after year one, a required capability plan in which our function's expansion must credibly feature, no virement without Treasury consent — and individual business cases still need approval as before. Practical implications: workforce growth must be permanent-heavy, year-one milestones are now portfolio-critical, and our capability plan becomes a funding document."
- C) "The settlement letter is broadly positive; details available on request."
- D) "£42m over three years. Note that the Treasury has capped our contractor spend at £6m per year."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B reproduces every operative element and translates each into a consequence. A repeats the approval error celebratorily; C abdicates comprehension; D corrupts the cap's basis (rendering a three-year combined cap as an annual, contractor-only one), a silent expansion that would surface as a real problem in year three. This tests completeness, faithfulness, and consequence-translation together.

*Passage 2 (for Questions 6–8)*

"Following the efficiency review, I am asking every head of profession to identify options for a 10% reduction in their function's running costs by the end of the month. I want to be clear about what this exercise is and is not. It is a request for costed options with impacts stated; it is not a decision to implement any of them. No option should assume recruitment freezes across the board — I would rather see targeted choices than uniform restraint. Options that trade short-term savings for larger long-term costs should be identified as such, not excluded. Where a function believes a reduction of this scale would compromise a statutory obligation or create unacceptable risk, the head of profession should say so explicitly in their return, with evidence. I expect returns to be honest rather than tactical, and I will treat them accordingly."

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comprehension**

What has the permanent secretary asked heads of profession to deliver?

- A) Implementation of a 10% reduction in running costs by month end.
- B) Costed options for a 10% reduction, with impacts stated — including, where believed, evidenced statements that the scale would compromise statutory obligations or create unacceptable risk — by month end, with no decision yet taken.
- C) A defence of current budgets against any reduction.
- D) A uniform recruitment freeze pending decisions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The letter is explicit about its own status — an options request, not an implementation instruction — with a built-in channel for evidenced risk statements. Option A converts an options exercise into an implementation instruction, the misreading the letter pre-empts; D asserts what the letter expressly discourages. This tests reading a tasking letter for deliverable, deadline, status and channel.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inference**

What can most safely be inferred about the permanent secretary's expectations of the returns?

- A) They anticipate that some functions may submit inflated impact claims to protect their budgets, and are signalling that such tactics will be recognised.
- B) They have already decided which functions will bear the reductions.
- C) They believe all functions can absorb 10% without harm.
- D) They will exempt any function that claims a statutory obligation.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "I expect returns to be honest rather than tactical, and I will treat them accordingly" names tactical returns as a category and warns against them. Option C is contradicted by the letter's own statutory-risk provision; D over-reads a dissent channel into an exemption rule with no such promise. This tests inference from the language of a pre-emptive warning, decisive for how a candidate should compose their own return.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

A return must flag that a 10% reduction would compromise assurance of legally significant casework analysis. Which formulation best matches the letter's request?

- A) "A 10% reduction is impossible for this function and we decline to model it."
- B) "We have modelled the requested options (Annex A). Option 2 reaches 10% but would remove independent review of analysis supporting legally significant casework decisions — the control that caught 14 defects last year, three in cases that proceeded to litigation. We assess this as creating unacceptable legal risk, evidenced in Annex B, and recommend capping this function's reduction at 6% (Option 1) unless the risk is explicitly accepted at board level."
- C) "Any reduction would compromise our statutory obligations."
- D) "We can achieve 10% with no material impact."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B provides costed options including the full 10% option, states the specific control lost with evidenced impact, and routes the residual decision to the level that owns the risk — exactly what "honest rather than tactical" looks like. A refuses the commission entirely; C is the unevidenced universal claim the letter warned would read as tactical; D is dishonest in the optimistic direction. This tests mapping a request's every requirement onto a compliant response.

*Passage 3 (for Questions 9–12)*

"This standard applies to analysis that materially informs a decision of a public body. Analysis materially informs a decision if the decision maker relies on it, or could reasonably be expected to rely on it, in reaching the decision. All such analysis must have a named analytical owner. Analysis rated business-critical must additionally undergo independent assurance proportionate to the decision's impact and reversibility; departments must maintain a register of business-critical analysis. The standard does not define 'business-critical': departments must publish their own definitions, which must at minimum capture analysis where error could lead to significant financial loss, legal challenge, or harm to individuals. Departments may exceed this standard. Departures below the standard must be approved by the department's analysis governance board and recorded, with rationale, in the departmental register. This standard takes effect for new analysis from 1 April; existing analysis must be brought into compliance within two years."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: "Analysis that a decision maker did not actually rely on can still fall within the standard's scope."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The scope test is disjunctive — "relies on it, or could reasonably be expected to rely on it" — so analysis meeting the second limb is in scope even without actual reliance. This tests reading a disjunction limb by limb and understanding its purpose: making scope determinable in advance is what an assurance regime requires.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A department whose definition of business-critical analysis omits legal challenge as a criterion is compliant, provided its governance board approved the definition."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The standard sets an unqualified minimum content for departmental definitions, and the board-approval mechanism applies to departures from the standard, not to setting the minimum content of a definition below it. This tests the layered reading of floor provisions and exception mechanisms — exactly what each mechanism can and cannot excuse.

**Question 11 (hard) — Applying the standard to a scenario**

On 1 June (after the standard takes effect), a benefits model built three years ago, updated quarterly, used by the investment board to approve programme funding, has a named owner but has never had independent assurance. The department's published definition rates analysis informing investment decisions above £10m as business-critical; the model informs decisions well above that. Under the standard, which statement is most accurate?

- A) The model is non-compliant and its use must cease immediately.
- B) The model is out of the standard's scope because it predates 1 April.
- C) The model falls within scope (it materially informs decisions), is business-critical under the department's definition (so requires proportionate independent assurance and registration), and as existing analysis it benefits from the two-year transition — so continued use is lawful under the standard while it is brought into compliance, though its unassured status on decisions of this size argues for prioritising it early in the transition plan.
- D) The quarterly updates make it "new analysis" from each update, so it required full compliance from 1 April.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Clause-by-clause: in scope (relied on by the board), business-critical (above the £10m threshold), and existing analysis benefiting from the two-year transition — a state that is not yet a breach. A ignores the transition provision; B misreads it as an exemption; D's reading would destroy the transition provision's purpose for any maintained model. This tests applying a multi-clause standard sequentially, ending in a prioritisation judgement about the risk this weight of decision carries.

**Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement in a consultation response**

A consultation response wants the standard to define "proportionate" independent assurance more precisely. Which drafting proposal is strongest?

- A) "Proportionality should be left to professional judgement, as now."
- B) "Replace 'proportionate to the decision's impact and reversibility' with: 'Assurance intensity must be determined by the department's published assurance matrix, which must map at least three tiers of assurance activity against decision impact and reversibility, and must specify the minimum tier for business-critical analysis. Where the matrix's application to a specific analysis is unclear, the analysis governance board determines the tier and records the rationale.'"
- C) "All business-critical analysis should receive maximum assurance regardless of proportionality."
- D) "The word 'proportionate' is too vague and should be deleted."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B keeps proportionality's substance while relocating it to a published, auditable instrument with floor content and a residual-ambiguity route. A defends the status quo the consultation exists to improve; C abolishes proportionality in the name of rigour, risking a standard that is quietly ignored; D removes the word without replacing its function. This tests repairing a vague standard word with a determinable instrument rather than deletion, maximalism, or trust.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate matches a briefing's verbs to the document's verbs exactly** — "noted" is not "approved".
- **Watch for whether a candidate checks a limit's structure** — combined or separate, per year or total, floor or ceiling — before applying it.
- **Note whether a candidate traces a condition to its stated consequence and stops there**, writing "consequence unspecified" as a finding rather than inventing one.
- **Note whether a candidate applies a purpose-check to a reading** — a construction that makes a provision pointless is almost never the intended one.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and expect a candidate to restate a governance paragraph as who-may-do-what before answering.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that upgrades "noted" to "approved".**
- **Missing when a candidate splits a combined cap into separate allowances.**
- **Crediting an answer that invents a consequence for an unspecified condition.**
- **Missing when a candidate reads a dissent or exception channel as a guarantee of success.**
- **Rewarding a reading that destroys a provision's evident purpose** — a transition period, exception mechanism or minimum-content rule read into meaninglessness.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks a candidate to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. There is rarely a provably correct action; responses are scored against a behavioural key built with experienced practitioners — for a head-of-function role, behaviours such as strategic influence, integrity under political pressure, capability stewardship, proportionate governance, inclusion, and care for the profession beyond the candidate's own organisation.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 25 to 45 minutes with 8 to 15 scenarios. Response formats include choosing the most effective response, the most and least effective, ranking all responses, or rating each independently. For senior UK government digital roles, the key is strongly informed by the Service Standard's user-centred ethos, Civil Service values, and the leadership behaviours expected of heads of profession.

This matters for a head of business analysis because the SJT is the closest thing psychometrics offers to a day in this job. At this level, every choice is also a precedent — the function watches how pressure is handled and learns what the real standards are, whatever the written ones say.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Strategic influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Consultancy**: scenarios test whether a candidate influences and negotiates with the most senior stakeholders — protecting evidence, relationships and progress simultaneously, and knowing which to spend when they conflict.
- **Evidence integrity as organisational infrastructure** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Business process improvement**: scenarios probe whether the analysis a function signs stays honest when boards, ministers' offices or spending reviews need it warmer.
- **Building capability, not dependency** maps to organisational capability-building and **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements**: talent, succession, standards-adoption and function-design scenarios test whether a candidate grows systems that outlast them rather than heroics that require them.
- **Proportionate governance** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Testing (business analysis)**: setting and iterating standards — and deciding when they bend, with what record — is tested through exemption, assurance and crisis scenarios where both rigidity and capitulation are designed distractors.
- **User focus and inclusion at portfolio scale** map to **User experience analysis**, **Digital and data systems analysis** and **Business modelling**: scenarios include constituencies with no seat at governance tables and reward the candidate who structurally represents them.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — The director general and the inconvenient baseline**

Analysis of a flagship programme shows its benefits baseline is unachievable — a finding due at the programme board next week. The director general, the programme's sponsor, asks to "hold the finding back until after the spending review announcement in three weeks; the timing could cost us the settlement". What is the most effective response?

- A) Hold the finding back as asked; three weeks changes little and the settlement matters more.
- B) Publish the finding at the board regardless, without further discussion.
- C) Decline to suppress the finding, but work with the DG on its presentation: the board sees it on schedule with the corrective options the function proposes, framed as the programme self-correcting — and offer to help prepare lines that show the Treasury a programme that finds and fixes its own problems.
- D) Tell the DG it will be held back, then brief the board chair privately.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The request has a legitimate core (settlement risk) wrapped around an illegitimate ask (suppressing a finding from its governance board). Option C refuses the suppression while taking the concern seriously and offers a genuinely better strategy than concealment. A makes the function complicit and re-prices every future finding's timing; D is a lie plus a back-channel. This tests protecting governance without humiliating the sponsor, converting a refusal into an alliance.

**Question 2 (easy) — The exemption precedent**

A crisis programme with genuine statutory deadlines requests exemption from an analysis assurance standard. A deputy recommends granting it informally — "just this once, no paperwork, it sets no precedent if it isn't written down". What is the most effective response?

- A) Grant the exemption informally as recommended; discretion avoids precedent.
- B) Refuse any exemption; the standard exists precisely for pressure like this.
- C) Grant a formal, recorded exemption: scoped to the statutory-deadline work, time-boxed, with the minimum assurance that still protects the legally significant elements, the rationale documented, and a review date — and correct the deputy's theory: unrecorded exemptions set the strongest precedents, because they teach people that the register is optional.
- D) Escalate the decision to the digital director to avoid owning the precedent.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Written, scoped, time-boxed exemptions create a governed exception path; unwritten ones are invisible to review and infinitely citable precisely because deniable. C grants proportionate relief, protects what cannot be relieved, and coaches the deputy in the moment. A buys ease at the register's expense; B treats the standard's letter as its purpose; D delegates upward a decision squarely within the role's authority. This tests proportionate governance combined with record-keeping as protection.

**Question 3 (easy) — The vanishing pipeline**

Workforce data shows no hires below senior level for three years, every vacancy filled with experienced contractors or senior permanent hires "because delivery pressure never left room to grow juniors". Leads privately agree the function has become excellent, expensive and demographically narrow, with no pipeline. What is the most effective response?

- A) Continue the senior-heavy model; it delivers, and juniors are a luxury for calmer times.
- B) Commit the function to a rebuilt pipeline as a strategic change: ring-fenced junior and apprenticeship posts in the workforce plan, mentoring loads built into seniors' objectives, entry routes designed to widen the intake beyond the usual sources, and the delivery cost of supervision priced honestly into engagement planning — presented to the board as the cost of the function existing in ten years.
- C) Convert two contractor posts to junior posts next quarter and see how it goes.
- D) Ask HR to run a graduate recruitment campaign.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three years of senior-only hiring is a strategic decision made by default. B treats the correction structurally (ring-fenced posts survive the next delivery squeeze), systemically (mentoring built into objectives), inclusively (entry routes widen actual intake), and honestly (supervision cost priced in). A eats the future the role exists to build; C is an unprotected gesture; D outsources design decisions that belong to the head of function. This tests treating workforce shape as strategy rather than a hiring habit.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two programmes, one analyst, and the truth between them**

Programme X and Programme Y are in open conflict over a shared platform's requirements, escalating toward the portfolio board. A senior analyst embedded in X discloses in confidence that X's leadership is knowingly overstating user-volume forecasts to win the argument, having seen the internal numbers. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing with the information; it was shared in confidence and embedding depends on trust.
- B) Pass the internal numbers to Programme Y so the argument is fair.
- C) Have the function formally validate both programmes' forecasts ahead of the portfolio board — a legitimate step for any escalated requirements dispute over shared infrastructure — so the overstatement surfaces through independent analysis rather than through the analyst, who is protected by never being cited; and separately, be clear with her that she was right to bring it forward.
- D) Report Programme X's leadership to the portfolio board for misconduct on the strength of her account.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three obligations collide — the decision must not rest on falsified forecasts, the analyst must be protected, and X's leadership deserves due process. C institutes independent validation as standard practice for any escalated shared-infrastructure dispute, surfacing the truth through methodology rather than testimony, and reinforces the analyst's confidence with a direct affirmation. A abandons the decision to known-bad evidence; B turns the head of function into a rival's intelligence source; D convicts on a private account. This tests protecting sources while acting on substance.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The methodology mandate from above**

A government-wide mandate requires all departments to adopt a standardised discovery framework that two instrumented pilots show works poorly for complex casework services. Colleagues across government are watching the response; a central-department counterpart who owns the mandate has publicly called early criticism "resistance to consistency". What is the most effective response?

- A) Adopt fully and silently; the mandate is decided and the counterpart is defensive.
- B) Adopt the framework in good faith across the portfolio while sharing the pilot evidence privately with the counterpart first — offered as implementation learning, with proposed amendments for casework contexts — then through the framework's formal review channel with the counterpart's knowledge; and tell the wider community exactly what is being done and why: complying, evidencing, and proposing improvement through the front door.
- C) Publicly present the pilot evidence at the next cross-government event to correct the record on "resistance to consistency".
- D) Adopt the framework nominally while quietly telling casework programmes to keep their existing approach underneath compliance-coloured reporting.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The counterpart's public defensiveness means evidence experienced as an attack will be fought, not weighed. B sequences private engagement first, formal channel second, and full transparency with the wider community third — comply, evidence, propose through the front door. A wastes the only instrumented evidence available; C wins the room and entrenches the defensiveness blocking amendment; D is institutional dishonesty that, once audited, ends standing to enforce any standard. This tests evidence-based dissent sequenced for adoption rather than confrontation or concealment.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The failing peer**

A fellow head of profession's function produces weak requirements work that a candidate's own analysts keep absorbing rework from — three programmes this quarter. The peer is respected and defensive; the portfolio director has begun asking pointed questions about "where all this rework comes from". What is the most effective response?

- A) Answer the portfolio director's question fully and first; the data is the data.
- B) Take the evidence to the peer privately before anyone else: the three programmes, the rework pattern, offered as one professional to another — with an offer of practical help (joint review standards, shared training, a loaned senior) — and agree how the pattern gets addressed and represented to the portfolio director, while being clear the pattern will not be misrepresented if raised directly.
- C) Say nothing to anyone; the other function is not this role's jurisdiction.
- D) Raise it at the heads-of-profession forum so the correction is collective rather than personal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is a peer-stewardship problem wearing a data problem's clothes. B goes to the person who can fix it, first and privately, with offered help that addresses the cause rather than assigns blame, and preserves honesty if asked directly. A converts a fixable professional problem into a political one first; C confuses jurisdiction with responsibility, since the rework lands on programmes served by this function; D is a public ambush with witnesses. This tests peer stewardship and cause-fixing over blame-fixing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inclusion versus the delivery narrative**

An organisation's transformation story — repeated by executives — is "loved by users, 92% satisfaction". Deeper analysis shows the 92% comes from post-completion surveys of digital-channel users only; assisted-channel users (18% of the base, skewed old, disabled and poor) were never surveyed, and their proxy indicators are worsening. Comms is about to publish the figure externally in a case study the chief executive will front. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let publication proceed; the figure is technically true and the case study is already written.
- B) Brief the chief executive's office and comms urgently: the figure as framed will not survive scrutiny (state the survey's actual base), the excluded 18% show worsening indicators, and provide corrected framing options — publish the digital-channel figure accurately labelled, alongside a stated commitment on assisted-channel improvement — so the story becomes honest without becoming a retraction.
- C) Contact journalists likely to cover the case study and correct the record directly.
- D) Publish the function's fuller analysis internally the same week, letting the contradiction speak for itself.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The figure is accurate within an unstated base, deployed to describe a population it excludes. B briefs the accountable principals, gives accurate labelling that preserves most of the story, and converts the excluded 18%'s worsening indicators into a stated executive priority. A lets leadership walk into a misleading claim; C is a loyalty and process breach; D stages an internal ambush without briefing first. This tests protecting principals from their own narratives through the people who can act.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The board seat**

A reorganisation proposal would move a function's reporting line from direct attendance at the digital and data board to reporting through a new chief delivery officer who "will represent analysis interests". Colleagues advise fighting for the seat as a matter of status. What is the most effective response?

- A) Fight the change on status grounds; heads of profession belong at the board.
- B) Accept the change gracefully; structures matter less than relationships.
- C) Make the case in functional, not status, terms: identify the specific board decisions where analysis evidence changed the outcome in the last year, the assurance and integrity questions that require a professional voice independent of delivery interests, and propose structural safeguards if the line moves anyway — a standing evidence slot, direct escalation rights on integrity issues, and attendance for business-critical analysis items.
- D) Accept the line change but negotiate a personal invitation to attend meetings informally.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The proposal's flaw is structural: routing evidence through delivery leadership filters exactly the inconvenient messages that matter most. C argues functionally with evidence and comes with fallback safeguards that preserve substance even if the org chart moves. A fights on the weakest available ground; B mistakes graciousness for stewardship; D personalises a safeguard that evaporates with the postholder. This tests arguing from function rather than status and negotiating structural, durable safeguards.

**Question 9 (hard) — Most and least effective: the spending review squeeze**

A function must lose 15% of its budget. The least-harmful path cuts two vacant posts, ends the contractor premium through conversions, and withdraws embedded analysts from four low-value programmes whose directors are vocal and well-connected. The alternative, favoured by the candidate's director because it is quieter, spreads the cut evenly — every programme keeps its analyst, every analyst loses training, community time and assurance backup. Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses.

- A) Implement the least-harmful path: present the evidence for concentrating the cut, absorb the four directors' anger by meeting each personally with the prioritisation rationale and a defined re-entry route, and protect the function's training, standards and assurance core as non-negotiable infrastructure.
- B) Implement the even spread the director favours; political capital matters more than operating model purity.
- C) Implement the least-harmful path but let the four directors hear it through the reorganisation announcement rather than directly.
- D) Delay the decision by commissioning further analysis of the options.

**Correct answer: Most effective A; least effective B**

**Explanation:** The even spread hollows out training, community and assurance — the infrastructure that turns individuals into a function — while keeping every flag on the map, decaying invisibly until escaped defects reveal it. A takes the harder, honest path with personal delivery of bad news and a re-entry route that converts withdrawal into prioritisation rather than rejection. C is A minus the courage, with a real cost — directors informed by announcement become permanent enemies. D spends money the analysis has already found is not there. This tests evidence-led hard choices that protect capability infrastructure over political comfort.

**Question 10 (hard) — The whistleblower and the flagship**

A junior analyst, two years in post and three levels below the head of function, uses an open-door route to report that a flagship programme's benefits reporting — signed by her lead and presented by her director — double-counts a major saving across two workstreams. She has raised it twice in her team and been told "the model has been through governance". She is visibly frightened. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank her, verify the claim through the reporting chain — asking her lead and director to walk through the model.
- B) Thank her and make three commitments then kept: the substance will be independently checked (a targeted model review by an analyst outside that programme's chain, on a routine-assurance footing that does not identify a trigger); she will hear back personally on the outcome either way; and any concern about how her raising it is treated comes straight back. Then, if confirmed, treat it as two findings: the model error, and the team culture that answered a specific technical challenge with "it's been through governance" twice.
- C) Send her back to raise it through her line management, the correct route, with encouragement.
- D) Immediately inform the programme director that an allegation has been made about the benefits model and ask him to investigate.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A routine-assurance footing protects her identity; independence from the signing chain protects the check itself; the personal-outcome commitment and retaliation channel are what "open door" means if it means anything; the two-findings framing captures both the error and the culture defect that will generate the next one. A and D route verification through the people who signed the model; C sends her back to a route that has already failed her twice. This tests protecting a discloser structurally rather than rhetorically.

**Question 11 (hard) — The definition of done for a candidate's own reform**

Eighteen months into a flagship engagement-model reform (triage criteria, tiered assurance, quality metrics), a board sponsor calls it a success and wants it showcased cross-government. A candid review finds triage works well; tiered assurance works where adopted but a third of programmes negotiated bespoke arrangements; quality metrics exist but nobody uses them for decisions. What is the most effective response?

- A) Showcase the reform as the sponsor wishes; momentum and reputation help the function, and the gaps are normal implementation noise.
- B) Decline the showcase until all three components are fully working.
- C) Showcase it honestly as a partial success: present triage as proven practice others can adopt now, the bespoke-arrangement pattern as a live finding about what drives assurance adoption (with countermeasures), and the unused metrics as an open problem being worked — and tell the sponsor before the event that this is the version being presented, and why it serves them better than the polished one.
- D) Quietly fix the two weak components first and showcase in a year.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests applying a function's own evidential standards to its own story under a sponsor's flattering pressure. C showcases and tells the truth together: proven practice travels, live findings teach, open problems recruit help, and the pre-brief protects the sponsor relationship. A spends evidential credibility on the one story it can least afford to spend it on; B and D confuse honesty with perfectionism, and D adds a concealment flavour to the delay. This tests reflexive self-application of the standards a candidate would demand of anyone else's claims.

**Question 12 (hard) — Most and least effective: the merger of functions**

A machinery-of-government change merges a department with a smaller one whose 9-analyst function has different standards, older methodology, lower average grades, and a head who is now deputy-designate, having applied unsuccessfully for the head of function role. The executive wants "one function, one way of working, within six months". Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses.

- A) Extend the existing standards and methodology to the incoming function on day one; the larger function's are newer and six months is short.
- B) Run a structured convergence: joint review of both functions' standards against evidence (the existing standards are probably better, but "probably" is a hypothesis — the older methodology may embed domain knowledge the newer one lacks); mixed-team engagements from month one so integration happens through work rather than announcements; the deputy-designate given genuine ownership of a substantial workstream — the convergence design itself — with visible backing; and the six-month milestone defined as "one standards framework, agreed and in use" rather than "everyone works like us".
- C) Let both functions keep their ways of working and revisit in a year once the dust settles.
- D) Ask the executive to extend the timeline to eighteen months before starting.

**Correct answer: Most effective B; least effective A**

**Explanation:** A is annexation dressed as efficiency — the incoming nine learn on day one that merger means erasure, the bruised deputy-designate becomes the natural leader of a resentful faction, and whatever the older methodology did better is discarded unexamined. B lets evidence decide the standards question visibly, integrates through shared work, and converts the most dangerous potential opponent into the merger's co-author through genuine ownership. C fails the executive's legitimate requirement and lets two cultures harden; D spends credibility asking to be excused from the first test before attempting it. This tests evidence over incumbency and converting rivals into owners.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate treats every response as a precedent** — asking what the function would learn if this became known as "how the head handles it".
- **Watch for whether a candidate finds the option that converts an opponent or a defensive counterpart into an owner** rather than only confronting or conceding.
- **Note whether a candidate protects a discloser or a junior colleague structurally** (routine footings, independent channels, closed loops) rather than only through warm words.
- **Note whether a candidate sequences influence for adoption** — private before public, evidence before argument, the owner's credit before their own.
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding sophisticated dishonesty** — technically-true figures, compliance-coloured reporting, tailored versions for different audiences.
- **Rewarding rigidity dressed as principle** — refusing all exemptions, declining every showcase until perfection, fighting every structural change.
- **Crediting an answer that routes verification through the interested chain** — asking the signatories of a claim to investigate themselves.
- **Rewarding a status-based defence** of a board seat, mandate or reporting line where a functional argument was available and stronger.
- **Missing when a candidate chooses the quiet option** — even spreads, unannounced withdrawals, decisions delivered by memo — that minimises this month's conflict at the cost of next year's damage.

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a head of business analysis: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested stress-testing standards libraries, delegation schemes and assurance regimes for the silences and incentives that undo them. The situational judgement section weighed the leadership dilemmas that fill a head of profession's calendar — sponsors who want findings delayed, exemptions that set precedents, whistleblowers who need structural protection, mergers that test whether a function's own ways of working are standards or merely habits.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can reason precisely at organisational scale, handle numbers that arrive pre-packaged and must survive scrutiny, read governing documents exactly, and exercise the leadership judgement that builds a function and a profession that outlast any one postholder — never the whole of that decision.
