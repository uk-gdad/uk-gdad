# Lead Product Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for lead product manager. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are standardised exercises that measure the cognitive capabilities and behavioural tendencies predictive of performance in a role. At lead level, the versions worth administering are job-specific: they draw on materials the role genuinely owns — portfolio roadmaps, programme dependencies, business cases, cross-team capacity plans, stakeholder strategies, community and coaching commitments — and the decisions it genuinely makes: shaping strategy across a product portfolio or end-to-end service, directing stakeholder relationships, predicting and mitigating life cycle risks before they land, and providing product leadership across multiple teams as a member of a product area's management team.

This matters at this level for three reasons. Consequence: reasoning operates at portfolio scale, where a misread trend or a mis-audited benefit figure propagates across teams and quarters rather than sprints. Leadership: the role coaches and manages product managers, contributes to assessing other teams, and is involved in recruiting at all levels — reading other people's reasoning for a living, and interpreting assessment evidence about candidates. Example: leaders who visibly sharpen their own fundamentals earn the standing to ask it of others.

Each of the four sections follows the same pattern: an overview of what it measures and why it matters at this level; how its dimensions map to the role's named skills; graduated practice questions with complete worked explanations tying the technique back to portfolio-level product work; then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. At lead level, distractors are calibrated to catch responses that were correct one or two levels down — doing the work personally, absorbing turbulence silently — as failures of delegation or governance at this level. Score for whether a candidate's response routes decisions through the right structures and people, corrects collective pictures in the collective room, and treats every decision as also a precedent and a piece of someone's development. Use each section's per-item timing as a guide for planning a session, and debrief against the explanations below.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes this role demands, using realistic material rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level that material is portfolio-shaped: multi-team programme plans with interacting constraints, portfolio risk registers, high-level roadmaps under competing pressures, assessment reports from other teams being guided, and strategy artefacts whose internal consistency this role is accountable for.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving, scored objectively against a managerial or senior professional norm group, with adaptive difficulty common.

Relevance at this level lies in three shifts from earlier roles: scale (constraint propagation over more moving parts with longer feedback loops), prediction (life cycle risk work explicitly includes predicting, preventing and mitigating before materialisation — pattern recognition pointed at the future), and guidance (contributing to other teams' assessments means much cognitive work is second-order — checking others' reasoning and articulating why an inference fails clearly enough that its author improves).

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Life cycle management** and **Managing product outcomes**: reading weak signals across a portfolio before they become incidents.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic ownership** and **Life cycle management**: valid inference from conditions, standards and evidence, with unknowns marked as unknowns.
- **Error checking** maps to the duties to **contribute to the assessment of other teams** and **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: reviewing others' artefacts for internal inconsistency.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Product management** and **Creating value for money**: portfolio ranking problems under interacting constraints.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Product leadership** and **Agile and Lean practices**: decomposing cross-team, politically loaded problems into testable parts.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Weak signals across a portfolio**

Across five teams this month: Team One's cycle time rose 15%; Team Two lost its user researcher to a leave of absence with no cover; Team Three's last two retrospectives were cancelled; Team Four shipped on schedule; Team Five's product manager escalated twice for decisions they would normally take themselves. Ask for the reading that best serves a lead responsible for predicting and preventing risk.

- A) Only Team One's metric matters — the rest is soft information.
- B) Four of five teams show early-warning signals of different kinds — a capability gap, eroding reflective practice, rising cycle time, and decision confidence dropping — each cheap to probe now and expensive to discover later; schedule brief check-ins with those four this week.
- C) Team Four is fine, so the portfolio is fundamentally healthy.
- D) Wait a month to see whether the signals persist before doing anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Each of four flagged items is a known precursor of a different kind, and each is cheap to check. Option A restricts evidence to the quantified, when soft information is where much portfolio risk lives first; option C commits the healthy-instance fallacy; option D converts early warning into late confirmation, spending the exact advantage weak signals exist to give. This tests calibrated, proportionate response to weak signals — brief check-ins, not overreaction — reflecting the ordering predict, prevent, mitigate, where each verb is cheaper than its successor.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an assessment standard**

An assessment standard states: "A service must demonstrate iteration in response to user research findings. Demonstrating iteration requires showing at least one substantive change made because of research, with the evidence trail from finding to change." A team has extensive research and many findings, but every design decision predated the research, which was then used to confirm the choices. Ask which conclusion follows.

- A) The team meets the standard — they have research and they have changes.
- B) The team does not currently meet the standard as stated: they can show findings but no change made because of a finding, so the required evidence trail cannot exist.
- C) The team meets the standard if the research was high quality.
- D) The standard is unreasonable and the assessors will waive it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The standard requires a causal relation (change because of finding), not merely both ingredients. Since decisions predated research, the causal direction runs the wrong way, and no evidence trail from finding to change can exist regardless of research quality (option C's red herring). Option A pattern-matches on ingredients while missing the required relation. This tests reading a standard for its causal or sequential requirement, not just its ingredient list — precisely what guiding other teams before their assessment requires.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a high-level roadmap**

A portfolio roadmap contains: (1) "Q2: Retire legacy booking system." (2) "Q3: Migrate remaining booking users to new platform." (3) "Q2–Q3: New platform accessibility audit and remediation." (4) "Q4: Decommission legacy data store (depends on booking retirement complete)." Ask which inconsistency most needs fixing before the meeting.

- A) Entry 4 should be in Q3.
- B) Entries 1 and 2 are out of order: the roadmap retires the legacy system a quarter before migrating its remaining users off it.
- C) Entry 3 spans two quarters, which roadmaps should not allow.
- D) There is no inconsistency.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Retiring the legacy system in Q2 while its remaining users migrate in Q3 leaves those users a quarter with neither system — sequencing must invert. Entry 4's Q4 slot is fine once retirement moves; multi-quarter entries (option C) are normal at portfolio altitude. This tests a pairwise-consistency review — asking of every pair of entries "can both be true in this order?" — the fastest high-yield roadmap review technique, since each entry read alone looks individually plausible.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation across portfolio demands**

Monday morning: (1) a quarterly reprioritisation paper is due for Wednesday's programme board, half a day's work; (2) a coached product manager has asked for a two-hour evidence-pack review before Friday's service assessment, and is anxious; (3) the head of product wants input to a community session next month; (4) a team's live service has degraded overnight (error rates tripled), the team investigating and not yet asking for help. Ask for the most defensible sequencing.

- A) 4 (confirm incident ownership and escalation route, ~30 minutes), 1, 2, 3
- B) 1, 2, 4, 3 — governance papers outrank operational noise
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3 — people being coached come first
- D) 4 (personally lead the incident until resolved), then whatever remains

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option B ranks a live user-facing degradation below paperwork; D personally commandeers an incident a competent team is already investigating, subtracting their ownership. Option A calibrates: a brief check that the incident has an owner and an escalation trigger, then trust the team, remaining interruptible — assurance of the response, not performance of it. This tests separating a two-minute triage response from the work itself across every demand, and impact-weighted sequencing thereafter.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Constraint reasoning across a programme**

Three deliverables feed a month-6 milestone: payments (P), notifications (N), reporting (R). Constraints: (i) P requires a security assessment bookable only in month 3 or 5, taking the full month, build complete beforehand; (ii) P's build takes 2 months; (iii) N depends on P's assessed build and takes 2 months; (iv) R can be built any time but its final month must overlap N's final month; (v) R takes 2 months. Ask, sequencing as early as possible, in which months N runs and whether the milestone is met.

- A) N runs months 4–5; milestone met with a month to spare
- B) N runs months 4–5; milestone missed
- C) N runs months 5–6; milestone met exactly
- D) The milestone cannot be met

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** P's build months 1–2; earliest assessment slot is month 3 (occupying it); N starts month 4, runs 4–5. R must overlap N's final month (5), so R runs 4–5 too. All complete by end of month 5, a month ahead of the month-6 milestone. This tests propagating a dependency chain and then, in a strong answer, identifying the binding constraint whose failure collapses the plan — the month-3 assessment slot — and recommending it be defended disproportionately, since missing it pushes the whole chain past the milestone.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Error checking another team's outcome claims**

A team's quarterly report states: "Our onboarding improvements succeeded: activation rose from 55% to 61%. We define activation as completing setup within 14 days. Note: this quarter we also began excluding accounts created by internal testers, who previously depressed the figure." Ask for the most important review finding.

- A) The improvement is confirmed: 61% exceeds 55%.
- B) The comparison is confounded: the definition's population changed in the same quarter (excluding testers, which the team itself notes previously depressed the figure), so an unknown share of the 6-point rise is definitional; the team should restate last quarter's figure on the new basis before claiming any effect.
- C) Activation is the wrong metric and the report should use satisfaction.
- D) The exclusion of testers is illegitimate and should be reversed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The population changed in the same measurement quarter, so 55% and 61% compare different things — the rise could be mostly real, mostly definitional, or a mixture. Option D overcorrects, since excluding testers is usually the right choice; the flaw is the unbridged transition. This tests recognising a metric-definition-and-population change in the same period as reversing the burden of proof, and the coaching move — restate the baseline on the new basis — that resolves it cheaply.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Choosing the delivery approach, not just the plan**

A team building a data-integration product with hard external dependencies (three departments' release schedules) keeps "failing" its two-week sprints because dependency slips invalidate plans mid-cycle; morale is sagging; the delivery manager proposes doubling sprint length to four weeks. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Approve four-week sprints — longer cycles absorb slips better.
- B) Insist on standard two-week Scrum — consistency across the portfolio matters more than local fit.
- C) Diagnose the mismatch first: the team's work is dependency-driven flow, not batch-plannable scope, so trial a flow-based approach — pull-based work intake, work-in-progress limits, explicit dependency-wait states made visible, cadenced (not scope-committed) reviews — evaluate against cycle time and team health after six weeks, and share what is learned across the portfolio.
- D) Escalate the three departments' slips to programme governance and keep the process unchanged.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Four-week sprints (option A) halve the frequency of plan invalidation without addressing its cause — the team's work is flow-shaped, not batch-shaped, since its inputs are external release schedules it does not control. Option C diagnoses correctly and trials a fitting method with evaluation criteria and a review date, sharing the learning portfolio-wide. Option B mistakes uniformity for discipline. This tests recognising when a team's persistent process "failure" is a fit problem rather than an execution problem, and tailoring the approach as an experiment rather than a decree.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading converging metrics across products**

Three products sharing one platform identity component (which released a "hardening upgrade" four months ago): Product A's completion rate drifted 84→82→80→79 over four months; Product B's support contacts per 1,000 transactions rose 61→64→70→75; Product C's satisfaction is stable but comment themes shifted toward "takes longer than it used to". Ask for the strongest reading.

- A) Three unrelated product-level issues; assign each team to investigate its own metric.
- B) The three signals are consistent with a common cause degrading a shared dependency — the identity component, whose hardening upgrade coincides with all three drifts; commission a joint investigation measuring identity-step timing and failure rates across all three products before team-level fixes.
- C) Product A's decline is the priority since completion is the most important metric.
- D) Satisfaction is stable on C, so nothing systemic is occurring.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three different metrics drifting in the same direction over the same window, across products sharing one dependency whose change coincides with onset, is a hypothesis worth measuring before rollback. Option A sends three teams to investigate locally, unable to find a cause outside their own product; D misreads C's stability, since satisfaction is lagging and blunt while the comment-theme shift is the leading edge. This tests seeing a cross-product pattern invisible to any single-product view — a diagnosis only available from the portfolio position — and choosing measurement over immediate local fixes.

**Question 9 (moderate) — The business case that proves too much**

A case for expanding a document-checking feature argues: "The feature saved 12,000 caseworker hours last year. Rolling it out to the remaining six casework teams, which are collectively four times our size, will therefore save 48,000 hours annually." The originating team was selected for the pilot because its work was the most document-heavy. Ask for the most important challenge to raise.

- A) The arithmetic: 12,000 × 4 is 48,000, so the case is sound.
- B) Selection effect: the pilot team was chosen for maximal document-intensity, so per-team savings elsewhere will likely be materially lower; the case needs the remaining teams' actual document volumes, with the saving re-estimated per team from their workloads rather than scaled from the best case.
- C) The feature should be rebuilt before any expansion.
- D) Hours saved is not a real benefit unless posts are cut.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Document-checking savings scale with document volume, and a pilot selected as most document-heavy will project the maximum observed effect onto populations with less of the driving characteristic. Option A audits only the arithmetic and not the premise; D overclaims by dismissing capacity-releasing benefits entirely. This tests recognising pilot-selection bias and recommending re-estimation from actual per-team workload data rather than uniform scaling — a challenge, not a rejection, since the case may still hold at a lower figure.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Feasibility under interacting people and programme constraints**

Three concurrent workstreams need staffing for a quarter: a flagship migration (senior-capable PM, full-time); a ministerial-priority discovery (PM strong with senior stakeholders, half-time); BAU coverage for two products (1.0 FTE, splittable). Available: PM Anna (senior-capable, strong with stakeholders, full-time); PM Ben (mid-level, full-time, developing stakeholder skills, has asked for a stretch opportunity); PM Chloe (senior-capable, strong with stakeholders, but committed half-time to a cross-government community role). Ask which allocation is feasible and best serves delivery and development.

- A) Anna: migration full-time; Chloe: discovery with her free half; Ben: BAU full-time — meets every constraint, but gives Ben no stretch this quarter unless deliberately compensated.
- B) Anna: discovery half-time plus BAU half-time; Ben: migration full-time; Chloe: BAU with her free half.
- C) Ben: discovery half-time (his stretch) plus BAU half-time; Anna: migration; Chloe: unassigned beyond community work.
- D) Anna: migration; Ben: discovery half-time plus BAU half-time; Chloe: BAU with her free half — with Chloe mentoring Ben on the discovery's stakeholder work.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The migration needs full-time senior capability, which only Anna can supply (Chloe has only half her time); the discovery then needs Chloe's free half; BAU falls to Ben full-time. Option B puts mid-level Ben on a senior-requiring migration; C staffs a ministerial discovery with developing stakeholder skills against a "strong" requirement; D leaves the discovery under-qualified with mentoring as mitigation rather than satisfaction of the constraint. This tests solving a multi-constraint staffing problem for feasibility first, and — the lead-level content — naming and compensating the development cost the feasible answer imposes (Ben's unmet stretch request), rather than treating resource arithmetic as the whole answer.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The flaw in a portfolio-level argument**

A peer argues at a management team meeting: "Across government, the products that survived spending reviews all had quantified benefits cases. Our portfolio should therefore require every product to produce a quantified benefits case, because that is what makes products survive reviews." Ask for the sharpest critique.

- A) The argument is sound: the correlation is strong and the recommendation is prudent.
- B) The argument infers causation from survivor data without the comparison class: products that died may also have had quantified cases (we are not told), and survivors may share other traits — political salience, delivery track record — that drive both survival and the resources to produce polished cases; requiring cases universally may still be right, but on different grounds, and believing quantification causes survival invites gaming the number rather than the value.
- C) The argument is wrong because spending reviews are unpredictable and preparation is pointless.
- D) The argument is irrelevant because this portfolio faces no review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The survivorship framing is uninformative without the base rate among non-survivors, and confounding traits (salience, track record) plausibly drive both survival and case quality. Option B separates the broken inference from the recommendation, which may still be sound on different grounds — a distinction that changes minds rather than losing the room. This tests catching a survivorship argument and articulating why the wrongness matters — a belief that quantification causes survival invites gaming the number rather than optimising the value.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Strategic alignment with contradictory mandates**

Three mandates: a digital strategy ("consolidate onto shared platforms; no new bespoke transactional builds"); a ministerial commitment (a dedicated application service for a resettlement scheme, live within six months); accessibility regulations (non-negotiable). The platform team's assessment: the shared platform can host the journey but its form-builder needs four months of component work to meet two accessibility requirements; a bespoke build meets everything in five months; the six-month clock is running. Ask for what best serves strategic alignment.

- A) Build bespoke — the ministerial deadline and accessibility both bind, and the strategy must yield; proceed quietly since the conflict resolves itself.
- B) Refuse the bespoke route entirely — the digital strategy prohibits it, so the platform gap defines the timeline and the ministerial commitment must move.
- C) Take the conflict, with the platform team's evidence and a costed recommendation, to the strategy's governance: propose funding the platform's four-month accessibility component work now (serving the scheme at month five via the platform, strengthening the platform for every future service, and preserving all three mandates), with the bespoke route priced as the explicit fallback if component work slips past a named checkpoint.
- D) Deliver on the platform as-is by month six and schedule the accessibility gaps for remediation after launch.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Accessibility is non-negotiable, ruling out D immediately; the ministerial commitment is politically binding; the strategy is organisationally binding but governed, with an owner and amendment process both A and B ignore. Option C satisfies all three mandates with a costed, governed decision and a named-checkpoint fallback. This tests ranking mandates by their real bindingness and designing an option that satisfies the rankable ones while taking the flexible one through its own governance, rather than resolving the conflict privately in either direction.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes weak signals worth cheap probing from noise requiring no action.
- Note whether the candidate extracts the required relation (causal, sequential) from a standard or rule, not just its ingredients.
- Watch for whether the candidate reviews artefacts pairwise for consistency rather than entry-by-entry.
- Score for whether the candidate names and compensates the people-cost of a feasible resourcing solution.
- Note whether the candidate identifies survivorship or selection bias in an argument reaching the management team room.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer restricted to quantified evidence when qualitative or "soft" signals were equally diagnostic.
- Missing when a candidate reviews a roadmap or plan line-by-line and misses a pairwise inconsistency.
- Accepting correct arithmetic built on an unexamined premise, such as pilot-selection bias.
- Rewarding uniformity ("consistency across the portfolio") over genuine fit-for-work-shape reasoning.
- Missing when a candidate resolves a mandate conflict privately, in either direction, rather than through governance.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures accuracy and judgement with the numbers this role genuinely handles — at lead level, portfolio numbers: multi-product KPI tables, programme budgets and burn rates, benefit profiles across several cases, capacity arithmetic spanning teams, and derived figures whose correct construction this role is accountable for and expected to catch when incorrect in others' work.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, calculator permitted, objectively and often adaptively scored against a senior professional norm group.

The lead-level case is a governance case: this role writes or contributes to business cases that must survive challenge from finance colleagues, and is the quality gate through which a portfolio's numbers pass on their way to boards — the last reviewer positioned to catch the unweighted blend, the shifted denominator, the pilot-inflated projection.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Budget, burn, and variance arithmetic** maps to involvement in **programme management activities**: reading spend against profile and distinguishing timing variances from structural ones.
- **Weighted rates and portfolio aggregation** map to **Managing product outcomes** and **Product management**.
- **Benefit modelling and audit** maps to **Creating value for money**: cases whose arithmetic reconciles, and auditing others' cases for the assumption doing the quiet work.
- **Capacity and allocation arithmetic** maps to **Product management** and people leadership.
- **Trend, projection, and scenario discipline** maps to **Strategic ownership** and **Life cycle management**: projections honest only when their assumptions, ranges, and break-points are stated.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio spend against profile**

Five months into the financial year, a portfolio has spent £1.35m against an annual budget of £3.6m, profiled evenly across the year. Ask for the position against profile.

- A) £150,000 underspent against profile
- B) £150,000 overspent against profile
- C) Exactly on profile
- D) £450,000 underspent against profile

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Profile to date: £3.6m ÷ 12 × 5 = £1.5m; actual £1.35m − profile £1.5m = −£150,000, underspent — option A. Option B inverts the sign. This tests correct profile arithmetic and, in a strong answer, recognising that a 10% underspend at month five often signals delayed starts or recruitment lag, worth raising as a delivery signal now rather than explaining in month eleven.

**Question 2 (easy) — Weighted completion across a portfolio**

Product A: 52,000 transactions at 81% completion; Product B: 13,000 at 69%; Product C: 35,000 at 90%. Ask for the portfolio completion rate.

- A) 80.0%
- B) 82.6%
- C) 83.3%
- D) 84.2%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weighted: (42,120+8,970+31,500)÷100,000=82.59% — option B, not the unweighted 80% mean. This tests summing numerators and denominators before dividing, and — in a strong answer — flagging Product B's 69% as twelve points adrift of the blend, worth surfacing alongside the headline rather than letting the blend anaesthetise it.

**Question 3 (easy) — Unit cost with shared platform costs**

Product X handled 40,000 transactions with £48,000 direct cost, plus an allocated equal third of a £90,000 platform cost shared with two other products. Ask for X's fully loaded cost per transaction.

- A) £1.20
- B) £1.95
- C) £2.45
- D) £3.45

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** £48,000 + (£90,000÷3) = £78,000; ÷40,000 = £1.95 — option B. Option A uses direct costs only; D triple-counts. This tests including allocated shared costs correctly, and — in a strong answer — recognising that an equal-thirds allocation is a modelling choice, not a fact, and that switching to usage-based allocation would materially change the figure — relevant whenever unit costs inform investment or retirement decisions.

**Question 4 (easy) — Benefit profile variance**

A business case profiled £75,000/quarter benefits. Actuals: Q1 £60,000, Q2 £81,000, Q3 £66,000. Ask for the cumulative variance at the end of Q3.

- A) −£18,000
- B) −£9,000
- C) +£6,000
- D) −£27,000

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Profile to date £225,000; actual £207,000; variance −£18,000 — option A. This tests basic cumulative variance calculation, and — in a strong answer — reading the quarterly pattern (−15k, +6k, −9k) as oscillation rather than trend, a materially different and better-evidenced governance message than either "on track" or "benefits failing".

**Question 5 (moderate) — Capacity arithmetic with ring-fenced work**

Four teams have quarterly capacities of 30, 24, 36, 30 team-weeks (120 total). Standing obligations: live-service support takes 20% of each team's capacity; the 36-week team must also ring-fence 6 further weeks for security remediation. Committed new delivery: 74 team-weeks. Ask for the position.

- A) 96 team-weeks free; 22 weeks of headroom
- B) 90 team-weeks free; 16 weeks of headroom
- C) 90 team-weeks free; 16 weeks over-committed
- D) 84 team-weeks free; 10 weeks of headroom

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Support: 24 team-weeks off the top, leaving 96; ring-fence: −6 = 90 available; against 74 committed: 16 weeks headroom — option B. This tests correctly sequencing standing obligations before committed work, and — in a strong answer — checking the distribution, not just the aggregate: the ring-fenced team's own position may be tight even while the portfolio total shows comfortable headroom.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing business cases with different shapes**

Case One: £200,000 cost, £90,000/yr benefit for 4 years, 85% delivery confidence. Case Two: £200,000 cost, £70,000/yr benefit for 8 years, 70% delivery confidence, with a year-4 policy review (70% chance benefits continue to year 8; 30% chance they stop at year 4). Ask, using expected value over each case's horizon, for the comparison.

- A) Case One EV ≈ +£106k; Case Two EV ≈ +£192k; Two leads strongly
- B) Case One EV ≈ +£106k; Case Two EV ≈ +£133k; Two leads modestly on expectation, but its edge is back-loaded and exposed to a policy risk it does not control
- C) Case One EV ≈ +£306k; Case Two EV ≈ +£333k
- D) The cases cannot be compared because their horizons differ

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** One: (£90k×4×0.85)−£200k=£106k. Two: expected gross benefit given delivery = (0.7×560k + 0.3×280k)=£476k; ×0.70 confidence=£333.2k; −£200k=£133.2k. Option A prices the policy fork at zero; C forgets to subtract cost from both. This tests building a two-layer probability tree correctly and, in a strong answer, recognising that a £27k gap built from stacked round-number probabilities is within assumption noise, shifting the decision to distribution properties — time-to-value, reversibility, whose hands hold the risk.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The programme dashboard's improving average**

A dashboard shows "average days from idea to live" improving from 120 to 96 over two quarters: earlier quarter 8 standard items averaging 100 days, 2 complex averaging 200 days (10 items); recent quarter 11 standard averaging 98 days, 1 complex at 274 days (12 items). Ask whether the improvement reflects faster delivery.

- A) Yes — 96 is better than 120, and both item types improved.
- B) Mostly mix, not speed: standard items barely moved (100 → 98) and the complex item got dramatically slower (200 → 274); the average "improved" because the mix shifted from 20% complex to 8% complex. Verify: earlier: (8×100 + 2×200) ÷ 10 = 120 ✓; lately: (11×98 + 1×274) ÷ 12 = (1,078 + 274) ÷ 12 = 1,352 ÷ 12 ≈ 112.7 — which does not equal 96, so the dashboard's 96 is also inconsistent with its own decomposition and needs auditing before any narrative is reported.
- C) Yes, and the complex-item slowdown is within normal variation.
- D) The dashboard should report medians, which would resolve everything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Within-type performance is flat-to-worse, and any genuine improvement could only come from mix shift — but recomputing the average from its own stated decomposition gives 112.7, not the dashboard's claimed 96: a doubly wrong headline. This tests recomputing an aggregate from its decomposition before accepting a narrative, and refusing to interpret a number its own components cannot reproduce — option D's "use a different statistic" is a non-sequitur that would let a broken dashboard escape audit.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Recruitment funnel arithmetic**

A campaign: 240 applications; 25% passed sift; 70% of those interviewed; 40% of attendees appointable (17); 12 of 17 appointable filled. Ask, for the same conversion rates, roughly how many applications a 20-hire campaign requires.

- A) About 405
- B) About 480
- C) About 290
- D) About 680

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Overall conversion: 0.25×0.70×0.40×(12÷17)≈4.94%; 20÷0.0494≈405 — option A. Option C assumes perfect fill, forgetting the historical loss between appointable and in-post. This tests chaining conversion rates correctly through every stage including fill rate, and — in a strong answer — identifying which stage is cheapest to improve (fill rate, typically a speed-of-offer problem rather than an assessment problem) to reduce the required application volume.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Savings claims that must be reconciled**

Team One claims a status tracker deflects 30% of progress-chasing calls (40% of all calls), saving 12% of a £1.8m contact budget (£216,000). Team Two claims redesigned guidance deflects 25% of confusion-driven calls (35% of all calls), saving 8.75% (£157,500). Ask whether the portfolio can book £373,500.

- A) Yes — the calculations are each internally correct, so the savings add.
- B) Each calculation is internally correct, but the claims may overlap: a progress-chasing call can also be confusion-driven, and both teams may be deflecting some of the same calls; the portfolio should measure actual call volumes against a shared baseline, book the observed reduction, and attribute by evaluation rather than by adding independent models.
- C) Neither saving is real until posts are removed.
- D) Book the larger claim only and discard the smaller.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both calculations check out individually, but nothing establishes the two call categories are disjoint — the addition assumes it without evidence. Option A audits each case and never the sum, exactly the gap a lead is positioned to close. This tests recognising double-counted benefit claims across teams and prescribing the remedy — measure the actual total, attribute by evaluation — rather than adding independent models or arbitrarily discarding one.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Projection with a capacity ceiling**

Digital uptake: 40%, 46%, 52%, 58% over four quarters (of 500,000 annual users). A board paper projects 76% by Q8 on trend. The non-digital population (42% = 210,000) breaks down as 90,000 "digital with support" (convertible with investment), 60,000 "unlikely digital", remainder "capable, not yet converted". Ask what ceiling this implies and how the Q8 projection should be treated.

- A) The linear trend is sound: 58% + 3 quarters × 6 points = 76% by Q8.
- B) The classification implies a practical ceiling near 88% (100% minus the 12% "unlikely digital"), so 76% remains arithmetically below the ceiling — but growth must slow as the easy conversions exhaust: the remaining "capable, not yet converted" pool is 60,000 users (12% of the base), sustaining at most two more quarters of 6-point growth before further gains require converting the "with support" group, which needs the investment the linear projection silently assumes; the honest paper shows the trend, the ceiling, the pool arithmetic, and the investment dependency.
- C) The ceiling is 58% and growth has already stopped.
- D) Project 100% eventually, since all populations convert given time.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The "capable, not yet converted" pool (60,000, 12% of base) sustains exactly two more 6-point quarters (each converting 30,000) before the frontier hits the "with support" population, whose conversion requires investment. This tests checking a projection against the finite population that supplies it — dividing gain per period by the remaining pool — rather than extrapolating linearly past pool exhaustion, and recognising the "unlikely digital" group as a permanent service population, not a conversion backlog.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Auditing a unit-cost comparison used for a retirement case**

A retirement case cites: "K costs £8.40 per transaction against the portfolio average of £3.10." K handles 15,000 transactions with £126,000 total allocated cost, including £54,000 as an equal one-third share of a legacy data centre also serving two products handling 90,000 and 45,000 transactions. The portfolio average uses usage-based allocation. Ask what a consistent comparison shows.

- A) K at £8.40 versus £3.10 stands; retire K.
- B) On usage-based allocation, K's data-centre share becomes 15,000 ÷ 150,000 = 10% of £162,000 = £16,200 (versus £54,000), so K's total falls to £126,000 − £54,000 + £16,200 = £88,200, and unit cost to £88,200 ÷ 15,000 = £5.88 — still above the portfolio's £3.10, but the retirement case's headline overstated K's excess cost by 43%, and the decision should be re-argued on consistent figures (and on K's users' alternatives, which unit cost alone cannot decide).
- C) K's unit cost is actually below portfolio average once corrected.
- D) Allocation methods never change decisions, only presentation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** K is costed with equal-shares allocation while the comparator uses usage-based — a cross-method comparison, meaningless until harmonised. Harmonised, K's unit cost falls to £5.88, still above average but far less dramatically than £8.40 claimed. This tests harmonising allocation methods before comparing unit costs across products, and recognising that even a corrected unit-cost figure is one input to a retirement decision, not the whole argument.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The quarter's story in five numbers**

A quarter's figures: transactions up 9% (1.10m→1.20m); total cost up 4% (£2.50m→£2.60m); completion down 1.2 points (83.4%→82.2%); support contacts up 15% (48,000→55,200); benefits realised £310,000 against £280,000 profile. Ask for the most accurate and useful single-paragraph synthesis.

- A) "A strong quarter: volumes up, benefits ahead of profile, and unit costs improved from £2.27 to £2.17 per transaction."
- B) "A mixed quarter requiring one investigation: demand grew 9% and unit cost improved 5% (£2.27 → £2.17), with benefits £30k ahead of profile — but completion fell 1.2 points and support contacts rose 15%, outpacing volume growth (contacts per 1,000 transactions up from 43.6 to 46.0), which together suggest a developing user-experience problem that the cost and benefit lines have not yet felt; diagnostic underway, findings by [date]."
- C) "A concerning quarter: completion is falling and contacts are rising, indicating portfolio decline."
- D) "A stable quarter: most figures moved only slightly."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Every figure in B checks out (unit cost improved to £2.17; contacts per 1,000 transactions rose from 43.6 to 46.0, outpacing volume), and B is the only synthesis holding all five figures while reading the leading pair (completion falling, normalised contacts rising) as the signal cost and benefit lines will lag. Option A is a true-facts-false-story synthesis that buries the leading indicators. This tests composing a synthesis where every clause survives recomputation and nothing that will matter next quarter is hidden.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate sums numerators and denominators before dividing, rather than averaging percentages.
- Note whether the candidate harmonises allocation methods before comparing unit costs across products.
- Watch for whether the candidate recomputes a dashboard aggregate from its own decomposition before accepting it.
- Score for whether a candidate checks a projection against the finite population supplying its growth.
- Note whether the candidate identifies possible double-counting when two independent benefit claims are added together.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an averaged percentage, or a blend reported without its spread.
- Missing an unharmonised allocation method used to compare unit costs across products.
- Accepting a dashboard aggregate that its own decomposition cannot reproduce.
- Rewarding a linear projection extended past the point its supplying population would be exhausted.
- Missing when a candidate adds two benefit claims without checking whether their populations overlap.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures the precision with which a candidate reads, interprets and infers from written material through which portfolios are actually governed at this level: programme board papers, strategy documents, assessment reports on other teams, procurement and supplier text, policy instructions, research syntheses, and recommendation papers drafted for review under this role's name.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes: short passages with true / false / cannot say questions, plus best-summary, assumption identification, inference, and clear-writing judgement items. The governing rule never changes: answer from the passage alone — at lead level, where domain experience makes almost every distractor plausible, that discipline is tested hardest.

This role operates at the altitude where words become commitments — reviewing and signing papers whose sentences will be quoted in governance for years, directing stakeholder relationship strategy, and contributing findings to other teams' assessments that carry consequence for their funding and morale.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Comprehension of governance, policy, and assessment text** maps to duties in **programme management activities**, **contributing to the assessment of other teams**, and **ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards**.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Applying user-centred insights**: portfolio claims must match portfolio evidence.
- **Assumption and inference analysis** maps to **Creating value for money** and **Strategic ownership**: surfacing unstated premises in cases, strategies and survivor stories.
- **Commitment-strength reading** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: perfect pitch for endorsed versus approved, anticipated versus committed, noted versus agreed.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Product leadership** and coaching: diagnosing others' prose precisely enough to improve it without rewriting it.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 6, the candidate decides whether the statement is True, False, or Cannot say. A fourth option is included where relevant.

**Question 1 (easy) — A programme instruction's actual scope**

Passage: "All programmes must submit quarterly benefits returns to the portfolio office. Programmes in closedown are exempt from quarterly returns but must submit a final benefits statement within 60 days of closure. The portfolio office may request interim returns from any programme at any time."

Statement: "A programme in closedown has no reporting obligations to the portfolio office."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, until closure completes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The exemption relieves only quarterly returns; the final statement and exposure to interim-return requests ("any programme at any time") both persist: False. Option D fails on the same grounds. This tests exemption-scoping precisely — "exempt from what, precisely?" — since teams granted a named exemption reliably behave as if granted a general one.

**Question 2 (easy) — What an assessment report found**

Passage: "The panel was impressed by the team's user research operation and its evidence trail from findings to iterations. However, the panel was not shown evidence that the service has been tested with users of assistive technology, and the team could not describe the assisted digital support route. The service is rated 'not met' on point 5. All other points are rated 'met'. Reassessment of point 5 only is required within three months."

Statement: "The panel found that the service is inaccessible to users of assistive technology."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, which is why point 5 was not met

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Not shown evidence" of testing is not evidence of inaccessibility; the finding is compatible with either a working or a failing service that simply was not evidenced: Cannot say. Option D repackages the conflation attractively but the failure was the evidence gap, not demonstrated inaccessibility. This tests translating panel findings at their exact strength — a team told inaccurately that it was "found inaccessible" may over-react; told accurately, it runs the right test.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a ministerial commitment precisely**

Passage: "We will introduce a digital application route for the carers' support scheme by April, and we expect the majority of applications to be made digitally within two years of launch. Paper applications will continue to be accepted for those who need them."

Statement: "The minister has committed to ending paper applications within two years."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, once digital uptake reaches a majority

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three clauses carry different strengths: a dated commitment (digital route by April), an expectation about uptake share (not commitment, and about a majority not the totality), and a commitment to paper's continuation, unbounded by date — directly contradicting the statement: False. This tests a clause-by-clause strength audit of ministerial text, since the expensive misreadings in government are usually upgrades made in good faith — expectations read as commitments, majorities read as totalities.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A supplier's contract-variation letter**

Passage: "Further to our discussions, we confirm we can accommodate the additional API capacity from 1 September, subject to agreement of the revised charging schedule attached. Absent such agreement by 31 July, we will continue to deliver at current capacity under the existing terms. We note that current capacity may be insufficient for your projected autumn volumes, as discussed, and we cannot accept liability for service degradation arising from volumes exceeding contracted capacity."

Statement: "If the revised charging schedule is not agreed by 31 July, the supplier will be in breach of contract should autumn service degradation occur due to excess volumes."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the supplier was warned about the volumes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Failing agreement, the supplier continues delivering at current capacity under existing terms — performance, not breach — and expressly declines liability for degradation from volumes exceeding contracted capacity: False. Option D reverses the direction of the warning, which protects the supplier (evidencing the customer knew) rather than the customer. This tests reading a carefully constructed commercial position — a dated condition, a priced remedy, a pre-built defence — for its actual legal effect.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The strategy sentence and the reorganisation**

Passage: "Product teams should be organised around end-to-end user journeys rather than internal organisational structures. Where legacy contracts or statutory boundaries make journey-based teams impractical, directorates must document the constraint and the planned path to journey alignment, reviewed annually."

Statement: "A directorate that keeps a team organised around an internal structure, documents that a legacy contract makes journey alignment impractical, and reviews the position annually, is compliant with the strategy as stated — provided it also documents a planned path to journey alignment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because the strategy prohibits structure-based teams outright

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The directorate invokes a qualifying constraint and discharges all three duties of the exception route (constraint, path, review); the statement's proviso supplies the third explicitly: True. Option D reads the default as absolute, erasing the exception route the passage constructs. This tests recognising a documented-exception route as a compliance mechanism, not a loophole, and distinguishing compliant-via-exception from simply non-compliant.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Research synthesis at portfolio altitude**

Passage: "Across the portfolio's three products, this quarter's research included 41 sessions. In Product A's sessions (n=18), users consistently struggled to understand eligibility rules. Product B's sessions (n=15) focused on caseworkers, who praised the new queue design but flagged slow document loading. Product C's sessions (n=8, all with existing frequent users) surfaced no significant usability issues."

Statement: "Product C has fewer usability issues than Products A and B."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, since its sessions surfaced none

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Product C's sessions were conducted entirely with existing frequent users — the population least likely to surface novice-facing problems — making "no issues found" weak evidence for the product and no evidence at all for a cross-product ranking: Cannot say. Option D converts absence of findings into absence of issues. This tests reading a synthesis's sampling line (who was recruited) before its findings line, and recognising that sampling artefacts, not just scope, silently qualify a claim.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Best summary of a contested board discussion**

Passage: "The board discussed the proposal to consolidate the two casework products. The finance member supported consolidation on cost grounds. The operations member opposed, citing migration risk during the winter peak. The chair concluded that consolidation is agreed in principle, subject to a migration plan that avoids the November–February peak, with the plan to return to the March board for approval before any migration activity begins."

Ask which relay to teams is most faithful.

- A) "The board approved consolidation; migration planning starts now for delivery from March."
- B) "Consolidation is agreed in principle. No migration activity is authorised yet: we must produce a migration plan that avoids November–February, and the March board must approve that plan before anything moves. Planning work can and should start now."
- C) "The board was split, so consolidation is on hold until finance and operations agree."
- D) "The board rejected consolidation because of migration risk."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The chair's conclusion decomposes into agreement in principle, a substantive condition, and a process condition; option B preserves all three at strength and adds the one legitimate inference (planning work, distinct from migration activity, is implicitly commissioned). Option A upgrades "agreed in principle" to "approved"; C mistakes the discussion for the decision. This tests decomposing a board conclusion into decision + conditions + reserved approvals, and relaying exactly what governance authorised — no less, no more.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The assumption under a portfolio-level argument**

Passage: "We should move all three products onto the shared design system, because it will eliminate the visual inconsistencies users currently experience when moving between our products during a single journey."

Ask which unstated assumption is most load-bearing.

- A) That users actually move between the three products within single journeys in meaningful numbers — the benefit is defined entirely by cross-product journeys, which the paper asserts exist but never evidences.
- B) That the shared design system is cheaper to maintain than three local ones.
- C) That designers prefer working in shared systems.
- D) That visual consistency is measurable.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The entire claimed benefit is scoped to cross-product journeys, which the paper asserts but never evidences — if such journeys are rare, the benefit approaches zero regardless of how completely inconsistencies are eliminated. This tests locating the load-bearing assumption inside a benefit argument's beneficiary clause — who exactly experiences the claimed gain, and how many exist — and recognising it as cheaply testable (analytics could count cross-product sessions this week).

**Question 9 (challenging) — Two documents and a delegated authority**

Passage 1 (scheme of delegation): "Leads may approve product spend up to £50,000 per item within approved programme budgets. Items exceeding £50,000, and any item that commits the organisation beyond the current financial year, require director approval regardless of value." Passage 2 (email): "Great news — the annual licence renewal came in at £48,000, under your £50k threshold, so I've told the supplier you'll sign this week. It's the usual 12-month term starting 1 February."

Statement: "Under the scheme of delegation, this renewal is within the lead's authority to approve."

- A) True — £48,000 is below the £50,000 threshold
- B) False — a 12-month term starting 1 February extends beyond the current financial year, triggering the director-approval requirement regardless of the item's value
- C) Cannot say — it depends on whether the programme budget is approved
- D) True — licence renewals are routine and the delegation is designed for exactly this

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two independent triggers exist; the renewal passes the value test but fails the duration test — a 12-month term from 1 February runs into the next financial year, triggering director approval "regardless of value": False. This tests checking a multi-trigger authority trigger by trigger, since the visible test (value) is routinely the decoy and the structural one (duration) is where breaches happen in good faith — and, in a strong answer, noting the commitment the product manager has already made to the supplier needs correcting alongside the approval routing.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement: the assessment finding**

The evidence for a service assessment finding: a team deploys weekly to staging but only quarterly to production, because a manual security review board meets quarterly; users waited up to three months for fixes to known issues, including two accessibility fixes. Ask which drafting best serves the assessment's purpose.

- A) "The team's deployment practices fall below expected standards and require significant improvement going forward."
- B) "Deployment cadence is constrained by governance outside the team's control: weekly staging deployments demonstrate strong engineering practice, but production releases occur only quarterly because the manual security review board meets quarterly. The user impact is concrete — fixes for known issues, including two accessibility fixes, waited up to three months. Recommendation: the department should enable continuous security assurance (automated checks plus risk-based review) so production cadence can match the team's demonstrated capability; the team should meanwhile agree an expedited-release route for accessibility and security fixes with the review board."
- C) "The security review board is the problem and should be disbanded."
- D) "Deployment happens weekly to staging and quarterly to production. The security review board meets quarterly. Two accessibility fixes were delayed. Further detail is available in the technical annex."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B locates causation correctly (departmental governance, not team capability), evidences impact concretely, and recommends at two levels addressed to the actors who can each act. Option A misdirects remediation at the actor who cannot fix it; C attacks the security function rather than its cadence, guaranteeing a defensive response; D is fact-complete and judgement-free, delegating the actual finding to nobody. This tests the four-part findings template — capability observed, constraint located, impact evidenced, recommendation addressed to the owning actor — that makes an assessment finding actionable rather than inert.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference across an evaluation and a decision memo**

Passage 1 (evaluation): "The proactive-renewal trial automatically renewed licences for users who had opted in, unless their details had changed. Auto-renewed users showed 98% retention against 84% for the manual-renewal comparison group. However, opt-in required an existing online account, and opt-in users were disproportionately long-tenured, high-engagement licensees." Passage 2 (decision memo): "The evaluation shows auto-renewal increases retention by 14 points. Extending auto-renewal to all licensees will therefore lift overall retention toward 98%."

Statement: "The memo's projection overstates the likely effect of extending auto-renewal to all licensees."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say — the extension has not been tried
- D) True only if the evaluation's arithmetic is wrong

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The trial's 98% was achieved on a self-selected, long-tenured, high-engagement population — characteristics independently predicting high retention — so extending to the whole (shorter-tenured, lower-engagement) population will not achieve the same figure: the direction of overstatement is determinate even though its size is not. Option C confuses "the precise effect is unknown" with "whether the memo overstates is unknown." This tests distinguishing direction of bias from magnitude of bias — a claim can be known to be biased upward from its method alone, before new data arrives.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The policy instruction and the three readings**

Passage: "In light of the efficiency review, directorates should prioritise work that reduces administrative burden on small businesses. New transactional services for business users must include a burden impact statement. Existing services need not produce statements retrospectively, but any material change to an existing business-facing service must be accompanied by one." Portfolio plan: (1) a new licensing application service for small businesses; (2) a major redesign of an existing export-declaration journey; (3) routine bug fixes and content updates to an existing grants portal; (4) a new internal casework tool for departmental staff.

Statement: "Under the instruction, items 1 and 2 require burden impact statements; items 3 and 4 do not."

- A) True
- B) False — item 4 also requires a statement, since efficiency applies to staff burden too
- C) Cannot say — "material change" is undefined, so item 2's status is unresolvable
- D) True, provided item 3's changes are genuinely non-material

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Items 1 and 2 clearly trigger the instrument; item 4 sits outside the instruction's stated scope (business users), ruling out B's expansion; item 3's exemption holds only while its changes stay genuinely routine — a content update that rewrites eligibility guidance could be material. Option C overcorrects, treating a delegated judgement term as an unresolvable defect. This tests applying an instruction's triggers to concrete items precisely, while recognising that one classification (item 3) is contingent on ongoing judgement rather than categorical.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate marks each verb as commitment, expectation, permission, or aspiration before answering statements about it.
- Watch for whether the candidate checks exemptions and multi-trigger authorities structurally — "exempt from what, precisely?", "which triggers, checked independently?"
- For research and evaluation passages, score on whether the candidate reads the sampling line before the findings line.
- For relay and summary questions, score on whether decision, conditions, and reserved approvals are all preserved.
- For findings-drafting items, score against the four-part template: capability, constraint, impact, addressed recommendation.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an upgraded claim strength — expectation into commitment, in-principle agreement into approval.
- Missing when a candidate treats absence of evidence as evidence of absence.
- Rewarding an exemption read too broadly, or a default read as an absolute prohibition.
- Missing when a candidate checks only the visible trigger of a multi-trigger authority.
- Confusing unknown magnitude with unknown direction in a biased projection.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to evaluate possible responses. Scoring keys are built from experienced practitioners' consensus and professional behavioural frameworks. At lead level, distractors include responses correct two levels down (doing the work personally), responses that flatter positional authority (decide, overrule, restructure), responses hiding behind process when judgement is asked for, and responses that optimise a portfolio while quietly externalising cost onto the wider organisation or the people being developed.

Typical format: 10 to 20 scenarios over 20 to 40 minutes, usually untimed. This is the closest assessment to the job's actual texture: the role manages people and products simultaneously, operates as a member of a product area's management team, provides leadership across programmes and the local community, and works closely with the head of product.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **People leadership scenarios** map to **manage people and manage products** and coaching duties: performance, development risk, succession, and fairness.
- **Management team scenarios** map to membership of the **product area's management team**: collective responsibility and the difference between representing a portfolio and governing the whole.
- **Programme and governance scenarios** map to **programme management activities** and **Life cycle management**: risk prediction, escalation design, and holding standards under delivery pressure.
- **Community leadership scenarios** map to **Product leadership**: leading community activities and growing the profession.
- **Strategic influence scenarios** map to **Strategic ownership** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: negotiating with senior stakeholders and challenging strategy with evidence.
- **Integrity and standards scenarios** map to the duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: evidential honesty, accessibility, fair process, data protection.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The product manager who wants the shape of the candidate's job, not its work**

A strong product manager asks to take on "portfolio-level work" — attending the management team, owning the high-level roadmap — citing readiness for lead roles. Their own product's outcomes are good, but their two direct coaching relationships (an associate and a new starter) have been visibly neglected: the associate has had one catch-up in two months. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Grant the request — ambition should be rewarded, and exposure creates growth.
- B) Decline until their product metrics improve further.
- C) Have the direct conversation: affirm the ambition, name the evidence gap — lead roles are made of developing people, and their two coaching relationships are the live audition they are currently failing — then agree a path where demonstrated people-development unlocks staged portfolio exposure.
- D) Grant the request but quietly reassign their coaching relationships to someone reliable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C affirms real ambition, names the evidence gap precisely, and converts feedback into a contract: people-development performance unlocking staged portfolio exposure. Option A rewards a misconception that leadership is altitude rather than multiplication of people; D launders the failure by removing the evidence of it rather than addressing it; B declines for the wrong reason. This tests mapping a request for advancement onto the evidence the next level actually requires, and making the gap the plan rather than granting or refusing outright.

**Question 2 (easy) — A candidate's portfolio interest versus the management team's decision**

A product area's management team is deciding where to place a single new user-researcher post; the objective case narrowly favours another lead's portfolio, whose flagship team has had no research capability for a quarter. The candidate's own portfolio would benefit visibly too. Ask for the most effective behaviour in the meeting.

- A) Advocate hard for the candidate's own portfolio — every lead is expected to fight their corner, and the system balances the fighting.
- B) State the comparative evidence accurately including where it favours the other portfolio, name the candidate's own need honestly, support the placement the evidence favours, and negotiate for the mitigations the candidate's teams need — research office hours, next post priority, shared panel access.
- C) Stay silent to avoid the appearance of self-interest.
- D) Support the other portfolio in the meeting, then raise the candidate's teams' research gap with the head of product separately as an urgent exception.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B states evidence straight, including against the candidate's own interest, supports the evidence-favoured decision, and negotiates open mitigations. Option A imports an adversarial lobbying model that discounts everything a lead says by their self-interest; D publicly supports one decision while privately relitigating it, which if discovered retroactively reveals the in-meeting support as theatre. This tests whether a candidate has made the transition from portfolio advocacy to collective governance — the currency of a management team is being believed, minted by stating evidence against oneself.

**Question 3 (easy) — The community commitment under delivery pressure**

Co-leading a department's product community (monthly sessions, mentoring scheme, cross-government show-and-tells), the candidate's portfolio is under heavy delivery pressure this quarter, and a director suggests parking community work as "discretionary." Two mentees have sessions booked; next month's community event has forty sign-ups. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Park it all as suggested — delivery pressure is real and the director has spoken.
- B) Keep everything unchanged — community work is important and pressure is always real.
- C) Triage rather than park: honour the booked mentoring sessions (small, scheduled, high-trust commitments), delegate the community event's lead role this month to a rising community member with the candidate's backing, and tell the director what is being kept, what is being delegated, and why the profession's infrastructure should not be the first thing sacrificed each hard quarter.
- D) Cancel the mentoring but keep the event, since forty people beat two.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C decomposes the commitment by cost: honouring small, high-trust mentoring promises; delegating the event as a development opportunity for someone else; reporting the triage and its reasoning to the director rather than accepting or ignoring the frame silently. Option A accepts "discretionary" uncritically; D ranks by headcount and cancels the commitment whose breach does the most durable damage. This tests decomposing rather than accepting a blanket instruction, and defending professional infrastructure out loud rather than either sacrificing or ignoring pressure.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A predicted risk is now arriving**

Two months ago, a candidate flagged in writing, with mitigation options, that a partner directorate's API migration would likely slip and strand a flagship team's autumn release. The board noted the risk without funding mitigation. The slip is now confirmed at four weeks, exactly as projected. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Return to the board with "as predicted", the confirmed impact, and a demand that the partner directorate be held accountable.
- B) Re-present the situation decision-ready: the confirmed slip, the release impact, and three costed options (descope to release on time, slip four weeks with stakeholder communications prepared, or buy back two weeks via the contingency mitigation originally proposed) — with a recommendation attached, while separately working the partner directorate relationship for any recoverable time.
- C) Absorb the slip quietly and manage stakeholder expectations downward without returning to the board.
- D) Instruct the team to build a temporary workaround for the missing API so the date holds.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B reopens the board's earlier "noted without funding" choice with current facts, three costed options, and a recommendation, running the relationship workstream in parallel. Option A spends being right on vindication rather than a decision; C hides a governance-owned decision inside stakeholder management; D spends the team's weeks on throwaway engineering to protect a date nobody has chosen to fund protecting. This tests converting an accurate prediction into decision-ready management when it lands, rather than treating being right as sufficient in itself.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The assessment a candidate is contributing to, and the friend**

On a panel assessing another directorate's beta service, the product manager being assessed is a friend and former colleague whose career would be damaged by a "not met." The evidence is mixed: strong research and iteration, but no demonstration of performance under realistic load, and a visibly improvised answer on data retention. Fellow panellists seem inclined to a generous "met." Ask for the most effective behaviour.

- A) Declare the relationship to the panel, then assess the evidence exactly as it would be assessed for a stranger: argue that the load and retention gaps are material, propose "not met" on the affected points with a tightly scoped reassessment, and write the finding so the remediation path is clear and achievable.
- B) Withdraw from the panel entirely and leave it one contributor short on assessment day.
- C) Go along with the panel's generous inclination — the gaps are fixable, the colleagues involved are experienced, and the friendship survives.
- D) Argue for "met" but privately warn the friend to fix the load and retention issues urgently.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option A manages the conflict by disclosure plus discipline — the standard that is stronger governance than either concealment or unnecessary recusal — and assesses unevidenced load performance and improvised retention answers as genuinely material for a beta. Option C is the panel drift the structure exists to resist; D corrupts the public record while a private warning proves the candidate knew. This tests holding assessment rigour under both relationship and social pressure, and recognising that a scoped "not met" with a clear remediation path serves a friend's career better than a live incident on an under-assessed service.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the head of product's flawed initiative**

A head of product announces every team must adopt a single standardised sprint cadence "so the portfolio moves as one," which would materially harm two teams (flow-based integration; ops-heavy support) whose work-shapes it does not fit, and the candidate suspects a delivery-framework vendor's influence. Ask for a ranking:

1. Go to the head privately with evidence: the two teams' work-shapes, the predicted costs of forced cadence, the outcomes their tailored approaches deliver — and propose the initiative proceed as a default-with-documented-exceptions, offering to draft the exception criteria and to present the evidence to the management team if useful.
2. Implement the mandate loyally across all the candidate's teams and monitor the damage for later discussion.
3. Publicly challenge the initiative at the next all-hands, with the vendor connection named.
4. Implement it for the teams it suits and quietly leave the two exposed teams on their current approaches without telling the head.

- A) Most effective: 1; least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4
- C) Most effective: 2; least effective: 3
- D) Most effective: 4; least effective: 2

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Response 1 goes private first, argues from evidence rather than motive-attribution, and proposes a default-with-exceptions that preserves the head's legitimate goal. Response 4 is least effective because it is concealed selective compliance — the head's picture of their own portfolio becomes false, unprotected teams surface the day it is discovered, and the candidate learns the habit of quiet nullification. This tests recognising that open wrongness can be corrected by the system while hidden "rightness" corrupts the shared information the system runs on.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Recruiting: the shortcut that favours the familiar**

Chairing a product manager recruitment panel, scores put an external candidate narrowly ahead of an internal candidate the panel chair knows and rates well. A fellow panellist argues the internal candidate can start Monday and is a "known quantity," urging the panel to appoint internally despite the scores. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Agree — panel judgement legitimately includes deliverability, and the internal candidate is genuinely strong.
- B) Hold the appointment to the published criteria and scores: the external candidate is appointed; explain to the panel that "known quantity" is familiarity bias wearing a business case, and that start-date logistics were not among the published criteria and cannot be smuggled in post hoc — then genuinely invest in the internal candidate's development and next opportunity.
- C) Re-run the interviews with deliverability added as a criterion.
- D) Appoint both by finding budget for a second post.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B holds merit-based appointment against published criteria, names the bias explicitly so the norm strengthens, and genuinely invests in the internal candidate's next opportunity. Option C is the same corruption with paperwork — criteria reverse-engineered from a preferred outcome after scoring. This tests holding recruitment integrity precisely when breaking it is attractive and locally beneficial, recognising that panels take their bend from the chair.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The team that's succeeding for the wrong reasons**

A product team consistently hits its outcome targets because its product manager quietly filters which user segments experiments run on, selecting favourable segments. Nothing is fabricated — the numbers are real, only the segments are curated. Challenged gently once, the product manager said "we focus our tests where we can learn fastest." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the explanation — the numbers are real, targets are met, and segment selection is a legitimate experimental choice.
- B) Reopen it as a measurement-integrity issue, not a performance accusation: require experiment designs to state segment selection and its rationale in advance, have results reported with segment coverage visible, jointly review what the product's outcomes look like across all segments — and coach the product manager explicitly on the line between focusing tests and curating evidence, watching what the fuller picture reveals about the targets.
- C) Publicly correct the team's belief that it is excelling.
- D) Move the product manager to a less measurement-sensitive product.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B converts an ambiguous practice into a structural fix — pre-registered segment rationale, visible coverage, an all-segments review — that lets good-faith focusing continue while making curation impossible to hide, alongside an explicit coaching conversation. Option A accepts an explanation from inside a pattern that contradicts it; D relocates the behaviour unrepaired. This tests distinguishing legitimate experimental focus from evidence curation, and fixing an integrity problem dressed as methodology by making the methodology inspectable rather than relying on vigilance or punishment.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The programme date, the management team, and the thing a candidate knows**

At a management team meeting, the head of product presents a flagship commitment as "on track" for March across all four portfolios. The candidate's own detailed planning this week shows their workstream will land in May at the earliest, and corridor conversations suggest at least one other lead's workstream is in similar shape but reported green. The meeting is moving to the next item. Ask for the most effective behaviour.

- A) Let the item pass — raise the workstream's position with the head privately after the meeting, and leave the other lead's reporting to the other lead.
- B) Stop the item: state the workstream's evidence-based May date in the meeting, invite the other leads to pressure-test their own dates with the same planning rigour, and propose the management team agree a corrected consolidated position and a re-baselined upward report before the current "on track" hardens further.
- C) Report the May date in the meeting but say nothing that might implicate the other lead's green.
- D) After the meeting, tell the head privately about both the workstream's own date and suspicions about the other lead's workstream.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B states evidence in the room where the collective position lives, invites peers to pressure-test rather than accuses, and produces a governance act (corrected consolidation, re-baselined report) before the optimistic position hardens further. Option A corrects only the candidate's own slice privately, leaving the collective report false; D routes both facts to the head as private intelligence, making the head the sole holder of the true picture. This tests correcting bad collective news collectively, in the room, and spending suspicion about peers as an open invitation to rigour rather than private currency.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The accessibility complaint about another lead's service**

A disability advocacy organisation writes to a director about severe accessibility barriers (unlabelled form fields defeating screen readers on a benefits-adjacent journey) in a live service belonging to another lead's portfolio; that lead is on leave for another week. The service's last accessibility statement is fourteen months old. The director forwards the complaint asking for thoughts. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Reply to the director that it's the other lead's portfolio and should wait for their return, to respect ownership boundaries.
- B) Treat severity as the clock, ownership as the protocol: verify the barriers immediately with an accessibility specialist, brief the other lead's deputy and the affected team the same day so interim mitigations start (fixing unlabelled fields is typically fast), draft the director a factual position — barriers confirmed or not, mitigation under way, full audit scheduled, statement being updated — and hand the whole thread to the other lead on their return with everything done in their portfolio's name, not yours.
- C) Take the opportunity to run a full accessibility review of the other lead's portfolio while they are away, reporting findings to the director.
- D) Reply to the advocacy organisation directly with reassurances, copying the director.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B runs the users' clock (same-day verification and mitigation) and the ownership clock (through the portfolio's own deputy and team, handed over on the lead's return) separately and correctly. Option A files users' exclusion under professional courtesy — ownership governs who acts, never whether severity is acted on; C converts a colleague's absence into empire-building under an accessibility pretext. This tests acting at the speed of harm through the structure of ownership, in the owner's name, rather than either deferring inappropriately or overreaching into another portfolio.

**Question 11 (challenging) — A strategy consultation the candidate disagrees with**

A department consults on dissolving product-area management teams (including the candidate's own) into programme-aligned delivery groups. The candidate believes, from evidence, this would damage product's independent voice on user needs and the profession's development infrastructure — and recognises their own position is among those removed, colouring how any objection will be read. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Submit a consultation response defending the current structure, and mobilise the product managers to submit similar responses.
- B) Stay out of it — the conflict of interest is disqualifying, and the consultation will find the right answer.
- C) Engage the consultation on its own objective: concede the layer-reduction goal openly, then evidence what the current structure actually produces (independent user-and-value challenge, profession development, recruitment quality — with concrete instances where product's independent voice changed delivery outcomes), name the conflict explicitly, and propose alternative designs that meet the alignment goal while preserving the named functions — engaging the head of product and cross-government peers to strengthen the evidence base beyond a single position.
- D) Accept the direction as inevitable and begin quietly positioning for a delivery-group role.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C concedes the legitimate objective, argues from evidenced function rather than structure, names the conflict of interest before others do, and proposes alternative designs — converting the candidate from the consultation's defendant into its most useful contributor. Option A mobilises reports in a way that spends their credibility on the candidate's own structure; B mistakes having an interest for being disqualified. This tests arguing with declared interest and evidence when role and personal position are entangled, rather than either silent advocacy or anticipatory surrender.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the succession decision**

Departing for a new opportunity, the candidate is asked to recommend interim leadership of their portfolio during recruitment of a successor. Options: PM One, brilliant but still developing as a people manager, three months into an agreed development path (Question 1's candidate), progressing genuinely; PM Two, solid and safe, unlikely to grow further or want the role permanently; an experienced external contractor, capable but context-free. Ask for a ranking:

1. Recommend PM One as interim with explicit scaffolding: named mentor from the management team, a structured handover concentrated on the people-leadership dimension, defined check-points with the head — presented honestly as a development-accelerating stretch with safeguards, alongside an honest assessment of the risk.
2. Recommend PM Two as the safe pair of hands, keeping the seat warm with minimal risk until the permanent hire.
3. Recommend the contractor, as the option that risks neither product manager's reputation on a stretch role.
4. Decline to recommend — the candidate is leaving, and the head should decide without a thumb on the scale.

- A) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 2; least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 1; least effective: 3
- D) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 converts the interim period into the development instrument the candidate's own coaching plan was already building, with explicit scaffolding and an honest risk assessment. Response 4 is least effective because it abandons the one duty uniquely the candidate's — nobody else holds this knowledge of these people and this portfolio — dressed as humility. This tests whether a candidate treats even their own departure as an occasion to honour development commitments and leave the system holding more information, not less.

### Administration tips

- Score for the recurring lead fingerprint: acting through structures and owners, correcting collective pictures in the collective room, spending suspicion as open invitation rather than private currency.
- Watch for whether a candidate's response was right one or two levels down but a delegation or governance failure at this level.
- For most/least formats, score the least-effective response as the one that corrupts the shared picture or converts trust into convenience, not merely the most timid one.
- Note whether the candidate holds non-negotiables (evidential honesty, fair recruitment, accessibility, assessment integrity) even when the threat arrives as a methodology choice or a generosity to a friend.
- Watch for succession, consultation, and self-interest scenarios, and score for whether the candidate's own exit or position changes their standard of evidence.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting portfolio-first behaviour at a management team — fighting a corner regardless of evidence, or public support with private relitigating.
- Missing a quiet fix or secret veto — absorbing slips without governance, exempting teams without telling the head.
- Rewarding vindication over usefulness — "as predicted" energy, or a public challenge with named insinuations.
- Missing the familiar-candidate slide — deliverability arguments introduced after scoring, criteria added post hoc.
- Crediting development sacrificed to convenience — coaching shed under pressure, succession decided purely by safety.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through weak-signal triage across teams, causal-requirement reading of standards, pairwise-consistency review of roadmaps, binding-constraint defence in programme plans, and selection-effect audits of arguments reaching management teams; portfolio aggregation done honestly, expected-value trees with their error bars showing, pool-limited projections, and the five-number quarterly synthesis that tells the true story including the part still unknown; exemption scoping, multi-trigger authorities, commitment-strength audits, sampling-frame reading, and the four-part findings template that makes assessment writing change things; and the lead fingerprints in situational judgement — development contracts honoured under pressure and across a candidate's own exit, collective pictures corrected in the collective room, recruitment integrity held from the chair, and severity clocks kept separate from ownership clocks when users are being harmed.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — many lead-level items reward the quality of reasoning and whether a response routes decisions through the right structures as much as the final choice. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
