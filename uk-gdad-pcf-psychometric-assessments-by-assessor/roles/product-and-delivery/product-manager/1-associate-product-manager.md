# Associate Product Manager - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for associate product manager. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A psychometric assessment is a structured, standardised exercise that measures the thinking skills and workplace behaviours that predict success in a role. The assessments described in this guide are job-specific: they use the everyday materials of the job — backlogs, user stories, kanban boards, sprint plans, user research findings, stakeholder emails, and simple product data — rather than abstract puzzles with no connection to the work.

The role this guide is built for is responsible for features, components, or part of a product, and contributes to the delivery of value. Day to day, an associate product manager works with product managers to advocate for user needs, helps prioritise so the greatest value is delivered first, and manages a smaller product with support. All of that depends on four abilities these assessments measure directly: thinking logically about product information (cognitive ability), working confidently with numbers such as velocity and satisfaction scores (numeric reasoning), reading and understanding written material accurately (verbal reasoning), and choosing sensible actions in tricky workplace situations (situational judgement).

Each of the four sections follows the same pattern: an overview of what it measures and why employers use it; how its dimensions map to the role's named skills — Agile and Lean practices, applying user-centred insights, and stakeholder relationship management among them; graduated practice questions with full worked explanations; then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. This is an entry-level assessment: expect a wide range of prior exposure to formal testing, and score for clear reasoning within the job's real materials rather than for polish or speed. Encourage the candidate to use any ungraded warm-up items to settle in. Use each section's per-item timing as a guide for planning a session, and debrief against the explanations below, since the explanations carry the technique behind each answer, not just the letter.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract shapes or riddles: user stories, acceptance criteria, kanban boards, sprint plans, simple roadmaps, and notes from user research sessions.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, compared against a norm group at a similar level, and many platforms adapt difficulty as the candidate progresses. Most platforms offer a few ungraded practice questions first.

Product management is, at its heart, structured thinking. Even at associate level, the role brings together the views of a multidisciplinary team, user needs and business needs, and helps frame problems clearly — checking that stories are consistent, noticing when a plan contradicts itself, helping decide what the team should do first. A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates those demands in miniature, which is why performance on it predicts performance in the job.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Agile and Lean practices**: visualising and making visible the work of the team, on a kanban board or a sprint plan, requires spotting recurring patterns — work that keeps getting stuck, stories that always miss a required field.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Product management** and **Managing product outcomes**: making or recommending an evidence-based decision means reasoning correctly from the evidence available, and only from that evidence.
- **Error checking** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Product management**: providing a clear, open, and transparent framework for delivery means keeping backlogs, boards, and plans internally consistent, and noticing quickly when they are not.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Product management** and the duty to prioritise delivery of greatest value.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Creating value for money**: defining the problem, deciding when there is enough insight to decide, and identifying opportunities for reuse.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition on a kanban board**

Present the candidate with this scenario: a team's kanban board has five columns in a fixed order: Backlog, Ready, In progress, In review, Done. The working agreement says every card must move one column at a time, left to right, and never skip a column. Reviewing four cards' histories:

1. Card A: Backlog → Ready → In progress → In review → Done
2. Card B: Backlog → Ready → In progress → In review
3. Card C: Backlog → In progress → In review → Done
4. Card D: Backlog → Ready → In progress

Ask which card has broken the working agreement.

- A) Card A
- B) Card B
- C) Card C
- D) Card D

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Card A visits every column in order. Cards B and D simply have not finished yet — nothing says a card must reach Done, so an incomplete journey is not a breach. Card C jumps from Backlog to In progress, skipping Ready: the breach. This tests whether the candidate tests each option against the stated rule alone, without penalising cards for being merely unfinished when the rule says nothing about finishing — a card that skips Ready may not have been refined or estimated, which can cause mid-sprint surprises.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a definition of done**

A team's definition of done states: "A story is done only if it has passed accessibility checks and has been reviewed by another developer." A colleague says: "Story 42 has passed accessibility checks." Ask which one statement must be true.

- A) Story 42 is done.
- B) Story 42 has been reviewed by another developer.
- C) Story 42 is not done.
- D) Story 42 might or might not be done — it cannot be determined from this information alone.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The definition sets two conditions, and only one is confirmed; nothing is known about the other. Neither "done" nor "not done" can be concluded, and option B invents information never given. This tests whether the candidate concludes only what the information guarantees — the same honesty about what evidence does and does not show that an evidence-based product decision requires.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking user story format**

Ask the candidate to check that new user stories follow the template: "As a [type of user], I want [goal], so that [benefit]," reviewing four stories:

1. "As a benefits claimant, I want to save my application part-way through, so that I don't lose my progress."
2. "As a caseworker, I want to filter cases by date, so that I can find the oldest cases quickly."
3. "Add a print button to the confirmation page."
4. "As a first-time visitor, I want a plain-English explanation of eligibility, so that I know whether to apply."

Ask which story does not follow the template.

- A) Story 1
- B) Story 2
- C) Story 3
- D) Story 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Stories 1, 2 and 4 each name a user, a goal, and a benefit. Story 3 is a solution instruction with no user perspective, which makes it impossible to prioritise or test against. This tests whether the candidate scans each item methodically against the template rather than trusting general impression, and recognises that a backlog full of solution statements hides the user needs the role is meant to advocate for.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation with a value and effort matrix**

A team uses a simple two-by-two matrix: value to users (high or low) and effort to build (high or low), with capacity for one item this sprint. Item W: high value, low effort. Item X: high value, high effort. Item Y: low value, low effort. Item Z: low value, high effort. Ask which item the team should pick up first.

- A) Item W
- B) Item X
- C) Item Y
- D) Item Z

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Prefer high value, and for equal value prefer low effort. Item W delivers high value for low effort — the classic quick win. This tests whether the candidate applies a given framework as stated, without importing extra considerations the question has not mentioned.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Logical deduction about sprint scope**

Sprint plan facts: (i) the team can complete at most three stories this sprint; (ii) story P must be completed this sprint because of a legal deadline; (iii) story Q can only be done in the same sprint as story R, because they change the same page and must be tested together; (iv) story S has no constraints. Candidate stories are P, Q, R and S. Ask which combination of three stories is possible this sprint.

- A) P, Q, S
- B) P, Q, R
- C) Q, R, S — dropping P to fit both halves of the pair
- D) P, S, and half of Q

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A breaks rule (iii) by including Q without R. Option C drops P, breaking the legal deadline rule. Option D splits a story, which no rule permits. Option B satisfies every rule. This tests whether the candidate eliminates options that break any stated rule, rather than judging by instinct — a plan that violates a hard constraint is not a plan, however attractive it looks.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in retrospective themes**

A log of the top issue at each of the last six sprint retrospectives: Sprint 1 "Stories were too big to finish"; Sprint 2 "Testing environment was down"; Sprint 3 "Stories were too big to finish"; Sprint 4 "Unclear acceptance criteria"; Sprint 5 "Stories were too big to finish"; Sprint 6 "Unclear acceptance criteria". Ask what the log suggests the team should tackle first.

- A) The testing environment is the biggest problem, because infrastructure always matters most.
- B) Story sizing is the most persistent issue, appearing in three of six sprints, with acceptance criteria an emerging second theme.
- C) There is no pattern — retrospectives raise random issues.
- D) The team should stop holding retrospectives because the same issues keep coming up.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sizing recurs three times; acceptance criteria twice, both in the second half; the environment issue once. Option A promotes a one-off issue based on a general belief rather than this data; C ignores obvious repetition; D confuses a process surfacing recurring problems with the process failing. This tests reading frequency and recency from a small log — a core part of visualising the team's work and helping it improve.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a roadmap against a plan**

A product roadmap says: "Quarter 1: private beta with invited users. Quarter 2: public beta. Quarter 3: add save-and-return feature. Quarter 4: live." A Quarter 1 sprint plan includes a story: "Promote the service on the department's public homepage so anyone can use it." Ask what the inconsistency is.

- A) There is no inconsistency; promotion is always good.
- B) The story belongs in Quarter 3, alongside save-and-return.
- C) The story invites the general public into the service during private beta, which contradicts "invited users only" and belongs no earlier than the public beta in Quarter 2.
- D) The roadmap is wrong and should be rewritten to match the sprint plan.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The roadmap restricts Quarter 1 to invited users; the story would open the service to anyone. Option D reverses the burden of proof — a single story does not overturn an agreed roadmap without a deliberate decision. This tests comparing two artefacts pairwise rather than judging each alone, part of providing a clear, open and transparent framework for delivery.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritisation of incoming requests**

Four requests arrive within an hour: (1) a developer reports a story blocked because acceptance criteria contradict each other; (2) a stakeholder emails asking for a new report feature "sometime soon"; (3) the product manager asks for two slides for Thursday's show-and-tell; (4) a user researcher shares interesting findings, no action requested. Ask for the most sensible order to deal with them.

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — stakeholders come first
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — unblock the team, then meet the Thursday deadline, then respond to the request, then read the findings
- C) 4, 1, 2, 3 — always read research first
- D) 3, 1, 4, 2 — the product manager outranks the team

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is urgent and high impact — the team is stopped now. Item 3 has a real deadline next. Item 2 needs only a brief acknowledgement today. Item 4 needs no action yet. Option A confuses stakeholder importance with urgency; D prioritises by seniority rather than impact. This tests the habit of asking "what happens if this waits an hour?" — for item 1, a whole team waits with it.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with user insight**

Analytics show 40% of users abandon an online form at the address page. Further evidence: (a) two of eight research participants could not find their address in the postcode lookup; (b) support desk logs show "address problems" as the most common call topic; (c) a stakeholder believes the page "looks old-fashioned". Ask for the strongest recommendation for the next step.

- A) Redesign the page's visual style, because the stakeholder's view came from the most senior person.
- B) Investigate and improve the postcode lookup, because three independent sources point to users failing to enter an address, then re-check the abandonment rate.
- C) Remove the address page entirely.
- D) Wait for another quarter of analytics before doing anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three independent sources converge on the postcode lookup — triangulation, the strongest evidence available here. Option A prioritises an opinion with no user evidence behind it; C deletes a step that may be necessary with no evidence it is optional; D delays despite already-converging evidence. This tests combining quantitative and qualitative insight, and — the final clause of the correct option — pairing a recommended change with a re-check, which is what managing product outcomes looks like in practice.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-step deduction about a release**

A team releases to live only on Wednesdays. A release must first pass a security review, taking exactly two working days and starting no sooner than the working day after code is finished. Code for a new feature finishes on a Thursday. Assuming no weekend working and no holidays, ask for the earliest Wednesday the feature can be released.

- A) The Wednesday six days after the code is finished
- B) The Wednesday immediately before the security review
- C) The Wednesday thirteen days after the code is finished
- D) The Wednesday of the same week the code is finished

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Review starts the next working day (Friday), takes two working days (Friday, Monday), completing end of Monday. The next release day is the following Wednesday — six days after the Thursday finish. Option D is impossible, since that Wednesday has already passed. This tests explicit, step-by-step calendar counting rather than mental juggling — a skipped day can silently cost a team a whole week when planning around fixed release windows.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Spotting the flaw in an argument**

At a planning meeting, a colleague argues: "Feature F was released last month, and this month our user satisfaction score went up. Therefore Feature F caused the improvement, and we should build more features like F." Ask for the best assessment of this argument.

- A) The argument is sound: the improvement came after the release, so the release caused it.
- B) The argument is weak: satisfaction rose after the release, but other changes or seasonal factors could explain it; the team should look for supporting evidence, such as usage of Feature F and user feedback mentioning it, before generalising.
- C) The argument is wrong: satisfaction scores never mean anything.
- D) The argument is irrelevant: only business needs matter, not satisfaction.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The flaw is the leap from "after" to "because of" — sequence does not prove causation. Options C and D discard useful evidence entirely, an equally unbalanced response. This tests recognising an unproven-not-false claim and seeking corroborating evidence, alongside understanding why a baseline and a comparison group are needed before a rising score becomes a conclusion rather than a hint.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling constraints across three artefacts**

Three artefacts: the roadmap says "the payment feature ships before the reminder feature"; the dependency note says "the reminder feature must ship in the same release as, or earlier than, the account page redesign"; the stakeholder commitment says "the account page redesign ships in Release 2." Releases are numbered 1, 2, 3 in time order, each feature shipping in exactly one release. Ask for the latest release in which the payment feature can ship.

- A) Release 1
- B) Release 2
- C) Release 3
- D) It cannot be determined

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The redesign is fixed at Release 2; the reminder must ship at or before that, so Release 1 or 2 at the latest; payment must ship strictly before reminder's release. If reminder took its latest slot (Release 2), payment must be Release 1 — and no valid arrangement allows reminder later than Release 2. This tests anchoring on the fixed fact and propagating constraints one step at a time, and precise reading of ordering words ("before" versus "no later than").

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate tests every option against every stated rule, rather than answering from general product opinion.
- Watch for whether the candidate treats an unfinished-but-rule-consistent item as a defect.
- Note whether the candidate ranks incoming work by impact and urgency together, rather than by who is most senior.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes correlation from causation and looks for corroborating evidence.
- Encourage written working on calendar and sequencing questions; penalise silent mental arithmetic errors gently at this level, since the habit of writing it down is itself part of what is being assessed.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer drawn from general product opinion rather than the facts given in the scenario.
- Marking an incomplete-but-rule-consistent item as if it were a defect.
- Rewarding an answer that ranks work by seniority of the requester rather than by impact and urgency.
- Accepting a causal claim supported only by sequence ("it happened after").
- Missing careless slips on early, easy questions under time pressure — these cost the same marks as errors on hard questions.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately a candidate works with the kinds of numbers this role genuinely produces: sprint velocity, completion rates, user satisfaction scores, uptake percentages, cost per transaction, and small tables and charts of analytics data. This is not an exam in advanced mathematics — percentages, ratios, averages, rates, and simple trends are the everyday arithmetic of product work.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes, with 15 to 25 questions. Each question presents a small dataset — a table, a chart, or a short description with numbers — and asks the candidate to extract, calculate, or compare values. A basic calculator is usually allowed.

Value, a central concern of this role, is very often expressed in numbers: estimating expected benefits, explaining the need for baseline data, using analytics as evidence for a decision, and comparing rough numeric estimates of value and effort when prioritising. A product person who misreads a percentage or confuses a rate with a count can steer a team confidently in the wrong direction, which is why this assessment matters even at entry level.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and rates** map to **Managing product outcomes**: completion rates, drop-off rates and satisfaction percentages, compared against baseline data.
- **Averages and simple trends** map to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Product management**: sprint velocity is an average, and its trend helps plan realistic increments.
- **Ratios and proportions** map to **Applying user-centred insights**: research findings arrive as proportions, and analytics as ratios of users taking different paths.
- **Cost and benefit arithmetic** maps to **Creating value for money**: estimating benefits and identifying savings from reuse.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: presenting numbers honestly starts with reading them correctly.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading a completion rate**

Last week, 1,200 users started an online application form and 900 completed it. Ask for the completion rate.

- A) 90%
- B) 75%
- C) 66%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 900 ÷ 1,200 = 0.75 = 75% — option B. This tests basic rate calculation, and, ideally, a note that the drop-off rate is the complementary 25% — a common trap is reporting the wrong one of the pair by reflex.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average sprint velocity**

A team completed 18, 22, 20 and 24 story points in its last four sprints. Ask for the average velocity.

- A) 20 points
- B) 21 points
- C) 22 points
- D) 24 points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (18+22+20+24) ÷ 4 = 84 ÷ 4 = 21 — option B. This tests simple averaging, and in a strong candidate's answer, the observation that an average smooths over real variation (18 to 24), so the range is also worth quoting in planning conversations.

**Question 3 (easy) — Percentage increase in users**

A service had 4,000 active users in March and 5,000 in April. Ask for the percentage increase.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 10%
- D) 125%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (5,000 − 4,000) ÷ 4,000 = 0.25 = 25% — option B. Option A divides the change by the new figure instead of the original. This tests anchoring percentage change on the starting value, a calculation used constantly when reporting product outcomes.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reading a simple table**

A monthly analytics summary shows sessions by device: Mobile 6,300; Desktop 2,700; Tablet 1,000. Ask what proportion of sessions came from mobile devices.

- A) 63%
- B) 6,300%
- C) 53%
- D) 70%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total 10,000; 6,300 ÷ 10,000 = 63% — option A. This tests computing the whole before the share, avoiding division by the wrong denominator, and recognises why "63% of sessions are on mobile" is exactly the kind of clear, evidence-based statement that moves a prioritisation conversation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Drop-off between form pages**

A five-page form had these page-view counts: Page 1 2,000; Page 2 1,800; Page 3 1,350; Page 4 1,300; Page 5 1,250. Ask between which two consecutive pages the largest percentage drop-off occurs, measured as a percentage of the earlier page's views.

- A) Page 1 to Page 2
- B) Page 2 to Page 3
- C) Page 3 to Page 4
- D) Page 4 to Page 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Page 1→2: 10%. Page 2→3: 25%. Page 3→4: ≈3.7%. Page 4→5: ≈3.8%. The largest is Page 2 to 3 — option B. This tests computing each step as a percentage of the earlier page rather than comparing raw drops, and recognises this as how a product team finds the problem page in a journey.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Satisfaction score change against baseline**

Baseline user satisfaction, before a redesign, was 72%. The target is 80% within two quarters. After one quarter the score is 76%. Ask for the statement that best describes progress.

- A) The target has been met.
- B) The score has improved by 4 percentage points, which is half of the 8-point improvement needed, with one of two quarters elapsed.
- C) The score has improved by 4%, so the redesign has failed.
- D) Without a baseline it is impossible to say anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Needed improvement: 8 points; achieved so far: 4 points — halfway, with half the time elapsed, on track. Option C blurs percentage points with percent. This tests working correctly with percentage points against a baseline and target, and precise language between points and percent.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Cost saving from reuse**

An address-lookup component costs 40 developer-days to build from scratch, or 10 developer-days to integrate a reused component. A developer-day costs £450. Ask how much money reuse would save.

- A) £4,500
- B) £18,000
- C) £13,500
- D) £22,500

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Days saved: 40 − 10 = 30; 30 × £450 = £13,500 — option C. Option B is the from-scratch cost alone, forgetting that reuse still costs something; A is only the integration cost. This tests the **Creating value for money** skill directly: identifying opportunities for reuse and putting a credible number on the saving.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratio of research findings**

In one round of usability testing, 6 of 8 participants completed the main task. In the previous round, 9 of 12 did. Ask for the correct comparison of completion proportions.

- A) This round was better: 6 of 8 is 75%, versus 9 of 12 at 66%.
- B) The rounds had the same completion proportion: both are 75%.
- C) The previous round was better: 9 completions beat 6 completions.
- D) The comparison is impossible because the group sizes differ.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 6÷8 = 75%; 9÷12 = 75% — equal, option B. Option C compares raw counts across different group sizes, the most common ratio error. This tests converting to proportions before comparing, and — in a strong candidate's answer — appropriate caution given how small both samples are.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Cost per transaction trend**

Q1: 20,000 transactions, £50,000 total cost. Q2: 25,000 transactions, £55,000 total cost. Ask what happened to cost per transaction from Q1 to Q2.

- A) It rose from £2.50 to £2.75.
- B) It fell from £2.50 to £2.20.
- C) It stayed at £2.50.
- D) It fell from £2.75 to £2.50.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Q1: £50,000÷20,000=£2.50. Q2: £55,000÷25,000=£2.20 — a fall, option B, even though total cost rose. This tests recognising that totals and unit rates can move in opposite directions, and being able to say why total cost rose (volume) while unit cost fell.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Projecting uptake against a target**

Target: 60% of applications online by end of Quarter 4. Q1: 8,000 online, 12,000 paper. Q2: 10,000 online, 10,000 paper. Q3: 12,000 online, 8,000 paper. Ask for the projected Q4 online share if the trend continues at the same rate of change per quarter, and whether the target would be met.

- A) 55%; the target would be missed.
- B) 60%; the target would be exactly met.
- C) 70%; the target would be exceeded.
- D) 65%; the target would be exceeded.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Online shares: Q1 40%, Q2 50%, Q3 60% — rising 10 points per quarter, so Q4 projects to 70%, comfortably exceeding the target already reached in Q3. This tests converting to shares before spotting a trend, and — in a strong answer — treating a straight-line projection as an assumption rather than a fact, since uptake curves often flatten among users who face real barriers to digital channels.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Weighted average satisfaction**

800 citizens rated satisfaction at 85%; 200 caseworkers rated it at 60%. Ask for the overall satisfaction across all 1,000 respondents.

- A) 72.5%
- B) 80%
- C) 85%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Satisfied citizens: 680; satisfied caseworkers: 120; total 800 ÷ 1,000 = 80% — option B, not the unweighted average of 72.5%. This tests weighting by group size rather than averaging percentages directly, and — in a strong answer — the observation that a blended 80% headline hides that caseworkers are far less happy, which should point the next round of research.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Estimating benefit of a feature**

Support desk receives 1,500 calls per month; 40% are password-reset requests, each costing £6 to handle. A proposed self-service feature removes 70% of password-reset calls. Ask for the expected monthly saving.

- A) £3,600
- B) £2,520
- C) £6,300
- D) £900

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Password-reset calls: 600; removed: 420; saving: 420 × £6 = £2,520 — option B. Option A assumes all 600 disappear; C applies 70% to all 1,500 calls. This tests applying each percentage to the correct base and writing intermediate figures down, and stating assumptions openly so stakeholders can challenge them.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate rehearses the four core recipes correctly: rate (part ÷ whole), change (new − old ÷ old), average (total ÷ count) and weighted combination.
- Note whether the candidate writes down intermediate results on multi-step questions.
- Watch for whether the candidate checks "percentage of what?" before dividing.
- Score for correct use of percentage points versus percent.
- Note whether the candidate treats a projection as an assumption rather than a certainty when the question allows for that distinction.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage change divided by the new value instead of the original.
- Missing when a candidate compares raw counts across groups of different sizes instead of converting to proportions.
- Rewarding an unweighted average of percentages from unequal groups.
- Accepting a "saving" that ignores the residual cost of the alternative (reuse still costs something; a feature only removes some calls).
- Missing when a candidate answers the wrong figure under time pressure — completion instead of drop-off, totals instead of unit cost.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads, understands and draws conclusions from written material of the kind this role deals with daily: service standards, policy extracts, stakeholder emails, user research summaries, meeting notes, and product documentation.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes. A short passage is followed by questions, most commonly "true / false / cannot say": whether a statement is guaranteed true by the passage, contradicted by it, or impossible to determine from it alone. Other formats include picking the best summary, identifying a sound inference, and judging which sentence communicates a point most clearly. The governing rule is: answer only from the passage, never from outside knowledge.

So much of product work is reading and writing under time pressure: reading policy and standards to ensure teams comply with them, reading research findings to know what they do and do not show, reading stakeholder emails to grasp exactly what is being asked, promised or assumed, and writing user stories, slides and updates where a fuzzy sentence can send a team in the wrong direction. Careful reading is also a quiet act of user advocacy, since many of the documents this role interprets exist to protect users.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Comprehension of standards and policy** maps to the duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: knowing precisely what a standard requires, permits, and is silent about.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Product management**: being honest about what evidence actually states, without stretching it.
- **Inference** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Strategic ownership**: drawing sound conclusions from documents and explaining how decisions align to organisational strategy.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Agile and Lean practices**: plain, unambiguous sentences.
- **Reading research summaries accurately** maps to **Applying user-centred insights**: qualitative findings read for exactly what they claim.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 6, the candidate reads each passage and decides whether the statement is True (definitely follows from the passage), False (definitely contradicted), or Cannot say (the passage does not give enough information). A fourth option is included where relevant.

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading a service standard extract**

Passage: "Teams building government services must carry out user research with people who have access needs. Research should take place throughout the service's development, not only at the start. Teams should also test their service with users of assistive technology."

Statement: "Teams must carry out user research with people who have access needs."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only during private beta

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The statement matches the passage's first sentence closely: True. Option D adds a restriction the passage contradicts, since research should happen "throughout the service's development". This tests careful matching against the exact words — confirming no qualifier has been quietly added or dropped.

**Question 2 (easy) — What the passage does not say**

Passage: "The department's content style guide requires services to use plain English. Sentences should average no more than 25 words. Technical terms may be used where the audience is specialist, provided they are explained on first use."

Statement: "The style guide bans all technical terms."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for citizen-facing services only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly permits technical terms in defined circumstances, directly contradicting a blanket ban: False. This tests the distinction between False (contradicted) and Cannot say (unaddressed) — a rule read for what it actually permits, not a stricter version that feels safer.

**Question 3 (easy) — Cannot say discipline**

Passage: "In last month's usability testing, five of the six participants completed the renewal journey without assistance. Two participants commented that the confirmation email arrived later than they expected."

Statement: "Most users of the service can complete the renewal journey without assistance."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because five out of six is 83%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Six research participants are not the whole user population, and the passage says nothing about how representative they were: Cannot say. Option D's arithmetic is correct but the leap from a small sample to "most users" is exactly the overgeneralisation being tested — deciding when there is enough insight to act versus when more is needed.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Inference from a stakeholder email**

Passage: "Thanks for the demo. I remain concerned that the new eligibility checker could increase calls to our contact centre if people don't understand their result. Before we agree to a public launch, I'd like to see evidence from the private beta about how users respond to 'not eligible' outcomes."

Statement: "The stakeholder has refused to allow a public launch."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, until the contact centre is expanded

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email sets a condition ("before we agree") rather than refusing — agreement is anticipated once the evidence is seen: False. This tests distinguishing a conditional "yes, if" from a "no" — hearing exactly what would satisfy a stakeholder is part of building consensus with them.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Comparing accounts in meeting notes**

Passage: "The developer reported that the postcode lookup fails for around 2% of lookups, mainly for new-build addresses. The service manager noted that all failed lookups currently result in a support call. The team agreed to add a manual address entry option in the next sprint."

Statement: "Adding manual address entry will eliminate support calls about addresses."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the team agreed to it

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes a chain of facts but "eliminate" is a stronger claim than the passage makes: Cannot say. Option D confuses a team's decision to try a fix with evidence it will fully succeed. This tests recognising the difference between an expectation and a promise — pairing a change with a measurable expectation is the professional habit being modelled.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Precise reading of a policy constraint**

Passage: "Applicants may submit supporting documents digitally. Where an applicant cannot use the digital route, a paper alternative must be provided. Paper submissions must be processed within the same service level as digital submissions."

Statement: "Paper submissions may be processed more slowly than digital submissions, provided applicants are warned."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, during peak periods only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The equal-service-level requirement directly contradicts the statement: False. No warning exception or peak-period exception (option D) appears anywhere. This tests whether the candidate accepts an invented, administratively plausible qualification not present in the passage — precise reading here is user advocacy for the people least able to complain.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Best summary of a passage**

Passage: "The team proposed three options for the renewal reminder. Option 1, email only, is cheapest but excludes users without email. Option 2, email plus SMS, costs more but reaches nearly all users. Option 3, email plus post, reaches everyone but costs the most and is slowest. The recommendation is Option 2, with post available on request for users who need it."

Ask which sentence best summarises the passage.

- A) The team recommends the cheapest reminder option.
- B) The team recommends email plus SMS reminders, with postal reminders on request, balancing reach against cost.
- C) The team recommends postal reminders because they reach everyone.
- D) The team cannot decide between the three options.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B preserves the decision and its reasoning without adding or losing substance. This tests finding a passage's conclusion sentence and checking each candidate summary against it — the show-and-tell skill of compressing a decision into one faithful sentence.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Identifying an assumption**

Passage (business case): "Moving the guidance content from PDFs to web pages will improve task completion, because users will no longer need to download files on mobile devices."

Ask which unstated assumption the argument relies on.

- A) That a meaningful number of users are on mobile devices and find downloads a barrier to completing tasks.
- B) That PDFs are cheaper to produce than web pages.
- C) That the guidance content is currently accurate.
- D) That users prefer longer documents.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The mechanism (no more mobile downloads) only produces the outcome if downloads are actually a barrier for enough users to move the completion figure — the unstated load-bearing premise: option A. This tests stripping an argument to its skeleton and asking "what would have to be true for this to work?" — a habit that points directly at what user research should test next.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Two-condition rule with an exception**

Passage: "All new features must have analytics tracking agreed before development begins, unless the feature is classified as a security fix. Security fixes must instead be reviewed by the security team before release. Feature F began development last week. Analytics tracking for Feature F has not been agreed."

Statement: "Feature F is classified as a security fix."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say — unless we also know Feature F complied with the rules
- D) The passage contradicts itself

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two possibilities remain consistent with the passage: F is a security fix and compliant, or F is an ordinary feature and non-compliant. This tests whether the candidate checks for a legitimate exception before concluding a rule breach — the same reasoning shape as real governance conversations, where an apparent breach might be a legitimate exemption.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement**

Ask which sentence best communicates a status update on a delayed feature.

- A) "Due to unforeseen circumstances impacting deliverables, timelines have been recalibrated going forward."
- B) "The document upload feature will now launch in June rather than April, because accessibility testing found issues with the file picker that we are fixing first."
- C) "There is a possibility that certain functionality may potentially experience some degree of rescheduling."
- D) "The feature is delayed. More soon."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B states what changed, why, and what happens next, in words a busy stakeholder can absorb in one reading. Option A is jargon with no facts; C stacks hedges until nothing is claimed; D is honest but too thin. This tests judging a sentence against those three tests, and recognising that an accessibility fix is a legitimate, nameable reason for delay rather than something to obscure.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference across a research summary**

Passage: "Across ten interviews, participants who had used the old paper process described the new online service as faster. However, three participants who had never used the paper process found the online identity step confusing, and two of them abandoned it during the session. All participants who completed the identity step went on to finish the whole journey."

Statement: "The identity step is the only part of the journey where participants abandoned during the sessions."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for paper-experienced participants only

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Since everyone who completed identity finished, nobody abandoned at any later step, and the passage records abandonment only at identity: True, within the sessions described. This tests combining two sentences correctly, and reading the scope of a claim precisely — had the statement claimed the identity step is the only problem "with the service" rather than "during the sessions", the answer would be Cannot say.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling a strategy statement with a request**

Passage: "Our organisation prioritises reducing failure demand — contact caused by services not working well — over adding new functionality. Teams should demonstrate how proposed work reduces avoidable contact before committing to new features." A stakeholder asks the candidate's team to build a new document-download feature "because another department has one".

Statement: "According to the strategy, the team should ask what avoidable contact the download feature would reduce before committing to build it."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only if the other department's feature reduced contact

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The strategy requires demonstrating reduced avoidable contact before committing to new functionality: True. Option D smuggles in an irrelevant condition. This tests the **Strategic ownership** skill of noticing when a request has skipped the strategy's test, and raising it using the strategy's own language, rather than refusing a stakeholder single-handedly.

### Administration tips

- Score for the three-way True/False/Cannot say distinction: could the statement be false while everything in the passage stays true?
- Watch for whether the candidate answers from the passage alone rather than from outside knowledge or plausibility.
- Note whether the candidate catches quantifiers and scope words (all, only, must, some, most) and exception clauses (unless, provided that).
- Score for whether the candidate generalises from a small research sample without noting the limits of the sample.
- For clear-writing items, score on whether every sentence answers "what, why, what next" rather than tone alone.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement marked True because it is plausible or matches general experience.
- Marking a statement False because the passage does not mention it, rather than Cannot say.
- Missing when a candidate overlooks an exception clause and answers as if the general rule had no boundary.
- Rewarding a generalisation from a small research sample to the whole user population.
- Accepting an invented qualifier in an answer option that is not present in the passage.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks the candidate to judge how effective different responses would be. Unlike the other assessments in this guide, there is rarely a single "provable" answer; responses are scored against what experienced practitioners and the profession's behavioural frameworks consider effective. Common formats include: pick the most effective response; pick the most and least effective; rank all responses; or rate each response independently.

Typical format: an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed, since it measures judgement rather than speed. There are no trick questions, but there are tempting wrong answers — usually responses that are fast, conflict-avoiding, or impressive-looking rather than genuinely effective.

This role is defined as much by behaviours as by knowledge: advocating for user needs throughout the organisation, working with multidisciplinary teams, communicating with stakeholders, and knowing when to handle something directly versus when to involve a product manager. At entry level, the judgement being tested is rarely "can the candidate solve this alone?" — it is whether they take ownership of what is theirs, seek support at the right moment, stay honest under pressure, and keep users at the centre.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **User focus scenarios** map to the duty to **work with product managers to advocate for user needs throughout the organisation** and to **Applying user-centred insights**: the effective response keeps user evidence in the conversation, especially when inconvenient.
- **Team collaboration scenarios** map to **Product leadership** and the duty to **work with multidisciplinary teams**: respecting the expertise of designers, developers and researchers.
- **Prioritisation and ownership scenarios** map to **Product management**: taking ownership of what is genuinely theirs at associate level, honest about trade-offs.
- **Escalation scenarios** map to **managing a smaller product with support**: knowing when to inform or involve a product manager; both over- and under-escalating are marked down.
- **Stakeholder scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: clear, early, honest communication and consensus built on evidence.
- **Standards and inclusion scenarios** map to **ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards**: accessibility and assisted digital treated as non-negotiable.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A stakeholder asks for research to be skipped**

A stakeholder tells the candidate, who looks after a small feature within a larger product: "We already know what users want — just build the new form and skip the research this time. It'll be quicker." The user researcher believes at least a short round of testing is needed. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Agree to skip research — the stakeholder knows the policy area better than the team.
- B) Refuse outright and tell the stakeholder that research is mandatory, ending the conversation.
- C) Acknowledge the time pressure, explain what a small, fast round of testing would cover and what risks it removes, and agree with the product manager and researcher a proportionate approach.
- D) Say yes to the stakeholder in the meeting, then quietly arrange the research anyway.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C keeps research in the plan while acknowledging time pressure, involves the right people, and stays honest. Option A abandons user advocacy on policy knowledge alone; B protects users but damages the relationship unnecessarily and skips involving the product manager; D is dishonest, and discovered dishonesty destroys future trust. This tests the pattern: acknowledge the pressure, offer a proportionate path, involve the right people, stay honest.

**Question 2 (easy) — Spotting a mistake of one's own**

The candidate prepared the sprint board on Monday, and on Wednesday realises they accidentally marked a story as "Done" that has not passed its accessibility checks. Show-and-tell is tomorrow. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say nothing — it will probably pass the checks eventually.
- B) Correct the board immediately, tell the team and the product manager what happened, and check whether the story can complete its checks before the show-and-tell or should be excluded from the demo.
- C) Quietly fix the board but tell no one, hoping nobody noticed.
- D) Wait until after the show-and-tell to fix it, to avoid awkward questions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B fixes the error at once, tells the people affected, and manages the consequence proactively. Options A and D let stakeholders be shown untrue information; C hides the learning from the team. This tests ownership and transparency around a candidate's own mistake — a particularly telling signal because the check being skipped is accessibility, a genuine user-exclusion risk if treated casually.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

A product manager asked the candidate on Monday to spend the week preparing the backlog for next sprint. On Wednesday, a senior stakeholder from another team emails directly asking for two days pulling together a usage-statistics report — both cannot be done. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Drop the backlog work — the stakeholder is more senior than the product manager.
- B) Ignore the stakeholder's email until next week.
- C) Reply to the stakeholder acknowledging the request, then talk to the product manager the same day about the conflict so the priority can be agreed together and a realistic answer given.
- D) Try to do both by working the weekend, telling no one there is a conflict.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C acknowledges promptly, surfaces the conflict to the accountable owner the same day, and returns a realistic, agreed answer. Option A lets seniority override an agreed plan without its owner knowing; D hides a real capacity problem and sets an unsustainable precedent. This tests whether the candidate recognises that resolving a genuine priority conflict is not theirs to decide alone at this level, without either capitulating or hiding.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Developers disagree with the priority**

In sprint planning, the candidate proposes fixing address-lookup problems, based on analytics and support-call data. Two developers argue the priority should be upgrading a software framework, warning the old version loses security support in four months. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Insist on the address fix — product priorities are set by product people, and the data supports it.
- B) Give in to the developers to keep the peace, and schedule the address fix for "sometime later".
- C) Ask the developers to help explain the upgrade's urgency and size, share the user data, and look for a plan that addresses both — for example sequencing the upgrade within its four-month window while the address fix proceeds — checking the proposal with the product manager.
- D) End the meeting and add both items to the backlog unranked, to be decided another time.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C treats the disagreement as missing information on both sides, finds room to sequence both, and checks the proposal at the right level given the security implications. Option A overrules technical risk advice by role authority; B silently deprioritises evidenced user harm; D postpones without a decision mechanism. This tests balancing user needs and business needs inside a multidisciplinary team, neither commanding nor capitulating.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Research findings are unwelcome**

Usability testing of a feature the team spent six weeks building shows most participants could not complete the journey. A colleague suggests presenting only the positive findings at tomorrow's show-and-tell "to keep morale up". Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Agree — morale matters, and the negative findings can come out later once fixes are underway.
- B) Suggest presenting the findings in full, framed as the research doing its job: what users struggled with, what the team will try next, and what was learned — and discuss with the researcher how to present it fairly.
- C) Present only the negative findings, to shock stakeholders into giving the team more time.
- D) Cancel the show-and-tell.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is honest, keeps user struggles as the headline the product must fix, involves the researcher, and protects morale through framing rather than omission. Option A is selective reporting that damages trust once the full picture emerges; C manipulates in the other direction; D avoids the conversation entirely. This tests recognising that any option whose mechanism is controlling what others are allowed to know is rarely the effective one.

**Question 6 (moderate) — An accessibility issue late in the sprint**

Two days before a planned release, a tester finds the new form cannot be completed using a keyboard alone — a barrier for users who cannot use a mouse, and a failure against accessibility standards. Fixing it delays release by about a week. A stakeholder urges: "Release now, fix it in the next iteration — hardly anyone uses keyboard-only navigation." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Release on time — the stakeholder has accepted the risk, and the fix is coming anyway.
- B) Explain that the barrier excludes users and breaches the standards the team must meet, recommend delaying the release, and raise it promptly with the product manager so the decision is made at the right level with full information.
- C) Release on time but add a note to the site apologising to keyboard users.
- D) Refuse to discuss any release until every known defect of any kind is fixed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B advocates clearly, recommends rather than unilaterally decides (right for this level on a release decision), and escalates promptly with full information before the deadline forces the issue. Option A treats "hardly anyone" as evidence rather than a guess; C ships a known exclusion with an apology attached; D overcorrects by refusing all nuance. This tests distinguishing barriers that exclude classes of users (release-blocking by default) from cosmetic defects, and putting the decision where accountability sits.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A quiet voice in the team**

Over several sprints, a junior content designer rarely speaks in planning but often shares sharp, useful observations one to one afterwards. In today's session, the team is about to commit to important wording, and the candidate suspects the content designer has concerns. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say nothing — if the concerns mattered, the content designer would raise them.
- B) After the meeting, tell the content designer they need to speak up more.
- C) In the meeting, create space directly: "Before we commit, I'd like to hear the content view — [name], what's your read on this wording?" — and separately explore with them how they'd prefer to feed in.
- D) Raise their concerns personally in the meeting, presenting them as the candidate's own observations.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C acts at the moment input can still change the outcome, gives the expert a direct opening, and follows up privately for a sustainable pattern. Option A mistakes silence for absence of views; B locates the problem entirely in the individual and arrives after the decision; D erases the designer's authorship. This tests designing moments where every discipline's insight reaches the decision, rather than assuming meeting confidence equals having no views.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Asked a question beyond the candidate's knowledge**

At a show-and-tell, a senior stakeholder asks directly: "What would it cost to extend this feature to the legacy casework system?" Integration has never been scoped, and the product manager is on leave. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Give a rough figure off the top of the head to appear competent, and refine it later.
- B) Say it is not known and move quickly to the next demo item.
- C) Say the integration has not yet been scoped, so any figure now would be a guess; offer to take the question away, involve the technical lead, and come back with a considered estimate by a stated date.
- D) Suggest the stakeholder ask the product manager when they return from leave.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C is honest about the current state, explains why guessing is harmful, and converts the question into an owned action with the right expertise and a committed date. Option A risks a guessed figure being repeated and defended long after it is forgotten as a guess; B drops the stakeholder's genuine need; D reads as deflection rather than ownership. This tests the "I don't know, and here is how you'll get a reliable answer" pattern, which builds more confidence than any improvised figure.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Pressure to inflate a benefits estimate**

A drafted benefits estimate is around £40,000 per year in reduced support costs, based on call-volume data. A stakeholder reviewing it says: "Can you make it £100,000? The bid will land better, and these numbers are always fuzzy anyway." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Change it to £100,000 — estimates are indeed uncertain, and the stakeholder owns the bid.
- B) Keep £40,000 in the draft, explain the evidence behind it, offer to show a range with stated assumptions (including any genuine upside the stakeholder can identify evidence for), and flag the conversation to the product manager.
- C) Compromise at £70,000 without new evidence, since it is between the two positions.
- D) Refuse and report the stakeholder to their line manager for attempted misconduct.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Options A and C cross an integrity floor — presenting figures known to be unsupported, whether inflated fully or halfway. Option B holds the evidential line while staying collaborative, offering a range with explicit assumptions, and appropriately informs the accountable manager without accusation. Option D jumps past every proportionate step. This tests whether the candidate stays firm on the number, warm with the person, and transparent with their manager.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective**

A team's velocity has dropped for three consecutive sprints. Developers say stories keep arriving without clear acceptance criteria, causing work to bounce back and forth. The candidate writes many of those stories, having recently taken on backlog preparation. Ask for the ranking:

1. Acknowledge openly that story quality is part of the problem, ask the team to help define what "ready" should mean, and agree a definition of ready to apply — reviewing together in two sprints whether bounce-backs have fallen.
2. Point out that developers should ask questions earlier instead of letting stories bounce.
3. Say nothing in the retrospective, but privately resolve to write better stories.
4. Suggest the team stops estimating velocity so the drop is no longer visible.

- A) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 2; least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 1; least effective: 2
- D) Most effective: 3; least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 models ownership without self-flagellation, converts criticism into a team-owned improvement, and builds in measurement. Response 4 does not merely fail to fix the problem — it dismantles the instrument that revealed it, corrupting transparency for every future issue too. Responses 2 and 3 are weaker but less destructive. This tests judging least-effective candidates by which one actively makes things worse, not merely which is unimpressive.

**Question 11 (challenging) — A shortcut with users' data**

A colleague suggests emailing a prototype link to real service users whose addresses appear in the live support system, "since they've contacted us anyway". The candidate is fairly sure those addresses were collected only for support queries, and the researcher usually recruits through a consented panel. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Send the emails — the users did contact the service, and feedback benefits everyone.
- B) Pause the idea, say that using support contact details for research may go beyond what the data was collected for, and check with the user researcher and, where needed, the team responsible for data protection before proceeding — offering the consented panel as the faster compliant route.
- C) Send the emails but add an unsubscribe link.
- D) Anonymously report the colleague to the information commissioner.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B notices the issue, names it plainly without accusation, routes it to the right people, and offers a compliant alternative that still meets the need for fast feedback. Option A treats "they contacted us" as consent, which it is not; C decorates rather than resolves the problem; D fails proportionality entirely before anything has even been sent. This tests raising a standards concern early, calmly and constructively, without needing to be the data protection expert.

**Question 12 (challenging) — A product manager is the problem**

A supportive product manager has verbally promised a delivery date to a senior stakeholder for a feature the team estimates will take at least twice that long. The team is being pressed to "make it work", with corners being discussed — skipping usability testing, deferring accessibility checks. The candidate is the most junior product person in the room. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Keep quiet — date commitments are the product manager's business, and challenging them could harm the working relationship.
- B) Speak to the product manager privately: share the team's estimate and the specific corners being discussed, ask how to help re-plan or re-scope, and say plainly that accessibility and usability checks being skipped is a worry.
- C) Email the senior stakeholder directly to say the date is impossible and was promised in error.
- D) Tell the team to ignore the date and work at a sustainable pace, and personally take responsibility.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is private (preserving the manager's standing), leads with evidence rather than blame, offers help, and is specific about which non-negotiables are being eroded. Option A is bystander silence despite having material information; C goes around the product manager to their own stakeholder; D countermands the manager and makes an unhonourable promise. This tests speaking up to one's own manager with evidence and respect, treating escalation as a ladder to climb one rung at a time rather than a binary between silence and going over someone's head.

### Administration tips

- Score for the recurring fingerprint across effective responses: users kept in the decision, honesty under discomfort, the right people involved at the right moment, alternatives offered rather than bare refusals.
- Check the candidate's instinct against the escalation ladder appropriate to this level: handle it directly → inform the product manager → involve the product manager → involve specialists → wider escalation.
- Note whether the candidate favours options that add transparency or measurement over options that merely feel decisive.
- For least-effective judgements, score for identifying the option that hides a signal, deceives someone, or actively worsens the situation — not merely the option that is timid.
- Watch for whether the candidate notices harms to users who are not in the room — accessibility, assisted digital, data protection.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that avoids conflict rather than resolves it — giving in, staying silent, hiding bad news.
- Rewarding a maximal escalation (reporting a colleague to a regulator or senior manager) as a first response, rather than the lowest effective rung of the ladder.
- Missing when a candidate overplays their authority at this level — unilaterally overruling developers, refusing releases single-handedly, countermanding a manager.
- Missing when a candidate underplays their responsibility — deflecting a follow-up that is squarely theirs to own.
- Crediting any option involving inflated figures, filtered findings, or quiet concealment, however sympathetic the motive.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through a substantial piece of assessment material, built entirely from the materials of the associate product manager role: checking boards and stories against rules, deducing only what evidence guarantees, sequencing work through constraints, and prioritising by impact and urgency; completion rates, velocity averages, percentage changes, weighted satisfaction scores, and benefit estimates built step by honest step; the true/false/cannot-say reading discipline, scope words, exception clauses, and plain writing; and the moments that define this role level — advocating for users under pressure, owning mistakes openly, escalating proportionately, and keeping honesty intact when it would be easier not to.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone, and remember that this is an entry-level assessment: a wide range of prior test-taking exposure is normal, and the harder questions are stretch items rather than a pass mark. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
