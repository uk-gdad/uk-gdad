# Product Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for product manager. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. For a product manager, that means far more than abstract puzzles: the assessments described here are job-specific, using the artefacts the role genuinely handles — prioritised roadmaps, KPIs, benefit analyses, user research findings, service standard assessments, stakeholder communications, and sprint data — and the decisions it genuinely makes, such as prioritising work for teams, deciding when a product should move to the next life cycle phase, escalating risks to value, and mediating between stakeholders whose needs pull in different directions.

A product manager is responsible for product quality and value, leading a product through the full life cycle from discovery through development, delivery and continuous improvement to retirement, balancing user needs and business needs, using trusted data to make decisions, and being open about how those decisions are made. The four assessments here map directly onto those demands: the cognitive section tests spotting logical inconsistencies in a roadmap; the numeric section tests interpreting a KPI dashboard accurately; the verbal section tests extracting precise meaning from a policy document or stakeholder email; and the situational judgement section tests choosing the most effective course of action when the team, the data, and the stakeholders point in different directions.

Each of the four sections follows the same pattern: an overview of what it measures and why employers use it; how its dimensions map to the role's named skills — from Agile and Lean practices to Strategic ownership; graduated practice questions with full worked explanations; then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score for clear reasoning grounded in the job's real materials, using each section's per-item timing as a guide for planning a session. Several situational and verbal reasoning items deliberately test judgement rather than a single provable answer — debrief against the explanations below, which carry the technique and the professional reasoning, not just the correct letter.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles: roadmaps, backlogs, KPI definitions, life cycle plans, dependency maps, and benefit chains.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level, often adaptive, and typically reported as speed versus accuracy rather than a single score. Most platforms offer short, ungraded practice questions first.

For a product manager, cognitive assessment is directly relevant because the core professional value of the role is structured decision-making amid competing signals: addressing uncertainty while managing scope, holding several moving parts in mind at once — which backlog items serve which outcomes, which dependencies constrain which releases, where a stakeholder claim contradicts the analytics. The role also carries a quality-assurance responsibility — ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards and that a product is developed in line with the right service standards for its phase — which is fundamentally an error-checking discipline.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Managing product outcomes**: spotting recurring signals in sprint data, retrospective themes and KPI movements, and distinguishing genuine trends from noise.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Product management** and **Creating value for money**: reasoning validly from evidence and constraints to their consequences, and no further.
- **Error checking** maps to **Life cycle management** and the duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: verifying that roadmaps, KPIs and benefit cases are internally consistent.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Creating value for money** and the duty to **make decisions on priorities for a team**: comparing effort to value and sequencing under constraint.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Product management**: decomposing messy, partly contradictory information into a decision the team can act on.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in sprint data**

A team's last six sprints completed: 20, 21, 19, 20, 12, 20 story points. During one of those sprints, two developers were seconded to a departmental incident response for a week. Ask which sprint most plausibly corresponds to the secondment, and what the pattern suggests about the team's underlying velocity.

- A) Sprint 5; underlying velocity is around 20 points, with sprint 5 an explained outlier.
- B) Sprint 5; underlying velocity is around 18.7 points, the average of all six sprints.
- C) Sprint 3; velocity is declining steadily.
- D) No sprint stands out; the data is too noisy to interpret.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Five sprints cluster tightly between 19 and 21; sprint 5's 12 sits far outside and coincides with the explained capacity loss. Underlying velocity is around 20 — option A. Option B's error is including a known, explained outlier in a planning average, dragging the figure down and misrepresenting real capacity. This tests distinguishing signal (the stable cluster) from explained noise (the secondment) — the same judgement needed to help teams measure and visualise outcomes.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a phase gate rule**

A governance framework states: "A service may move from private beta to public beta only if it has passed a service standard assessment and its accessibility audit has no outstanding high-severity issues." A service has passed its service standard assessment. Ask which one statement must be true.

- A) The service may move to public beta.
- B) The service may not move to public beta.
- C) The service may move to public beta if and only if its accessibility audit has no outstanding high-severity issues.
- D) The accessibility audit has been completed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two jointly necessary conditions govern the phase move; one is confirmed, the other unknown, so neither A nor B can be concluded — what remains is that the second condition is now decisive: option C. Option D confuses the requirement being met with the audit having occurred. This tests enumerating gate conditions explicitly — satisfied, failed, or unknown — rather than stopping at the first favourable fact, which is the daily texture of life cycle management.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a KPI definition**

Four drafted KPIs for a renewals service: (A) "Percentage of renewals completed online without assisted digital support, reported monthly." (B) "Median time from starting a renewal to receiving confirmation, reported monthly." (C) "Users feel more confident about the renewals process." (D) "Number of support calls per 1,000 completed renewals, reported monthly." Ask which one is not actually measurable as written.

- A) Option A
- B) Option B
- C) Option C
- D) Option D

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A, B and D each define a quantity, a population and a reporting cadence. C names a worthy outcome but no measure — no instrument, no baseline, no cadence. This tests whether the candidate tests each item against "could an analyst compute this tomorrow?" rather than judging plausibility, and — in a strong answer — proposes how to operationalise the aspiration (for example, a scaled survey question against a baseline) rather than simply rejecting it.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation by value and effort**

Four candidate roadmap items: Item 1 value 8, effort 2. Item 2 value 8, effort 8. Item 3 value 3, effort 2. Item 4 value 5, effort 5. Using value-to-effort ratio as the ordering rule, ask for the correct priority order.

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4
- B) 1, 3, 4, 2
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 1, 4, 3, 2

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Ratios: Item 1 = 4.0, Item 2 = 1.0, Item 3 = 1.5, Item 4 = 1.0. Ordering by ratio gives 1, 3, then a tie between 4 and 2, broken by preferring the smaller commitment (Item 4) first — giving 1, 3, 4, 2, option B. This tests computing and applying a given ratio-based framework consistently, including sensible tie-breaking, without importing unstated considerations.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction across roadmap dependencies**

Roadmap constraints: (i) identity integration must be live before save-and-return; (ii) save-and-return and the dashboard redesign must launch in the same quarter, since both change the account area; (iii) identity cannot go live before Q2, when the supplier contract starts; (iv) the dashboard redesign needs one full quarter of design work, starting no earlier than Q1. Ask for the earliest quarter in which save-and-return can launch.

- A) Q1
- B) Q2
- C) Q3
- D) Q4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Identity is live earliest during Q2; save-and-return needs identity live first, so cannot launch before Q3. The dashboard's design can start Q1 and be ready from Q2, so it can launch alongside save-and-return in Q3 without forcing a later date. This tests chaining constraints forward correctly, avoiding the common errors of treating "identity live in Q2" as compatible with a Q2 launch, or unnecessarily serialising the two parallel workstreams into Q4.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits chain**

A business case claims: "Automation will process checks faster → caseworkers will spend less time on routine checks → caseworker time will be redirected to complex cases → complex-case backlogs will fall → citizen waiting times for complex decisions will improve." Ask which link deserves the most scrutiny before the case goes to the investment board.

- A) Automation → faster processing of routine checks
- B) Faster routine processing → less caseworker time on routine checks
- C) Less routine time → time redirected to complex cases
- D) Falling complex backlogs → improved citizen waiting times

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Links A, B and D are close to mechanical or near-definitional. Link C assumes freed time automatically converts into redirected productive time, when in practice it can be absorbed by vacancies, leak elsewhere, or require training not yet in place — a technical effect quietly becoming an organisational outcome with no one deciding it. This tests scanning a causal chain for the link where a different kind of actor must act — almost always the weak one — a core part of analysing benefit against anticipated outcomes.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Prioritisation under competing pressures**

Monday morning, four items compete: (1) the analytics dashboard shows completions fell 30% over the weekend, cause unknown; (2) a service standard assessment is scheduled Thursday, with an evidence pack needing final review (about two hours); (3) a senior stakeholder wants a meeting "this week" about a new roadmap feature; (4) the fortnightly retrospective is tomorrow, usually needing prompt preparation. Ask for the most defensible sequencing.

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — governance and stakeholders before operational noise
- B) 1, 2, 4, 3 — investigate the completion drop first, then the Thursday assessment pack, then tomorrow's retro prep, then schedule the stakeholder meeting
- C) 3, 2, 1, 4 — senior stakeholders always come first
- D) 4, 1, 2, 3 — team rituals protect everything else

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is a live, unexplained service signal needing immediate triage. Item 2 has a hard Thursday deadline and known cost. Item 4 is small and imminent. Item 3 needs only a brief scheduling reply today. Option A treats a possible live user-facing failure as noise beneath an undated meeting; C ranks by seniority rather than impact; D protects ritual over users. This tests ranking by impact-weighted urgency rather than by who is most senior or which item feels most like "proper" governance work.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across user feedback channels**

In the same fortnight: (a) support calls mentioning "code not arriving" rose from ~40 to ~130 per week; (b) new one-star feedback on the sign-in page mentions text messages; (c) analytics show sign-in success down 9 percentage points; (d) the SMS provider's status page reports "degraded delivery in some regions" starting twelve days ago. Ask for the most reasonable synthesis.

- A) Four unrelated issues that should each be logged separately.
- B) The evidence converges on degraded SMS delivery harming sign-in: the provider incident is the plausible common cause, and the team should verify SMS delivery rates, engage the supplier, and consider a fallback channel.
- C) Users have become less patient; no product action is needed.
- D) The analytics drop proves the sign-in redesign failed and should be rolled back.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Each channel is individually ambiguous, but all four point at one mechanism with matching timing — triangulation. Option B also states the hypothesis and names a verification step before committing to remedies. Option A wastes the convergence; C explains away quantitative movement without evidence; D imports an assumption (a redesign) the scenario never mentions. This tests assembling correlated signals into a testable hypothesis rather than treating them as independent tickets or an unexplained mood shift.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving on reuse**

A team needs a booking component. Bespoke build: 60 developer-days. A cross-government component used by eleven services: 10 days integration, but missing recurring appointments (scheduled next quarter on its own roadmap). A neighbouring team's abandoned alpha code: 25 days, unmaintained, no accessibility testing history. Recurring appointments affect about 15% of bookings and are not needed until public beta, two quarters away. Ask for the strongest recommendation.

- A) Build bespoke — full control, no compromises.
- B) Adopt the cross-government component now, plan for recurring appointments when its next-quarter roadmap delivers, and agree a fallback with the component team in case that slips.
- C) Reuse the abandoned alpha code — 25 days is cheaper than 60.
- D) Delay the decision until recurring appointments are needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The bespoke build pays a large premium for a temporary, two-quarters-away gap; the abandoned alpha is a false economy carrying unknown quality and no accessibility history; delaying confuses deferring a feature with deferring the whole booking journey's component decision. Option B fits the timeline and, crucially, names the residual risk (the component's roadmap slipping) with a pre-agreed fallback. This tests weighing reuse against bespoke build with the constraints actually given, and recognising when reuse is inappropriate as much as when it is a good idea.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

Four activities across six weeks: a discovery review (D), a stakeholder workshop (W), a service assessment dry run (R), and the service assessment itself (A). Constraints: (i) A is fixed in week 6; (ii) R must happen exactly two weeks before A; (iii) W needs D's outputs, written up over one full week after D; (iv) the room and facilitator for W are only available in weeks 2 and 5; (v) at most one activity per week. Ask in which week the workshop must take place.

- A) Week 2
- B) Week 3
- C) Week 5
- D) Either week 2 or week 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A is week 6, so R is week 4. W must be week 2 or 5. Testing week 2 fails: D would need to occur early enough that its write-up completes before week 2, which is infeasible. Testing week 5 succeeds: D can be weeks 1–3, with the write-up completing before week 5 either way. This tests anchoring fixed events first, propagating relative constraints, then testing each candidate slot for actual feasibility rather than mere eligibility — option D is the trap for checking a slot is permitted without checking it is achievable.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Spotting the flaw in a metrics argument**

At a quarterly review: "Since we introduced the new onboarding flow, average session duration has increased 40%. Users are clearly more engaged, so the flow is a success and we should extend the same design to the renewals journey." Ask for the best critique.

- A) The argument is sound: a 40% increase is too large to be chance.
- B) Session duration is ambiguous for a government service — longer sessions may mean engagement or may mean users are stuck; the argument also assumes causation from timing alone, and "success" should be tested against task-completion and user-feedback measures before the design is extended.
- C) The argument is wrong because session duration always indicates confusion.
- D) The argument is irrelevant because engagement does not matter for government services.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two stacked flaws: metric validity (rising duration is directionally ambiguous for a transactional service — it could mean engagement or being stuck) and causal inference (sequence is not causation). Options C and D make the mirror-image errors — replacing one absolute reading with another, or discarding measurement entirely. This tests asking, in order, whether a metric means what an argument needs it to mean, and whether a change actually moved it, before it drives a scaling decision.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling three sources under a deadline**

Thursday's investment board will decide whether a product proceeds to public beta. The performance report says all four KPIs met their private beta targets in each of the last two months. The user research summary says screen reader participants could not complete the eligibility step in three of four recent sessions. The service assessment report, five weeks old, rated the service "met" on all standards, noting "accessibility testing in progress; results to be confirmed." A draft board paper states: "All quality gates are green; no outstanding concerns." Ask for the most accurate characterisation of that draft.

- A) Accurate — the KPIs are met and the assessment was passed.
- B) Inaccurate — the research reveals a serious unresolved accessibility failure that postdates the assessment's "to be confirmed" caveat, so a known concern is outstanding and the paper must disclose it with a remediation plan.
- C) Inaccurate — because KPI reports can never be trusted.
- D) Accurate for the board's purposes — accessibility detail is too granular for an investment decision.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The assessment's accessibility caveat was left open, and newer, more relevant research evidence closes it unfavourably. "All green, no outstanding concerns" is false on the evidence its own authors hold. Option D treats omitting a known access barrier from an investment decision as acceptable summarisation, when it is material omission. This tests reconciling conflicting sources by date, scope and caveat — and recognising that an old document's caveat remains open until newer evidence resolves it, not until time simply passes.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate enumerates gate conditions explicitly (satisfied, failed, unknown) before concluding, rather than treating "unknown" as "satisfied".
- Watch for whether the candidate writes out working on scheduling and dependency questions, anchoring fixed events before propagating relative constraints.
- Note whether the candidate interrogates a metric's numerator, denominator and period before trusting an argument built on it.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes an explained outlier from a genuine trend when reading a data series.
- Watch for whether the candidate reconciles conflicting artefacts by date, scope and caveat rather than picking a source and dismissing the others.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that imports an assumption the scenario never states.
- Missing when a candidate concludes a multi-condition gate is passed on the strength of one satisfied condition.
- Rewarding a priority ranking driven by seniority of the requester rather than impact and urgency.
- Accepting a causal chain that quietly switches actors — a technical effect assumed to become an organisational outcome with no one deciding it.
- Missing when a candidate skims an artefact-comparison question and overlooks a caveat such as "results to be confirmed".

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and confidently a candidate works with the kinds of numbers this role genuinely produces and consumes: KPI tables, completion funnels, cost-per-transaction figures, uptake trends, benefit estimates, and sprint metrics. The mathematics is everyday — percentages, ratios, averages, rates, trends — applied with realistic wrinkles.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, each presenting a small dataset and asking the candidate to extract, calculate, compare, or project. A calculator is usually permitted, and scoring is objective, often adaptive.

For a product manager, numeric fluency is the language in which the role's central responsibilities are transacted: estimating roadmap value, comparing effort to value, analysing benefit of investment against anticipated outcomes, and reading KPIs monthly while distinguishing genuine movement from noise. A product manager who misreads a funnel or blends unequal groups into a misleading average will prioritise the wrong work with complete confidence, which is why this is tested directly.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages, rates and funnels** map to **Managing product outcomes**: completion rates, drop-off analysis and KPI movements against baseline.
- **Averages, weighted averages and trends** map to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Life cycle management**: velocity planning, satisfaction across user groups, uptake trends across life cycle phases.
- **Ratios and unit economics** map to **Creating value for money**: cost per transaction, value-to-effort ratios and benefit-per-pound comparisons.
- **Projection and target arithmetic** maps to **Strategic ownership** and **Product management**: honest, assumption-stated projections underpinning a strategy's investment case.
- **Reading tables and charts under pressure** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: extracting the right figure quickly and correctly, in the meeting itself.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate from a funnel**

Last month, 8,000 users started an application journey and 6,200 completed it. Ask for the completion rate and the drop-off rate.

- A) Completion 77.5%; drop-off 22.5%
- B) Completion 62%; drop-off 38%
- C) Completion 78.5%; drop-off 21.5%
- D) Completion 77.5%; drop-off 32.5%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 6,200÷8,000 = 77.5%; drop-off is the complement, 22.5% — option A. Option D pairs a correct completion rate with a slipped complement, a reminder that the second half of a two-part answer is often the discriminating half. This tests basic funnel arithmetic and verifying both figures, not just the first.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change in a KPI**

Support calls per week fell from 480 in January to 384 in March. Ask for the percentage change.

- A) A fall of 24%
- B) A fall of 20%
- C) A fall of 25%
- D) A fall of 96%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (384−480)÷480 = −20% — option B. Option C divides by the new figure instead of the original; D reports the absolute change as if it were a percentage. This tests anchoring correctly on the starting value, and — in a strong answer — recognising the asymmetry it creates (a 20% fall needs a 25% rise to reverse), which regularly confuses stakeholders reading such figures.

**Question 3 (easy) — Average from a KPI table**

Monthly satisfaction scores: April 78%, May 82%, June 86%. Ask for the quarterly average and the trend.

- A) 82% average; rising by 4 percentage points per month
- B) 82% average; stable
- C) 84% average; rising
- D) 78% average; rising by 4 percentage points per month

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** (78+82+86)÷3 = 82; each month rises 4 percentage points — option A. This tests reporting both level and direction together, and using the correct unit — percentage points, not "4%", since a 4% rise from 78% would be a different, smaller number.

**Question 4 (easy) — Cost per transaction**

Online channel: 30,000 transactions/month, £36,000 cost. Phone channel: 5,000 transactions/month, £40,000 cost. Ask for the cost per transaction for each.

- A) Online £1.20; phone £8.00
- B) Online £1.20; phone £5.00
- C) Online £0.83; phone £8.00
- D) Online £1.00; phone £8.00

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £36,000÷30,000=£1.20; £40,000÷5,000=£8.00 — option A. This tests basic unit-cost calculation and, in a strong answer, recognising the strategic implication (each phone transaction costs far more than its online equivalent) alongside the caveat that the remaining phone users may include people who cannot use the digital route.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Funnel drop-off localisation**

A five-step journey recorded: Step 1: 10,000; Step 2: 9,200; Step 3: 6,900; Step 4: 6,555; Step 5: 6,424. Ask which step loses the greatest proportion of users who reach it.

- A) Step 1 to Step 2
- B) Step 2 to Step 3
- C) Step 3 to Step 4
- D) Step 4 to Step 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 2→3 loses 25% of those who reached Step 2, the largest of the four step-losses — option B. This tests computing each step's loss as a percentage of those reaching the earlier step (not the original total), which locates the actual problem step precisely.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Weighted satisfaction across user groups**

1,800 citizens rate satisfaction at 84%; 600 caseworkers rate it at 56%. A slide reports "average satisfaction: 70%." Ask for the correct overall figure and what is wrong with the slide.

- A) 70% is correct: (84 + 56) ÷ 2.
- B) 77%: the groups must be weighted by size — and the slide's unweighted average conceals that the true blended figure is 7 points higher while caseworker satisfaction remains a serious outlier.
- C) 84%: the larger group's figure should be used.
- D) 66.5%: weight by the inverse of group size.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weighted: (1,800×0.84 + 600×0.56)÷2,400 = 77% — option B, not the unweighted 70% the slide uses. This tests weighting by group size correctly, and — in a strong answer — reporting the split alongside the blend, since 77% still conceals a 28-point gap between groups.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Value-to-effort with money attached**

Item A: benefit £90,000/yr, effort 30 developer-days. Item B: benefit £60,000/yr, effort 15 developer-days. A developer-day costs £500. Ask for the strongest statement.

- A) Item A first: it has the larger total benefit.
- B) Item B first on benefit-per-day (£4,000 versus £3,000), and its £7,500 build cost is recovered in under seven weeks of its annual benefit — though with capacity for both across the quarter, sequencing B then A captures the better ratio first.
- C) Item A first: £90,000 minus £15,000 build cost beats £60,000 minus £7,500.
- D) They are equivalent, so the order does not matter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item B yields £4,000 per developer-day against Item A's £3,000, and recovers its build cost in under seven weeks. This tests per-unit reasoning against the scarce resource (effort) rather than ranking by gross benefit or net benefit alone, and recognising a capacity-permitting scenario as a sequencing decision, not an either/or.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a two-way table correctly**

Digital uptake by age band: Under 35: 5,400 of 6,000; 35–64: 6,300 of 9,000; 65+: 1,500 of 5,000. Ask for the correct statement.

- A) The 35–64 band has the highest online uptake rate, because it has the most online applications.
- B) Online uptake rates are: under 35: 90%; 35–64: 70%; 65+: 30% — so the under-35 band has the highest rate even though the 35–64 band has the highest count.
- C) The 65+ band accounts for most of the paper applications, so overall uptake is 30%.
- D) Overall uptake cannot be calculated from this table.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rates: 90%, 70%, 30% respectively — option B, separating count (35–64 leads) from rate (under-35 leads). Option C conflates a true observation about paper-application share with a false overall rate (the true overall rate is 66%). This tests computing rate correctly per group and not conflating it with count or an unrelated aggregate.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Benefit estimate with chained percentages**

2,400 support calls/month; 35% attributed to status-tracking issues; a proposed feature deflects 60% of those; each call costs £5.50. Ask for the expected annual saving.

- A) £2,772
- B) £33,264
- C) £55,440
- D) £16,632

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 2,400×0.35=840; 840×0.60=504; 504×£5.50=£2,772/month; ×12=£33,264/year — option B. Option A is the monthly figure mistaken for annual, a common slip in benefits papers. This tests chaining percentages to their correct bases and annualising correctly, with assumptions stated openly.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Projecting uptake against a strategy target**

Strategy target: 75% digital uptake by end of Q6. Quarterly uptake: Q1 48%, Q2 54%, Q3 60%, Q4 66%. A stakeholder says: "We're growing 6 points a quarter — we'll hit 78% by Q6 and beat the target." Ask for the most accurate assessment.

- A) The stakeholder is right, and no further analysis is needed.
- B) The straight-line projection does reach 78% by Q6 (66 + 6 + 6), but the claim rests on the assumption that growth stays linear; uptake curves typically flatten as the remaining non-digital users face higher barriers, so the team should treat 75% as at risk and monitor the quarterly increment for early signs of slowing.
- C) The projection is wrong: growth is 6% per quarter, so Q6 uptake will be 66 × 1.06 × 1.06 ≈ 74%, missing the target.
- D) Targets should not be projected; wait for Q6 actuals.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's linear arithmetic is internally correct (78% by Q6); option C misapplies compound growth to a pattern that is arithmetic, not geometric. The judgement option B adds is that a projection is only as good as its continuation assumption, and digital uptake is a canonical saturation case. This tests verifying the arithmetic first, then applying a risk judgement with a monitoring trigger — neither blind confidence nor analytical abstention.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Unit cost versus total cost movement**

Q1: 40,000 transactions, £96,000 cost, 3,200 calls. Q2: 55,000 transactions, £121,000 cost, 3,850 calls. A stakeholder says: "Costs are up 26% and calls are up 20% — the service is deteriorating." Ask what the unit figures show.

- A) The stakeholder is right: both totals rose materially.
- B) Cost per transaction fell from £2.40 to £2.20, and calls per 1,000 transactions fell from 80 to 70 — both unit measures improved; totals rose because volume rose 37.5%, so the service became more efficient while serving many more people.
- C) Cost per transaction rose from £2.20 to £2.40, confirming deterioration.
- D) The data is contradictory and no conclusion is possible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cost per transaction fell (£2.40 to £2.20); calls per 1,000 fell (80 to 70); volume rose 37.5%, explaining both rising totals despite unit improvement. Option C transposes the two quarters' unit costs. This tests normalising by volume before judging performance — totals alarm, units inform — while still recognising that rising demand needs funding even when efficiency improves.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Comparing an A/B test's results honestly**

Variant A (current page): 12,000 users, 8,640 completions. Variant B (redesign): 3,000 users, 2,250 completions, same fortnight. A colleague says: "A had 8,640 completions and B only 2,250 — the redesign failed." Ask what the rates show and what the sound conclusion is.

- A) The colleague is right: 8,640 beats 2,250.
- B) Completion rates are A: 72%, B: 75% — the redesign performed 3 percentage points better per user served; the raw counts differ because A received four times the traffic, and the sound conclusion is that B is promising and the improvement should be checked for consistency across user groups before full rollout.
- C) The rates are A: 72%, B: 75%, so B should immediately replace A with no further checks.
- D) The test is invalid because the groups were different sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A: 72%; B: 75% — B leads per user served despite fewer raw completions, since it received a quarter of A's traffic. Option D overcorrects; unequal group sizes are routine in A/B testing and affect precision, not validity. This tests converting to rates before comparing, and the judgement that a promising fortnight of data needs a segmented check before rollout, rather than either dismissal or immediate adoption.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate applies each percentage to its correct base and writes intermediate results down on chained calculations.
- Note whether the candidate normalises (rate, unit, weighted) before comparing dramatic raw-number contrasts.
- Watch for confusion between percentage points and percent, and between monthly and annual figures.
- Score for whether the candidate treats a straight-line projection as an assumption, naming what would bend it and defining an early-warning check.
- Note whether the candidate reads totals and unit measures separately before concluding a trend is good or bad news.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage change divided by the new value instead of the original.
- Missing when a candidate compares raw counts across unequal groups instead of converting to rates.
- Rewarding an unweighted average of group percentages, or a blended figure reported without its group split.
- Accepting a monthly figure presented as annual, or vice versa.
- Missing when a candidate reads rising total cost as deterioration without checking the unit measure.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely a candidate reads, interprets and draws conclusions from written material this role runs on: service standards, policy extracts, governance papers, stakeholder emails, research summaries, supplier communications, and strategy documents.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes, most commonly "true / false / cannot say": True means the passage guarantees the statement; False means the passage contradicts it; Cannot say means it does neither. Other formats include best-summary selection, inference identification, assumption spotting, and clear-writing judgement. The cardinal rule is to answer from the passage alone.

For a product manager, verbal precision is load-bearing: ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards means reading standards for what they actually require, permit and omit; consulting and mediating with stakeholders means hearing the exact commitment, condition, or concern in an email; working in the open means writing updates that survive being quoted out of context; and using research summaries to represent users means claiming exactly what the evidence supports.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Comprehension of standards and policy** maps to the duties to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards** and to ensure the product is developed **in line with the appropriate service standards for a phase**.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Managing product outcomes**: claiming exactly what evidence supports.
- **Inference and assumption spotting** maps to **Creating value for money** and **Strategic ownership**: finding the unstated assumptions on which benefit cases and strategies stand.
- **Reading stakeholder communications** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: distinguishing a condition from a refusal, a concern from an instruction, a hope from a commitment.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Product management** and **Product leadership**: prose that says what changed, why, and what happens next.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 6, the candidate decides whether the statement is True, False, or Cannot say. A fourth option is included where relevant.

**Question 1 (easy) — Service standard extract**

Passage: "Before a public beta assessment, teams must show they have tested the service with users who have access needs, and that identified accessibility issues have either been fixed or have a documented remediation plan with dates. Teams should also show how assisted digital support will be provided."

Statement: "A team may pass the public beta assessment with unfixed accessibility issues, provided those issues have a documented, dated remediation plan."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only for low-severity issues

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage's explicit either/or ("fixed or have a documented remediation plan with dates") matches the statement precisely: True. Option D imports a severity restriction not in the text. This tests reading a standard for its actual structure — requirement, alternative, condition — rather than a stricter remembered version.

**Question 2 (easy) — What silence means**

Passage: "The department's API strategy requires all new services to expose data through the central API gateway. Services must publish their API documentation in the developer hub. Rate limits are set by the gateway team."

Statement: "The API strategy requires existing services to migrate to the central gateway."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, within twelve months

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The requirement is scoped to "new services"; existing services are addressed neither by requirement nor exemption — Cannot say. Option D invents both a requirement and a deadline. This tests catching a scope-limiting word ("new") that governs the whole passage — one of the most common hinges in standards-reading questions and in real compliance conversations.

**Question 3 (easy) — Research summary discipline**

Passage: "In eight moderated sessions on the redesigned eligibility checker, six participants completed the check unaided. Two participants, both using screen magnification, could not operate the postcode field. Participants who completed the check described the result page as clear."

Statement: "The redesigned checker works well for users with access needs."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because two participants failed

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Users with access needs" is a broad population; the passage documents difficulty for two screen-magnification users and says nothing about others — the evidence neither confirms nor strictly contradicts the broad claim. Cannot say. This tests matching a claim's scope to the evidence's actual scope, in both directions — the magnification finding demands a fix, but the broad claim in either direction is not supported by six sessions.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A supplier's carefully worded email**

Passage: "We anticipate the API rate-limit increase will be deployed in the June release, subject to change-board approval on 28 May. Should approval be delayed, the increase would move to the July release. We will confirm within two working days of the board's decision."

Statement: "The supplier has committed to deploying the rate-limit increase in June."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because June is stated as the plan

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Anticipate... subject to" is a conditional expectation, not a commitment, and the email names a fallback if the condition fails — directly contradicting the statement's claim of commitment: False. This tests distinguishing verbs of commitment from verbs of expectation, and relaying upward only the strength of claim actually made.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Governance minutes and what was decided**

Passage: "The board reviewed the proposal to retire the legacy reporting module. Members noted the migration plan and the low remaining usage (4% of users). The board requested a data-retention impact note and agreed to take a decision at its July meeting. The product team was thanked for a clear paper."

Statement: "The board has approved the retirement of the legacy reporting module."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, effective July

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Agreed to take a decision at its July meeting" explicitly defers the decision — directly contradicting "has approved": False. Option D converts a decision date into an effective date for an approval that has not happened. This tests tracking governance verbs precisely — reviewed, noted, requested, deferred — distinct from approved.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Policy with an exception and a condition**

Passage: "Teams must release changes through the standard deployment pipeline. Emergency fixes may bypass the pipeline where a live incident is in progress, provided the change is retrospectively reviewed within five working days. Bypasses without an active incident are not permitted under any circumstances."

Statement: "A team that bypassed the pipeline during a live incident, and completed the retrospective review seven working days later, has complied with the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the incident justified the bypass

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The exception has two parts — a trigger and an obligation attached to using it. The team met the trigger but breached the five-day proviso: False. Option D treats the trigger as the whole test, ignoring the attached proviso. This tests extracting both a trigger and its provisos from an exception clause and checking each separately.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Best summary of a prioritisation note**

Passage: "We assessed four candidate features against user value, delivery effort, and strategic fit. The payment-reminder feature scored highest on value and fit, with moderate effort, and is recommended for the next quarter. The document-upload feature scored well on value but requires a security review before any commitment. The remaining two features scored low on value and are deferred to the backlog. We will revisit the deferred items if user research changes the value picture."

Ask which sentence best summarises the passage.

- A) Four features were assessed; payment reminders are recommended for next quarter, document upload awaits a security review before commitment, and two low-value features are deferred with a review trigger.
- B) Payment reminders and document upload are both committed for next quarter, with two features cancelled.
- C) The team assessed four features and deferred them all pending research.
- D) Payment reminders are recommended because they are the easiest to build.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option A preserves the recommendation, the conditional item, the deferrals and the revisit trigger at correct strength. Option B upgrades a conditional into a commitment and a deferral into a cancellation. This tests preserving the strength of each claim — recommended versus committed, deferred versus cancelled — which is the precision roadmap discussions with stakeholders require.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Spotting the assumption in a benefits argument**

Passage: "Introducing web chat will reduce telephone waiting times, because a proportion of callers will choose web chat instead, and each web chat agent can handle three conversations concurrently while a telephone agent can handle only one."

Ask which unstated assumption is most critical to the argument.

- A) That enough callers will actually switch to web chat, and that the staffing model converts the concurrency advantage into freed telephone capacity rather than absorbing it elsewhere.
- B) That web chat software is cheaper than telephone systems.
- C) That telephone waiting times are currently measured accurately.
- D) That users prefer speaking to typing.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The mechanism needs both sufficient uptake and a staffing model that actually reallocates freed capacity — both unstated. Options B, C and D are not load-bearing to the argument as stated. This tests reducing an argument to its skeleton and asking what would have to be true for the mechanism to work, the verbal twin of the cognitive section's benefits-chain question.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Two documents in tension**

Passage 1 (strategy, January): "Our priority for this year is consolidating our three case-handling products into a single platform. New feature development on the legacy products should be limited to essential compliance changes." Passage 2 (email, September, operations director): "The legacy casework team urgently needs a bulk-reassignment feature — their workaround costs hours each week. I understand the consolidation strategy, but this is a productivity issue affecting statutory timeliness targets."

Statement: "Approving the bulk-reassignment feature on the legacy product would be consistent with the strategy only if it qualifies as an essential compliance change."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because the director's request overrides the strategy

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The strategy permits exactly one category of legacy development; consistency therefore requires membership in that category, precisely what the statement asserts: True. The statement tests the logical structure, not whether the feature actually qualifies (which the email gestures towards without establishing). Option D confuses organisational pressure with textual logic. This tests testing a request against a strategy's own exception category rather than treating pressure as automatically rewriting the strategy.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement for a difficult update**

A feature was pulled mid-sprint after user research raised concerns. Ask which opening sentence for the stakeholder update is best.

- A) "Despite headwinds, the team continued to drive velocity across multiple workstreams in line with evolving priorities."
- B) "We paused the document-sharing feature mid-sprint after research showed users misunderstood who could see their documents — a privacy risk we're redesigning to fix, with a revised plan by 14 June."
- C) "Owing to a research-driven reprioritisation event, deliverable timelines have been recalibrated pending stakeholder alignment."
- D) "The sprint didn't go to plan; details on request."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B states what changed, why (with the risk named precisely), and what happens next, with a date. Options A and C are jargon with no facts, and present a research-driven pause as something to obscure. Option D is honest but transfers all effort of discovery to the reader. This tests judging a sentence against "what changed, why, what next" — the practical grammar of working in the open.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference across a research and analytics passage**

Passage: "Analytics show that 22% of started applications are abandoned at the evidence-upload step. In interviews, every participant who abandoned at this step said they did not have the required documents to hand; none mentioned difficulty operating the upload itself. Two participants said they returned and completed the application later that week. The upload component passed usability testing in March with no significant findings."

Statement: "Improving the usability of the upload component is unlikely to substantially reduce abandonment at the evidence-upload step."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say — the passage does not address usability improvements
- D) True, and abandonment is therefore not a problem

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Interviews attribute abandonment entirely to document availability, explicitly ruling out operational difficulty, and independent usability testing found no issues — converging evidence supports the hedged claim: True. Option C is over-cautious given two independent sources bearing directly on the mechanism; option D bolts on an unsupported second claim, since the passage hints at a different fix (document checklists, save-and-return). This tests a genuine inference from converging evidence, and targeting an intervention at the actual cause rather than the visible symptom.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Precision about what a metric claim licenses**

Passage: "The new appointment-booking journey launched on 1 April. In April, 68% of bookings were made online, against 54% in March. The online booking KPI target for the year is 65%. April also saw the closure of two walk-in centres, which previously handled around 10% of monthly bookings."

Statement: "The new booking journey caused online bookings to exceed the KPI target."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the target was exceeded in the same month the journey launched

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The factual claim (68% exceeds 65%) is verified, but the causal claim has a rival explanation the passage itself supplies — the walk-in centre closures — with no evidence apportioning the effect between the two causes: Cannot say. Option D is the post hoc fallacy dressed with a KPI. This tests actively scanning a passage for facts that complicate a headline causal claim, particularly ones placed quietly at the end.

### Administration tips

- Score for the three-way True/False/Cannot say test applied mechanically, rather than by plausibility.
- Watch for whether the candidate catches scope words ("new", "all", "only") and modal verbs ("anticipate" versus "committed") that govern a passage's actual claim.
- Note whether the candidate extracts both the trigger and every proviso of an exception clause.
- For summary and clear-writing items, score on preserved claim strength (recommended vs committed, deferred vs cancelled) and the "what, why, what next" test.
- Watch for whether the candidate notices a load-bearing extra fact placed at the end of a passage, rather than answering from the headline alone.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement marked True because it is plausible from general product experience.
- Marking a statement False because the passage does not mention it, rather than Cannot say.
- Missing when a candidate upgrades claim strength while reading — anticipation into commitment, recommendation into decision.
- Rewarding an answer that treats an exception's trigger as the whole test, ignoring its proviso.
- Accepting a causal claim supported by same-timing evidence alone, when the passage itself supplies a rival explanation.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Formats vary: most effective; most and least effective; full ranking; or independent ratings. Scoring is built from experienced practitioners' judgements and the profession's behavioural expectations rather than a formula, though consistent principles run through it.

Typical format: an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed, since it measures judgement rather than speed. Distractors are engineered from real failure modes: conflict avoidance dressed as diplomacy, unilateral heroics dressed as decisiveness, maximal escalation dressed as integrity.

For a product manager, situational judgement is arguably the most job-relevant of the four assessments. The role's summary is dense with behavioural verbs: consult and mediate with stakeholders; protect the team from unnecessary distractions and requests to change direction; be open about decision making; advocate for research with all types of users; decide how and when to escalate risk to value. At this level, most situations are expected to be handled independently — the calibration being tested is which things to resolve directly, which to escalate, and whether users, evidence and honesty stay intact while doing both.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Stakeholder mediation scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Product leadership**: consulting, mediating, resolving issues, influencing effectively.
- **Team protection scenarios** map to **Product leadership**: protecting the team from unnecessary distractions and mid-sprint direction changes, without blocking legitimate change.
- **Prioritisation and scope scenarios** map to **Product management** and **Creating value for money**: addressing uncertainty while managing scope, escalating risk to value at the right moment.
- **Evidence and openness scenarios** map to **Managing product outcomes** and **Applying user-centred insights**: honesty about decisions, representing users including inconvenient findings.
- **Standards and phase scenarios** map to **Life cycle management** and the duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**.
- **People and community scenarios** map to the role's people dimension: supporting senior colleagues, developing team members, participating in the product community.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A mid-sprint change request**

Three days into a two-week sprint, a senior stakeholder emails: "Please get the team to add a data-export option to this sprint — it came up in a directors' meeting and shouldn't take long." The sprint goal is completing a payment journey ahead of a fixed compliance deadline. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Forward the email to the team and ask them to fit the export in.
- B) Reply that sprint scope is locked, decline the request, and copy the team so they see this being defended.
- C) Reply promptly: acknowledge the request, explain the sprint is protecting a compliance deadline, ask about the export's driver and urgency, and offer to assess it for the next sprint — bringing evidence of its value and effort to the conversation.
- D) Add the export to the backlog silently and say nothing.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C shields the team from disruption entirely, engages the stakeholder rather than walling them off, and routes the request into evidence-based prioritisation. Option A passes disruption straight to the team; B protects the sprint but performs the defence in front of the team and forecloses a possibly urgent need; D leaves the stakeholder uninformed and waiting. This tests protecting the team while engaging the requester, rather than choosing between the two.

**Question 2 (easy) — Inconvenient research before a big demo**

A demo to a director's group that has championed a new eligibility checker is tomorrow. Today the researcher reports that four of six participants this week misinterpreted a "likely eligible" result as a guarantee, and two said they would have made financial decisions based on it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Proceed with the demo as planned and share the research afterwards, once fixes are designed.
- B) Include the finding in tomorrow's demo: show the checker, present the misinterpretation risk and its potential harm plainly, and set out the team's proposed response and timeline.
- C) Cancel the demo until the issue is fixed.
- D) Ask the researcher to soften the finding to "some users wanted clearer wording".

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A material harm-risk finding belongs with the audience that can shape the response, at the first opportunity, undiluted, with a plan attached. Option A gambles credibility on a quiet fortnight; C forfeits both momentum and transparency; D corrupts the evidence chain and poisons trust with the research function. This tests moving material user-harm findings to decision-makers immediately, paired with a proposed response.

**Question 3 (easy) — Two teams, one component**

A team in a neighbouring directorate is three weeks into building a consent-management component nearly identical to one the candidate's team finished last month. The candidate has never met their product manager. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Do nothing — their duplication is their directorate's problem.
- B) Contact their product manager directly: share what the component does, offer a walkthrough with a view to them adopting or extending it, and if adoption looks viable, flag the reuse opportunity to both directorates' leads.
- C) Email their director stating that the team is wasting public money.
- D) Tell the candidate's own team lead and take no further action.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Peer-to-peer, concrete and generous engagement gives adoption a real chance without embarrassing anyone in front of leadership. Option C leads with an accusation aimed above the people who can fix it; A and D discharge minimal responsibility without action in time to matter. This tests seeking reuse opportunities across organisational boundaries constructively.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The velocity conversation with a delivery deadline**

An evidence-based forecast says a migration will complete in November. A senior product manager, under programme board pressure, asks the candidate to "commit to September — the team always sandbags estimates anyway." The candidate believes November is sound. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Commit to September to support the senior colleague, and push the team to speed up.
- B) Walk the senior colleague through the forecast's basis, state that September cannot honestly be committed to, and offer alternatives with trade-offs made explicit: a smaller September scope, added capacity with its costs and risks, or the full scope in November — so they carry real options to the board.
- C) Refuse to discuss dates other than November.
- D) Agree to September in the meeting, then let the slip emerge gradually through sprint reports.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Supporting a senior colleague means equipping them with defensible positions, not echoing indefensible ones. Option B answers the "sandbagging" suspicion with evidence and offers genuine scope-for-date trade-offs. Option A manufactures a false commitment the senior colleague will be left defending; D is an engineered ambush; C refuses the mediation the role exists to provide. This tests trading dates, scope and capacity openly rather than trading them against credibility.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A KPI moving the wrong way before a board paper**

Three of four KPIs improved; the fourth — completion rate for assisted digital users — fell for a second consecutive month, undiagnosed. A colleague suggests: "Lead with the three good ones and put the fourth in the annex — we'll have an explanation by next month." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Take the suggestion — annexes exist for detail, and next month's explanation will be better.
- B) Report all four KPIs with equal prominence, state plainly that the assisted digital decline is undiagnosed, and set out the investigation under way with a date for findings.
- C) Delay the board paper until the diagnosis is complete.
- D) Report the fourth KPI but reframe it as "within normal variation" to buy time.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Bad news should travel at the same speed and prominence as good news, labelled as uncertainty with a plan. Option A de-prioritises the metric belonging to users least able to raise the alarm themselves; D makes an unsupported factual claim that will discredit future reassurances if discovered; C sacrifices governance rhythm to perfectionism. This tests honesty about metrics moving unfavourably, including whose metric it is.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Standards pressure at a phase boundary**

Public beta assessment is in three weeks. The delivery manager proposes skipping planned research with assistive technology users to protect the date, since "automated checks pass and the assessment only needs testing to be planned." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Agree — the assessment criteria will technically be satisfied.
- B) Insist the research happens as planned, explain that automated checks are not user testing and that barriers found post-assessment cost far more to fix, and work with the delivery manager to fit a proportionate round — even a reduced one — before the assessment, escalating the trade-off jointly if it truly cannot fit.
- C) Report the delivery manager to the assessment panel.
- D) Postpone the assessment by two months to allow exhaustive testing.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B corrects the technical misconception, makes the economic case, negotiates a proportionate round, and escalates jointly and visibly if the trade-off genuinely cannot fit. Option A games the standard rather than serving the users it protects; C skips every collaborative rung over a proposal, not yet a bad act; D swings to unnecessary maximalism. This tests holding a standard's substance while offering proportionality and escalating residual risk visibly.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Mediating stakeholders who want opposite things**

Policy colleagues insist a grant application must collect a full financial history "so decisions are robust"; operations colleagues insist it must be shorter "because abandonment is killing us." Both have escalated. Data shows abandonment concentrates on financial-history pages; legal advice says some financial information is statutorily required. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Side with operations — the data on abandonment is decisive.
- B) Side with policy — statutory requirements always win.
- C) Convene both parties around the evidence: separate what is statutorily required from what is merely customary, test with users a journey that collects only the required minimum upfront with the remainder deferred or retrieved from existing government data, and agree success measures both sides own.
- D) Build both versions and let a senior board choose.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The apparent binary survives only while nobody separates the statutory floor from customary padding — a move both sides' own positions already support. Options A and B each create a losing side that will relitigate the decision; D outsources the role's core mediation work at real cost. This tests dissolving a false binary through evidence rather than picking a side or escalating unnecessarily.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A team member's growth versus a safe pair of hands**

A high-visibility discovery in a politically sensitive service area is starting. An associate product manager, capable but untested with senior stakeholders, asks to lead the stakeholder engagement as a development opportunity; the stakeholders involved are known to be demanding. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Decline — the sensitivity is too high for development experiments; they can lead something smaller later.
- B) Agree fully and step back — people learn by doing, and intervening would undermine them.
- C) Agree with scaffolding: they lead the engagement with defined scope, the approach is agreed together beforehand, attend the first sessions in a supporting role, debrief after each, and set explicit points for stepping in.
- D) Split the role ambiguously — tell them they are leading, but attend everything and answer questions personally when stakes rise.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C transfers real ownership while bounding risk with agreed step-in triggers and a debrief loop. Option A protects short-term optics at the cost of the development pipeline; B confuses development with unsupported exposure; D nominally delegates while retaining real authority, undermining both the associate and the stakeholders' trust. This tests scaffolded delegation that genuinely develops people without engineering an unsurvivable failure.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Pressure to reframe a benefits case**

A business case promised £400,000 annual savings; verified data a year in shows £150,000. A programme lead preparing the annual review says: "Add the £180,000 of 'staff satisfaction and error-reduction benefits' from the wellbeing survey to close the gap — it's all benefit, after all." The survey is real but its monetisation was explicitly flagged by analysts as not robust. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Include the £180,000 — benefits are benefits, and the review needs to land well.
- B) Report the £150,000 verified against the £400,000 promise, analyse why the model overestimated, present the wellbeing findings qualitatively (or with their monetisation caveats fully stated), and bring options for closing the genuine gap — further automation, scope change, or a revised benefits baseline.
- C) Report only the £150,000 with no explanation, and let the review draw its own conclusions.
- D) Ask the analysts to rework the monetisation method until it clears the robustness bar, then include whatever figure results, delaying the review if needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The proposed padding is not fabricated evidence, only unsound monetisation, which is exactly how benefits cases decay incrementally. Option B holds verified benefit against promise, treats the wellbeing evidence honestly without laundering it into the headline, and brings options for the real gap. Option A includes a flagged-unsound figure over analysts' objection; D reverses rigorous method into method-shopping for a predetermined figure. This tests holding an evidential line even when the padding is only partly unsound, not wholly invented.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the failing experiment**

Six weeks ago, with stakeholder fanfare, a team launched an opt-in "smart reminders" pilot the candidate championed. Uptake is 2%, no measurable effect on missed appointments, two complaints about timing. Ask for a ranking:

1. Run a structured review against the pilot's original success criteria, share the results and a recommendation openly with the team and stakeholders — including that the candidate championed it — and either stop or run one bounded, hypothesis-driven iteration if the review identifies a specific fixable cause of low uptake.
2. Extend the pilot with a bigger promotion budget, since the idea deserves a fair chance.
3. Quietly wind the pilot down without a stakeholder announcement, folding its budget back into the roadmap.
4. Continue the pilot unchanged for another quarter to gather more data before any decision.

- A) Most effective: 1; least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- C) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 3; least effective: 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 judges the pilot against its own criteria, is open about outcome and authorship, and leaves one evidence-gated path forward. Response 3 reaches a defensible operational outcome by a method that corrodes trust — stakeholders discover the disappearance later, and the team learns failed experiments are shameful secrets. Responses 2 and 4 waste resources without deceiving anyone. This tests judging the least-effective response by what damages trust or hides signal, not merely by which wastes the most money.

**Question 11 (challenging) — A data request that feels wrong**

A policy team urgently drafting ministerial advice asks for "the raw user feedback database, including the free-text comments," which frequently contain volunteered personal details — health conditions, family circumstances, occasionally names and addresses. The purpose is legitimate and the deadline real. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Send the database — the purpose is legitimate, the requesters are civil servants, and the deadline is real.
- B) Refuse on data protection grounds and suggest they submit a formal request through information governance, which typically takes three weeks.
- C) Establish what question the policy team actually needs answered, and meet it with proportionate data — aggregated themes, redacted or sampled comments prepared with the analyst — while checking the sharing basis with data protection colleagues in parallel, at the pace the deadline requires.
- D) Send the database but ask the policy team to promise to delete the personal details after use.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Interrogating the need behind a request usually reveals that aggregated analysis serves the purpose better than raw data, transforming a hard data-protection question into an easier one, with governance checked in parallel rather than in sequence. Option A treats requester legitimacy as a lawful basis; D contracts the problem away without resolving it; B is technically defensible but a practical refusal that teaches urgent requesters to route around governance. This tests meeting a request's substance while refusing its risky form.

**Question 12 (challenging) — A candidate's own product should probably be retired**

An internal reporting product managed for two years shows a 60% usage decline as teams migrate to a new analytics platform; the last user survey drew 31 responses, mostly from one directorate; compliance work will need roughly 40% of team capacity next year. Nobody senior has raised retirement; funding renews by default, the team likes the work, and retirement would shrink the candidate's own portfolio. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Continue delivering well — retirement decisions belong to whoever funds the product, and no one has raised it.
- B) Build the analysis personally: usage trends, cost of continuation including the security work, the migration path for the remaining users (especially the dependent directorate), and bring a retirement-or-invest recommendation to the senior product manager and governance, even though it may argue the candidate's own product away.
- C) Quietly reduce effort on the product and let it degrade until retirement becomes inevitable.
- D) Commission a survey campaign to boost engagement and justify the product's continuation before anyone examines the numbers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Leading a product through the life cycle to retirement includes the clause with no natural constituency — nobody lobbies for retirement, and self-interest cuts the other way. Option B applies the same investment-grade analysis to continuation as to any new investment, with an honest migration path for the dependent users. Option A hides behind formal accountability; C reaches retirement through managed neglect; D manufactures evidence to defend the product against scrutiny. This tests applying evidential standards to a candidate's own product with the same rigour as to anyone else's.

### Administration tips

- Score for the recurring fingerprint of effective responses: engage rather than avoid; proportionate rather than maximal; convert conflict into evidence; move bad news at the speed of good news; protect users who are not in the room.
- Check a candidate's instinct against the escalation ladder appropriate to this level: resolve directly → mediate with evidence → escalate jointly and openly → escalate alone as last resort.
- For most/least formats, score the least-effective choice as the one that hides a signal, deceives someone, or corrodes trust — not merely the one that is timid or wasteful.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes negotiables (dates, scope, capacity) from non-negotiables (evidence integrity, user safety, accessibility, data protection).
- Watch for scenarios that test self-interest or sunk cost — a candidate's own championed pilot, portfolio, or estimate — and score for whether evidence still wins.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting conflict avoidance dressed as diplomacy — quiet wind-downs, annexed bad news, softened findings, unanswered requests.
- Rewarding unilateral heroics — refusing all date conversations, cancelling demos, building both versions, absorbing impossible commitments personally.
- Missing when a candidate escalates maximally as a first move rather than working the lower rungs of the ladder.
- Confusing support for a senior colleague with echoing an indefensible position.
- Missing self-interest or sunk-cost bias affecting a candidate's judgement about their own prior decisions.
