# Service Owner - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for service owner. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. For a service owner, that means far more than abstract puzzles: the assessments described here are job-specific, using the kinds of artefacts the role genuinely handles — service performance dashboards, business cases, benefits realisation tables, governance papers, service assessment reports, user research findings, supplier contracts and stakeholder correspondence — and the kinds of decisions it genuinely makes, such as prioritising between user needs, business requirements, and policy; deciding when a team should continue, change direction or stop; allocating money across competing priorities; and negotiating with senior stakeholders so that work can progress.

The role is accountable for the quality, performance, benefits, and outcomes of an end-to-end service. Accountability of that breadth demands sustained, high-quality reasoning under pressure: reading a performance dashboard and knowing within seconds whether the service is genuinely healthy; extracting the one material fact from a dense assessment report; testing whether a benefits claim in a business case actually survives arithmetic; and choosing a defensible course of action when a minister's office, a policy team and user researchers are pulling in three directions.

Each of the four sections follows the same pattern: an overview of what it measures and why employers use it for service leadership roles; a mapping to the role's named skills; graduated practice questions with full worked explanations; then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score for auditable working on numeric items — the discipline of showing working is also the discipline that makes benefits claims and funding cases credible — and for calibrated claims throughout, since several situational and verbal items reward exactly what the evidence supports, no more and no less. Use each section's per-item timing as a guide for planning a session, and debrief against the explanations below.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract shapes and sequences: performance dashboards, benefits maps, roadmaps, governance schedules, operating model diagrams, and assessment reports.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, containing 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a similar level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

For a service owner, cognitive assessment is particularly relevant because the core professional value of the role is structured judgement across an entire service: providing end-to-end direction, prioritisation, risk management and decision making for teams; using evidence to decide when a team should continue, change direction or stop; and holding the whole system in mind — users, operations, policy, funding, suppliers — while deciding what matters most this quarter.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Leading performance and benefits** and **Applying user-centred insights**: noticing the trend that matters in a dashboard of forty numbers.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance** and **Life cycle management**: reasoning validly from standards, spend controls and assessment conditions to their consequences.
- **Error checking** maps to **Financial ownership** and **Leading performance and benefits**: catching a benefits table that does not reconcile before it reaches an investment board.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the duty to **prioritise areas of development, balancing user needs, business requirements and policy**.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Operational management** and **Strategic ownership**: identifying and overcoming operational constraints, and turning strategic intent into a sequence of achievable moves.

Because this role also coaches others, several questions place a candidate in a reviewing position — checking a team's paper, spotting the flaw in a proposed measure, or deciding what feedback most improves someone else's reasoning.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a performance dashboard**

A service dashboard, five weeks: digital completion rate 71%, 72%, 71%, 73%, 72%; phone channel share 18%, 18%, 19%, 18%, 18%; user satisfaction 4.2, 4.2, 4.1, 4.2, 4.2; average processing time 6.1, 6.0, 6.4, 7.1, 8.2 days. Ask which metric most warrants investigation this week.

- A) Digital completion rate
- B) Phone channel share
- C) User satisfaction
- D) Average processing time

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Completion rate, phone share and satisfaction are all stable within a narrow band. Processing time has broken pattern — after two stable weeks it has risen three weeks running, with accelerating gaps (+0.4, +0.7, +1.1). This tests reading trajectory, not just level, and recognising an accelerating trend as a compounding problem that will not self-correct — satisfaction is still flat only because users have not yet felt the delay.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a spend control rule**

A spend control rule: "Any digital service spend above £100,000 requires spend approval, unless the spend is within a programme's existing approved envelope." A colleague reports the payments team committed £250,000 last month without seeking spend approval. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The payments team breached the spend control rules.
- B) The spend was within an existing approved envelope.
- C) Either the spend was within an existing approved envelope, or the rules were breached.
- D) The payments team's service owner authorised the spend.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one exception; the facts cannot establish which case applies, so neither A nor B follows alone, and D introduces information the facts never state. This tests deducing only what the evidence guarantees, and the restraint of verifying which case applies before accusing a team of a breach.

**Question 3 (easy) — Prioritisation of a service owner's morning**

9:00am Tuesday, four items: (1) the document-upload function has been failing intermittently since 7:30, roughly a quarter of users affected, team investigating; (2) the policy director's office wants comments on a draft ministerial submission by Thursday; (3) a supplier account manager wants to discuss next year's contract renewal this week; (4) a delivery manager needs approval of revised sprint goals before 10:00 stand-up. Ask what should be addressed first.

- A) Item 2 — ministerial business takes precedence
- B) Item 1 — a live incident affecting users outranks everything schedulable
- C) Item 4 — it takes two minutes and unblocks a team immediately
- D) Item 3 — contract renewals have long-term financial consequences

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is the only item currently causing user harm, requiring immediate engagement — confirming severity, ensuring the incident process runs, deciding on user communications. Option C's logic would be right if nothing were on fire — a common trap of clearing a satisfying small approval while a live incident goes unmanaged. This tests recognising user harm now as outranking stakeholder requests measured in days, whatever the requester's seniority.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Deduction through a service assessment condition**

A beta assessment condition: "The service may enter public beta only after (i) the assisted digital support route is live, and (ii) accessibility issues rated critical are resolved. A follow-up review of these conditions will be held eight weeks after the assessment." Assessment 1 March; assisted digital live 22 March; two critical accessibility issues resolved 5 April; a third found 12 April, still open. It is 20 April. Ask which statement must be true.

- A) The service may enter public beta, because the conditions were met on 5 April.
- B) The service may not yet enter public beta.
- C) The service must wait until the follow-up review on 26 April before entering public beta.
- D) The service's beta assessment result is void because a new critical issue was found.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Condition (i) is met; condition (ii) requires critical issues to be resolved as a current state, not a historical event, and a third critical issue is currently open — the condition is not met today. Option A misreads the condition as a one-time event; C invents a gating role for the scheduled review that the text does not state; D invents a voiding mechanism. This tests distinguishing a condition (a state that must hold), a gate, and a scheduled review as three different mechanisms.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits table**

A benefits table: Reduced phone contact £480,000 (60,000 fewer calls × £8); Faster processing £375,000 (25,000 hours × £15); Reduced error rework £150,000 (12,000 fewer errors × £12.50); Total £1,005,000. Ask how many lines are internally consistent, and whether the total is correct.

- A) All three lines are consistent, and the total is correct.
- B) All three lines are consistent, but the total is wrong.
- C) Two lines are consistent, one is not, and the total matches the stated line values.
- D) One line is consistent, two are not.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Each line reconciles (£480,000; £375,000; £150,000) and the sum (£1,005,000) is correct. This tests certifying clean work confidently rather than assuming an "error checking" question must contain an error — a false positive costs analyst time and teaches teams to discount future reviews, and it is a different check entirely from whether the underlying forecasts (60,000 fewer calls, say) are defensible evidence.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across the operating model**

Four regions, comparable volumes. Cases completed per staff member per week: North 42, Midlands 44, South 43, East 29. First-time resolution rate: North 81%, Midlands 79%, South 80%, East 91%. Ask for the most reasonable first interpretation.

- A) East is underperforming and needs a performance improvement plan.
- B) East may be trading throughput for quality — or handling a different case mix — and warrants investigation before judgement; the other regions cluster on both measures.
- C) North, Midlands and South are cutting corners and East is the only region doing the job properly.
- D) The figures cannot be compared because regions differ.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** East is a joint outlier — low throughput, high resolution — while the other three cluster on both measures, suggesting a deliberate or structural difference (case mix, local process, genuine trade-off) rather than a simple performance gap. This tests reading two metric series together rather than separately, and investigating before judging in either direction.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction about life cycle sequencing**

Rules: (i) the legacy case system cannot switch off until all case types migrate; (ii) the final case type (appeals) cannot migrate until policy signs off revised guidance; (iii) dual running is fundable for a maximum of six months without additional funding; (iv) dual running began 1 April. Policy sign-off is expected 1 July; appeals migration takes two months after sign-off. Ask, if everything runs to expectation, what follows.

- A) Legacy switch-off occurs by 1 September, within the dual-running window.
- B) Legacy switch-off occurs by 1 September, breaching the dual-running window.
- C) Legacy switch-off cannot occur before 1 September, leaving one month of funded dual running as contingency.
- D) Additional funding is already unavoidable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Appeals migration completes 1 September, so switch-off cannot occur before then; the six-month dual-running window (from 1 April) ends 1 October, leaving one month of contingency — the logically guaranteed statement, more precise than A's assumption of instant switch-off. This tests chaining dependencies to their guaranteed conclusion and recognising a single month of contingency as thin risk appetite worth actively managing.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking a governance paper's logic**

A paper argues: "User satisfaction has risen from 3.9 to 4.3 since we launched the new interface in January. This proves the redesign caused the improvement. We should therefore apply the same design patterns to the staff-facing casework system, where satisfaction will rise similarly." Ask for the weakest link in the argument.

- A) The satisfaction data itself — a 0.4 rise is within measurement noise and should be ignored.
- B) The causal claim — other changes since January (a fee reduction, faster processing, seasonal case mix) could explain some or all of the rise, so "proves" overstates.
- C) The transfer claim — staff users, tasks, and contexts differ from citizens', so even a genuine citizen-facing gain does not imply a similar staff-facing gain.
- D) Both B and C are weak, but C is the weakest because it compounds the causal leap with a generalisation to a different population.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The causal claim is a post hoc fallacy (B); the transfer claim inherits that flaw and compounds it with an unexamined population change (C), making D the fullest correct grading. This tests grading multiple linked flaws in an argument rather than identifying only the first one, and recognising that a strong case for one investment does not automatically license a different one.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling of governance events**

Three events, one per day, Monday–Friday: a service assessment mock (M), a quarterly performance review with the director (Q), a supplier contract negotiation (N). Constraints: (i) M on Monday or Tuesday; (ii) the director is available only Tuesday and Thursday, and must attend Q; (iii) the commercial lead must attend both N and Q, unavailable Tuesday; (iv) N must be later in the week than Q; (v) the team attending M is fully occupied with a production release Monday. Ask on which days the three events take place.

- A) M Monday, Q Tuesday, N Thursday
- B) M Tuesday, Q Thursday, N Friday
- C) M Monday, Q Thursday, N Friday
- D) M Tuesday, Q Wednesday, N Thursday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Q needs both the director (Tuesday/Thursday) and the commercial lead (not Tuesday) — only Thursday satisfies both. N must follow Q and include the commercial lead — only Friday. M must avoid Monday (release) — Tuesday. This tests pinning the most constrained event first, then verifying the surviving option against every constraint — option C fails only the fifth constraint, rewarding a candidate who checks mechanically rather than stopping at "looks right."

**Question 10 (hard) — Operational constraint analysis**

Four-stage pipeline: intake (capacity 500/day), verification (300/day), decision (350/day), notification (600/day). Demand 400/day. A proposal offers £200,000 to raise decision capacity from 350 to 500 to "clear the growing backlog." Ask for the flaw in the proposal.

- A) There is no flaw — decision capacity below demand is the constraint.
- B) The constraint is verification at 300/day; raising decision capacity leaves throughput at 300/day and the backlog still growing by 100/day, so the £200,000 buys nothing until verification is expanded.
- C) The constraint is intake, which should be reduced to 300/day to stop backlog forming.
- D) Notification's spare capacity should be redeployed to decision instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Pipeline throughput equals the minimum stage capacity — verification at 300/day, below demand of 400/day. Decision at 350/day already has slack relative to what verification releases to it; raising it further adds slack to a non-constraint stage. This tests finding the true bottleneck before evaluating an investment proposal, since investment anywhere else is stranded capacity.

**Question 11 (hard) — Prioritisation across the whole service**

Budget for two of four candidate investments: (1) rebuild the address-change journey, removes 35% of avoidable contact, £300k; (2) accessibility remediation, legally required, resolves all known WCAG failures, £150k; (3) a native mobile app duplicating the web journey, no new capability, £250k, adds permanent run cost; (4) automation of a manual back-office step, £180k/yr saving, £200k cost, ~13-month payback. Strategy commits to reduced failure demand, improved accessibility, and sustainable running costs. Budget: £500k. Ask which pair best serves the strategy.

- A) 1 and 3
- B) 2 and 3
- C) 1 and 2
- D) 2 and 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option 2 is a legal obligation, not discretionary, ruling out any pair without it; option 3 advances none of the three strategic outcomes and adds permanent cost, ruling out B and A. Between 1+2 (£450k) and 2+4 (£350k), option 1 directly attacks the largest failure-demand source the strategy names, and fits budget alongside the legal obligation, while option 4's strong payback case can wait a year and be funded from the headroom option 1 creates. This tests filtering by obligation and strategy fit before weighing financial attractiveness alone.

**Question 12 (hard) — Coaching a team's reasoning before a board**

A pitch rehearsal: "Velocity is up 20% since March, so the team is 20% more productive, so we can commit to the expanded scope in the same timeline. Also, we've had no severity-1 incidents for six months, which shows our quality engineering investment has eliminated major incident risk, so we can reduce the on-call rota." Ask for the most useful coaching feedback.

- A) Both arguments are sound; the pitch is board-ready.
- B) The velocity argument confuses a relative, gameable internal measure with productivity, and the incident argument treats absence of rare events as proof of eliminated risk; both conclusions (scope commitment, rota reduction) need stronger evidence — though the underlying trends are worth presenting honestly as positive signals.
- C) Velocity should never be mentioned to boards; remove the first argument entirely.
- D) The incident argument is sound because six months is a long time; only the velocity argument needs work.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Velocity is a gameable relative measure that cannot carry a public timeline commitment; six months without a rare severity-1 event is weak evidence of eliminated risk, and reducing on-call is precisely the action that would convert a future incident from managed to catastrophic. This tests identifying two distinct reasoning flaws feeding two risky conclusions, while preserving the genuinely positive underlying trends at their honest strength rather than discarding them (C) or endorsing them uncritically (A, D).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads trajectory (accelerating trend) rather than level alone in a dashboard scenario.
- Note whether the candidate certifies clean work confidently rather than manufacturing an error in a checking question.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes a condition, a gate, and a scheduled review as different governance mechanisms.
- Score for whether the candidate finds the true bottleneck in a pipeline before evaluating an investment proposal.
- Note whether the candidate filters investment options by hard obligation and strategic fit before weighing financial attractiveness.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that imports outside organisational knowledge the stem does not supply.
- Missing when a candidate reads only one metric series where the signal lies in reading two together.
- Rewarding a claim that verified arithmetic settles a question that is actually about the evidence quality behind the inputs.
- Accepting an answer that invests in a non-bottleneck stage of a pipeline.
- Missing when a candidate lets a small, satisfying task jump the queue ahead of active user harm.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures the ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from quantitative information presented as it actually arrives in this role: service performance tables, benefits realisation trackers, budget forecasts, supplier pricing schedules, channel-shift charts, and user research statistics. The arithmetic rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, averages, rates and simple projections — the difficulty lies in reading the data accurately, choosing the right operation, and resisting plausible-looking wrong answers under time pressure.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions based on tables, charts, or data extracts.

For a service owner, numeric reasoning is the language of accountability: ensuring a business case is in place that provides sustainable funding, identifying measurable benefits that represent good value for money, allocating money across competing priorities, and demonstrating the value of the service to stakeholders through data.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and variances** map to **Financial ownership**: spend against budget, benefit against forecast, unit costs against benchmarks.
- **Rates, unit costs and trends** map to **Leading performance and benefits**: cost per transaction, take-up rate, completion rate, failure demand rate.
- **Ratios and proportions** map to **Operational management**: staff-to-volume ratios and capacity-to-demand across pipeline stages.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Applying user-centred insights**.
- **Benefits and investment arithmetic** maps to **Financial ownership** and **Strategic ownership**: business cases, break-even analysis, and benefits realisation tracking.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Digital take-up percentage**

45,000 transactions last month, 33,750 completed online. Ask for the digital take-up rate.

- A) 70%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 33,750÷45,000=75% — option C. This tests basic rate calculation and, in a strong answer, defining the base explicitly (transactions, not users) and asking what the remaining 25% represents — assisted digital working as designed, or failure demand — before drawing any conclusion.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average cost per transaction**

Monthly running cost £360,000, 45,000 transactions. A comparable service reports £7.20 unit cost. Ask for the cost per transaction and the comparison.

- A) £8.00 — 80p higher than the comparator
- B) £8.00 — 80p lower than the comparator
- C) £7.20 — the same as the comparator
- D) £12.50 — £5.30 higher than the comparator

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £360,000÷45,000=£8.00, 80p above the comparator — option A. This tests basic unit-cost calculation and, in a strong answer, questioning whether the two figures share the same cost boundary (overheads, depreciation) before treating the comparison as meaningful.

**Question 3 (easy) — SLA percentage from a volume table**

Cases resolved within SLA: Monday 210/230, Tuesday 190/200, Wednesday 160/200, Thursday 180/190, Friday 160/180. Ask for the whole-week SLA achievement rate.

- A) 88%
- B) 90%
- C) 92%
- D) 94%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum both columns: 900÷1,000=90% — option B, not the average of daily percentages (which would diverge badly with more variable daily volumes). This tests summing numerators and denominators before dividing, and — in a strong answer — noticing Wednesday's 80% outlier hidden inside the 90% weekly headline.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Channel shift savings projection**

Phone transactions cost £6.50 each; online £0.90 each. 12,000 phone transactions/month; a proposal moves 40% online. Ask for the projected monthly saving.

- A) £31,200
- B) £26,880
- C) £74,880
- D) £22,464

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 4,800 transactions move; saving per transaction £5.60 (not the full £6.50, since online still costs something); total £26,880 — option B. This tests netting a channel-shift saving against the receiving channel's cost, and — in a strong answer — noting that phone costs are partly fixed, so cash savings depend on capacity actually being reduced or redeployed.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Percentage points versus percentages in a KPI report**

Draft: (i) "Digital take-up rose from 68% to 75% this year — a 7% increase." (ii) "Failure demand fell from 20% of contact to 15% — a 25% reduction." Ask which statement uses its terminology correctly.

- A) Both
- B) Only (i)
- C) Only (ii)
- D) Neither

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** (i) is 7 percentage points, a 10.3% relative rise — "a 7% increase" conflates points with percent. (ii)'s 5-point fall is correctly a 25% relative reduction. This tests distinguishing points from percent, and, in a strong answer, publishing both forms when the distinction could mislead an audience.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Weighted service performance score**

Weights: satisfaction 40%, completion 30%, cost-versus-target 20%, accessibility 10%. Scores: 80, 90, 60, 100. Ask for the weighted score.

- A) 80.0
- B) 82.5
- C) 81.0
- D) 79.0

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** (0.40×80)+(0.30×90)+(0.20×60)+(0.10×100)=81 — option C, not the unweighted 82.5 mean. This tests weighted averaging correctly, and — in a strong answer — presenting the composite alongside its worst component (cost at 60) rather than letting the headline conceal it, and questioning whether legally required measures (like accessibility) belong inside a weighted composite at all.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Reading a benefits realisation tracker**

Benefit baselined £1,200,000 over 4 years, £300,000/year profile. Actuals: year 1 £180,000, year 2 £240,000. Commentary: "Realisation is accelerating; we remain on track for the four-year total." Ask for a realistic four-year total and whether the commentary is fair.

- A) £1,200,000 — the commentary is fair
- B) About £1,020,000 — realisation is genuinely accelerating, but reaching the full baseline requires years 3 and 4 to over-deliver against profile, which the commentary should say plainly
- C) £420,000 — the benefit has failed
- D) £840,000 — assume years 3 and 4 repeat year 2

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Projecting the improving trend (+£60,000/year) gives roughly £1.0–1.1m, in option B's band; reaching £1,200,000 needs years 3–4 at 130% of profile. This tests projecting a trend honestly, and recognising that "accelerating" is true while "on track for the total" overstates — the commentary should state position, trajectory, and the explicit condition for full recovery.

**Question 8 (moderate) — User research sample interpretation**

Usability testing: 4 of 5 completed the new journey unaided, versus 2 of 5 on the old. Survey (n=1,200): satisfaction 71% ± 3% (95% confidence). A stakeholder claims: "Research proves the new journey doubles completion (80% vs 40%), and satisfaction is definitely between 68% and 74%." Ask for the most accurate critique.

- A) The summary is fine — it restates the figures correctly.
- B) The usability sample (n=5) supports a directional finding, not stable percentages like "80% vs 40%", and "proves" and "doubles" overstate it; the survey claim is roughly right in spirit, though 95% confidence means "very probably", not "definitely".
- C) Both studies are worthless because the samples differ in size.
- D) The survey is invalid because 1,200 is too few of the service's users.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Five-participant usability testing supports directional findings, not stable percentages claimed as proof; the survey's confidence interval is close to right but "definitely" overstates a 95% confidence level slightly. This tests grading two different research methods on their own appropriate terms, protecting each from both overstatement and dismissal — D misunderstands that 1,200 is ample for ±3% regardless of population size.

**Question 9 (hard) — Supplier pricing comparison across volume**

Supplier A: £2.10/document, no minimum. Supplier B: £1.60/document plus £14,000/month platform fee. Current volume 24,000/month, forecast +25% next year. Ask (a) which is cheaper now, and (b) the break-even volume.

- A) A is cheaper now; break-even at 28,000 documents
- B) B is cheaper now; break-even at 28,000 documents
- C) B is cheaper now; break-even at 20,000 documents
- D) A is cheaper now; break-even at 35,000 documents

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** At 24,000: A=£50,400, B=£52,400 — A cheaper by £2,000. Break-even: £0.50×V=£14,000, V=28,000. Option A pairs both correctly. This tests computing a fixed-fee break-even volume, and — in a strong answer — applying the 25% growth forecast (30,000, past break-even) to note that B would win at forecast volume, making contract length and forecast confidence part of the real decision.

**Question 10 (hard) — Backlog and recovery arithmetic**

5-day outage, 400 applications/day continuing to arrive; normal capacity 460/day against 400/day demand. Ask (a) how many working days to clear the backlog after restoration, and (b) roughly how many days of full-capacity processing the backlog represents.

- A) 20 days to clear; the backlog represents about 10 days of full-capacity processing
- B) About 33 days to clear; the backlog represents about 4.3 days of full-capacity processing
- C) 20 days to clear; the backlog represents about 20 days of full-capacity processing
- D) 5 days to clear; the backlog represents about 2.5 days of full-capacity processing

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Backlog 5×400=2,000; net recovery rate 460−400=60/day; days to clear 2,000÷60≈33.3; backlog in full-capacity terms 2,000÷460≈4.3 days. This tests recognising that recovery runs on thin headroom (60/day), not full capacity, so a small outage produces a disproportionately long recovery — a recovery multiplier (arrival rate ÷ headroom) worth computing before an incident, not during one.

**Question 11 (hard) — Business case break-even with optimism bias**

Build cost £800,000 spread over years 1–2; net benefits £0 years 1–2, then £320,000/year from year 3. Treasury guidance: 40% optimism bias on build cost, 20% benefit reduction. Ask for the cumulative break-even year after adjustment.

- A) End of year 5
- B) During year 6
- C) During year 7
- D) During year 8

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Adjusted build £1,120,000 (£560,000/year); adjusted benefit £256,000/year from year 3; cumulative position turns positive during year 7. Option A is the unadjusted case. This tests applying Treasury-style appraisal adjustments before modelling, and recognising the adjusted figure as the planning basis — the gap between adjusted and unadjusted is the delivery challenge, not a number to quote as the plan.

**Question 12 (hard) — Interpreting a demand forecast against capacity plans**

Applications: 18,000 (two years ago), 21,600 (last year), 25,920 (this year). A proposal adds the recent average annual increase (~3,960) to this year's volume. Ask, if growth is actually compound, for the correct forecast and the shortfall in the proposed plan.

- A) Plan for 29,880; no shortfall — the proposal matches
- B) Plan for 31,104; the proposal underestimates by about 1,224
- C) Plan for 31,104; the proposal underestimates by about 5,184
- D) Plan for 33,696; the proposal underestimates by about 3,816

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Ratios (21,600÷18,000=1.20; 25,920÷21,600=1.20) confirm 20% compound growth; correct forecast 25,920×1.20=31,104, against the linear proposal's 29,880 — a shortfall of 1,224. This tests checking whether a series is linear or compound (two ratio checks) before extrapolating, since linear extrapolation of compound growth systematically undershoots by a growing margin each year.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate names the base before computing a rate or percentage change.
- Note whether the candidate nets a gross saving against a receiving-channel or receiving-stage cost.
- Watch for whether the candidate checks a series' growth pattern (linear versus compound) before extrapolating.
- Score for whether the candidate applies specified appraisal adjustments (optimism bias, benefit haircuts) before modelling a business case.
- Note whether the candidate questions a tracker's qualitative commentary ("on track," "accelerating") against its own arithmetic.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an averaged percentage across different volumes rather than aggregated numerators and denominators.
- Missing when a candidate counts a gross saving as net, without deducting the receiving channel or stage's cost.
- Rewarding points-versus-percent confusion in a KPI claim.
- Accepting a single-volume-point comparison of fixed-fee-versus-per-unit pricing without a break-even calculation.
- Missing linear extrapolation applied to a demonstrably compound growth series.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate extracts meaning from written material under time pressure, and how well they distinguish between what a text states, what it implies, and what it simply does not address. The materials mirror this role's working reading diet: service standard guidance, assessment reports, policy instructions, ministerial correspondence, supplier letters, user research summaries, and board minutes.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 15 to 30 questions, most commonly true / false / cannot say, plus inference strength, main-point identification, and clear-writing judgement. The cardinal rule is answering from the passage alone.

For a service owner, verbal reasoning is core infrastructure: informing and influencing stakeholders and building consensus that enables work to progress, guiding teams through governance and assurance processes, and sharing performance narratives that must be exactly as strong as the evidence beneath them.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Comprehension of dense passages** maps to **Governance and assurance**: extracting an assessment report's or spend control letter's meaning exactly, including hedges and conditions.
- **True/False/Cannot Say discipline** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and the role's accountability for decisions.
- **Inference judgement** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Leading performance and benefits**.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Strategic ownership** and influencing and persuading stakeholders.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to the duty to **inform and influence stakeholders, getting consensus that enables work to progress**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot Say on service standard guidance**

Passage: "An assessment at the end of discovery is not required; however, services with over 100,000 transactions per year must be assessed before moving from alpha to private beta, and again before moving from private beta to public beta. Assessment reports are published openly unless publication would compromise security."

Statement: "All assessment reports are published openly."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stated exception ("unless publication would compromise security") contradicts "all": False. This tests hunting for the "unless" before answering an absolute statement, and, in a strong answer, recognising that the exception's existence does not establish how often it is exercised — a weaker claim like "some reports are withheld" would be Cannot Say.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a spend approval letter precisely**

Passage: "Approval is granted for expenditure of up to £2.4 million on the case management replacement, conditional on: (a) reuse of the department's existing hosting arrangements; (b) a benefits review at the six-month point; and (c) return to this board before any contract extension beyond the initial two-year term. Approval does not constitute endorsement of the proposed supplier."

Statement: "The board has approved £2.4 million of spending on the case management replacement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Approval "of up to £2.4 million" for the named purpose accurately supports the statement; the conditions qualify how the approval must be exercised without negating it. This tests reading an approval at its exact scope — amount, purpose, and what was pointedly not approved (the supplier).

**Question 3 (easy) — Identifying the main point of an assessment report extract**

Passage: "The team demonstrated a strong understanding of its users, supported by regular research with participants across the full range of access needs. The service performed well in usability testing. However, the panel was not shown evidence that the service can operate within its projected running costs, and the operating model relies on manual steps that will not scale at public-beta volumes. The panel's view is that these operational and financial gaps, not the user experience, represent the principal risk to the service's sustainability."

Ask for the report's main message.

- A) The team does not understand its users.
- B) The service's usability needs improvement before public beta.
- C) The principal risk to sustainability is operational and financial, not user experience.
- D) The service should be stopped.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The main point is explicitly stated after the pivot ("however"), directly naming operational and financial gaps as the principal risk. A and B contradict the praised elements; D overreaches beyond what the panel recommends. This tests finding the load-bearing sentence in a "praise, however, verdict" structure rather than leading with the opening praise.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Cannot Say discipline on a policy letter**

Passage: "Ministers have agreed in principle to the simplification of the eligibility rules from next April. Detailed regulations are being drafted and will be subject to consultation this autumn. We anticipate the consultation will conclude by December. The service should therefore begin exploratory work on implementing the simplified rules, while recognising that final policy positions may change through the consultation process."

Statement: "The simplified eligibility rules will take effect next April."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Agreed in principle" and "may change through the consultation process" together neither guarantee nor rule out the April date: Cannot Say. This tests reading "in principle" as a load-bearing qualifier, not a settled fact, and — in a strong answer — planning exploratory work now while not committing hard delivery dates to the unconfirmed date.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from board minutes**

Passage: "The board received the service's quarterly performance report. Members noted the improvement in digital take-up and the reduction in failure demand. The chair asked for a deep-dive on accessibility compliance at the next meeting. The proposed expansion to a second user group was deferred pending the outcome of the spending review. The service owner was asked to provide monthly, rather than quarterly, financial reporting until further notice."

Ask for the best-supported inference.

- A) The board is dissatisfied with the service's overall performance.
- B) The board has increased its financial scrutiny of the service.
- C) The expansion to a second user group has been cancelled.
- D) The service has failed its accessibility obligations.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The move to monthly financial reporting is a concrete, recorded act of increased scrutiny. Option A overreaches (heightened scrutiny coexists with recorded praise); C distorts "deferred pending" into "cancelled"; D converts a requested deep-dive into a finding. This tests calibrated inference at exactly the strength the evidence supports.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot Say on supplier correspondence**

Passage: "We acknowledge the service credits applied for the October performance failures. We have implemented the agreed remediation plan, and November performance met all service levels. Given this recovery, we request that the credits for October be waived as a gesture of partnership. We note that continued application of credits may require us to review the resourcing model for this account."

Statement: "The supplier has threatened to withdraw from the contract if the credits are not waived."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Review the resourcing model" is a pointed but unspecific signal, not a stated threat to withdraw: Cannot Say. This tests distinguishing a vague commercial pressure signal from an actual stated threat, while still acting on the subtext (raise it at the next service review) without logging it as fact.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Judging the clearest rewrite**

A draft reads: "It is considered that the currently observed deterioration in the timeliness of case processing could potentially be attributable, at least in part, to the fact that the volume of incoming cases has been trending upwards, although other contributing factors cannot at this stage be definitively ruled out." Ask which rewrite is clearest while preserving the meaning.

- A) "Case processing has slowed because case volumes have risen."
- B) "Case processing has slowed. Rising case volumes are the likely main cause; we are checking other possible factors."
- C) "Rising volumes may possibly be a potential factor in timeliness deterioration, among others not yet excluded."
- D) "Case processing timeliness has deteriorated and volumes have risen, but no conclusions can be drawn."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B preserves the fact, the leading probable cause at calibrated confidence, and the active caveat, in plain words. A over-claims settled causation; C reproduces the fog with fewer words; D discards the genuine probable-cause finding. This tests coaching writing to state the fact, give the leading explanation at its honest probability, and convert caveats into owned actions.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Translating research findings for a policy audience**

Passage: "Across 18 usability sessions with participants who have low digital confidence, 14 abandoned the identity verification step. The most common failure point was the requirement for a photocard driving licence or passport: 11 of the 14 who abandoned held neither document. Participants who reached the alternative postal route completed it successfully, but 9 participants did not notice the postal option existed."

Ask which two-sentence briefing for a policy director is accurate.

- A) "Users cannot complete identity verification; the service is failing and needs a policy change to remove verification."
- B) "Most low-digital-confidence users in our research abandoned at identity verification, mainly because they lack passports or driving licences; the postal alternative works, but most participants never found it. We should make the postal route prominent now, and this evidence supports the case for accepting a wider range of documents."
- C) "14 of 18 users failed the service, proving that 78% of citizens cannot verify their identity."
- D) "There are some usability issues at verification which the team is addressing; no policy involvement is needed."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B preserves the segment scope, the causal chain, and splits response correctly between what the team can fix now (postal route prominence) and what needs policy movement (accepted documents). Option A inflates a segment finding into "users" generally; C converts 18 qualitative sessions into a population percentage; D hides the policy-relevant fact. This tests translating findings across a technical-policy boundary without gaining or losing strength.

**Question 9 (hard) — Cannot Say under diplomatic drafting**

Passage: "We recognise the dependency your service has on the address-lookup component and the importance of its availability to your users. Funding for the component beyond March is part of our current planning round, in which a number of priorities are being weighed. We will communicate the outcome as soon as decisions are made. In the meantime, we suggest your continuity planning reflect the full range of possible outcomes."

Statement: "Funding for the address-lookup component will end in March."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The letter states no decision has been made either way: Cannot Say. This tests distinguishing text from subtext — the letter's careful wording ("a number of priorities... being weighed," "reflect the full range of possible outcomes") is a real warning worth acting on, while the text itself establishes neither continuation nor cessation as fact.

**Question 10 (hard) — Evaluating argument strength in a prioritisation debate**

Passage: "At the prioritisation board, the operations head argued: 'Complaint volumes rose 30% in the quarter after we launched the new portal. The portal is clearly driving complaints and we should roll it back.' The product manager responded: 'Total transactions rose 45% in the same period, so complaints per transaction actually fell. Moreover, the complaint categorisation shows the rise concentrated in fee disputes, which relate to the fee change that took effect the same month as launch. Rolling back the portal would sacrifice the take-up gain without addressing the actual driver.'"

Ask for the most accurate analysis.

- A) The operations head's argument stands because absolute complaint volumes are what users experience.
- B) The product manager's response is stronger: it normalises by volume and offers a competing cause with distinguishing evidence — though "fee disputes" categorisation would still benefit from verification before the rollback question is finally closed.
- C) Both arguments are equally valid interpretations of the same data.
- D) The product manager has proven the portal caused no complaints.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Normalising by volume shows the complaint rate actually fell, and a competing, evidenced cause (the fee change) defeats a post hoc causal claim — but the categorisation itself deserves verification before the question is fully closed. This tests crediting the stronger argument's method (rate over count, evidenced alternative cause) while not overclaiming it as proof.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reading a governance framework against a scenario**

Passage: "Services in public beta must report performance monthly to the service board. Where a service misses a key performance indicator for two consecutive months, the service owner must present a recovery plan to the board. Where a KPI is missed for three consecutive months, the board must consider whether the service should return to private beta. A KPI change may be proposed at any time but takes effect only from the start of the next reporting quarter."

Scenario: A service missed its completion-rate KPI in April and May. In early June a revised (lower) KPI was proposed, accepted by the board on 20 June. June performance met the revised threshold but missed the original one. It is early July. Ask for the accurate statement.

- A) No recovery plan is required, because the revised KPI was met in June.
- B) A recovery plan was already required after May's miss, and June counts as a third consecutive miss against the original KPI — which remained in effect through June — so the board must consider a return to private beta.
- C) The June KPI change applies retrospectively to the whole quarter, so only April and May count as misses.
- D) The board may not consider a return to private beta because a KPI change was in progress.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The recovery-plan duty triggered after April–May; the KPI change takes effect only from the next reporting quarter, so the original KPI governed June, making it a third consecutive miss, triggering the "must consider" duty (not a mandated outcome). Options A and C misapply the KPI change too early; D invents a suspension mechanism. This tests applying a multi-clause governance framework's timing rules exactly against a scenario.

**Question 12 (hard) — Synthesising three documents into a decision brief**

Passage: Three documents about a proposed service expansion. (1) User research: "Research with 24 participants from the proposed new user group indicates their needs differ substantially from current users; 60% of tested journeys required assisted support. Serving them well would require significant journey redesign." (2) Finance: "Expansion within the current operating model adds an estimated £900,000 annual cost against £600,000 in attributable annual benefits. A redesigned model could reduce the cost gap but carries delivery risk and an unquantified build cost." (3) Policy steer: "Ministers attach high priority to coverage of the new group within this Parliament and expect a credible plan by year end; timing within that window is for the department to propose."

Ask which decision brief most accurately synthesises the three documents.

- A) "Research, finance, and policy all point against expansion; we should decline and record the risk."
- B) "Ministers require immediate expansion; we should proceed with the current model and manage the losses."
- C) "The documents converge on a credible path: not whether but how and when — a redesigned journey and operating model, sequenced to produce a plan by year end, meeting policy priority within this Parliament while avoiding the current model's £300,000 annual shortfall and the research-evidenced mismatch."
- D) "The documents conflict irreconcilably; the decision should be escalated to ministers to resolve."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** None of the three documents opposes expansion outright — each specifies conditions (redesign, timing flexibility) rather than a veto, and read together they support expanding differently and on a timeline the department controls. Option A reads every caveat as a veto; B invents urgency the policy steer explicitly delegates; D escalates a decision the documents already equip the reader to make. This tests finding the reading that honours every genuine constraint across multiple stakeholder documents, rather than declaring false conflict or false consensus.

### Administration tips

- Score for the three-way test applied mechanically — could the statement be false while the passage stays true?
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes stated fact from evident subtext in diplomatically drafted correspondence.
- Note whether the candidate reads qualifier vocabulary ("in principle," "anticipate," "may require") at its exact commitment level.
- For argument-evaluation items, score on whether the candidate credits the stronger method without overclaiming proof.
- For multi-document synthesis items, score on whether the candidate finds a reading that honours every genuine constraint rather than manufacturing false conflict or consensus.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that imports outside professional or organisational knowledge the passage does not state.
- Missing when a candidate reports subtext as established fact in a Cannot Say scenario.
- Rewarding a statement that treats an intention ("agreed in principle," "deferred pending") as a settled decision.
- Accepting an absolute statement as true on partial support, or as false without a stated contradiction.
- Missing when a candidate fails to notice a stakeholder document delegates timing or method while fixing only the destination.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Formats vary: most effective, most and least effective, full ranking, or independent rating. Scoring reflects the judgement of experienced practitioners and the employer's professional behaviour framework — for GDAD roles, behaviours such as user focus, collaboration, ownership, transparency, proportionate escalation, and inclusion.

The typical format is 12 to 25 scenarios in 25 to 40 minutes, usually untimed per item but timed overall.

For a service owner, the SJT is arguably the most role-relevant of the four assessments: accountable for outcomes delivered through teams to be empowered rather than commanded, prioritising between user needs, business requirements and policy, acting as escalation point for major operational issues and financial risk, and creating the open, trust-based culture in which teams do their best work.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Accountability and escalation scenarios** map to being **an accountable leader** and **the escalation point for major operational issues**.
- **Stakeholder scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and the duty to **inform and influence stakeholders, getting consensus that enables work to progress**.
- **User-versus-pressure scenarios** map to **Applying user-centred insights**.
- **Financial and supplier scenarios** map to **Financial ownership**.
- **Governance scenarios** map to **Governance and assurance**.
- **Team and culture scenarios** map to **creating the culture and environment for the team to do their best work**, **Life cycle management**, and **Operational management**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The dashboard nobody wants shown**

Preparing for the service board, this month's completion rate has dropped four percentage points — the first decline in a year. A delivery manager suggests leaving it off the summary slide as "probably a blip." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Agree — presenting unverified bad news wastes board time and may be reversed next month.
- B) Include the figure with what is known so far and the investigation under way, and brief the chair before the meeting so the board hears it with context from the candidate rather than discovering it.
- C) Include the figure but present it as a data-quality issue until the cause is confirmed.
- D) Postpone the board's performance item until the cause is understood.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B keeps the record true, gives it context, and pre-briefs the chair (respect, not spin). Option A gambles credibility on an unverified guess; C makes an unevidenced claim to the board; D holds governance hostage to comfort. This tests defending the truthfulness of a performance story even when the news is unwelcome, and recognising that curated dashboards, once discovered, discredit every future green.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit where it is due**

At a cross-government showcase, a director praises "your brilliant redesign of the eligibility checker," when it was conceived and led by an interaction designer standing nearby. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Thank the director and describe the results the redesign achieved.
- B) Redirect the credit immediately and specifically — name the designer, their role in conceiving it, and invite them into the conversation.
- C) Say "the whole team worked hard on it" and continue.
- D) Thank the director now and pass the compliment to the designer privately later.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Public, specific credit sponsors the designer's visibility and signals to everyone watching that good work is seen and named — accountability and credit are different currencies. Option C launders a specific achievement into an anonymous collective; D pays a private currency for a public moment. This tests specific public credit as a cheap, high-value culture-building act.

**Question 3 (easy) — The minister's office wants a date**

A minister's office wants a public launch date; the honest team position is high confidence within a three-month window, with private beta results in six weeks narrowing it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Give the middle of the window as the announced date — a 50/50 chance is acceptable for announcements.
- B) Give the end of the window as the announced date, privately treating the margin as contingency.
- C) Explain the confidence position to the director, recommend announcing the window now or a firm date in six weeks when beta evidence lands, and offer to help draft lines that give the office something concrete without a guess.
- D) Decline to provide any date until delivery is certain.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C converts uncertainty into stakeholder-usable honesty — a window now, or a firmer date in six weeks, both announceable, with practical help drafting lines. Option A manufactures a coin-flip commitment; B announces the worst case as the plan; D mistakes rigidity for integrity. This tests reshaping an unanswerable ask into something honest and useful, rather than guessing or stonewalling.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Policy pressure against user evidence**

A policy team, under deadline pressure, wants a mandatory declaration step added within six weeks; user research shows significant drop-out among low-literacy users with this kind of step, and accessibility flags likely compliance issues with the proposed wording. The policy intent has ministerial backing. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Implement as requested — policy with ministerial backing outranks service preferences.
- B) Refuse to implement until full research on the specific proposal is complete.
- C) Take the policy intent as fixed and the implementation as negotiable: bring the evidence to the policy team, co-design an alternative that achieves the declaration's legal purpose with accessible wording and lower-friction placement, test it quickly, and agree the six-week plan around the revised design — escalating jointly only if the intent-versus-evidence conflict proves real.
- D) Implement the step as specified but add an analytics dashboard to monitor drop-out so the impact is documented.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C separates the fixed policy intent from the negotiable implementation details, bringing evidence as design input rather than veto. Option A treats implementation detail as ministerial instruction it is not; B spends relationship capital on a maximal position; D documents known harm without preventing it. This tests balancing user needs, business requirements and policy as synthesis, not ranking.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The supplier relationship and the record**

A supplier has missed its availability SLA for two consecutive months; a well-liked account director asks to hold service credits "in abeyance" while an inherited platform problem is fixed, to avoid triggering their internal red-account process. The contract requires automatic credits on breach. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Hold the credits in abeyance for one quarter in exchange for a written recovery commitment.
- B) Apply the credits as the contract requires, tell the account director openly that this is being done and why, and offer genuine partnership on the recovery plan — including senior air-cover for their engineers' remediation time.
- C) Apply double scrutiny: credits, plus immediate escalation to a formal contract dispute, to establish deterrence.
- D) Apply the credits but agree privately to refund them via a future change order if recovery succeeds.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B applies the contractual mechanism as required while doing everything legitimate for the relationship openly. Option A trades organisational remedies for an unenforceable promise; C is disproportionate before recovery is attempted; D is A in disguise plus a procurement irregularity. This tests keeping the record and the relationship as separate tracks that never trade against each other.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Major incident, imperfect information**

A service has been intermittently failing for 40 minutes, ~30% of users unable to submit applications, cause unknown; the press office wants a public line within the hour; the director is unaware; the incident team's best guess at resolution is "hours, not minutes." Ask for the most effective first actions.

- A) Wait for the cause to be identified before communicating anything — premature statements create risk.
- B) Confirm the incident process is running with a clear lead; get a brief, honest holding line to the press office (impact acknowledged, workaround and alternative channel signposted, update time promised); send the director a two-line heads-up; ensure users see status information in the service itself; and schedule the next internal update cycle.
- C) Take direct command of the technical response personally, relocating to sit with the incident team until resolution.
- D) Issue a public apology accepting responsibility and promising resolution by end of day.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B manages five information flows correctly: the fixing system (confirmed, not commandeered), users, the press office, the director (before they hear from anyone else), and update rhythm. Option A confuses cause with impact (already known and already public); C decapitates the incident lead's coordination role; D over-commits on responsibility and a deadline that is only a guess. This tests major incident response as an information-flow role, not a heroic-technical one.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The benefits figure that will not survive scrutiny**

An annual benefits report's largest benefit, £1.1m of "staff time released," rests on a two-year-old assumption operational data now shows was roughly double reality; the corrected figure is nearer £600,000. The original figure has been quoted publicly and cited in a live funding case. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Report the £1.1 million this year — the assumption was legitimate when made — and quietly correct the methodology from next year.
- B) Correct the figure now, brief the director before the report lands (with the reason, the corrected number, and revised lines to take), and re-cut the funding case on the corrected benefit — strengthening it with other evidenced value where the case needs it.
- C) Report a range of £600,000 to £1.1 million to acknowledge uncertainty without singling out the old figure.
- D) Ask the finance team to decide which figure to use, since benefits methodology is their domain.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Once the assumption is known to be wrong, £1.1m is a misstatement, not an estimate; option B corrects it while it is smallest, briefs the director before ambush, and rebuilds the funding case on defensible ground. Option A is knowing misstatement with a rationalisation; C launders a known error as a range; D delegates a non-delegable accountability. This tests correcting a known error at the point of knowledge rather than at the point of discovery by someone else.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Two teams, one culture problem**

Team A ships steadily with honest retrospectives and early problem reporting; Team B ships erratically but presents polished, always-green updates — and a departing developer reveals Team B's stand-ups have become status theatre, with real blockers discussed privately afterwards. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Commission an independent health check of both teams to gather comparable evidence before acting.
- B) Move Team A's delivery manager to run both teams, since their approach demonstrably works.
- C) Address the system before the individual: examine whether personal reactions reward green reporting and punish candour; talk with Team B's delivery manager directly about what has been observed and what is actually valued; make problem-surfacing visibly safe and rewarded across the service; and follow up with Team B's members to hear the unfiltered picture.
- D) Raise Team B's delivery manager's reporting behaviour as a performance concern through line management.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C starts with examining whether the assessor's own reactions have rewarded polish over candour, then addresses the individual with a direct conversation and follow-up. Option A buys evidence already largely available at the cost of months; B punishes candour and rewards polish; D prosecutes a probable system problem as an individual case at the first move. This tests owning the incentive system a leader has created before addressing an individual's adaptive response to it.

**Question 9 (hard) — The assessment a team wants to game**

A beta assessment is in three weeks; a delivery manager proposes scheduling the demo around strongest journeys, avoiding mention of a real manual workaround used in 15% of cases, and saying monitoring is "in development" if asked. The workaround has a credible removal plan. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the approach — assessments are adversarial by nature, and every team presents its best face.
- B) Overrule it and instruct the team to present everything, including a full account of every known weakness.
- C) Reframe the preparation: showing the strongest journeys is legitimate; concealment is not — so present the workaround proactively with its usage data and removal plan, answer metric questions with what actually exists, and use the remaining three weeks to rehearse honest, evidence-backed answers rather than avoidances.
- D) Postpone the assessment until the workaround is removed next quarter, avoiding the issue entirely.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C draws the line between legitimate curation (choosing strong journeys) and concealment (briefing avoidance, misdescribing absent monitoring), and converts a known weakness into evidence of self-aware competence. Option B overcorrects into unstructured confession; D delays value and teaches the team gaps are scheduling problems. This tests distinguishing legitimate presentation from concealment, and using disclosed weaknesses as evidence of a well-governed service.

**Question 10 (hard) — Stop, pivot or persevere**

A personally sponsored workstream (18 months, £700,000) shows plateaued accuracy, no credible supplier fix, and two comparable services abandoning similar approaches; the team believes six more months and £200,000 "might crack it," while the manual process it was meant to replace deteriorates, and a cheaper, better-evidenced alternative (£150,000 workflow redesign) exists. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Fund the six-month extension — the £700,000 investment is only recovered if the automation eventually works.
- B) Stop the automation workstream on the evidence, fund the workflow redesign, and handle the ending deliberately: harvest what was learned and built, communicate the decision as evidence-led to the team and stakeholders, and protect the team members' standing so that working on a stopped initiative carries no stigma.
- C) Fund both the extension and the redesign in parallel, hedging the decision.
- D) Ask the team for one more assessment report in three months before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three converging evidence lines (plateau, no supplier fix, two comparators abandoning) point to stop, and the £700,000 is sunk, irrelevant to the marginal £200,000 decision. Option B also harvests residual value and protects the team's standing so future teams still bring forward evidence that their own workstream is failing. Options A and C fund the weaker option in some form; D purchases delay to learn what is already decisive. This tests using evidence to stop a personally sponsored initiative, and managing the ending so honesty about failure remains safe.

**Question 11 (hard) — The funding squeeze and the invisible investment**

Facing a 15% mid-year budget cut, the choice is pausing either (a) a visible front-end improvement roadmap, or (b) a resilience programme addressing rising legacy component failure rates that caused two outages last year. A senior stakeholder urges protecting the visible roadmap: "Nobody thanks you for outages that don't happen." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Protect the visible roadmap — stakeholder confidence funds the service, and resilience can resume next year.
- B) Protect the resilience programme, and treat the stakeholder's warning as the communications requirement it really is: present the decision with the failure-rate trend and the cost and user harm of last year's outages, publish leading indicators so the invisible work has visible progress, and give stakeholders reduced but honest front-end commitments.
- C) Split the cut evenly between both programmes to avoid choosing.
- D) Escalate the choice to the director, since stakeholder relations are at stake either way.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rising failure-rate trend makes pausing resilience a choice to schedule a future incident; option B fixes the visibility problem the stakeholder correctly identifies, rather than the priority, using leading indicators and honest reduced front-end commitments. Option A trades a known rising risk for applause; C halves both programmes' effectiveness; D escalates a decision squarely within the role's accountability without a recommendation. This tests making invisible preventive value visible rather than letting visibility alone set priority.

**Question 12 (hard) — The confidence a junior colleague risked**

A junior analyst privately reports that monthly performance figures sent to the board have been "smoothed" for at least three months by a well-liked team leader, understating downtime, and asks not to be identified. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Thank them, then wait and watch next month's figures closely to catch the smoothing directly before acting.
- B) Confront the team leader immediately, without naming sources, and demand the practice stop and the figures be restated.
- C) Act on the candidate's own authority over the data rather than on the tip: initiate a routine-framed reconciliation of reported figures against raw monitoring data (a check the candidate's accountability justifies at any time), which will surface the discrepancy independently; then address what it finds — corrected reporting, restatement to the board, and a considered conversation with the team leader about what "removing noise" has meant — while checking back with the analyst and watching for any sign of reprisal.
- D) Refer the matter to internal audit as a data-integrity concern and let the formal process run.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C uses a routine reconciliation — justified by the role's own accountability, needing no source — to surface the discrepancy independently, protecting the analyst structurally rather than by promise alone, then corrects the record and has an evidence-led conversation with the team leader. Option A leaves known-wrong reports standing; B acts on a single unverified account in a small team where the source can be inferred; D is premature and heavy before independent evidence exists. This tests protecting a source structurally while acting decisively on a known misstatement in the record.

### Administration tips

- Score for the recurring fingerprint: acting through evidence and systems rather than unilateral command; correcting records at the point of knowledge, not discovery.
- Watch for whether a candidate's response protects a disclosure or truth-telling channel, rather than kindly-motivated concealment.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes the fixed part of a request (a policy intent, a ministerial priority) from the negotiable implementation.
- Score for whether the candidate treats sunk cost as irrelevant to a marginal stop/continue decision.
- Note whether the candidate makes invisible value (resilience, prevention) visible through evidence, rather than letting only visible work win priority.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting decisive-looking unilateral action (commandeering an incident, confronting on an unverified tip) that skips evidence-gathering or collaboration.
- Missing kindly-motivated concealment — curated dashboards, held-back declines, smoothed figures — as the heavily penalised pattern it should be.
- Rewarding a sunk-cost argument for continuing a failing initiative.
- Missing when a candidate either escalates a decision squarely within their own accountability, or exercises it silently without briefing seniors.
- Accepting an even split or hedge as a legitimate way to avoid a prioritisation decision the evidence actually resolves.

## Conclusion

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, particularly where substance and sequence (the right decision, delivered in the right order) both matter. **Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them.
