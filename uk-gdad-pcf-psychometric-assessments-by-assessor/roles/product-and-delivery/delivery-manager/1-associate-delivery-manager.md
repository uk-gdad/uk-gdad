# Associate Delivery Manager - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for associate delivery manager, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At this level a candidate works on a small or mature Agile team, learns on the job, and potentially works alongside a delivery manager on a larger team under guidance and mentorship. The material is job-specific: it uses the artefacts an associate delivery manager genuinely works with — sprint boards, burndown charts, stand-up updates, dependency logs, retrospective notes, simple team budgets, and messages between technical and non-technical colleagues — rather than abstract puzzles.

This document covers four types of assessment, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — pattern spotting, logical thinking, error checking, and prioritisation using delivery artefacts.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — percentages, averages, velocity, capacity, and simple budgets, all with step-by-step arithmetic.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — reading and understanding the kinds of passages met at work, such as Service Manual extracts, emails, and reports.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — realistic dilemmas from the life of an associate delivery manager.

Each section explains what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, maps its dimensions to the named skills in the role, gives a substantial set of practice questions from easy to harder with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, then gives administration tips and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Offer any unscored practice questions the platform provides. This is an entry-level role where the candidate is expected to be learning on the job under mentorship, so expect and allow for a range of prior exposure to structured assessment — judge reasoning and care over polish. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures how well a candidate thinks with the actual materials of the job, rather than with abstract puzzles. Instead of shapes and number sequences, a candidate sees sprint boards, dependency logs, team calendars, and simple delivery rules, and is asked to reason about them quickly and accurately.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is typically norm-referenced and often adaptive. Most platforms offer unscored practice questions before the timer starts; encourage the candidate to take them.

This kind of assessment matters for an associate delivery manager because so much of delivery management is careful noticing. Spotting that two cards on the board describe the same task, or that a dependency has quietly slipped past its date, saves a team days of confusion. The scenarios in this section are deliberately drawn from the day-to-day life of a small Agile team — stand-ups, boards, simple plans, and supervised work — matching the world of this role level.

### How this assessment maps to the role

Each dimension of the cognitive assessment lines up with a named skill in the role:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and the duty to visualise and make visible the work of the team. Spotting that cards on a board follow (or break) a convention, or that the same blocker keeps returning sprint after sprint, is pattern recognition applied to delivery.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum**. Tracking, managing, escalating and communicating dependencies means constantly reasoning in if-then steps.
- **Error checking** maps to **Making a process work** and **Agile and Lean practices**. Helping a team establish a process only works if the process is followed consistently, which means noticing when a card skips a column or a definition of done is missed.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum** and **Planning**. Actively addressing risks and issues, and knowing when to escalate them, depends on judging what matters most right now.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Planning** and **Team dynamics and collaboration**. When the sprint plan meets reality — someone is ill, a dependency slips, a meeting clashes — a workable answer is needed.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition on a sprint board**

A team's board has five columns, and every card must move through them in order: Backlog, Ready, In progress, Review, Done. Yesterday's card history: (1) Card A moved from Ready to In progress; (2) Card B moved from In progress to Review; (3) Card C moved from Ready to Review; (4) Card D moved from Review to Done. Which card broke the board's rule?

- A) Card A
- B) Card B
- C) Card C
- D) Card D

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Cards A, B, and D each moved exactly one column to the right; Card C jumped from Ready straight to Review, skipping In progress. This tests spotting a broken convention on a board — a card that skips a column often means work was done without being visible, which undermines the honesty that makes stand-ups and planning trustworthy.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction about a dependency**

A team's plan includes this rule: "The form cannot go to user testing until the content designer has approved the question wording." At stand-up, someone says: "The form is going to user testing on Thursday." Which one of the following must be true if the plan's rule is being followed?

- A) The content designer approved the wording before Thursday's testing.
- B) The content designer will approve the wording on Friday.
- C) A mistake has been made.
- D) The form does not need content approval.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** If the rule is being followed and testing happens Thursday, approval must have happened first. Option B puts approval after testing, breaking the rule; C and D invent facts the question does not give. This tests reasoning strictly from what a plan and the facts actually say, not from assumption — the discipline behind tracking and communicating dependencies accurately.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a stand-up summary**

A stand-up summary reads: "1) Priya finished the payment page and it is now in Review. 2) Sam is blocked waiting for test data from the Data team. 3) Jo will demo the prototype at Thursday's show and tell. 4) Sam finished the payment page and moved it to Done." Which two points cannot both be true as written?

- A) Points 1 and 2
- B) Points 1 and 4
- C) Points 2 and 3
- D) Points 3 and 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Point 1 says Priya finished the payment page and it is in Review; point 4 says Sam finished the same page and it is in Done — the same work cannot be finished by two people and sit in two columns at once. This tests catching a contradiction in a summary before it is sent, since inconsistent updates undermine external trust in a team's information.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation of morning tasks**

At 9:00, four things are waiting: (1) stand-up starts at 9:30 and the board has not been updated with yesterday's changes; (2) a supplier has emailed asking for confirmation of a meeting time next week; (3) a delivery manager has asked for the risk log to be tidied by end of week; (4) a developer has messaged: "The test environment is down — the whole team is blocked." What should be done first?

- A) Update the board so stand-up runs smoothly.
- B) Reply to the supplier to be polite and prompt.
- C) Start tidying the risk log while the morning is quiet.
- D) Find out more about the test environment problem and start getting help.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Item 4 is both urgent and high impact — the whole team is blocked, so every hour costs progress — and the role names actively removing or minimising risks, issues or dependencies, and knowing when to escalate. Item 1 can be covered in two minutes at stand-up; items 2 and 3 have looser deadlines. This tests weighing urgency and impact together rather than defaulting to routine tasks.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across retrospectives**

The top "what slowed us down" item from the last four retros: Sprint 1, "Waited three days for security review"; Sprint 2, "Story unclear — had to rework after demo"; Sprint 3, "Waited four days for security review"; Sprint 4, "Waited two days for security review." What is the most useful observation to bring to the retrospective?

- A) The team complains a lot and should focus on positives.
- B) Waiting for security review is a repeating pattern across three of four sprints and is worth investigating as a team.
- C) Sprint 2 proves the real problem is unclear stories.
- D) The waits are getting shorter, so the problem is solving itself.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three of four sprints name the same slowdown — a recurring pattern retrospectives exist to surface. Option D is tempting but the numbers do not steadily fall (they rose between sprints 1 and 3), and two days is still a real cost. This tests encouraging reflective practice with evidence, bringing a pattern rather than a complaint or a single anecdote to the team.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction about sprint scope**

A working agreement has three rules: (i) a story can only enter the sprint if it has been estimated; (ii) any story larger than 8 points must be split before it is estimated; (iii) the product owner must agree before a new story is added mid-sprint. Mid-sprint, a stakeholder asks for a new 13-point story to be added immediately. What is the minimum that must happen before this story can enter the sprint?

- A) The product owner agrees, and the story is added as it is.
- B) The story is split, the resulting stories are estimated, and the product owner agrees.
- C) The story is estimated at 13 points and added with the product owner's agreement.
- D) Nothing can be done; stories can never be added mid-sprint.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The 13-point story exceeds 8, so rule (ii) requires splitting before estimation; rule (i) then requires estimation before entry; rule (iii) requires product owner agreement since it is mid-sprint. This tests applying every given rule in sequence rather than stopping at the first one remembered — knowing a team's working agreements well enough to apply them calmly under pressure from a stakeholder.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a dependency log**

A dependency log: D1, API test credentials, needed 12 May, received 10 May. D2, Welsh translation, needed 20 May, in progress. D3, accessibility audit slot, needed 18 May, booked for 25 May. D4, sign-off on privacy notice, needed 30 May, not yet requested. Today is 14 May. Which dependency most needs attention today?

- A) D1
- B) D2
- C) D3
- D) D4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** D1 is complete; D2 is on track; D4 has time but should be requested soon; D3 is already guaranteed to be a week late unless something changes — the earlier this is acted on, the more options remain. This tests comparing promised dates against needed-by dates, the heart of tracking, managing, escalating and communicating dependencies.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritisation with a hidden dependency**

With a delivery manager away, four items are on the list: (1) prepare slides for tomorrow's show and tell (about an hour); (2) chase the Platform team about test credentials developers need tomorrow morning; (3) book a room for next month's planning day; (4) write up Monday's retrospective actions before people forget the context. Which order is most defensible?

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4 — the show and tell is the most visible event
- B) 2, 1, 4, 3 — unblock tomorrow's work first, then prepare tomorrow's event, then capture fading context, then the distant booking
- C) 3, 4, 1, 2 — quick tasks first to clear the list
- D) 4, 2, 1, 3 — retro actions are about team improvement, which always comes first

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 2 has a hard dependency deadline and a short action that may need the rest of the day to land elsewhere, so it goes first; item 1 follows since it is due tomorrow; item 4 loses value as memory fades; item 3 is weeks away. This tests sending anything that starts a clock on someone else's desk as early as possible — the essence of maintaining delivery momentum.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with team capacity**

A team of four developers planned 40 points for a two-week sprint, assuming full availability. On day two, one developer is called away for the rest of the sprint, and a bank holiday costs everyone one further day. Roughly what share of the planned capacity remains, and what is the most sensible immediate step?

- A) About 90% remains; no change needed.
- B) About 70% remains; talk to the product owner about which stories matter most.
- C) About 50% remains; cancel the sprint.
- D) Exactly 40 points remain; capacity does not depend on people.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four developers over ten days is 40 developer-days; losing one developer for eight remaining days plus a bank holiday across the team removes roughly 11–12 developer-days, leaving about 70%. This tests quick capacity arithmetic followed by an honest priority conversation with the product owner rather than quiet overcommitment or overreaction.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-rule deduction about a release**

A release checklist: (i) releases only happen Tuesday or Wednesday; (ii) no release on the last two working days before a public holiday; (iii) every release needs a completed accessibility check, taking two full working days, finishing before release day; (iv) the accessibility checker is only available Monday to Wednesday. A public holiday falls Friday of week 1; the check has not started as of Monday morning of week 1. What is the earliest release day?

- A) Tuesday of week 1
- B) Wednesday of week 1
- C) Tuesday of week 2
- D) Wednesday of week 2

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The check can finish by end of Tuesday of week 1 at the earliest, making Wednesday of week 1 the earliest possible release day under rule (iii) — but rule (ii) bans the last two working days before the Friday holiday (Wednesday and Thursday), ruling that out. The next permitted day under rule (i) is Tuesday of week 2. This tests checking every rule against a candidate answer, not stopping once two of four rules have been satisfied.

**Question 11 (hard) — Spotting the real blocker**

Over the last three sprints, stories move quickly to Review, then sit there four to six days before moving to Done. Developers say they are working hard; the product owner is frustrated that "nothing is finishing." Review currently holds seven cards; In progress holds two. What is the most accurate diagnosis, and the most constructive first step?

- A) The developers are too slow; ask them to work faster.
- B) Work is piling up at the Review step; bring this pattern to the team and ask what would help reviews happen sooner.
- C) The product owner is too impatient; ask them to wait.
- D) The board has too many columns; remove the Review column so cards reach Done faster.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The queue sits in Review, not development — seven cards waiting against two in progress — so blaming developer speed misdiagnoses the bottleneck and would push even more cards into an already backed-up queue. This tests diagnosing from where work actually accumulates and bringing the observable pattern to the team without blame, rather than deleting the signal (removing the column) or dismissing the concern.

**Question 12 (hard) — Prioritising an escalation decision**

Supporting a delivery manager on leave until Monday (today is Thursday), three situations arise: (1) a supplier says their deliverable will now arrive two weeks late, delaying the team's beta assessment; (2) two developers had a tense disagreement in stand-up about a technical approach, and have agreed to discuss it tomorrow with the tech lead; (3) tomorrow's show and tell has a room double-booking. For which is it most important to escalate beyond the team today?

- A) Situation 1, because a supplier delay affecting a beta assessment has consequences beyond the team and needs senior awareness now.
- B) Situation 2, because conflict is always an emergency.
- C) Situation 3, because tomorrow's event is the nearest deadline.
- D) None; everything should wait for the delivery manager's return on Monday.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Situation 1 involves a contracted supplier and threatens a beta assessment date that matters beyond the team, including governance and possibly commercial colleagues — delaying the news until Monday costs two working days of response time. Situation 2 already has a sensible next step in hand; situation 3 is within the candidate's own power to fix directly. This tests calibrating between handling something directly, letting the team handle it, and raising it promptly with people who need to know.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate distinguishes "must be true" from "could be true"** in deduction items.
- **Watch for whether a candidate checks every rule in a multi-rule question** rather than stopping after the first one or two.
- **Note whether a candidate diagnoses a bottleneck from where work queues up**, not from who appears busy.
- **Note whether a candidate weighs urgency and impact together** in prioritisation items rather than defaulting to visibility or routine.
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer built on outside experience** rather than the rules and facts given in the question.
- **Missing when a candidate stops checking after the first rule** in a multi-constraint item.
- **Crediting an answer that confuses busy with blocked** in a diagnosis question.
- **Missing when a candidate treats every disagreement or delay as equally urgent**, rather than calibrating.
- **Penalising a candidate for a defensible answer to a hard question** — this is an entry-level role, so look for improving reasoning across the section, not a flawless score.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately a candidate works with the numbers this role genuinely produces: sprint velocity, story points, team capacity in days, percentages of work completed, burndown charts, simple budgets, and supplier day rates. The arithmetic is percentages, ratios, averages, differences, and simple rates.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 30 minutes, with 15 to 25 questions, each presenting a table, a short chart description, or a few figures in a sentence, followed by a short calculation. A calculator is often permitted; offer rough paper.

This kind of assessment matters for an associate delivery manager because delivery is full of small, consequential sums — a capacity question, a burndown comparison, a supplier cost — and being able to do them quickly and calmly helps a team plan honestly, spot problems early, and understand the financial impact of the work, which the role explicitly names.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Agile and Lean practices** and visualising the work of the team: a completion figure is a percentage waiting to be calculated and communicated.
- **Averages and rates** map to **Planning**: understanding planning and forecasting starts with averaging past velocity and using it to sense-check the next sprint's plan.
- **Capacity arithmetic** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: setting a sustainable cadence means knowing how many working days a team really has after leave, bank holidays and ceremonies.
- **Budget and cost calculations** map to **Financial management** and **Commercial management**: understanding financial impact, and acting as a point of contact for contracted suppliers, both involve day rates and simple cost comparisons.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: translating a table of delivery data into a sentence a stakeholder can act on must be numerically right.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of sprint completed**

A team planned 40 story points and completed 30. What percentage of the planned points did the team complete?

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 30 ÷ 40 = 75%. This tests dividing completed by planned (not the reverse) — dividing the wrong way gives an answer over 100%, a useful self-check when work was left over.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average velocity**

A team's velocity over the last four sprints: 28, 32, 30, 34. What is the average velocity?

- A) 30 points
- B) 31 points
- C) 32 points
- D) 33 points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum = 124; ÷4 = 31. This tests a basic average, and a strong candidate's answer treats it as a starting point for conversation rather than a promise when comparing it to a planned figure.

**Question 3 (easy) — Team capacity in days**

A team of 5 has a 10-working-day sprint. One member is on leave for 3 days, and one bank holiday affects everyone. How many person-days of capacity does the team have?

- A) 40 person-days
- B) 42 person-days
- C) 45 person-days
- D) 47 person-days

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 50 (maximum) − 5 (bank holiday, all 5 people) − 3 (one person's leave) = 42. This tests subtracting losses from maximum capacity in the right order, and, for a strong candidate's answer, checking whether the leave overlaps the bank holiday rather than assuming.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reading a burndown**

A two-week sprint starts with 40 points, burning down evenly across 10 working days. At the end of day 5, how many points should ideally remain?

- A) 15 points
- B) 20 points
- C) 25 points
- D) 30 points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Daily ideal reduction = 4 points; after 5 days, 20 points remain. This tests reading an ideal burndown line, which lets a specific gap ("we are 10 points behind the ideal line") be described rather than a vague impression.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Supplier day-rate cost**

A supplier provides a specialist at £650 per day, booked 3 days per week for 4 weeks. What is the total cost?

- A) £6,500
- B) £7,150
- C) £7,800
- D) £8,450

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Days = 12; £650 × 12 = £7,800. This tests a simple rate calculation, useful when checking figures in emails and purchase orders as a point of contact for contracted suppliers.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change in velocity**

Velocity was 25 points in sprint 8 and 30 in sprint 9. What was the percentage increase?

- A) 5%
- B) 17%
- C) 20%
- D) 25%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Change = 5; 5 ÷ 25 (the original) = 20%. Option B divides by the new value instead. This tests always dividing by the starting value, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting that one good sprint is not yet a trend.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Story completion ratio**

A team completed 12 stories, carried over 4, and removed 2 by agreement with the product owner. Of the stories that stayed in the sprint, what fraction was completed?

- A) 2/3
- B) 3/4
- C) 4/5
- D) 12/18

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Stories that stayed = 12 + 4 = 16; 12 ÷ 16 = 3/4. Option D wrongly includes the removed stories. This tests reading precisely which population a question is about, since "% of what we committed to after the scope change" and "% of the original scope" are different, true statements.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Budget remaining**

A quarter's budget is £120,000; £45,000 spent on staff time, £18,000 on a supplier, £7,000 on software licences. What percentage of the budget remains?

- A) 41.7%
- B) 50%
- C) 58.3%
- D) 70%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total spend = £70,000; remaining = £50,000; 50,000 ÷ 120,000 ≈ 41.7%. Option C is the percentage spent, not remaining. This tests confirming which direction a question asks for ("remains" vs "spent") before answering — the two always sum to 100%, a useful check.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Cycle time average with an outlier**

Five recent stories took 2, 3, 3, 4, and 13 days from "In progress" to "Done". What are the mean and the most representative typical value to share with the team?

- A) Mean 5; the mean best represents a typical story.
- B) Mean 5; the median of 3 better represents a typical story because of the outlier.
- C) Mean 3; there is no outlier.
- D) Mean 13; the largest value dominates.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Mean = 25 ÷ 5 = 5, but four of five stories took 4 days or fewer; the median (3) better represents the typical story. This tests recognising when an outlier makes the mean unrepresentative, and choosing the figure that tells the truth to the team.

**Question 10 (hard) — Forecasting with average velocity**

There are 93 points of work left; the last three sprint velocities were 29, 31, and 30, and sprints are 2 weeks. Using average velocity, how many sprints remain, and roughly how many weeks?

- A) 2 sprints; about 4 weeks
- B) 3 sprints; about 6 weeks
- C) 4 sprints; about 8 weeks
- D) 5 sprints; about 10 weeks

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Average velocity = 30; 93 ÷ 30 = 3.1 sprints, rounded up to 4 (a fraction of a sprint still needs a whole sprint on the calendar); 4 × 2 = 8 weeks. This tests rounding up rather than down when forecasting whole sprints, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting the forecast assumes stable velocity and no new work added.

**Question 11 (hard) — Comparing two suppliers**

Two suppliers for an 8-week, 5-day-per-week tester engagement: Supplier X, £480/day; Supplier Y, £520/day but 10% off the total if the engagement is 6 weeks or longer. Which is cheaper, and by how much?

- A) X is cheaper by £400
- B) X is cheaper by £480
- C) Y is cheaper by £480
- D) Y is cheaper by £400

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Days = 40; X total = £19,200; Y before discount = £20,800, and the 8-week engagement qualifies for the discount, giving £18,720. Y is cheaper by £480. This tests checking whether a conditional discount's condition is actually met before applying (or omitting) it — a common structure in real supplier quotes.

**Question 12 (hard) — Reading a delivery data table**

Four teams' planned and completed points: Red 60/48, Blue 80/68, Green 50/45, Yellow 70/49. Which team had the lowest completion percentage, and what was it?

- A) Red, at 80%
- B) Blue, at 85%
- C) Yellow, at 70%
- D) Green, at 90%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Red 80%, Blue 85%, Green 90%, Yellow 70%. Every option states a true percentage; the test is answering the actual question asked (lowest). This tests distinguishing raw numbers from proportions when comparing teams of different sizes, and, for a strong candidate's answer, framing the finding constructively rather than as blame.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate checks the direction of a division** — completed over planned, change over original — before calculating.
- **Watch for whether a candidate writes each calculation step down** rather than attempting mental arithmetic on multi-step problems.
- **Note whether a candidate recognises when a mean is skewed** and a median would better represent typical performance.
- **Note whether a candidate rounds up rather than down** when forecasting whole sprints of remaining work.
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that reports the spent share when the remaining share was asked** (or the reverse).
- **Crediting an answer that divides by the new value** in a percentage-change calculation.
- **Missing when a candidate includes excluded items in a denominator.**
- **Crediting an answer that applies (or omits) a conditional discount without checking its condition.**
- **Missing when a candidate trusts a skewed mean** without considering a median.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads, understands, and draws conclusions from written material — the kinds of documents this role actually involves. For an associate delivery manager, that means extracts from the GOV.UK Service Manual, working agreements, stakeholder emails, meeting notes, supplier messages, and short reports.

The most common question style is True / False / Cannot say. Other styles include choosing the best summary of a passage, identifying what can properly be inferred, and judging which of several sentences communicates a point most clearly. These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions.

This kind of assessment matters for an associate delivery manager because so much of the job is reading and relaying — a policy note, a supplier email, Service Manual guidance — and getting the reading right, without adding assumptions or missing conditions, is a professional skill this assessment measures directly. The single most important rule: answer only from the passage. "Cannot say" is not a weak answer — it is correct whenever a passage does not settle the question, whatever general knowledge might suggest.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading comprehension** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: before translating between audiences, the exact meaning of what a developer, policy colleague, or supplier has written must be extracted — no more and no less.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: when communicating risks, issues and dependencies, the difference between "the supplier said the API will be late" and "the supplier might be late" is the difference between a fact and a guess.
- **Inference** maps to **Life cycle management**: Service Manual guidance often states principles rather than instructions, and explaining what each phase is for means drawing sound inferences about what the guidance implies.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to being an advocate for the team externally and supporting discussions within a multidisciplinary team: choosing the clearest sentence in a test mirrors choosing the clearest sentence in a stand-up summary or stakeholder update.
- **Careful reading of conditions** maps to **Commercial management** and **Making a process work**: contracts, processes, and working agreements are written in conditions, and applying them correctly starts with reading them correctly.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–4)*

"Government services move through phases. In the discovery phase, the team learns about users and their needs; no service is built during discovery. In the alpha phase, the team builds prototypes to test hypotheses with users. A service moves from alpha to beta only after passing a service assessment. In the public beta phase, the service is available to real users while the team continues to improve it. Teams should not treat beta as a finished state: a service remains in beta until it meets the Service Standard criteria for live."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "During discovery, the team builds an early version of the service."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True only for large services

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states directly that "no service is built during discovery." This tests catching a direct contradiction and not blurring adjacent phases — the passage does say prototypes are built, but that happens in alpha, not discovery. Option D invents a distinction the passage never makes.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "A service must pass a service assessment before moving from alpha to beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) The passage does not mention assessments

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage says the move happens "only after passing a service assessment", which the statement accurately rephrases. This tests checking whether a rephrasing preserves meaning — the same act of translation as turning a formal document into a plain-English team summary.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "Most services spend at least six months in public beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because beta is usually shorter

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says nothing about how long beta lasts or about "most services". This tests treating "cannot say" as a genuine, confident answer rather than reasoning from outside knowledge — the honest reply if a stakeholder asked the same question from this guidance alone.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Best summary**

Which option best summarises the passage?

- A) Services should leave beta as quickly as possible.
- B) Services progress through learning, prototyping, and improving phases, with defined conditions for moving between them.
- C) The discovery phase is the most important phase.
- D) Real users only encounter a service once it is live.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage describes a sequence of phases and conditions for moving between them, which B captures faithfully. Option A adds advice the passage never gives (it warns against rushing); D contradicts the passage, which says beta services are available to real users. This tests summarising without adding or distorting.

*Passage 2 (for Questions 5–7)*

"Hi — quick update on the search component work. Our developer completed the build on Friday and it has passed our internal tests. However, we have not yet run the accessibility checks, which are scheduled for Wednesday. We would therefore recommend not deploying to your beta environment before Thursday. Separately, please note that our invoice for May remains unpaid; our finance team will apply a late payment charge if payment is not received within 10 working days of this email. Could you confirm receipt and let us know your intended deployment date?"

**Question 5 (easy) — Fact finding**

According to the email, which of the following has already happened?

- A) The accessibility checks
- B) The deployment to the beta environment
- C) The build and the supplier's internal tests
- D) The late payment charge

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Only the build and internal tests are complete; accessibility checks are scheduled, deployment is recommended for later, and the late charge is conditional and future. This tests sorting a message's contents into done, planned, and conditional before relaying it — exactly how a supplier update should be summarised at stand-up.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The supplier will apply a late payment charge."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the invoice is unpaid

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The charge is conditional on payment not arriving within 10 working days, and the email gives no information about whether payment will be made in time. This tests distinguishing "there will be a charge" from "there will be a charge unless we pay within 10 working days" — very different messages to relay to finance colleagues.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inference and action**

Which of the following is the best-supported inference from the email?

- A) The supplier's work is behind schedule.
- B) Deploying to beta on Tuesday would go against the supplier's recommendation.
- C) The search component has failed accessibility checks.
- D) The invoice dispute means the component cannot be deployed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The supplier recommends "not deploying... before Thursday", so a Tuesday deployment would go against that recommendation directly. C contradicts the text (the checks have not run yet); A and D are unsupported. This tests taking the smallest step beyond the text that the text fully supports, since over-claiming ("they're behind schedule") from this email would spread an inaccuracy that could sour a commercial relationship.

*Passage 3 (for Questions 8–10)*

"Stand-up is at 9:30 each working day and lasts no more than 15 minutes. Team members who cannot attend should post a written update in the team channel before 9:30. Discussions that need more than a brief exchange must be taken offline: the people involved arrange a follow-up conversation after stand-up. The board must be updated before stand-up, not during it. Visitors are welcome to observe stand-up but should not contribute unless invited by the facilitator."

**Question 8 (easy)**

Statement: "A team member who cannot attend stand-up should post a written update before 9:30."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only with the facilitator's permission

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** This is stated almost word for word. Option D borrows the facilitator's permission requirement from a different rule (about visitors), a common blending of neighbouring rules. This tests keeping each rule attached to its own subject, which is central to accurately quoting a working agreement.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applying the rules**

During stand-up, two developers begin a detailed debate about database indexing. According to the working agreement, what should happen?

- A) The debate continues, because technical topics take priority.
- B) The facilitator invites a visitor to give an outside view.
- C) The people involved take the discussion offline and arrange a follow-up after stand-up.
- D) Stand-up is extended by 15 minutes to finish the debate.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A detailed debate is clearly "more than a brief exchange", triggering the offline rule directly. A invents a priority; B misapplies the visitor rule; D breaks the explicit time cap. This tests finding and applying the correct rule from several — the job asks for applying it kindly, but the assessment tests finding it.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

Stakeholders need to be told the team will not finish a promised feature this sprint. Which sentence communicates this most clearly and professionally?

- A) "Due to unforeseen circumstances impacting velocity, the deliverable timeline has experienced slippage."
- B) "We will not finish the payments feature this sprint. It will be completed next sprint, by 21 June. The delay is due to a dependency on test data that arrived late."
- C) "Unfortunately things have been crazy and the feature isn't done, sorry!!"
- D) "The feature is delayed. More information will follow at some point."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B is clear, specific (feature name, new date, cause), and helps the reader act. A hides the message in abstract jargon; C is honest but unprofessional and information-poor; D is vague about what comes next. This tests the three-part standard for clear delivery communication: clear message, specific facts, and a next step.

**Question 11 (hard) — True, false, or cannot say with a subtle condition**

Passage: "The department's spend approval process requires prior approval for any single purchase over £5,000. Purchases of £5,000 or less may be approved retrospectively by a budget holder. Software subscriptions are treated as a single purchase covering the full contract term." Statement: "A £450-per-month software subscription with a 12-month term may be approved retrospectively."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because £450 is under £5,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The subscription is treated as a single purchase for the full term: £450 × 12 = £5,400, which exceeds £5,000 and requires prior approval. Option D applies the threshold to the monthly figure instead of the contract value. This tests noticing a sentence that changes the unit of analysis, and reading procurement thresholds against the correct total.

**Question 12 (hard) — Identifying the author's purpose**

Passage, from a delivery report: "Sprint 14 completed 27 of 32 planned points. The two carried-over stories both depend on the identity platform team, whose release was postponed for the second consecutive sprint. We have raised this dependency at the programme board and requested a committed date. If no date is received by 12 July, we will re-plan the quarter's roadmap to sequence non-dependent work first." What is the primary purpose of this passage?

- A) To blame the identity platform team for missed work.
- B) To report progress, flag a recurring external dependency, and set out a clear conditional plan.
- C) To announce that the quarter's roadmap has been re-planned.
- D) To request more developers for the team.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage reports results, identifies a factual cause without blame language, describes escalation already taken, and sets a conditional next step with a trigger date. C confuses a conditional future action with a completed one. This tests reading what a text is doing, not just what it says — and this structure (numbers, cause, action taken, conditional plan with a trigger date) is a model worth copying in a candidate's own reports.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate treats "cannot say" as a genuine answer** rather than avoiding it.
- **Watch for whether a candidate notices small condition words** — "if", "unless", "only after", "within 10 working days".
- **Note whether a candidate keeps neighbouring rules attached to their own subjects** rather than blending them.
- **Note whether a candidate answers from the passage alone**, not from outside knowledge of how things usually work.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer drawn from outside knowledge** rather than the passage.
- **Missing when a candidate blends two neighbouring rules** meant for different subjects.
- **Crediting an answer that treats a conditional as a certainty.**
- **Missing when a candidate overlooks a sentence that changes the unit of analysis** (such as monthly figure versus contract total).
- **Crediting a summary that adds advice or judgement** the passage does not contain.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks which response is most or least effective. Unlike the other assessments, there is often no single provably correct answer; responses are scored against what experienced practitioners agree is more or less effective behaviour for the role. Common formats include choosing the most effective response, the least effective response, the best and worst from four options, or rating each response.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 20 to 30 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed because they measure judgement rather than speed. The scenarios are drawn from the real dilemmas of an associate delivery manager on a small Agile team, learning on the job and supporting a more experienced delivery manager.

This kind of assessment matters for an associate delivery manager because judgement is the heart of the job — choosing well when values pull in different directions: transparency versus diplomacy, team autonomy versus intervention, handling something directly versus escalating. At this level, the assessment also looks for a healthy relationship with guidance and mentorship: knowing what can be handled directly, what the team should own, and when to involve a delivery manager is a strength, never a weakness.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Team-focused scenarios** map to **Team dynamics and collaboration**: the test probes understanding of team dynamics, valuing feedback, and supporting an environment where a team can deliver, including when dynamics get difficult.
- **Blocker and escalation scenarios** map to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: actively addressing risks and issues and knowing when to escalate them is a judgement call the SJT simulates repeatedly.
- **Stakeholder scenarios** map to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: managing differing stakeholder perspectives, hosting discussions with difficult dynamics, and advocating for the team externally.
- **Supplier scenarios** map to **Commercial management**: acting as the point of contact for contracted suppliers means judging when an issue is routine and when it needs internal contacts or escalation.
- **Process scenarios** map to **Making a process work** and **Agile and Lean practices**: guiding teams to focus on outputs rather than process requires judgement about when to intervene and when to let the team decide.
- **Learning scenarios** map to the role level itself: learning on the job, potentially working alongside a delivery manager under guidance and mentorship, so the assessment rewards asking, admitting uncertainty, and using a mentor well.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — A mistake in a candidate's own work**

A weekly delivery update sent yesterday reported the wrong sprint completion figure: 90% instead of the true 75%. Nobody has commented. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing; the next update will quietly contain the right figure.
- B) Send a short correction to the same audience today, with the right figure, and tell the delivery manager what happened.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone notices, and correct it only if asked.
- D) Correct the figure only in the version stored on the team drive.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B corrects the record promptly with the same audience and keeps the delivery manager informed with accurate situational awareness, since they may have repeated the figure onward. A, C, and D all leave stakeholders holding a wrong number longer. This tests honesty, transparency, and ownership — a calm, fast correction builds credibility rather than damaging it.

**Question 2 (easy) — A quiet team member**

In the last three retrospectives, a recently joined developer has said almost nothing while two other members do most of the talking. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing; some people are just quiet, and participation is optional.
- B) In the next retro, put the new developer on the spot: "You've been quiet — what do you think?"
- C) Change the retro format to include silent writing time before discussion, and separately, privately check in with the new developer about how they are finding the team.
- D) Ask the two talkative members to stop talking so much, in front of the team.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C changes the structure so quieter voices have a route in, and pairs it with a private, low-pressure conversation. Option B forces public contribution, which usually makes quiet people quieter; D punishes engaged colleagues publicly. This tests inclusion combined with respect for individual difference — the strongest options change the system kindly and talk to individuals privately.

**Question 3 (easy) — Asked something not known**

A senior manager asks directly: "What is the team's plan if the identity platform dependency slips again?" The candidate does not know the answer. What is the most effective response?

- A) Improvise a plausible-sounding plan so the team looks prepared.
- B) Say: "I don't want to guess — I'll check with the team and get you an answer by tomorrow," and then do exactly that.
- C) Say the question would be better directed to someone senior, and move on.
- D) Stay silent and hope someone else answers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is honest about the limit of current knowledge and commits to a specific, followed-through action. Option A risks an improvised plan being repeated upwards and colliding with reality later; C deflects a question the candidate is well placed to carry back to the team. This tests honesty combined with reliable follow-through — being an advocate for the team externally includes taking questions back to them.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Pressure to inflate a report**

A programme manager asks for a team's progress to be described as "on track" in a weekly report, though a key dependency has slipped and the forecast shows the milestone landing two weeks late. "It'll probably sort itself out — no need to worry the director." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree; the programme manager is senior and owns the report.
- B) Refuse angrily and tell the team the programme manager tried to cook the report.
- C) Say discomfort at reporting "on track" against the current forecast, propose accurate wording with mitigation actions underway, and tell the delivery manager about the conversation.
- D) Report "on track" but keep a private note of disagreement.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C holds the line on accurate reporting without hostility, offers a constructive alternative, and informs the delivery manager — the right move when someone senior pressures inaccurate reporting. A makes the candidate complicit; D is quiet complicity that changes nothing. This tests honesty, transparency, and appropriate escalation when facing pressure to misreport.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Two seniors, two instructions**

A product owner asks for the sprint review to move to Thursday so a visiting policy team can attend; an hour later, the delivery manager messages to keep it on Friday "as usual — the team's rhythm matters." What is the most effective response?

- A) Book Thursday; the product owner asked first.
- B) Book Friday; the delivery manager is the line of guidance.
- C) Tell each of them what the other asked for, and get the two of them (with the candidate) to agree the date quickly, sharing what each option affects.
- D) Book both slots and decide on the day.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C surfaces the conflict to the people who can resolve it, cheaply and fast, rather than arbitrating it secretly. A and B each pick a side using an arbitrary rule, leaving one senior colleague surprised. This tests collaboration over silent obedience — managing differing stakeholder perspectives in its everyday, prompt, even-handed form.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The struggling supplier contact**

As day-to-day contact for a small supplier, their developer has missed two informal check-ins and the last update was vague; the formal deliverable is due in three weeks, and the delivery manager is busy with a service assessment this week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Wait until the deliverable date; judging progress before then is unfair.
- B) Email the supplier a firm warning that contract penalties will apply.
- C) Arrange a short call with the supplier this week to get a specific progress picture, ask what they need from your side, and give the delivery manager a brief heads-up note.
- D) Ask a developer to secretly start rebuilding the component in-house, just in case.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C investigates while three weeks still allow recovery, approaches the supplier as a partner (the blocker may be on this side), and respects the delivery manager's bandwidth with a heads-up rather than a dump. B jumps to contractual threats likely outside the candidate's authority. This tests early engagement and following the correct escalation ladder: engage directly, inform the delivery manager, involve commercial specialists if facts warrant.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Retro actions going stale**

The last two retrospectives produced action lists, and almost none of the actions have been done, though the retros themselves are pleasant. What is the most effective response?

- A) Stop holding retrospectives, since the actions are not happening anyway.
- B) At the next retro, present the undone actions without blame, and ask the team how they want to make actions stick — perhaps fewer actions with named owners and a check at stand-up.
- C) Personally do all the outstanding actions this week.
- D) Email the team a list of who has failed to do which action.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B makes follow-through visible and lets the team redesign its own mechanism, matching the role's duty to help a team find a process that works for them. C sets an unsustainable precedent; D is public blame likely to create defensiveness. This tests continuous improvement with team ownership rather than abandoning the practice, doing it all directly, or naming and shaming.

**Question 8 (moderate) — An excluded voice in a technical discussion**

In sprint planning, developers debate two technical approaches at speed with heavy jargon; the content designer, whose work the choice directly affects, has stopped following and looks disengaged. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it run; interrupting productive technical discussion damages morale.
- B) Pause the discussion briefly: "Before we go further — this affects content. Could someone recap the two options in plain terms so we can get the whole picture?"
- C) After the meeting, apologise privately to the content designer.
- D) Ask the developers to email their decision to the content designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B intervenes lightly, without blame, and improves the decision by bringing in information the developers need. C treats a live, fixable problem as an after-the-fact apology once the decision is already made; D makes the designer a recipient rather than a participant. This tests real-time inclusion that is also good decision-making — noticing who has fallen out of a conversation and gently reopening the door.

**Question 9 (hard) — Overheard concern about burnout**

A developer is overheard telling another they have worked the last three weekends to keep a story on track, exhausted but not wanting "to let the team down." Sprint commitments have been met and nobody else seems aware. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing; commitments are being met and the developer chose to work weekends.
- B) Announce at stand-up that nobody is allowed to work weekends.
- C) Check in privately with the developer with care and without revealing surveillance ("How are you finding the workload lately?"), and raise the sustainability pattern with the delivery manager; look at whether the team's commitments match its real capacity.
- D) Publicly praise the developer's weekend work at the sprint review to recognise their dedication.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C acts on both levels — a private, gentle check-in and a systemic look at whether commitments exceed capacity — since the green metrics are concealing their own cost. D would institutionalise overwork as what recognition looks like. This tests sustainable pace combined with care for individuals, often hidden behind the least dramatic option.

**Question 10 (hard) — A blocker with no owner**

A story is blocked because a firewall change needs security sign-off; the network team says security must sign off, security says the request form must come from network — each waiting for the other for four days. The delivery manager is on leave until next week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Wait for the delivery manager to return; cross-team blockers are above this level.
- B) Get the two teams into one short call (or shared thread), state the impact plainly, and ask them to agree who does what by when; if they cannot agree, escalate to the delivery manager's cover or the service owner with a factual summary.
- C) Tell the developers to make the firewall change without sign-off, since the process is clearly broken.
- D) Raise a formal complaint about both teams to their heads of profession.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is a circular dependency where both sides are behaving reasonably by their own rules; collapsing the loop by getting both parties into one conversation is the highest-leverage move, escalating only if it fails. C trades a process problem for a security breach, never the effective answer regardless of frustration. This tests active blocker removal through legitimate means, and that a delivery manager's absence does not pause a team's momentum.

**Question 11 (hard) — Credit taken for a candidate's work**

A colleague from another team presents a dependency-tracking spreadsheet designed by the candidate as "something we've put together", with no mention of the candidate or their team; several managers praise it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Interrupt the meeting to state that the spreadsheet is yours.
- B) Say nothing ever; credit does not matter.
- C) Afterwards, speak to the colleague privately and directly ("I was surprised the tracker was presented without mentioning where it came from — can we make sure joint work is credited accurately in future?"), and mention the situation to the delivery manager if it recurs.
- D) Email the meeting's attendees a correction with version history attached as proof.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C addresses the person who caused it, privately, promptly, and specifically, setting a boundary without a public conflict. A creates public confrontation over attribution; D turns a repairable slight into a distributed accusation. This tests direct communication and proportionality — one incident earns a conversation, a pattern earns an escalation.

**Question 12 (hard) — Choosing least effective: the slipping sprint goal**

Mid-sprint, it becomes clear the sprint goal will probably not be met because a story turned out much larger than estimated; the team is working well and morale is good. Which of the following is the **least effective** response?

- A) Facilitate a quick conversation with the team and product owner about options: split the story, reduce scope, or accept the miss and learn from the estimate.
- B) Update the relevant stakeholders early with the honest forecast and what the team is doing about it.
- C) Say nothing outside the team and quietly encourage the team to work extra hours so the goal is met and nobody needs to know.
- D) Make sure the estimation surprise is raised at the retrospective so the team learns from it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C combines concealment (stakeholders plan on what they know) with unsustainable pressure (extra hours to protect appearances, actively encouraged). A, B, and D form a healthy pattern: adapting the plan with the right people, communicating early and honestly, and learning at retro. This tests catching the harmful option among otherwise good practices — a sprint goal missed honestly, adjusted openly, and learned from is normal and healthy.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate's response acts sooner, involves the right people, and stays transparent** — the general "sooner, together, openly" pattern behind strong answers.
- **Watch for whether a candidate follows the escalation ladder appropriate to this level**: handle directly → help the team handle it → inform or involve the delivery manager → involve specialists or senior cover.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes a dramatic response from a calm, private, proportionate one.**
- **Note whether a candidate treats "passive" and "escalate everything" as equally poor responses** to a live signal.
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding a dramatic response** — public confrontation, formal complaint, unilateral ban — over a calm, private, proportionate one.
- **Rewarding a passive response** ("wait and see", "it will sort itself out") when the scenario contains a live signal.
- **Crediting an answer that protects appearances over accuracy** in a report, board, or stakeholder update.
- **Missing when a candidate imposes a fix on the team** rather than making a problem visible and facilitating the team's own fix.
- **Missing the "least effective" flip** — in a least-effective item, three plausible-looking options may be good practice and the fourth the trap.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to an associate delivery manager: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested the noticing skills this role runs on: spotting broken patterns on boards, deducing what rules really require, checking artefacts for contradictions, and prioritising by impact and urgency rather than by noise. The numeric section tested the friendly arithmetic of delivery — completion percentages, average velocity, capacity after leave and holidays, supplier costs, and honest forecasts, with every step written down. The verbal section tested reading exactly what a passage says, holding the line at "cannot say", and choosing the clearest way to deliver news. The situational judgement section tested the dilemmas of real delivery life: correcting a candidate's own mistakes, including quiet voices, resisting pressure to inflate reports, unblocking circular dependencies, and telling the difference between drama, passivity, and genuinely effective action.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on a different answer, particularly on the harder items, is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score, and particularly valuable at this level, where the goal is as much development as selection.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can think carefully with delivery artefacts, work accurately with the numbers of a small team, read precisely, and exercise sound judgement while learning under supervision — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
