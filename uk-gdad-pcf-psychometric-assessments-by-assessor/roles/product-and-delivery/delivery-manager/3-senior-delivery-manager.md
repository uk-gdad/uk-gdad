# Senior Delivery Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for senior delivery manager, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At this level a candidate is accountable for the effective delivery of complex, high-risk products and services, with greater responsibility and accountability as the main point of escalation, engaging senior stakeholders and coaching delivery managers. The material is job-specific: it uses the artefacts a senior delivery manager genuinely handles — multi-team dependency maps, business cases, contract schedules, benefits profiles, escalation papers, assessment evidence, budget envelopes negotiated across programmes — rather than abstract puzzles.

This document covers four types of assessment, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — pattern recognition across portfolios, multi-constraint deduction, error checking of governance artefacts, prioritisation under escalation pressure, and problem solving where ownership is unclear.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — budgets, business-value propositions, supplier negotiation arithmetic, cross-team forecasting, and trend analysis.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — precise comprehension of contract clauses, assessment reports, policy extracts, and board papers.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — dilemmas of escalation, mediation, coaching delivery managers, supplier relationships, and organisational influence.

Each section explains what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, maps its dimensions to the named skills in the role, gives a substantial set of practice questions from easy to challenging with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, then gives administration tips and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Offer any unscored practice questions the platform provides. At this level what reaches a candidate is what process could not resolve elsewhere, so several situational judgement items deliberately present more than one defensible option and reward second-order thinking about what a response teaches — score for that quality of reasoning, not only a single "correct" letter. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role actually demands, using realistic delivery material rather than abstract items. At senior level, the material scales up: instead of one team's board, a candidate reasons over portfolios of teams, chains of cross-service dependencies, governance artefacts, and evidence bases for continue/pivot/stop decisions.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is typically norm-referenced and often adaptive, with senior variants using denser stimuli — multi-column tables, layered rules, longer scenario stems — engineered so that surface-plausible answers fail under complete checking.

This kind of assessment matters for a senior delivery manager because the role concentrates exactly this demand: as the main point of escalation, what reaches this level is the residue that simpler reasoning failed to resolve. Optimising delivery flow across several teams simultaneously requires seeing systemic patterns single-team eyes miss, and deciding on evidence whether a team should continue, change direction or stop requires separating signal from noise at a standard that survives challenge from a director or an assessment panel.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Maintaining delivery momentum**: optimising flow across teams means detecting portfolio-level signatures.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Planning** and **Life cycle management**: leading a continual planning process, and using evidence to decide whether a team should continue, change direction or stop, are sustained exercises in valid inference under many constraints.
- **Error checking** maps to **Financial management** and **Commercial management**: business cases, benefits profiles, and contract schedules fail through internal inconsistency far more often than through arithmetic.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the accountability as the main point of escalation: ranking complicated risks, issues and dependencies by impact-weighted urgency, including where ownership exists outside every team or nowhere.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum** ("identify innovative ways to unblock issues") and **Team dynamics and collaboration**: decomposing an organisationally tangled situation into a tractable intervention that strengthens rather than substitutes for the teams involved.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a portfolio**

Sprint completion percentages across four teams over four sprints: Alpha 92, 90, 91, 93. Bravo 88, 84, 79, 73. Charlie 85, 94, 78, 90. Delta 90, 89, 88, 90. Which team most warrants attention, and why?

- A) Charlie — its results vary the most from sprint to sprint.
- B) Bravo — it shows a consistent, compounding downward trend.
- C) Alpha — its consistency suggests padded commitments.
- D) Delta — its stability suggests stagnation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Charlie oscillates around a healthy mean with no direction; Bravo declines monotonically, roughly five points per sprint, four sprints running — a system signalling that something has changed and is still changing. Options C and D manufacture pathology out of health. This tests distinguishing variance from trend across a portfolio — a slope visible across sprints should trigger inquiry before the team itself raises a hand.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a stop/continue rule**

A governance rule: "A private beta may continue for a further quarter only if (a) its benefits profile has been re-validated in the last six months, and (b) either its user satisfaction is at or above 75%, or a remediation plan approved by the service owner is in place." Service X's benefits profile was re-validated four months ago; user satisfaction is 68%; a remediation plan exists but the service owner has not yet approved it. What follows?

- A) X may continue; the remediation plan exists.
- B) X may not continue as things stand; condition (b) is unmet until the plan is approved.
- C) X may not continue under any circumstances this quarter.
- D) X may continue if satisfaction improves to 70%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Condition (a) is satisfied; condition (b) is a disjunction, and both limbs currently fail — satisfaction is below 75% and the plan, while existing, is not yet approved. Option C overshoots since the situation is curable by approval; option A ignores the approval requirement. This tests reading a compound condition with the exactness its drafters intended, and identifying precisely which lever cures the failure.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a benefits profile**

A business case claims: "Automating the renewals process will release 12 FTE of caseworker effort. Total annual benefit: £780,000, based on a fully loaded cost of £65,000 per FTE. Of the released effort, 8 FTE will be redeployed to the complex-casework backlog and 6 FTE will be taken as headcount reduction." What is wrong?

- A) The £780,000 figure is miscalculated.
- B) The redeployment plan allocates 14 FTE of a 12 FTE benefit.
- C) £65,000 is an implausible fully loaded cost.
- D) Nothing is wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 12 × £65,000 = £780,000 checks out, and £65,000 is unremarkable, but 8 + 6 = 14 FTE are dispensed from a 12 FTE pool — two FTE spent twice, the signature error of benefits profiles assembled for different audiences. This tests summing the dispositions of a claimed benefit and comparing them against the pool — benefits double-counting is a common finding when programmes are audited, and it silently overstates an investment case.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation as the point of escalation**

At 08:45 four escalations arrive: (1) a production release failed overnight, degrading a service, with the team mid-incident and operations engaged; (2) a supplier's commercial director threatens to pause work Friday over an invoice dispute finance believes is the supplier's error; (3) the programme director wants a recommendation by Wednesday on whether a discovery should proceed to alpha; (4) two delivery managers separately raised the same complaint about a shared platform team's unresponsiveness, the third such complaint this month. Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1, 2, 4, 3 — confirm the incident is properly led and supported (minutes, not hours), then defuse the Friday supplier cliff-edge, then act on the pattern, then the Wednesday recommendation.
- B) 2, 1, 3, 4 — commercial threats outrank operational noise.
- C) 3, 4, 1, 2 — strategic decisions first, and incidents belong to teams.
- D) 1, 3, 2, 4 — deal with the incident fully and personally before anything else.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 needs verification and air cover, not the candidate taking over the incident (option D duplicates the incident lead and stalls everything else); item 2 has a hard Friday deadline addressable with a Monday phone call; item 4 is a systemic pattern measured in days; item 3 has a deliberate deadline needing reflective quality. This tests allocating attention by asking what each item needs and by when, rather than ranking by category (B, C) or absorbing an operational task personally (D).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in a cross-team dependency map**

A dependency register over a quarter: Team Alpha waited on the Identity platform 3 times (11 days); Team Bravo waited on Identity twice (9 days) and on Team Charlie once (2 days); Team Delta waited on Identity twice (8 days); Team Charlie waited on Data Engineering once (6 days). Next quarter all four teams plan more Identity integration. What is the most important conclusion?

- A) Team Charlie is the programme's main bottleneck.
- B) The Identity platform is a shared systemic constraint (7 waits, 28 days this quarter) and demand on it is about to rise — it needs a structural response now, not per-team chasing.
- C) The teams should each escalate their own Identity waits harder.
- D) Dependencies are normal in programmes; no action is needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Identity accounts for 7 of 9 waits and 28 of 36 lost days across three consumer teams, and demand on it is about to rise further. Option A misreads the table (Charlie mostly waits on others); option C is the anti-pattern the question exposes — four teams independently escalating against one constrained platform raises temperature, not throughput. This tests reading dependency data by the column that receives the arrows, not the rows that send them, and drawing a structural rather than individual conclusion.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction under layered planning constraints**

Sequencing three go-lives — Payments (P), Accounts (A), Notifications (N) — across four monthly windows (March–June). Constraints: (i) P requires fraud-screening capability, completing end of April; (ii) A must go live at least one month before N; (iii) a change freeze occurs in June; (iv) only one go-live per window. Which schedule is forced?

- A) A in March, P in April, N in May
- B) A in March, N in April, P in May
- C) P in March, A in April, N in May
- D) A in March, P in May, N in April

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** P cannot go live before May, and June is frozen, so P is fixed at May; A must precede N by a month, and with only March and April remaining, A takes March and N takes April. This tests deriving a schedule from constraints and then verifying each constraint against the result — the "natural order" (A) survives every constraint except a date calculation, and D violates the A-before-N ordering.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking an escalation paper**

A delivery manager's escalation paper: "Risk: the supplier's data-migration tooling is 4 weeks late, threatening our October go-live. Impact: go-live slips to November, delaying £150,000/month of benefits. Mitigation underway: we have re-sequenced testing to absorb 2 weeks. Ask: the board is asked to note the risk." What is the paper's most significant weakness?

- A) The benefits figure should be annualised.
- B) The ask is misaligned with the content: a live issue with a quantified residual impact needs a decision or support request, not a "note."
- C) The mitigation should not be disclosed, as it weakens the negotiating position with the supplier.
- D) Risks should never name suppliers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A two-week residual slip carries roughly £75,000 of delayed benefit on the paper's own figures, and content of that shape demands a genuine ask — a decision, support, or contingent authority — not a bare "note." This tests checking whether an escalation's ask matches its impact, since boards given nothing to decide will minute it and, when the impact lands, note only that they were "asked to note."

**Question 8 (moderate) — Problem solving where ownership is absent**

Three teams consume a legacy reference-data system owned by a directorate outside the programme; it fails monthly, costing each team one to two days per failure. The owning directorate acknowledges the fragility but has no funded remediation. Escalations from individual teams over six months have returned sympathy and no change. What is the strongest next move?

- A) Instruct the three teams to build independent workarounds immediately.
- B) Keep escalating monthly through the same route; persistence eventually wins.
- C) Aggregate the quantified cross-programme impact (roughly 3–6 team-days per month, converted to cost and delivery risk), take it jointly with the other affected seniors to the governance tier that spans both organisations, and present costed options: funded remediation, a supported workaround, or an accepted risk with explicit ownership.
- D) Accept it as legacy reality and pad all plans by two days per month.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Six months of fragmented, low-tier escalation achieved nothing because each team's loss reads as absorbable noise to a directorate with its own priorities and a tier that cannot fund or compel. This tests repairing both defects — aggregation converts noise into a quantified recurring cost, and routing to the governance tier spanning both organisations reaches the only place able to allocate money or accept risk across the boundary, forcing a visible decision rather than a default.

**Question 9 (hard) — The confounded improvement claim**

A delivery manager reports: "Since we moved to trunk-based development in April, our change failure rate has fallen from 18% to 9%. The practice works — the other three teams should adopt it now." The same team also gained a senior site-reliability engineer in April and reduced its release frequency from daily to twice-weekly. What is the soundest assessment?

- A) The halved failure rate vindicates the practice; mandate adoption across all teams.
- B) Three changes coincided (practice, staffing, release cadence), so attribution is currently impossible; moreover, the lower release frequency mechanically flatters the rate's interpretation. Treat trunk-based development as promising, examine which factor carries the effect, and let other teams trial it deliberately rather than by mandate.
- C) The improvement is probably the SRE alone; trunk-based development is irrelevant.
- D) Change failure rate is a flawed metric; ignore the claim.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With practice, personnel, and cadence all changing in the same window, and failure rate's denominator (number of changes) itself altered, the halving supports no single attribution. This tests holding a full position — credit the improvement as real, refuse premature attribution, propose how to learn more, and reject a mandate — since an expert endorsement on confounded data spends organisational trust on a guess.

**Question 10 (hard) — Deduction across a contract and a plan**

A supplier contract: (i) acceptance of each milestone triggers payment; (ii) if acceptance is not granted or refused within 15 working days of delivery, the milestone is deemed accepted; (iii) milestone 4 is a precondition for milestone 5 commencing. Milestone 4 was delivered 1 July; today is 21 July (day 14, no holidays), and the test team, short-staffed, has not examined it; the evidence pack is rumoured to be thin. What is the most important immediate implication?

- A) Nothing is urgent; acceptance is a formality.
- B) Tomorrow, milestone 4 will be deemed accepted — triggering payment and unlocking milestone 5 — unless acceptance is granted or refused today or tomorrow; an immediate review-or-refuse decision is required now.
- C) Milestone 5 cannot start for another 15 days regardless.
- D) The rumour should be investigated over the coming weeks.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Deemed acceptance triggers tomorrow (day 15), automatically triggering payment and unlocking milestone 5 for potentially inadequate evidence. This tests chaining contract clauses against a calendar and recognising a deemed-acceptance clause is not a paperwork technicality — knowing which clocks are running is central to commercial management, and "coming weeks" (D) misses that the clock expires in one day.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reading a cumulative flow signature across teams**

Two teams use the same design authority, with a stated 10-day turnaround. Team Alpha's "awaiting approval" band widens steadily over eight weeks; Team Bravo's is stable. Alpha submits designs averaging 40 pages; Bravo submits 6-page decision-focused summaries. Alpha's delivery manager wants the authority's "failure to meet turnaround" escalated. What does the evidence best support?

- A) Escalate the design authority's under-resourcing; turnaround commitments are being missed.
- B) The differential pattern (one consumer served within standard, one not; submissions differing sevenfold in size) suggests Alpha's submission format is a major driver; coach Alpha to trial Bravo-style decision-focused submissions before escalating, while checking the authority's view of the workload.
- C) Alpha's designs are more complex, so nothing can be compared.
- D) Merge both teams' submissions to average the load.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A shared service meeting its standard for one consumer and missing it for another, where the consumers differ visibly in submission size, points the first inquiry at the differing input. This tests coaching a controllable factor first, verifying with the shared service, and holding escalation in reserve — escalating on unaudited evidence (A) risks an embarrassing reply that undermines a senior escalation's credibility.

**Question 12 (hard) — Continue, pivot, or stop**

Eight months into private beta: completion rate for the core journey 84% (target 80%); cost per transaction £7.20 against a target of £3.50, flat for four months; user satisfaction 82%; projected volumes revised down 40% following a legislative change; the team proposes 6 more months of beta to "optimise costs." What is the soundest recommendation?

- A) Continue as proposed; the service works and users like it.
- B) Stop immediately; the cost target is missed.
- C) The decision hinges on the revised economics, not the service quality: with volumes down 40% and unit cost flat at more than double target, the original value proposition needs re-validation before more delivery spend — recommend a short, time-boxed re-examination of the business case, with continue/pivot/stop decided on that analysis rather than defaulting to either extension or cancellation.
- D) Continue, but relabel the cost target as aspirational.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Service quality (completion, satisfaction) and viability (unit cost, volumes) are independent findings; the cost trend is flat rather than improving, and the demand shift is structural. This tests routing the decision through a time-boxed analysis that keeps continue, pivot, and stop all genuinely live, rather than funding six more months on affection (A), destroying option value prematurely (B), or the actively corrosive move of relabelling a failed target (D), which changes the instrument rather than the reality.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate distinguishes variance from trend** across a portfolio of teams.
- **Watch for whether a candidate derives a schedule from constraints and then audits it**, rather than selecting a plausible-looking pattern.
- **Note whether a candidate aggregates dependency data by the receiving column** before diagnosing a bottleneck as belonging to one team.
- **Note whether a candidate names confounding factors before crediting a cause**, especially in claims that will be used to justify organisation-wide change.
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that reads oscillation as pathology or a monotonic slope as noise.**
- **Missing when a candidate accepts a "plausible" schedule that violates a calculated constraint.**
- **Crediting an answer that diagnoses a shared constraint as one team's individual failing.**
- **Missing when a candidate escalates on evidence that has not been pre-audited** for an obvious counter-reply.
- **Crediting an answer that lets service quality settle a viability question**, or that changes a measure rather than the underlying reality.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures fluency with the quantitative material this role genuinely handles — at senior level, business-case arithmetic, budget negotiation, supplier cost structures, cross-team capacity and forecasting, benefits profiles, and trend interpretation robust enough to survive board scrutiny. The operations remain percentages, ratios, rates, averages, and weighted comparisons; the stimuli are denser and the consequences of modelled errors larger.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 30 minutes, with 15 to 25 questions, data presented in tables, chart descriptions, or prose. A calculator is usually available.

This kind of assessment matters for a senior delivery manager because the numbers move real money and real decisions: negotiating or setting budgets in complex environments, writing or inputting into business cases, negotiating with suppliers and getting good value from contracts. The person who can rebuild a supplier's quote or catch a business case double-count in the meeting, rather than after signature, is worth a great deal.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Budget and variance arithmetic** map to **Financial management**: negotiating, influencing or setting budgets requires run rates, committed-versus-actual positions, and variance projections computed quickly and defended clearly.
- **Business-case and benefits calculations** map to **Financial management**: costs, benefits, break-even, and sensitivity to assumptions.
- **Supplier cost modelling** maps to **Commercial management**: decomposing quotes into rates, volumes, and conditions, and comparing structures rather than headline prices.
- **Cross-team forecasting and capacity arithmetic** map to **Planning** and **Maintaining delivery momentum**: throughput-based forecasts, net rates, and honest treatment of uncertainty and outliers.
- **Trend and distribution reading** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Life cycle management**: distinguishing trends from noise, means from medians, and rate changes from denominator changes.
- **Presenting figures** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: the final step of every calculation is the sentence a director can act on, accurate and honest about its assumptions.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio budget share**

Four teams' annual budgets: Alpha £1.2m, Bravo £0.9m, Charlie £1.5m, Delta £0.4m. What percentage of the total portfolio is Charlie's?

- A) 30%
- B) 33%
- C) 37.5%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total £4.0m; 1.5 ÷ 4.0 = 37.5%. This tests a basic share calculation — in portfolio conversations, shares matter because absolute budgets mislead across teams of different sizes, and the follow-up question ("is Charlie delivering three-eighths of the value?") only becomes askable once the share is right.

**Question 2 (easy) — Day-rate blend for a negotiation**

A supplier proposes 2 architects at £950/day and 4 engineers at £700/day. What is the blended day rate per person?

- A) £783
- B) £800
- C) £825
- D) £850

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total daily cost £4,700 ÷ 6 people ≈ £783. Option C is the simple, unweighted average of the two rates, ignoring the 2:4 mix. This tests weighting by headcount, and, for a strong candidate's answer, decomposing a blended rate to identify which roles drive it in a negotiation.

**Question 3 (easy) — Benefits break-even**

An automation costs £360,000 to build and £5,000/month to run, saving £45,000/month. Ignoring discounting, after how many months does it break even?

- A) 8 months
- B) 9 months
- C) 10 months
- D) 12 months

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Net monthly benefit £40,000; £360,000 ÷ £40,000 = 9 months. Option A divides by the gross saving, forgetting running costs offset the benefit every month. This tests netting a benefit against its own running cost, and, for a strong candidate's answer, stating break-even with its assumptions attached.

**Question 4 (easy) — Budget variance percentage**

A £6.4m delivery budget has a mid-year forecast outturn of £6.88m. What is the projected overspend as a percentage of budget?

- A) 6.5%
- B) 7%
- C) 7.5%
- D) 8%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Overspend £0.48m ÷ £6.4m (the budget, not the forecast) = 7.5%. This tests using the correct denominator for a variance, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting that identifying a gap at mid-year leaves months to close it, unlike the same finding surfacing in month eleven.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Comparing supplier proposals with different structures**

Supplier P: fixed £840,000 over 12 months, equal instalments. Supplier Q: £60,000/month for 6 months, then £75,000/month for 6 months, plus a £30,000 transition fee in month 1. Which is cheaper over the full term, and by how much?

- A) P, by £30,000
- B) Q, by £30,000
- C) P, by £5,000
- D) They cost the same

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Both total £840,000. This tests recognising when identical totals shift the comparison to structure — cash-flow phasing, exit points, and extension rates — rather than stopping at a headline price, since Q's back-loaded profile costs less in the first half but more if the engagement extends at the final rate.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Cost of delay across a portfolio**

Three initiatives compete for one team slot next quarter, each taking the full quarter: X worth £120,000/month once live, Y worth £80,000/month, Z worth £50,000/month. Choosing X first delays Y and Z by three months each. What is the total cost of delay imposed on the unchosen initiatives?

- A) £240,000
- B) £360,000
- C) £390,000
- D) £510,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Y delayed 3 × £80,000 = £240,000; Z delayed 3 × £50,000 = £150,000; total £390,000. This tests answering precisely what is asked — the cost imposed on the unchosen items, not X's own gain or the net position — converting "my initiative is important" into a comparable, de-personalised number.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Weighted average with a data correction**

A quarterly report shows weighted average cycle time across three teams: Alpha 24 items at 5 days, Bravo 36 items at 8 days, Charlie 20 items at 4 days. Bravo's item count is corrected to 30. What is the corrected weighted average (to one decimal place)?

- A) 5.7 days
- B) 6.1 days
- C) 6.0 days
- D) 5.9 days

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** (24×5 + 30×8 + 20×4) ÷ 74 = 440 ÷ 74 ≈ 5.9. Option B is the stale, uncorrected figure; option A is the unweighted average of the three day figures. This tests weighting by volume and recomputing an aggregate whenever underlying data is corrected — a report carrying a stale aggregate quietly spends the credibility of every future figure it holds.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Capacity for a coaching commitment**

Coaching five delivery managers fortnightly (1 hour each), a monthly community session (2 hours prep, 1 hour delivery), and two governance boards monthly (1.5 hours plus 1 hour prep each), over a 4-week month (20 working days at 7.2 hours). What percentage of working time do these commitments consume?

- A) 9%
- B) 12%
- C) 15%
- D) 18%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Coaching 10 hours + community 3 hours + governance 5 hours = 18 hours; ÷144 available hours ≈ 12.5%. This tests summing individually small commitments to reveal the true load — a diary audit worth running quarterly on real commitments, since only the summed percentage reveals over-commitment.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Interpreting a benefits realisation table**

Planned versus actual monthly benefit (£000) over 6 months: Planned 40, 40, 60, 60, 80, 80. Actual 20, 28, 40, 52, 68, 82. Which statement best characterises the position at month 6?

- A) The programme has failed: cumulative actual (£290k) is well short of cumulative planned (£360k).
- B) Benefits are ramping slower than planned but converging: the monthly gap has narrowed from £20k to −£2k, with actual exceeding plan in month 6; the cumulative shortfall of £70k is real but the run rate has caught up.
- C) Benefits have exceeded plan, so realisation is complete.
- D) The numbers are too mixed to interpret.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cumulative shortfall £70k, but monthly gaps narrow steadily to negative (actual above plan) by month 6. This tests holding both the cumulative position and the current trajectory at once — a board needs both, and the honest sentence names the shortfall and the recovering rate together, since one good month does not repay five light ones and a converged run rate is the strongest predictor of the next quarter.

**Question 10 (hard) — Rebuilding a supplier's "savings" claim**

A supplier proposes replacing 3 contractors (£680/day, 220 days/year each) with a managed service at £400,000/year, claiming savings "over £50,000 a year." Analysis shows the service covers only 2.5 contractor-equivalents, requiring 0.5 FTE of a civil servant (£70,000/year fully loaded) to absorb the remainder. What is the true annual position?

- A) The claim is right: about £49,000 of savings — close enough to "over £50,000."
- B) Comparing like for like, the managed service plus absorption costs £435,000 against £448,800 for the contractors: a true saving of about £13,800, roughly a quarter of the claim.
- C) The managed service costs £35,000 more per year than the contractors.
- D) The proposal saves £84,000 a year.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Current cost £448,800; supplier's implicit comparison inflates the claim already; adding £35,000 for the missing 0.5 FTE gives a true arrangement cost of £435,000, a genuine saving of £13,800. This tests rebuilding a supplier claim on a like-for-like basis — supplier claims are usually not false but un-adjusted, and identifying the missing coverage and absorption cost either extracts a scope commitment or re-prices the deal.

**Question 11 (hard) — Forecast with scope growth and an uncertainty range**

A 480-point backlog grows 5% (24 points) per quarter, flat. Three teams contribute 120 points/quarter combined, but one team (40 points/quarter) is diverted elsewhere for the first quarter. In which quarter does the backlog complete, and what should accompany the point estimate?

- A) Quarter 4; no caveats needed with arithmetic this clear.
- B) Quarter 6, accompanied by the assumptions (flat scope growth, stable throughput after Q1) and the sensitivity — for example, scope growth at 10% instead of 5% pushes completion further out again.
- C) Quarter 6, because forecasts should always add a safety quarter to whatever the arithmetic says.
- D) Quarter 5, presented as a firm commitment to the board.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Modelling quarter by quarter with net reduction (throughput minus scope growth) leaves 40 points after Q5, so completion falls early in Q6. Option D rounds the boundary wrongly and presents a model as a commitment. This tests netting scope growth against throughput, walking the final period's remainder explicitly, and — the distinction between B and C, which reach the same quarter — reaching it through genuine modelling with stated assumptions rather than an arbitrary safety margin bolted onto an unexamined figure.

**Question 12 (hard) — Choosing the honest headline**

Monthly transactions post-launch: 10,000, 11,000, 12,100, 13,310, 14,641 (months 1–5). A colleague drafts: "Growth accelerating: monthly increases rose from 1,000 to 1,331 transactions." Which assessment is most accurate?

- A) The headline is correct: increases are growing every month.
- B) The absolute increases grow, but the growth rate is constant at 10% per month — the pattern is steady compounding, not acceleration; the honest headline is "sustained 10% monthly growth," and projecting it forward requires knowing when saturation effects will bend the curve.
- C) The growth is linear at roughly 1,150 per month on average.
- D) The data shows growth slowing in percentage terms.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Every month grows exactly 10% — constant-rate compounding, not acceleration (which would require a rising rate). This tests computing period-on-period ratios rather than trusting rising absolute increments, since "accelerating growth" invites a board to fund capacity for an ever-steepening curve where "steady 10% compounding" invites the right questions about saturation.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate weights an average by volume** rather than averaging averages.
- **Watch for whether a candidate rebuilds a supplier or business-case claim from its components** before accepting a headline figure.
- **Note whether a candidate uses the correct denominator for a variance or share calculation.**
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes compounding from acceleration** when reading growth data.
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that averages averages** rather than weighting by volume.
- **Crediting an answer that accepts an un-adjusted comparison** — a missing coverage gap, absorption cost, or conditional discount.
- **Missing when a candidate uses the wrong denominator for a variance calculation.**
- **Crediting an answer that confuses compounding with acceleration.**
- **Missing when a candidate presents a point estimate as a firm commitment** rather than a model with stated assumptions.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures the precision with which a candidate extracts, tests, and acts on written meaning — using the document classes a senior delivery manager actually confronts: contract schedules and variation clauses, assessment reports, board minutes, policy and spend-control extracts, benefits narratives, and escalation correspondence. A passage is presented; questions probe what it states, implies, permits, requires, and leaves unsettled.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions. Senior variants use denser text with layered conditions, embedded exceptions, and rhetorical framing engineered to reward the reader who separates what a document says from what its author wants the reader to take away.

This kind of assessment matters for a senior delivery manager because reading has consequences and relaying has reach at this level: mediating between people and representing positions at all levels, moderating difficult discussions about high-risk topics within constrained timescales, speaking to large audiences. A misread contract clause becomes a commercial exposure; a misrelayed board condition becomes a programme working to the wrong gate.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise comprehension of conditions** maps to **Commercial management**: identifying appropriate contractual frameworks and getting good value from contracts rest on exact reading of obligations, triggers, exceptions, and time limits.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: mediating and managing expectations at all levels requires relaying exactly what was written or decided, with every qualifier intact.
- **Inference calibration** maps to **Life cycle management** and evidence-based continue/pivot/stop judgement: deciding what an assessment finding supports without overclaiming.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to **Financial management**: business cases and escalation papers are arguments to strengthen when sound and catch when overreaching, before they reach boards.
- **Purpose and framing detection** maps to **Team dynamics and collaboration** and mediation duties: reading what is asserted versus what is framed is the precondition of fair mediation.
- **Summary fidelity** maps to speaking on behalf of, and representing, the community to large audiences: positions must survive compression intact.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–4)*

"Either party may propose a variation to the Services by written notice. No variation shall have effect unless agreed in writing by both parties' authorised representatives. Where a variation increases the Charges by more than 10% of the annual contract value, the Customer's approval must additionally be confirmed at its commercial board. The Supplier shall not unreasonably refuse a variation proposed by the Customer that reduces the scope of the Services, provided that the Customer remains liable for costs already reasonably incurred in respect of the descoped Services. For the avoidance of doubt, oral agreements, custom and practice, and conduct of the parties shall not constitute agreement to any variation."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "A variation agreed verbally by both parties' authorised representatives has contractual effect."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, if later documented

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two clauses close this decisively: no variation without written agreement, and oral agreement expressly excluded from constituting agreement. Option D rescues by importing later documentation, but the statement as put describes a verbal agreement, judged as put. This tests reading a no-oral-variation clause accurately, protecting both parties from drift.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: "A variation increasing the Charges by 8% of the annual contract value requires approval at the Customer's commercial board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because all variations need board approval

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The commercial-board requirement attaches only above 10%; 8% does not cross the threshold, so only ordinary written agreement is required by this clause. This tests distinguishing which side of a stated threshold a case falls on, and, for a strong candidate's answer, distinguishing contractual requirements from an organisation's own stricter internal policy.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "If the Customer proposes a descoping variation, the Supplier must accept it."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, provided incurred costs are paid

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Shall not unreasonably refuse" is not an obligation to accept; a reasonable refusal remains permitted, and the cost proviso conditions the Customer's entitlement, not the Supplier's obligation. This tests distinguishing absolute obligations from qualified ones ("reasonable endeavours", "shall not unreasonably...") — a distinction with real commercial consequences.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strongest inference**

Which of the following is best supported by the clause as drafted?

- A) The drafters anticipated disputes about informal variation and deliberately closed that route.
- B) The Supplier drafted the clause to disadvantage the Customer.
- C) Variations below 10% do not require written agreement.
- D) The Customer can descope without any cost consequence.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** An "avoidance of doubt" sentence expressly excluding oral agreement, custom, and conduct exists because informal-variation disputes are common — drafting evidence of an anticipated failure mode. C and D are directly contradicted; B asserts unstated motive. This tests inferring drafting purpose from textual features without inventing facts.

*Passage 2 (for Questions 5–8)*

"The panel was impressed by the team's user research practice and its clear articulation of user needs. However, the service did not demonstrate that it can meet demand at scale: performance testing was conducted against 40% of projected peak volume, and the team could not evidence a tested failover arrangement. The panel also noted that two of the six team members responsible for operating the live service are contractors whose engagements end within three months, with no documented knowledge-transfer plan. The panel's decision is not met. The service may not progress to public beta until a reassessment demonstrates: (1) performance at projected peak volume; (2) a tested failover arrangement; (3) a credible operational staffing and knowledge-transfer plan. The panel encourages the team to seek reassessment promptly and commends the strength of its user-centred practice."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "The service failed the assessment primarily because of weaknesses in user research."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, since research is always decisive

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report praises user research twice; the not-met decision rests on scale, failover, and staffing grounds. This tests keeping a failure's actual grounds attached to an outcome as it travels through relays — a team whose genuine strength is blamed for a failure it did not cause loses morale and the incentive praise was meant to create.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "If the team demonstrates performance at projected peak volume at reassessment, the service may progress to public beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because performance was the main concern

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three conditions are set conjunctively; meeting one does not satisfy a conjunction. This tests the single most common misreading of governance conditions — necessary-versus-sufficient confusion — with a real workplace cost: a team that sprints on one condition alone fails on the others and burns an assessment slot.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Strongest inference**

Which is best supported by the report?

- A) The contractors are underperforming and should be replaced.
- B) The panel regards operational continuity — not just current capability — as within the assessment's scope.
- C) Performance testing at 40% of peak is contrary to government policy in all cases.
- D) The team will pass reassessment within three months.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Making staffing tenure and knowledge transfer a formal condition is direct evidence the panel's scope includes continuity of operation, not just present state. This tests scope inference — what a report conditions on reveals what the assessing regime considers itself entitled to examine — over unsupported performance judgements (A), overgeneralised policy claims (C), or unwarranted forecasts (D).

**Question 8 (moderate) — Summary fidelity under compression**

Which summary of the outcome to a programme director is most faithful and useful?

- A) "Assessment failed. Major concerns across the board. Team needs support."
- B) "Not met, on three specific grounds: performance untested at peak (only 40%), no tested failover, and an operational staffing/knowledge-transfer gap as two contractor engagements end within three months. User-centred practice was explicitly commended. Reassessment requires all three fixed; team is planning a prompt return. Support that would help: [X]."
- C) "Mostly positive — user research was praised. A few technical items to tidy before beta."
- D) "The panel penalised us for using contractors, which is unfair and worth challenging."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B carries the decision, all three grounds, the commendation, the forward plan, and an ask. A deflates and misallocates worry; C inflates and hides a gating decision; D converts a condition into a misread grievance. This tests fidelity in both directions when compressing a document for a senior audience — both bad news and good news must survive compression intact.

*Passage 3 (for Questions 9–10)*

"The Board considered the paper on the consolidation of the two case-management platforms. The Director of Operations expressed strong support. The Finance Director noted that the stated savings of £1.4m over three years depended on decommissioning timelines that had slipped twice before, and asked that the savings profile be re-baselined. The Board endorsed the direction of travel, requested a re-baselined savings profile and an independent technical assurance review before the business case is submitted for approval, and agreed that no procurement activity should commence in the interim. Action: Programme SRO to return to the January Board."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: "The Board approved the consolidation business case."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True in principle

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The Board "endorsed the direction of travel" and expressly noted the business case has not yet been submitted for approval, blocking procurement in the interim. This tests distinguishing graduated governance verbs — noted, endorsed, agreed, approved — since relaying "approved" after this meeting could start procurement the Board explicitly forbade.

**Question 10 (hard) — Reading advocacy inside the record**

Comparing the minute's treatment of the two directors, which reading is best supported?

- A) The Finance Director opposes consolidation.
- B) The record shows support qualified by a specific, evidenced concern: the FD challenged the savings profile's credibility (citing two prior slips) without opposing the direction — and the Board's conditions adopted the FD's concern almost exactly.
- C) The Director of Operations' support was decisive in the Board's endorsement.
- D) The Board considered the savings figure of £1.4m to be overstated.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The FD "noted" a dependency and "asked" for re-baselining — challenge to the evidence base, not opposition — and the Board's own conditions map onto that concern. This tests carrying a party's position at its actual strength, neither inflating a challenge into opposition (A) nor claiming causal weight the record cannot support (C, D) — the mediation-grade reading needed to represent positions to people not in the room.

**Question 11 (hard) — Argument evaluation in an escalation paper**

An escalation paper: "The shared platform team's API delays have now impacted three of our four service teams, costing an estimated 22 team-days this quarter. The platform team's own roadmap shows their capacity fully committed for two quarters. Unless the programme intervenes, these delays will continue and worsen as integration demand grows. We therefore request that the programme board direct the platform team to prioritise our four integration requests above its other commitments." Which is the most accurate critical assessment?

- A) The argument is sound and the request should be granted as asked.
- B) The evidence establishes a real, quantified, likely-worsening problem, but the requested remedy does not follow from it: the paper never examines what the platform team's other commitments are, so "prioritise us above everything" may simply relocate the harm to other consumers — the evidence supports a programme-level prioritisation decision across all demands, not a directed answer to it.
- C) The argument fails because 22 team-days is a trivial cost.
- D) The argument fails because platform teams cannot be directed by programme boards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The problem statement is well-evidenced, but the remedy assumes the requester's four requests outrank everything else on the platform's roadmap without examining what that "everything else" is. This tests separating an argument's descriptive strength from its prescriptive overreach — the evidence supports asking the decision forum to prioritise across all demands, not pre-empting that decision in the requester's own favour.

**Question 12 (hard) — True, false, or cannot say across two texts**

Text 1 (policy): "Departments must obtain Cabinet Office approval for any digital or technology spend over £1m, unless the spend falls within a pre-agreed exemption category." Text 2 (email): "Good news — the £1.3m platform renewal falls within our pre-agreed hosting exemption, so no Cabinet Office approval is needed. I've confirmed the exemption category covers hosting renewals up to £1.5m." Statement: "The £1.3m platform renewal requires Cabinet Office approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because the colleague has confirmed the exemption applies

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Text 2 is a colleague's testimony about the exemption, not the exemption instrument itself; whether the renewal is genuinely covered is outside what the texts establish. This tests distinguishing an in-passage assertion from independent verification — option D treats a claim within the evidence as settling the evidence, exactly the error a senior reader is paid to catch before adopting its risk in a submission.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads governance verbs at their exact grade** — noted, endorsed, agreed, approved.
- **Watch for whether a candidate maps a condition list as conjunctive or disjunctive** before answering.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes a claim quoted inside a passage from independent verification of that claim.**
- **Note whether a candidate separates an argument's evidence from its conclusion's overreach.**
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that converts a qualified obligation into an absolute one** — "shall not unreasonably refuse" read as "must accept".
- **Missing when a candidate treats one satisfied condition as meeting a conjunctive gate.**
- **Crediting an answer that accepts an in-passage assertion as an established fact.**
- **Missing when a candidate reads scrutiny of evidence as opposition to a proposal.**
- **Crediting an answer that lets a well-evidenced problem carry an unexamined remedy.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic role dilemmas and asks a candidate to identify the most effective response, the least effective, best-and-worst pairs, or to rate each option's effectiveness. Scoring reflects the consensus of experienced practitioners on effective professional behaviour — user focus, honest information flow, proportionate escalation, empowerment over substitution, inclusion, and sustainable delivery — calibrated to the seniority of the role.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 20 to 35 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, generously timed. At senior level, scenarios involve senior stakeholders, contracted suppliers, cross-organisational ambiguity, and second-order consequences — because at this level every response is also a precedent, watched by the delivery managers being coached.

This kind of assessment matters for a senior delivery manager because the role is defined by its judgement calls: as the main point of escalation, what reaches this level is what process could not resolve. There is rarely a clean option; the assessment measures whether a candidate consistently finds the response that is honest, proportionate, early, and capability-building.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Escalation and unblocking scenarios** map to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: addressing the most complicated risks, issues and dependencies, including where ownership sits outside every team or nowhere, and finding innovative ways to unblock.
- **Coaching scenarios** map to the accountability to coach and mentor delivery managers: whether a candidate builds their capability or absorbs their problems.
- **Supplier scenarios** map to **Commercial management**: taking responsibility for complex supplier relationships and knowing when commercial machinery should engage.
- **Stakeholder and mediation scenarios** map to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: mediating between people, managing expectations, moderating high-risk discussions within constrained timescales.
- **Life cycle and evidence scenarios** map to **Life cycle management**: holding the evidence line on continue/pivot/stop decisions under pressure from sunk cost, enthusiasm, and dates.
- **Team-system scenarios** map to **Team dynamics and collaboration**: reading team health signals, rectifying dynamics, choosing the right feedback mode.
- **Financial scenarios** map to **Financial management**: budget pressure, business-case integrity, and the escalation judgement when money and honesty intersect.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — The delivery manager who brings everything to the candidate**

A capable delivery manager, six months in post, brings every non-trivial decision to the candidate — escalations, stakeholder messages, process choices — each individually reasonable, the pattern not. What is the most effective response?

- A) Keep deciding for them; it is faster and the decisions are sound.
- B) Tell them to stop bringing things to the candidate.
- C) Name the pattern kindly and explicitly, reset the contract — "bring me your proposed decision and reasoning, not the decision itself; I'll add value where I disagree" — and hold the line by asking "what would you do?" whenever they revert, while making it safe to get some calls wrong.
- D) Escalate the dependency problem to the candidate's own manager.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option A trains the dependency deeper; B removes support without building capability. C restructures the interaction, relocating the decision back where it belongs, converting each regression into practice, and addressing the fear that usually drives the pattern with explicit safety. This tests coaching capability rather than either rescuing or withdrawing, since retaining decisions someone else could make is flow the candidate has stolen from themselves.

**Question 2 (easy) — The director's corridor commitment**

A director enthusiastically announces having "agreed with the minister's office" that a flagship service will launch three months earlier than the evidence-based plan supports, with nothing yet in writing. What is the most effective response?

- A) Congratulate them and re-plan to the earlier date; the commitment is made.
- B) Promptly and privately give the director the evidence picture: what the earlier date would require, what the current plan supports, the specific risks of the gap, and options (a descoped early launch, a phased launch, the original date with confidence) — so they can manage the expectation while it is still soft.
- C) Ignore it until it arrives through formal channels.
- D) Tell teams to start working weekends now to make the date feasible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Nothing is in writing, meaning the director still has room to shape what the commitment turns into — but only if given the evidence before it hardens. This tests moving at the speed the situation rewards, privately and with options, rather than converting an unexamined remark into a plan (A), gambling that a political commitment will die quietly (C), or spending team health before testing whether the date is negotiable (D).

**Question 3 (easy) — The health check the candidate administered**

Quarterly Agile health checks show a sharp drop in "confidence in leadership" for one team, with free-text comments pointing at their delivery manager — someone the candidate coaches and rates highly, and who is visibly shaken. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reassure the delivery manager that surveys exaggerate and the team will come round.
- B) Share the scores with the team and ask them to be more constructive.
- C) Support the delivery manager through it without dismissing the data: help them read the signal seriously but proportionately, plan how they will respond to the team (acknowledgement, listening session, visible changes), and increase coaching support through the response — treating the episode as recoverable and developmental.
- D) Begin quietly planning the delivery manager's move to another team.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C holds care for the person and respect for the team's signal simultaneously — the data is treated as real, the response is theirs to lead, and coaching support intensifies where it matters most. A would deepen exactly the defensiveness that caused the problem; B weaponises the survey against its authors; D converts one bad quarter into a verdict. This tests treating identified issues through health checks as an instrument that worked, with the response as the skill being tested.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The supplier negotiation with an information gift**

A supplier's delivery lead, in an excellent working relationship, privately lets slip that their firm priced an extension assuming no realistic alternative exists, and that their account director "expects you to push back to about 8%, so there's room." What is the most effective use of this information?

- A) Use it fully and immediately: open by demanding far more than 8%, citing what was said.
- B) Use the substance without burning the source: sharpen the negotiating position with independent evidence (benchmarks, alternative options, scope levers) that justifies the stronger push on its own merits, and never reveal or rely on the slip — while noting privately that the relationship's informality now needs more careful boundaries on this side too.
- C) Report the delivery lead to their account director for unprofessional disclosure.
- D) Ignore the information entirely; using it at all would be improper.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The information was volunteered, not stolen, and cannot be un-known, but citing it (A) identifies its source in one conversation and destroys the relationship and every future channel of candour. Option B captures the value legitimately, building an independently defensible position. This tests full use of a negotiating position without spending a person who trusted the relationship.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Mediating the product-architecture standoff**

A lead product manager and lead architect are deadlocked over a politically visible feature versus a platform migration deadline, each escalating and asking for the other to be overruled; their relationship has deteriorated into copied-in emails and meeting avoidance. What is the most effective response?

- A) Rule for the architect; technical foundations outlast political moments.
- B) Rule for the product manager; user-facing value outranks internal plumbing.
- C) Refuse the overrule framing: get them in a room, establish the shared facts (what does the deadline actually require? what does the migration actually block, and when?), surface options neither has explored under adversarial framing (partial migration, feature subset on new infrastructure, a thin release), facilitate toward a joint recommendation — and separately name the email-and-avoidance pattern to both as unacceptable for leaders, whatever the outcome.
- D) Send the decision to the programme board with both positions attached.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Whichever side is crowned (A, B), both leaders learn that deadlock summons a verdict, guaranteeing every future dispute reaches the same desk. Re-establishing shared facts often reveals both positions are softer than advertised, opening options invisible under the adversarial frame; the behavioural pattern needs addressing separately and symmetrically. This tests mediating a technical disagreement while treating a degraded working relationship as its own issue, escalating only a genuine strategic fork the mediation itself surfaces.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The evidence against the flagship**

A publicly announced, ministerially sponsored flagship programme shows accumulating trouble — invalidated benefits assumptions, unit costs double the case, two assessments passed only narrowly. A programme director asks to "keep the noise down while we get through the spending review." What is the most effective response?

- A) Comply; the spending review matters more than internal reporting hygiene.
- B) Refuse loudly and copy the SRO.
- C) Tell the programme director that evidence-based reporting cannot be suppressed, but offer what they legitimately need: a rigorous, fair account — problems, their causes, the recovery options, and what the programme has demonstrably achieved — so the spending review case rests on defensible ground; and if pressure to suppress continues, escalate the concern through the management line or the SRO with the evidence, not the grievance.
- D) Keep reporting accurately but more quietly, in documents few people read.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Keep the noise down" spans a spectrum from illegitimate suppression to legitimate rigour; C answers the whole spectrum, refusing suppression while offering a case that actually survives scrutiny — often the move that dissolves the pressure. A participates in suppressing information during a process meant to allocate money accurately; D is suppression with an audit trail. This tests moderating a difficult discussion with a director about high-risk information within a constrained timescale.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The innovation that worked, and its cost**

A delivery manager's AI-assisted triage tool cut backlog processing time by 40% in a pilot, but was procured on a corporate card skirting procurement thresholds, and no data protection assessment was done though triage data includes personal information. They want to roll it out next month. What is the most effective response?

- A) Halt everything, admonish the delivery manager, and file the tool under lessons learned.
- B) Roll out next month on the pilot's momentum; governance can catch up.
- C) Protect the innovation and fix its foundations: celebrate the result publicly, be straight with the delivery manager privately about the two compliance gaps and why they are not optional (personal data and procurement thresholds are legal exposure, not bureaucracy), and sequence the rollout through the necessary DPIA and procurement route — using seniority to accelerate those processes rather than bypass them.
- D) Report the procurement and data issues to compliance and step back personally from the pilot.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The two gaps are genuinely uncuttable (legal exposure to the department and users), but the innovation itself is exactly the experimentation this level is charged with encouraging. C holds both — genuine public celebration, private educational correction, and legitimate acceleration of the compliant route. A punishes initiative and kills the improvement with the lesson; B adopts legal exposure at scale; D is self-protection wearing a compliance costume. This tests championing an experiment while guarding its guardrails, not choosing one over the other.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Choosing the feedback mode**

An experienced delivery manager routinely interrupts and speaks over their own team members in sprint reviews, twice this month visibly deflating a junior developer; their team's health check shows declining "voice" scores, and the delivery manager seems unaware. A scheduled one-to-one is tomorrow; a written quarterly review is due next week. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Cover it in the written quarterly review, where it becomes part of the formal record.
- B) Raise it in tomorrow's one-to-one: specific observed instances, their visible effect, the corroborating health-check data — as a development conversation with concrete alternatives (letting team members present their own work) — and keep the written review for themes, not ambushes.
- C) Mention it lightly at the next team retro so the whole team can discuss it.
- D) Say nothing specific but model better behaviour in the candidate's own meetings.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The behaviour is specific, recent, and unconscious — the profile that responds best to prompt, private, evidenced conversation, with the health-check data converting "I noticed" into "the team is telling you too" evidence. A's delay costs another cycle of the behaviour and reads as an ambush when first raised in writing; C outsources a leadership conversation to the least safe venue for it; D assumes modelling substitutes for feedback the person does not know they need. This tests choosing the right feedback mode at the right time and ensuring the discussion sticks.

**Question 9 (hard) — The assessment a candidate contributes to**

Peer-reviewing another programme's service assessment, the candidate finds cherry-picked performance data and an operational support model unstaffed beyond month two; the service belongs to a directorate whose influential head reacts badly to criticism, and the candidate's own programme needs that directorate's cooperation next quarter. A colleague on the panel suggests "landing the concerns softly — maybe as recommendations rather than conditions — given the relationships involved." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree; recommendations preserve the relationship and the substance survives.
- B) Contribute an honest assessment with the evidence, support conditions where the evidence warrants conditions, and be rigorous about tone — factual, specific, respectful, no blindsiding: ensure the service team hears the concerns and their basis directly. Treat the relationship risk as real but not purchasable with assessment integrity, and manage it separately through engagement on the shared platform.
- C) Withdraw from the panel, citing the conflict of interest.
- D) Soften the written report but tell the directorate head the full truth privately.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Converting evidence-warranted conditions into recommendations alters substance, not tone — conditions gate progression, recommendations only request consideration. B holds assessment integrity while doing everything legitimate for the relationship through rigour of tone and direct communication, managing relationship risk separately from the record. A trades users' future interests for organisational comfort; C removes the panel's most independent voice; D builds a divergence between what is written and what is known. This tests separating substance from tone under relationship pressure in an assessment role.

**Question 10 (hard) — The budget raid**

A portfolio board will consider reallocating £600k from the candidate's remaining budget to a sister programme, citing underspend — real but deliberate, as phased buffer for Q4 supplier payments and contingency for a fragile migration. The paper goes to board Friday; the candidate is not on the attendee list. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the outcome; boards allocate, delivery managers deliver.
- B) Get the true position in front of the decision before it is made: brief the programme director immediately with the evidence (the underspend's composition — committed Q4 phasing plus risk-based contingency, and the specific consequence of losing each), request attendance or ensure a written response accompanies the paper, and propose constructive alternatives if the sister programme's need is real — a smaller sum, a loan against Q4, or jointly approaching the portfolio's actual contingency.
- C) Spend the buffer quickly before Friday so there is nothing to reallocate.
- D) Lobby sympathetic board members informally to vote the paper down.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Underspend" as presented conflates unallocated money with phased commitments and priced risk, and the correction must reach the decision through the front door before Friday, with speed, evidence, presence, and constructive alternatives. A abdicates legitimate input to a resource decision; C validates the raid's premise through defensive spending; D routes influence through a back channel that undermines a case strong enough not to need it. This tests negotiating and influencing budgets in a complex environment under time pressure.

**Question 11 (hard) — The exhausted high performer**

The candidate's strongest delivery manager, running the highest-risk service, has for two months been first online and last off, thinner-skinned, and this week snapped uncharacteristically at a junior colleague before apologising profusely; they have twice deflected "how are you doing?" with "fine, just a big quarter." A director now asks for them by name to also take on an incoming crisis programme, "since they're clearly your best." What is the most effective response?

- A) Pass them the crisis programme with extra thanks; high performers rise to demand, and declining the director is politically costly.
- B) Decline the director on their behalf without telling them, to protect them.
- C) Have the real conversation, past the deflection — name the specific changes observed and that this is concern, not evaluation; jointly re-examine their load with actual relief on the table (what will be taken, delegated, or delayed); and handle the director's request honestly — the crisis programme gets a properly supported alternative, not this person's last reserves — while making clear that protecting their sustainability is a leadership decision owned by the candidate, not a verdict on their capability.
- D) Schedule them a resilience course and revisit next quarter.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Extended hours, thinning composure, an uncharacteristic snap, and repeated deflection form a burnout pattern in a high performer whose identity makes admitting overload feel like failure. C names observations rather than conclusions, offers concrete relief, and handles the director's request with honesty and an alternative rather than spending the candidate's strongest asset toward absence or resignation. B removes agency from a capable professional; D treats a load problem as a resilience deficit. This tests enforcing sustainable pace for a team member against organisational appetite, and occasionally against their own.

**Question 12 (hard) — Choosing least effective: the failing supplier at the gate**

A critical supplier has missed two milestones; the agreed recovery plan is now also slipping. The contract allows termination for material breach after the next missed milestone, in five weeks. Retendering would take six months; the product is deeply integrated; the supplier's delivery team, distinct from management, is competent and candid. Which response is **least effective**?

- A) Intensify structured pressure now: weekly senior-to-senior reviews with the supplier's account director, explicit recovery criteria for the five-week window, and commercial colleagues engaged on the termination-versus-continuation analysis so the decision at the gate is prepared, not improvised.
- B) Begin contingency work in parallel: honestly assess the six-month retender path, interim options, and the true cost of each, so that five weeks from now the department chooses between real options rather than hostage positions.
- C) Signal privately to the supplier's account director that termination "isn't really on the table given the integration," to keep the relationship collaborative through the recovery.
- D) Tell the supplier plainly that the next milestone is decision-critical, that both recovery and exit paths are being prepared, and that their delivery team's candour is valued and should continue.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A, B, and D form a coherent playbook — structured pressure, genuine alternatives, and honest signalling. C privately concedes the one hard lever, converting it into a known bluff that removes the supplier's incentive to fund a genuine recovery, and the "collaborative" framing disguises disarming yourself rather than achieving real collaboration. This tests never paying for a relationship with leverage — the supplier's management is watching what is done with the gate, and so is the candidate's own team.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate audits an option's second-order effects** — what a response teaches the delivery managers watching, the supplier pricing the next move, the team learning whether feedback is safe.
- **Watch for whether a candidate distinguishes acting personally, coaching the person whose job it is to act, and routing to the owning tier** before choosing a response.
- **Note whether a candidate separates substance from tone** rather than trading one for the other under relationship pressure.
- **Note whether a candidate converts an illegitimate ask into its legitimate cousin** rather than complying with or bluntly refusing it.
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that rules on an expert dispute** rather than mediating toward a joint answer, reserving escalation for genuine strategic forks.
- **Crediting an answer that rescues rather than coaches** — absorbing a delivery manager's decisions caps their growth and bottlenecks the organisation.
- **Rewarding an answer that purchases a relationship with integrity** — a softened assessment, suppressed reporting, or conceded leverage.
- **Missing when a candidate confuses the political cost of honesty with its wisdom**, choosing deferral over an honest but costlier option.
- **Rewarding an answer that protects a colleague by removing their agency** rather than addressing them directly as a capable professional.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a senior delivery manager: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested reading portfolios rather than boards: trend versus variance across teams, shared constraints hidden in dependency registers, schedules derived from constraints and audited against them, confounded improvement claims, contract clocks running silently against a candidate, and continue/pivot/stop decisions held to the evidence. The numeric section tested the arithmetic that moves real money — blended rates decomposed, supplier savings claims rebuilt like-for-like, benefits tables read for cumulative position and trajectory, forecasts walked to their boundaries with assumptions stated. The verbal section tested holding governance verbs at their exact grade, keeping qualified obligations qualified, and grading arguments by the gap between evidence and remedy. The situational judgement section tested the calls that define seniority: coaching instead of absorbing, mediating instead of ruling, refusing suppression while offering rigour, protecting innovation and its guardrails simultaneously, and never paying for relationships with leverage or integrity.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. On the situational judgement items especially, score for second-order thinking about what a response teaches others, since several items deliberately have more than one defensible option. A debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can reason across systems of teams, handle numbers that move real money and inform business cases, read governance and contract text exactly, and exercise judgement as the main point of escalation — never the whole of that decision.
