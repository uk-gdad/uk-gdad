# Business Architect (Associate Business Architect) - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor evaluating a candidate for associate business architect in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are a line manager, a panel member, or a reviewer, this guide gives you a structured, job-specific psychometric assessment you can administer fairly and consistently.

A psychometric assessment is a structured exercise that measures the mental skills and judgement a candidate brings to their work. It is not about trick questions or obscure knowledge. Instead, it focuses on the abilities the role genuinely uses: making sense of complex organisational information, reading policy documents carefully, working with figures such as budgets and capacity plans, and choosing sensible courses of action when situations get tricky.

Why does this matter for this role level? An associate business architect breaks down complexity, brings together disconnected information to inform decision making, explains complex business problems using clear language and artefacts, and recognises issues that cut across different areas of the organisation. Each of those duties depends on the underlying abilities that psychometric assessments measure: cognitive ability, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

This document is organised into four main sections, one for each assessment type:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** - pattern spotting, logical deduction, error checking, and prioritisation using the kinds of artefacts an associate business architect handles, such as capability maps and process models.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** - tables, percentages, ratios, and budget figures drawn from realistic public sector scenarios.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** - careful reading of policies, standards, emails, and reports, with True / False / Cannot Say questions.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** - workplace dilemmas involving stakeholders, project teams, and senior leaders, scored on which responses are most and least effective.

Each section explains what the assessment measures, maps it to the specific skills in this role profile, provides a full set of items with worked explanations, and finishes with administration tips and common pitfalls. The worked answers and explanations are your answer key: hold them back from the candidate until after the assessment, and use them only for scoring and the debrief that follows.

Administer this fairly and consistently. Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time limit; brief every candidate the same way; and keep the conditions (room, equipment, breaks) as similar as you reasonably can between candidates. After scoring, debrief the candidate honestly and constructively, without necessarily walking through every question and answer, since this material may be reused with future candidates.

This is one input among several in a hiring or promotion decision, never the sole basis for one - see the advisory below.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment is a targeted psychometric test that measures the exact mental processes needed for the associate business architect role. Unlike a generic IQ test, it uses practical workplace scenarios and data formats drawn from the everyday tasks of the job, which makes it a fairer and more useful predictor of how a candidate will perform at work.

For this role, that means the questions look like the artefacts an associate business architect genuinely handles: capability maps, process models, organisation charts, service catalogues, project plans, and strategy documents. The candidate will be asked to spot patterns, follow logical rules, check for errors, prioritise competing items, and solve small structured problems.

Administer this as a timed test, most often between 15 and 30 minutes for the sample below, with the same limit for every candidate. Questions are multiple choice; score them objectively against the answer key and, where practical, compare results against a benchmark or norm group rather than judging performance in isolation. If your platform offers a few short, ungraded practice questions first, use them consistently for every candidate so nobody is disadvantaged by unfamiliarity with the format. Consider recording speed and accuracy separately, since working carefully matters as much as working quickly in this role.

Reassure the candidate before they start that nothing in this section requires secret knowledge; everything needed is in the question, and it rewards clear, methodical thinking.

### What it measures

The cognitive assessment maps directly onto the skills in this role profile:

- **Business architecture**: supporting the production of a multidimensional view of the organisation requires holding several layers of information in mind at once (capabilities, processes, systems, teams) and spotting where they connect or conflict. Pattern recognition and logical deduction questions test exactly this.
- **Consultancy**: framing problems so they can easily be understood, and troubleshooting where required, depends on breaking a messy situation into clear parts. Problem-solving questions measure how well the candidate decomposes complexity.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: making decisions characterised by managed levels of risk and complexity means weighing rules and constraints correctly. Logical deduction and prioritisation questions reflect this.
- **Methods and tools**: selecting the most appropriate tools and techniques requires comparing options against criteria systematically, which is the essence of many cognitive test items.
- **Strategic thinking**: working within a strategic context means checking whether detailed activities align with higher-level goals, a form of error checking and rule following.
- **Communicating information** and **aggregating and disaggregating complex information**: several question types below ask the candidate to reconcile information from different sources, mirroring the duty to bring together disconnected information to inform decision making.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate in order. Each is labelled with an approximate difficulty for your own reference when scoring.

#### Question 1 (easy) - Capability map pattern

A department's capability map groups capabilities into three tiers. The candidate notices the numbering follows a pattern:

- Tier 1: Strategy Development (1.0), Policy Design (2.0), Service Planning (3.0)
- Tier 2: Strategy Development has 1.1, 1.2, 1.3; Policy Design has 2.1, 2.2, 2.3
- Tier 3: Capability 1.1 has 1.1.1 and 1.1.2; capability 1.2 has 1.2.1 and 1.2.2

Following the same pattern, how many Tier 3 capabilities should be expected under Service Planning (3.0) in total?

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 6
- D) 9

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Each Tier 1 capability has three Tier 2 children (for example, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3). Each Tier 2 capability has two Tier 3 children (for example, 1.1.1 and 1.1.2). So Service Planning (3.0) should have three Tier 2 capabilities (3.1, 3.2, 3.3), and each of those should have two Tier 3 capabilities: 3 x 2 = 6. This is a simple example of the hierarchical decomposition used when breaking down complexity into clear outputs. The item rewards a candidate who identifies the rule at each level before multiplying.

#### Question 2 (easy) - Error checking a RACI matrix

The candidate is reviewing a RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) matrix for a business change project. The project standard says: "Every activity must have exactly one Accountable role."

| Activity | Service Owner | Business Architect | Programme Manager |
|---|---|---|---|
| Define target operating model | C | A | A |
| Approve process changes | A | R | C |
| Communicate changes to staff | I | C | A |
| Update capability map | C | A | I |

Which activity breaches the standard?

- A) Define target operating model
- B) Approve process changes
- C) Communicate changes to staff
- D) Update capability map

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Scan the table one row at a time, counting only the letter A. "Define target operating model" has two A entries (Business Architect and Programme Manager), which breaks the rule of exactly one Accountable role per activity. All other rows have exactly one A. This item rewards a disciplined, row-by-row scan rather than reading the whole table at once. In the real role, spotting a double-accountability like this is exactly the kind of cross-cutting issue a candidate is expected to recognise and raise.

#### Question 3 (easy) - Logical deduction from architecture principles

An organisation has three architecture principles:

1. All citizen-facing services must meet the GOV.UK Service Standard.
2. Any service that meets the GOV.UK Service Standard must have completed a service assessment.
3. The Licence Renewal service is a citizen-facing service.

Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The Licence Renewal service has completed a service assessment.
- B) All services that complete a service assessment are citizen-facing.
- C) The Licence Renewal service is the only citizen-facing service.
- D) Internal services never complete service assessments.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Chain the statements: Licence Renewal is citizen-facing (principle 3), so it must meet the Service Standard (principle 1), so it must have completed a service assessment (principle 2). Option B reverses the logic (the rules say meeting the standard requires an assessment, not the other way round). Options C and D go beyond what the statements say. This item tests whether the candidate concludes only what follows strictly from the given rules, just as when applying architectural methods following direction and wider strategy.

#### Question 4 (moderate) - Prioritisation with constraints

The candidate has four tasks on Monday morning and can complete only three before a 14:00 governance board:

- Task W: Update the business capability heat map the board will review (takes 2 hours).
- Task X: Respond to a stakeholder email about next month's workshop (takes 30 minutes).
- Task Y: Fix a version-control error in the strategy alignment document that the board will also review (takes 1 hour).
- Task Z: Draft a first-cut process model for a project starting in three weeks (takes 2 hours).

The day starts at 09:00 with a 1-hour lunch break at 12:00. Which combination completes the most board-critical work on time?

- A) W, X, Z
- B) W, Y, X
- C) Y, Z, X
- D) W, Y, Z

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First identify what matters for the 14:00 deadline: the board will review the heat map (W) and the strategy alignment document (Y), so both are board-critical. W + Y = 3 hours. Adding X (30 minutes) gives 3.5 hours of work. From 09:00 to 12:00 is 3 hours; lunch 12:00 to 13:00; then 13:00 to 14:00 is 1 more hour, giving 4 hours available. W, Y, and X fit comfortably (3.5 hours) and cover everything the board needs. Option D also includes both board-critical items but totals 5 hours, which does not fit. Options A and C each miss one board-critical task. This item tests whether the candidate separates "urgent for the deadline" from "important but later", then checks the arithmetic of the time budget.

#### Question 5 (moderate) - Reconciling disconnected information

The candidate is aggregating information for a decision paper. Three sources describe the same business area:

- The HR system says the Casework directorate has 120 staff.
- The finance report says the Casework directorate's staff costs equal 130 full-time-equivalent (FTE) salaries.
- The directorate's own organisation chart shows 118 posts, of which 10 are vacant.

Which statement best describes what a candidate can reliably conclude?

- A) The three sources agree once vacancies are considered.
- B) The sources disagree, and the differences should be flagged before the decision paper relies on any single figure.
- C) The finance report is wrong because it shows the largest number.
- D) The HR system is correct because HR systems are authoritative for staff numbers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compare the figures: 120 staff (HR), 130 FTE costs (finance), 118 posts with 10 vacant, meaning 108 filled posts (org chart). Even allowing for vacancies, the numbers do not reconcile: 108 filled posts is not 120 staff, and 130 FTE of cost exceeds both. There is not enough information given to tell which source is right, so options C and D assume too much, and option A is factually wrong. The professionally sound conclusion is B: flag the discrepancy. This mirrors the duty to bring together disconnected information to inform decision making; a strong candidate's answer notices that sources conflict rather than picking a number.

#### Question 6 (moderate) - Process model logic

A target-state process model for grant applications has these rules:

- Every application goes through Validation first.
- Applications that fail Validation go to Rejection Notice.
- Applications that pass Validation go to Assessment.
- Applications scoring 70 or above in Assessment go to Approval; all others go to Panel Review.
- Panel Review can send an application to Approval or to Rejection Notice.

An application has just arrived at Approval. Which of the following must be true?

- A) It scored 70 or above in Assessment.
- B) It passed Validation.
- C) It went through Panel Review.
- D) It scored below 70 in Assessment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace every route into Approval. There are two: directly from Assessment (score 70+) or via Panel Review. Both routes require the application to have passed Validation first, because only passing applications reach Assessment at all. So B must be true. A is not certain (it could have arrived via Panel Review), C is not certain (it could have come straight from Assessment), and D is only true for the Panel Review route. Process-model items like this reward tracing every possible path rather than the most obvious one, a core habit for a candidate who will produce and quality-check process artefacts.

#### Question 7 (moderate) - Spotting the cross-cutting issue

The candidate reviews four project summaries:

- Project 1 (Benefits directorate) is building a new document-upload feature for citizens.
- Project 2 (Licensing directorate) is procuring a document-upload component from a supplier.
- Project 3 (Casework directorate) reports delays because its in-house document-upload tool failed accessibility testing.
- Project 4 (Communications) is redesigning the department's intranet home page.

As an associate business architect, which observation is the most valuable to raise?

- A) Project 4 should be stopped because intranet work is low value.
- B) Three projects are independently solving the same document-upload need, which suggests an opportunity for a shared solution.
- C) Project 3 should abandon accessibility testing to catch up.
- D) Project 2 should be praised for using a supplier.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The role level description calls for the postholder to "recognise issues that cut across different areas of the organisation and present opportunities for improvement". Projects 1, 2, and 3 are all tackling document upload separately: duplicated effort, duplicated cost, and inconsistent citizen experience. Raising this pattern is classic business architecture value. Option A makes a judgement with no evidence behind it. Option C is unacceptable: accessibility is a legal and ethical requirement in UK public services. Option D is merely a comment, not an insight. This item tests pattern recognition across independent items, and rewards a candidate who reads all items before judging any one of them.

#### Question 8 (moderate) - Sequence and dependency logic

A business change roadmap has five activities with these dependencies:

- Activity B cannot start until Activity A finishes.
- Activity C cannot start until Activity A finishes.
- Activity D cannot start until both B and C finish.
- Activity E cannot start until C finishes.

Activities take: A = 2 weeks, B = 3 weeks, C = 1 week, D = 2 weeks, E = 4 weeks. All activities start as early as possible. After how many weeks does the whole roadmap finish?

- A) 7 weeks
- B) 8 weeks
- C) 9 weeks
- D) 12 weeks

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work out the earliest finish of each activity. A finishes at week 2. B runs weeks 2 to 5. C runs weeks 2 to 3. D needs both B (week 5) and C (week 3), so it runs weeks 5 to 7. E needs C (week 3), so it runs weeks 3 to 7. The latest finisher is week 7 (both D and E). So the answer is 7 weeks. A common wrong answer comes from adding all durations (12 weeks), which ignores that B and C, and later D and E, run in parallel. Dependency reasoning like this underpins the work of ensuring business change is implemented effectively and sequenced sensibly.

#### Question 9 (moderate) - Rule application with exceptions

An organisation's design authority applies these rules to proposals:

1. Proposals over £100,000 require a full business case.
2. Proposals that reuse an existing approved component are exempt from rule 1, unless they involve personal data.
3. All proposals involving personal data require a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA).

A proposal costs £150,000, reuses an existing approved component, and involves personal data. What does it require?

- A) Nothing beyond standard approval
- B) A full business case only
- C) A DPIA only
- D) Both a full business case and a DPIA

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Apply each rule in turn. Rule 1: over £100,000, so a full business case is needed. Rule 2: it reuses an approved component, which would normally grant an exemption, but the exemption does not apply because the proposal involves personal data, so the full business case is still required. Rule 3: personal data means a DPIA is required. Therefore both documents are needed. Exception-within-exception items like this are common in cognitive tests and in real governance frameworks alike; they test whether the candidate slows down and checks whether the exemption's own conditions are met before applying it.

#### Question 10 (hard) - Consistency across architecture views

The candidate maintains three views of the same business area, and a colleague asks them to check them for consistency:

- The capability view says the Contact Management capability is delivered by two teams: Front Office and Digital Services.
- The process view says the "Handle enquiry" process is performed only by the Front Office team.
- The system view says the CRM system supports the "Handle enquiry" process and is administered by Digital Services.
- A note says: every team that delivers a capability must perform at least one process within that capability.

Assuming "Handle enquiry" is the only process in the Contact Management capability, which inconsistency should be flagged?

- A) The CRM system should be administered by Front Office.
- B) Digital Services delivers the capability but performs no process within it, contradicting the note.
- C) Front Office should not perform "Handle enquiry".
- D) There is no inconsistency.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule says every team delivering a capability must perform at least one process in it. Digital Services is listed as delivering Contact Management, but the only process in that capability ("Handle enquiry") is performed solely by Front Office. So Digital Services delivers the capability while performing none of its processes: a direct contradiction. Administering a supporting system (option A's territory) is not the same as performing a process, so it does not satisfy the rule. This item mirrors real multidimensional modelling: different views of the organisation must reconcile, and finding the mismatch is a core associate-level task.

#### Question 11 (hard) - Analytical reasoning about stakeholder statements

Three stakeholders comment on a proposed target operating model:

- The Finance lead says: "The model reduces running costs and requires no new roles."
- The HR lead says: "The model requires at least one new role."
- The Operations lead says: "The model does not reduce running costs."

Two facts have been independently verified: exactly one of the three stakeholders is fully correct, and the model definitely requires at least one new role. Which stakeholder is fully correct?

- A) The Finance lead
- B) The HR lead
- C) The Operations lead
- D) It cannot be determined

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Start from the verified fact: at least one new role is required. That immediately makes the HR lead's statement true, and it makes the Finance lead's statement false, because Finance's claim is a conjunction ("reduces costs AND no new roles") and the "no new roles" clause fails. So HR is correct and Finance is not. Now apply the other verified fact: exactly one stakeholder is fully correct. Since HR is already correct, the Operations lead must be wrong, meaning the model does in fact reduce running costs. Everything is consistent: costs are reduced, a new role is required, and HR alone is fully correct. The practical lesson this item tests is real: compound stakeholder claims ("X and Y") should be decomposed and verified clause by clause, because one true clause often hides one false clause. When resolving disputes between peers and stakeholders, a strong candidate breaks every assertion into its smallest testable parts before judging who is right.

#### Question 12 (hard) - Problem decomposition

A director tells the candidate: "Our licensing service is too slow." This must be broken down before any solution is proposed. Which decomposition is the most useful first artefact?

- A) A list of the five newest technologies that could speed things up
- B) A breakdown of the end-to-end licensing journey into stages, with the average elapsed time and volume at each stage
- C) A survey asking staff whether they agree the service is slow
- D) A proposal to hire more staff in the licensing team

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Too slow" is a symptom, not a defined problem. The associate business architect's job is to break down complexity and produce clear outputs: decomposing the journey into stages with times and volumes turns a vague complaint into measurable parts, revealing where delay actually accumulates. Option A jumps to solutions before understanding the problem. Option C gathers opinions rather than evidence about where time goes. Option D commits resources before knowing whether staffing is the bottleneck. This item, like real "what should you examine first" questions, rewards the answer that structures the problem rather than the answer that acts fastest.

#### Question 13 (hard) - Integrated reasoning

An organisation's strategy names three goals: improve citizen satisfaction, reduce cost per transaction, and increase staff capability. A programme proposes four initiatives:

- Initiative P: automate document checks (reduces cost per transaction; needs staff retraining)
- Initiative Q: extend call-centre hours (improves satisfaction; increases cost per transaction)
- Initiative R: launch a staff learning academy (increases staff capability; cost-neutral)
- Initiative S: replace the branding on all letters (no measured effect on any goal)

The programme can fund exactly two initiatives and must ensure every funded initiative advances at least one strategic goal without setting any goal backwards. Which pair complies?

- A) P and Q
- B) Q and R
- C) P and R
- D) R and S

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each initiative against the rule "advances at least one goal without setting any goal backwards". P advances cost reduction; retraining is a need, not a goal set backwards, and it even connects to staff capability: compliant. Q advances satisfaction but increases cost per transaction, setting a goal backwards: non-compliant, which eliminates options A and B. R advances staff capability and harms nothing: compliant. S advances no goal, so funding it breaches the rule that every funded initiative must advance at least one goal, eliminating D. That leaves C: P and R. This is exactly the alignment-checking involved in ensuring design work from projects and programmes aligns with strategy: test every item against every stated criterion, and let the eliminations do the work.

### Administration tips

- **Encourage the candidate to work from their own kind of artefact where useful for orientation, not for the scored items themselves.** A brief warm-up conversation about a capability map, RACI matrix, or roadmap can settle nerves, but keep the timed, scored items identical for every candidate.
- **Watch for path-tracing shortcuts.** Many wrong answers in logic items come from a candidate following only the most obvious route through a process or dependency chain; a strong candidate's answer shows they checked for alternative paths.
- **Start untimed for calibration, then apply the same timed limit to every candidate.** If you want to see a candidate's method before adding time pressure, do this consistently for all candidates, not selectively.
- **Ask the candidate to explain their reasoning.** Verbalised reasoning is good evidence of the "explain complex business problems using clear language" skill this role needs, and it helps you distinguish a lucky guess from real understanding when scoring.
- **Set fair conditions.** Offer the same practice questions, the same quiet space, and reasonable notice of what to expect to every candidate, so nerves and unfamiliarity do not distort the result.

### Common pitfalls

- **Scoring a table-reading item by impression rather than by rule.** If the standard says "exactly one Accountable", the correct method is to scan for the letter A row by row; do not mark down a candidate whose written working shows this systematic approach even if it looks slow.
- **Treating parallel-running dependencies as additive.** Note when a candidate's wrong answer stems from adding durations that actually run in parallel; this is a specific, common error pattern worth recording for the debrief.
- **Crediting an answer that concludes more than the rules state.** Answers that reverse an if-then statement, or that generalise from one case to all cases, are classic traps and should be marked wrong even if confidently argued.
- **Letting one hard question dominate the session.** Most tests reward moving on and returning later; keep the overall time limit consistent rather than allowing extra time on a single item for one candidate and not another.
- **Missing exception-within-exception rules when marking.** Real governance rules, and items modelled on them, often contain an exception to the exception; check the candidate's answer against the whole rule, not just its first clause.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates a candidate's ability to analyse, interpret, and make logical decisions using numerical data. It mimics the data-handling demands of the actual role, focusing on practical business arithmetic rather than abstract formulas. There is no calculus, no algebra beyond simple rearrangement, and no memorised equations. The core toolkit is percentages, ratios, differences, averages, and careful reading of tables and charts.

For an associate business architect, the numbers in this assessment look like the numbers in the working week: programme budgets, staff full-time-equivalent (FTE) counts, transaction volumes, cost per transaction, benefits projections, capacity figures, and survey results. The question inputs are typically tables, simple charts, and short data statements, exactly like the evidence packs a candidate would assemble when assessing the impact of strategies on the organisation or bringing together disconnected information to inform decision making.

Administer this section under a strict time limit, often around 20 to 30 minutes for 15 to 25 questions, roughly one minute per question. Offer or provide an onscreen calculator consistently to every candidate, since the assessment prioritises interpretation over mental arithmetic. Speed matters, but accuracy matters more: quick and accurate data extraction is the skill being measured, and getting stuck on one hard multi-stage problem is the classic way a candidate loses marks.

Reassure the candidate that this section rewards method, not brilliance: reading the table headings, noting the units, identifying exactly which figures the question needs, and doing the arithmetic step by step. All of that can be practised.

### What it measures

Numeric reasoning maps to this role profile's skills as follows:

- **Business architecture**: assessing the impact of strategies on the organisation, and highlighting risks and issues, almost always involves quantifying that impact: costs, savings, headcount changes, and volume shifts.
- **Strategic thinking**: communicating how activities meet strategic goals frequently means comparing performance metrics against targets, calculating gaps, and projecting trends.
- **Consultancy**: providing advice, guidance, and recommendations is far more persuasive when grounded in correctly calculated evidence. A recommendation built on a misread percentage undermines the candidate's credibility.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: decisions characterised by managed levels of risk and complexity often hinge on numbers: which option costs less per outcome, which delivers benefits sooner, which stays within capacity.
- **Stakeholder relationship management**: working to improve stakeholder relationships "using evidence to explain decisions" means being able to extract, verify, and present figures accurately and honestly.
- **Aggregating and disaggregating complex information**: rolling figures up from teams to directorates, or breaking a departmental total down into its parts, is numerical aggregation and disaggregation in its purest form.

### Practice questions

An onscreen or desk calculator is appropriate for all of these. The worked arithmetic is shown in full so you can see exactly where a slip happened when marking.

#### Question 1 (easy) - Percentage of a budget

A business change programme has a total budget of £2,400,000. The business architecture workstream is allocated 15% of the total. How much is allocated to the business architecture workstream?

- A) £160,000
- B) £240,000
- C) £360,000
- D) £400,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 15% of £2,400,000 = 0.15 x 2,400,000 = £360,000. A quick sense-check: 10% would be £240,000 and 5% would be £120,000, so 15% is £240,000 + £120,000 = £360,000. This ten-percent sense-check is a useful habit for catching slips under time pressure, and it is exactly the mental arithmetic used when scanning a budget table in a governance paper.

#### Question 2 (easy) - Reading a volumes table

The table shows monthly application volumes for a licensing service:

| Month | Online applications | Paper applications |
|---|---|---|
| April | 8,200 | 1,800 |
| May | 8,900 | 1,600 |
| June | 9,400 | 1,400 |

What percentage of total applications in June were made online?

- A) 84%
- B) 87%
- C) 90%
- D) 94%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** June total = 9,400 + 1,400 = 10,800. Online share = 9,400 / 10,800 = 0.8703..., which is 87% to the nearest whole percent. Note the trap in option D: 9,400 looks like "94" but the percentage is of the total, not of 10,000. The candidate needs to compute the denominator explicitly rather than assume it is a round number. Channel-shift percentages like this appear constantly in the strategy alignment work an associate business architect supports.

#### Question 3 (easy) - FTE arithmetic

A directorate's target operating model reduces its workforce from 240 FTE to 210 FTE. What is the percentage reduction?

- A) 10%
- B) 12.5%
- C) 14.3%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The reduction is 240 - 210 = 30 FTE. As a percentage of the original: 30 / 240 = 0.125 = 12.5%. Option C (14.3%) is what results from wrongly dividing by the new figure (30 / 210). Percentage change is always measured against the starting value unless the question says otherwise. Option D confuses the absolute change (30 FTE) with a percentage. Being precise about the base of a percentage is essential when highlighting the impact of a strategy on the organisation.

#### Question 4 (moderate) - Cost per transaction comparison

The candidate is comparing two service delivery options for an evidence pack:

| Option | Annual running cost | Annual transactions |
|---|---|---|
| Current service | £1,840,000 | 460,000 |
| Proposed digital service | £1,275,000 | 425,000 |

By how much would the cost per transaction fall under the proposed option?

- A) £0.50
- B) £1.00
- C) £1.50
- D) £4.00

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Current cost per transaction = £1,840,000 / 460,000 = £4.00. Proposed cost per transaction = £1,275,000 / 425,000 = £3.00. The fall is £4.00 - £3.00 = £1.00. Option D is the trap for a candidate who reports the current unit cost instead of the difference. Notice also that total cost falls AND volume falls; unit-cost comparisons handle that gracefully, which is why cost per transaction is a favourite metric in public sector business cases and a favourite calculation in numeric assessments.

#### Question 5 (moderate) - Ratios in a capability assessment

A capability maturity review rates 60 business capabilities. The results are in the ratio 1 : 3 : 2 for "leading", "established", and "developing" respectively. How many capabilities are rated "developing"?

- A) 10
- B) 20
- C) 30
- D) 40

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The ratio 1 : 3 : 2 has 1 + 3 + 2 = 6 parts. Each part is 60 / 6 = 10 capabilities. "Developing" has 2 parts: 2 x 10 = 20. As a check, the full split is 10 leading, 30 established, 20 developing, which sums to 60. Ratio items are solved by finding the value of one part first. Maturity distributions like this are exactly what an associate business architect summarises when supporting a multidimensional view of the organisation.

#### Question 6 (moderate) - Benefits realisation projection

A business case projects savings of £150,000 in year 1, with savings growing by 20% each year as adoption increases. What are the projected savings in year 3?

- A) £180,000
- B) £210,000
- C) £216,000
- D) £270,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Growth of 20% per year compounds. Year 2 = £150,000 x 1.2 = £180,000. Year 3 = £180,000 x 1.2 = £216,000. Option B (£210,000) is the result of adding 20% of the original figure twice (simple rather than compound growth): £150,000 + £30,000 + £30,000. Growth statements need careful reading: "growing by 20% each year" means each year's figure is 1.2 times the previous year's figure. Compounding appears in benefits profiles, demand forecasts, and cost inflation, so fluency with it is worth testing directly.

#### Question 7 (moderate) - Weighted scores in an options appraisal

A team scores two options against three criteria. Criteria weights: strategic fit 50%, cost 30%, deliverability 20%. Scores are out of 10:

| Criterion | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic fit | 8 | 6 |
| Cost | 5 | 9 |
| Deliverability | 6 | 7 |

Which option wins, and by how many weighted points?

- A) Option A wins by 0.4
- B) Option B wins by 0.4
- C) Option B wins by 3.0
- D) Option A wins by 1.0

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Multiply each score by its weight, then sum. Option A: (8 x 0.5) + (5 x 0.3) + (6 x 0.2) = 4.0 + 1.5 + 1.2 = 6.7. Option B: (6 x 0.5) + (9 x 0.3) + (7 x 0.2) = 3.0 + 2.7 + 1.4 = 7.1. Option B wins by 7.1 - 6.7 = 0.4 weighted points. Option C is the trap for a candidate who ignores the weights: the unweighted totals are 19 versus 22, a difference of 3.0. Weighting changes outcomes: Option A leads on the heaviest criterion (strategic fit) but loses overall because Option B's advantages on cost and deliverability outweigh it. Weighted options appraisals are standard in the methodologies proposed in consultancy work at this level, so fluency with them pays off twice.

#### Question 8 (moderate) - Aggregating directorate figures

The candidate is rolling up quarterly transaction volumes from three directorates into a departmental total for a strategy paper:

| Directorate | Q1 | Q2 |
|---|---|---|
| Benefits | 125,000 | 138,000 |
| Licensing | 64,000 | 58,000 |
| Casework | 41,000 | 44,000 |

What was the percentage change in the departmental total from Q1 to Q2, to one decimal place?

- A) +4.3%
- B) +4.5%
- C) +10.4%
- D) -4.3%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Aggregate first, then compare. Q1 total = 125,000 + 64,000 + 41,000 = 230,000. Q2 total = 138,000 + 58,000 + 44,000 = 240,000. Change = 240,000 - 230,000 = 10,000. Percentage change = 10,000 / 230,000 = 0.04347... = +4.3% to one decimal place. A tempting wrong method is averaging the three directorates' individual percentage changes, which gives a different (wrong) answer because the directorates differ in size. This item tests whether the candidate sums first and calculates percentages on the totals, which is the correct approach when aggregating complex information from across the organisation, unless a genuine per-directorate view is wanted.

#### Question 9 (moderate) - Capacity and demand

A casework team can process 1,250 cases per week at full capacity. Current demand is 1,100 cases per week and is forecast to grow by 5% per quarter. After how many quarters will demand first exceed capacity?

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 4
- D) 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compound the demand quarter by quarter. After 1 quarter: 1,100 x 1.05 = 1,155. After 2 quarters: 1,155 x 1.05 = 1,212.75. After 3 quarters: 1,212.75 x 1.05 = 1,273.39. Capacity is 1,250, so demand first exceeds capacity after 3 quarters (1,273 > 1,250; at 2 quarters, 1,213 < 1,250). This "when does the line cross" pattern appears in capacity planning, benefits tracking, and risk analysis. A strong candidate computes one step at a time and stops as soon as the threshold is crossed, rather than over-calculating.

#### Question 10 (hard) - Multi-step cost model

A proposed shared corporate service would replace three separate team functions:

| Current function | Annual staff cost | Annual system cost |
|---|---|---|
| Function 1 | £420,000 | £85,000 |
| Function 2 | £310,000 | £60,000 |
| Function 3 | £270,000 | £55,000 |

The shared service would cost £750,000 per year in staff and £120,000 per year for a single system. Transition costs are a one-off £280,000. In the first year (including transition costs), what is the net financial position of moving to the shared service?

- A) Net saving of £330,000
- B) Net saving of £50,000
- C) Net cost of £50,000
- D) Net saving of £610,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: current total = (420,000 + 310,000 + 270,000) + (85,000 + 60,000 + 55,000) = 1,000,000 + 200,000 = £1,200,000. Step 2: shared service annual cost = 750,000 + 120,000 = £870,000. Step 3: annual saving = 1,200,000 - 870,000 = £330,000. Step 4: first year includes one-off transition costs, so net position = 330,000 - 280,000 = £50,000 saving. Option A is the trap for a candidate who forgets the transition costs; option D is the trap for one who compares staff costs only against the full shared cost. Multi-step items like this reward writing intermediate results down rather than chaining everything mentally, exactly as a candidate would in a real impact assessment where their working must be transparent to stakeholders.

#### Question 11 (hard) - Survey data interpretation

A stakeholder survey about a new operating model receives 480 responses. 65% of respondents are from operational teams and the rest are from corporate teams. Among operational respondents, 40% support the model; among corporate respondents, 75% support it. How many respondents support the model in total?

- A) 251
- B) 276
- C) 291
- D) 312

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Operational respondents = 0.65 x 480 = 312. Corporate respondents = 480 - 312 = 168. Operational supporters = 0.40 x 312 = 124.8, which in a survey context rounds to about 125 people; keep it as 124.8 for now. Corporate supporters = 0.75 x 168 = 126. Total = 124.8 + 126 = 250.8, approximately 251. Notice the overall support rate is 251 / 480, about 52%: closer to the operational figure because operational staff dominate the sample. This is a weighted-average effect: 40% and 75% should never simply be averaged to get 57.5% when group sizes differ. Understanding whose voice dominates aggregated figures is vital when using evidence to explain decisions to stakeholders fairly.

#### Question 12 (hard) - Reading a burn-down of programme budget

A transformation programme has a £3,600,000 budget over 12 months, planned to be spent evenly. At the end of month 8, actual spend is £2,700,000. If the current average monthly overspend rate continues for the remaining months, by how much will the programme exceed its total budget?

- A) £150,000
- B) £300,000
- C) £450,000
- D) £900,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Planned monthly spend = 3,600,000 / 12 = £300,000, so planned spend by month 8 = £2,400,000. Actual spend = £2,700,000, meaning actual monthly average = 2,700,000 / 8 = £337,500. If that continues for the remaining 4 months: 4 x 337,500 = £1,350,000. Projected total = 2,700,000 + 1,350,000 = £4,050,000. Overrun = 4,050,000 - 3,600,000 = £450,000. Option B is the trap for a candidate who reports only the overspend to date (2,700,000 - 2,400,000 = 300,000) without projecting forward. The question stem needs reading twice: "if the current rate continues" signals a projection, not a snapshot. Spotting an emerging overspend and quantifying its trajectory is exactly the risk-highlighting the business architecture skill calls for.

#### Question 13 (hard) - Disaggregating a combined figure

A department reports that its two contact channels together handled 180,000 contacts last quarter at a combined average cost of £2.90 per contact. The phone channel's average cost is £4.50 per contact and the digital channel's is £1.30 per contact. How many contacts came through the phone channel?

- A) 60,000
- B) 72,000
- C) 90,000
- D) 108,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Let P be phone contacts, so digital contacts are 180,000 - P. Total cost two ways: 4.50P + 1.30(180,000 - P) = 2.90 x 180,000. Left side: 4.50P + 234,000 - 1.30P = 3.20P + 234,000. Right side: 522,000. So 3.20P = 288,000, and P = 90,000. Sense-check: £2.90 sits exactly halfway between £1.30 and £4.50, so the channels must have equal volumes, and 90,000 each fits perfectly. This "unmixing" of a blended average is disaggregation in action: taking one combined figure and recovering the parts behind it, a skill this role profile names directly.

#### Question 14 (hard) - Percentage points versus percentages

Digital uptake for a service rose from 60% of transactions to 72% of transactions after a campaign. A colleague drafts a slide saying uptake "rose by 12%". As the reviewer, which correction is right?

- A) No correction needed; 12% is accurate.
- B) Uptake rose by 12 percentage points, which is a 20% increase on the previous level.
- C) Uptake rose by 20 percentage points, which is a 12% increase on the previous level.
- D) Uptake rose by 12 percentage points, which is a 12% increase on the previous level.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The move from 60% to 72% is a rise of 12 percentage points (72 - 60). As a relative change it is 12 / 60 = 0.20 = a 20% increase. Confusing percentage points with percentages is one of the most common numerical errors in government reporting, and this item tests it deliberately. When communicating complex information about the organisation to stakeholders, precision here protects a candidate's credibility: "12 percentage points" and "20% increase" describe the same fact from two angles, and a good slide states which one it means.

#### Question 15 (moderate) - Choosing the right chart figure

The candidate is preparing a one-page summary for a director. The strategy sets a target: "reduce average end-to-end processing time to 10 days by year end". The latest data shows:

| Quarter | Average processing time (days) | Cases processed |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 16 | 22,000 |
| Q2 | 14 | 24,500 |
| Q3 | 12.5 | 25,000 |

If the average processing time continues to fall by the same absolute amount as it did between Q2 and Q3, what will it be at the end of Q4, and does it meet the target?

- A) 11 days; target met
- B) 11 days; target not met
- C) 10 days; target met
- D) 12 days; target not met

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The fall from Q2 to Q3 is 14 - 12.5 = 1.5 days. Continuing that absolute trend, Q4 = 12.5 - 1.5 = 11 days. The target is 10 days, and 11 days is above the target, so the target is NOT met. Option A is the trap for a candidate who computes the projection correctly but misreads the direction of the target: "reduce to 10 days" means the figure must be at or below 10. Two separate skills are tested at once here: trend projection (arithmetic) and target interpretation (careful reading). This role's communication of how activities meet strategic goals means being honest when a trajectory is positive but insufficient, which is exactly the story this data tells: improving, yet still short of the goal. That nuance, "on track in direction but not in pace", is one of the most useful phrases a business architect can bring to a governance discussion, and it comes straight from doing this simple arithmetic correctly - it is worth noting whether a candidate's own summary captures it.

#### Question 16 (easy) - Sense-checking a stakeholder's claim

A stakeholder tells a meeting: "Digital applications have doubled this year, so we can halve the paper-handling team." Last year there were 40,000 digital applications and 30,000 paper applications; this year there are 80,000 digital and 27,000 paper. The paper-handling team's workload depends only on paper volumes. What does the data actually support?

- A) The claim is fully supported; paper work has halved.
- B) Digital applications have doubled, but paper applications have fallen by only 10%, so halving the paper team is not supported by this data.
- C) Paper applications have halved, so the team can be halved.
- D) Digital applications have not doubled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each part of the claim separately. Digital: 80,000 / 40,000 = 2, so digital applications have indeed doubled; option D is wrong. Paper: 27,000 versus 30,000 is a fall of 3,000, and 3,000 / 30,000 = 10%; paper volumes have fallen only 10%, not 50%, so options A and C are wrong. The stakeholder has linked two true-sounding numbers with a false inference: growth in one channel says nothing direct about workload in the other. Option B is correct. This is perhaps the most job-relevant numeric skill of all for this role: politely testing the arithmetic inside a confident claim, using evidence to explain decisions, before the claim becomes a staffing plan.

### Administration tips

- **Provide the same core formulas or reference sheet to every candidate, if your organisation allows it.** Nearly every item in this test type is built from percentages, ratios, averages, and percentage change; deciding in advance whether to offer a reminder of these, and doing so consistently, keeps the assessment fair.
- **Confirm the denominator convention before marking.** Most traps in numeric items live in the denominator; when reviewing an incorrect answer, check whether the candidate used the wrong base for a percentage.
- **Look for written intermediate results.** Multi-step items are handled safely by candidates who note each stage (totals, unit costs, differences) instead of chaining calculations mentally; this is also good evidence of a checkable, professional working style worth noting in your assessment record.
- **Provide the same calculator to every candidate.** It removes arithmetic strain but cannot choose the right numbers for the candidate; extracting the figures and planning the calculation is what is actually being tested.
- **Manage the clock consistently.** Apply the same overall time limit and the same guidance about skipping and returning to every candidate sitting this level's assessment.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading chart and table labels when marking.** Confusing monthly with annual data, or missing scale qualifiers like "in thousands", is a specific, identifiable error; check the candidate's working before marking an answer wrong outright.
- **Letting a wrong base for percentage change go unnoticed.** Dividing by the new value instead of the original, as in Question 3, is a common and specific mistake worth flagging when reviewing incorrect answers.
- **Missing the weighted-average trap.** As Question 11 showed, group sizes matter; a candidate who simply averages percentages across different-sized groups has made a specific, teachable error.
- **Conflating percentage points with percentages.** A rise from 60% to 72% is 12 points but a 20% increase; this distinction is worth checking explicitly in a candidate's working.
- **Penalising a sensible estimate-first approach.** A candidate who visibly sense-checks (like the halfway observation in Question 13) before calculating precisely is demonstrating strong numeric judgement, not skipping a step.
- **Letting one difficult multi-stage item consume disproportionate session time.** Encourage skipping and returning, consistent with how the section is designed to be timed for every candidate.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well a candidate understands, analyses, and draws correct conclusions from written business information. It mirrors the exact reading and comprehension demands of the role: an associate business architect spends much of their time reading strategy documents, policy papers, standards, meeting notes, and stakeholder emails, then explaining what they mean using clear language and artefacts. This assessment measures the reading half of that skill.

The classic format gives the candidate a short, dense passage followed by statements. For each statement they choose one of three answers:

- **True**: the statement definitely follows from the passage.
- **False**: the statement definitely contradicts the passage.
- **Cannot Say**: the passage does not give enough information to decide either way.

Some assessments also include multiple-choice comprehension questions, such as "which of the following best summarises the author's main point?" or "according to the passage, which action is required?".

The golden rule the candidate should follow is fact-based logic: answer based strictly on the text provided, ignoring outside knowledge. Even if the candidate knows from their own experience that something is usually true in government, if the passage does not say it, the answer is Cannot Say. This discipline feels strange at first but becomes natural with practice, and it is genuinely useful professionally: separating "what the document actually says" from "what I assume it means" prevents costly misunderstandings in architecture work.

Administer this section under time pressure, often giving less than one minute per question to simulate workplace deadlines. Passages use relevant terminology from reports, emails, and policy documents, exactly like the material below. No specialist knowledge beyond careful reading is required.

### What it measures

Verbal reasoning maps to this role profile's skills like this:

- **Communicating information**: being "flexible and capable of proactive and reactive communication" starts with accurately understanding what others have written. Misreading an email or policy is the fastest way to communicate the wrong thing.
- **Business architecture**: clearly communicating business architecture frameworks, principles, and techniques requires first reading and digesting those frameworks precisely. Standards documents reward exact reading.
- **Consultancy**: framing problems so they can easily be understood means extracting the true problem from wordy source material, and providing recommendations means understanding precisely what guidance documents do and do not permit.
- **Stakeholder relationship management**: tailoring communication to stakeholders' needs depends on correctly interpreting what stakeholders have said and written, including nuance and qualifications.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: resolving disputes "considering all views and opinions" requires representing each written view accurately, not as assumed.
- **Strategic thinking**: working within a strategic context means reading strategy documents closely enough to know what they commit to, what they merely aspire to, and what they leave open.

### Practice questions

Present each passage in full, then have the candidate answer the statements strictly from the text. Explanations point back to the exact wording, so you can see precisely why each answer is what it is when marking.

#### Passage A - Internal architecture standard

Read this extract from a departmental standard:

"All programmes must produce a business capability impact assessment before their first gate review. The assessment must identify every capability the programme will change, and must rate the scale of change to each capability as minor, moderate, or major. Programmes rated as changing any capability at the 'major' level must present their assessment to the Design Authority. The Design Authority meets fortnightly and may require programmes to revise their delivery plans. Programmes changing capabilities only at minor or moderate levels may proceed without Design Authority review, although they are encouraged to seek advice from the business architecture team."

#### Question 1 (easy)

Statement: Every programme must produce a business capability impact assessment before its first gate review.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True)**

**Explanation:** The first sentence says exactly this: "All programmes must produce a business capability impact assessment before their first gate review." The statement is a near-direct restatement, so it is True. Easy items like this check that the candidate can match a claim to explicit text.

#### Question 2 (moderate)

Statement: The Design Authority meets every week.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage says "The Design Authority meets fortnightly", meaning every two weeks. "Every week" directly contradicts "fortnightly", so the statement is False, not Cannot Say. The distinction matters: False requires an actual contradiction with the text, which is present here. This item tests attention to frequency words (fortnightly, quarterly, annually), which assessments frequently swap out.

#### Question 3 (moderate)

Statement: A programme that changes three capabilities at the moderate level must present its assessment to the Design Authority.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The trigger for Design Authority review is "changing any capability at the 'major' level". A programme changing capabilities only at moderate level, however many of them, falls under the sentence "Programmes changing capabilities only at minor or moderate levels may proceed without Design Authority review". Three moderates do not add up to one major; the passage gives no such accumulation rule. The statement contradicts the text, so it is False. This mirrors real governance reading: thresholds trigger on severity, not volume, unless the standard says otherwise.

#### Question 4 (hard)

Statement: The business architecture team must review programmes that change capabilities at the minor level.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** Note the careful wording: the passage says such programmes "are encouraged to seek advice from the business architecture team". "Encouraged to seek advice" is optional for the programme, and nothing anywhere says the business architecture team "must review" anything. Because the passage states these programmes "may proceed without Design Authority review" and only encourages advice-seeking, a mandatory review requirement contradicts the permissive wording of the text. Note the subtlety: if the statement had been "the business architecture team sometimes advises programmes", the answer would be Cannot Say (plausible but not stated as fact). Modal verbs (must, may, should, encouraged) carry the legal weight in standards, and this item tests whether the candidate reads them with that precision.

#### Question 5 (hard)

Statement: The Design Authority has required at least one programme to revise its delivery plans.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say)**

**Explanation:** The passage says the Design Authority "may require programmes to revise their delivery plans". That establishes a power, not an event. Whether the power has ever been used is simply not stated, so we cannot say. This is the classic Cannot Say pattern: the text describes what is possible or permitted, and the statement asserts what has actually happened. This item tests the distinction between capability and occurrence, which appears in almost every verbal reasoning assessment and in plenty of real strategy documents too.

#### Passage B - Stakeholder email

Read this email from a delivery manager:

"Hi. Following yesterday's health check, the team flagged two issues. First, the process maps we received last month do not match how caseworkers actually handle exceptions; the maps show exceptions going to a supervisor queue, but in practice caseworkers resolve most exceptions themselves and only escalate complex ones. Second, the team is unclear which version of the target operating model is current, because the shared drive contains three versions with different dates. We would like the business architecture team to confirm the current version by Friday, as our sprint planning depends on it. If we do not hear back by then, we will plan against the March version, which the programme board saw."

#### Question 6 (easy)

Statement: The team raised two issues following the health check.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True)**

**Explanation:** The email states: "Following yesterday's health check, the team flagged two issues." The statement matches directly. The health check detail echoes the Communicating information skill, which includes identifying issues through Agile health checks and working with others to address them; this passage is drawn from the associate business architect's real working world.

#### Question 7 (moderate)

Statement: According to the email, caseworkers escalate all exceptions to a supervisor queue.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The email says the maps show exceptions going to a supervisor queue, "but in practice caseworkers resolve most exceptions themselves and only escalate complex ones". The statement claims all exceptions are escalated, which contradicts the described practice. This item tests whether the candidate answers about reality as the email describes it, not about what the process maps show; the whole point of the email is that the two differ. Distinguishing the documented process from the actual process is a foundational business architecture skill.

#### Question 8 (moderate)

Statement: If the business architecture team does not reply by Friday, the delivery team will stop sprint planning.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The email says the opposite of stopping: "If we do not hear back by then, we will plan against the March version." The stated fallback is to proceed using the March version, not to halt planning. The statement contradicts the text, so it is False. Spotting a stated fallback like this is crucial when managing stakeholder expectations in real life: silence from the business architecture team will not pause the work; it will bake an assumption into it.

#### Question 9 (hard)

Statement: The March version of the target operating model is the current version.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say)**

**Explanation:** The email tells us three versions exist with different dates, that the team is unclear which is current, and that the March version "the programme board saw" will be used as a fallback. None of that establishes which version is actually current. Board visibility is evidence of status, but the email does not state it as fact, and the sender explicitly asks for confirmation, signalling genuine uncertainty. This item resists the pull of plausibility: the March version might well be current, but "might well be" is exactly what Cannot Say is for.

#### Passage C - Strategy extract

Read this extract from a departmental digital strategy:

"Over the next three years the department will consolidate its fourteen customer contact points into a single front door for citizens. This consolidation is expected to reduce failure demand, although the department recognises that some user groups, particularly those with limited digital access, will continue to need assisted routes. Consequently, telephone and face-to-face support will be retained where user research demonstrates need. Funding for the consolidation programme has been approved for the first year only; funding for years two and three is subject to the next spending review. The department aims to reduce overall contact-handling costs by 25% by the end of year three."

#### Question 10 (moderate)

Statement: The department will close all telephone support within three years.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage states that "telephone and face-to-face support will be retained where user research demonstrates need". A commitment to retain telephone support under evidenced need directly contradicts a claim that all telephone support will be closed. Note the connection to public sector values: assisted digital routes protect users with limited digital access, and the strategy text reflects that duty. This item tests whether the candidate treats absolute words like "all", "never", and "every" in a statement as a cue to hunt for a qualification in the text.

#### Question 11 (hard)

Statement: The consolidation programme is fully funded for three years.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The text is explicit: "Funding for the consolidation programme has been approved for the first year only; funding for years two and three is subject to the next spending review." A programme funded for one year with the remainder subject to a future review is not fully funded for three years, so the statement contradicts the passage. Spotting funding conditionality is precisely the kind of risk highlighted when assessing the impact of strategies on the organisation: a three-year commitment resting on one year of funding is a delivery risk worth naming.

#### Question 12 (hard)

Statement: The department will achieve a 25% reduction in contact-handling costs by the end of year three.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say)**

**Explanation:** The passage says the department "aims to reduce overall contact-handling costs by 25%". An aim is an intention, not an achieved or guaranteed outcome. The passage gives no information about whether the aim will be met, so we cannot say. Contrast this with Question 11, where the funding facts were stated outright. Strategy documents mix commitments, aims, expectations, and facts in adjacent sentences; this item tests whether the candidate keeps those categories separate when communicating the strategy onward to stakeholders.

#### Question 13 (moderate) - Multiple choice comprehension

Which of the following best summarises the main point of Passage C?

- A) The department is abandoning non-digital contact channels to save money.
- B) The department plans to consolidate contact points into a single front door while retaining assisted routes where evidence shows need, though funding beyond year one is not yet secured.
- C) The department has already reduced contact-handling costs by 25%.
- D) User research has shown that no users need telephone support.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B captures all three elements of the passage: the consolidation plan, the retained assisted routes based on user research, and the funding conditionality. Option A contradicts the retention commitment. Option C converts a year-three aim into a completed achievement. Option D contradicts the passage's recognition that some user groups will continue to need assisted routes. Summary items like this reward the option that is complete and faithful, not the one that is boldest. This is the same judgement exercised when aggregating complex information and communicating it to stakeholders without distortion.

#### Passage D - Governance meeting minutes

Read this extract from Design Authority minutes:

"Item 4: The Pensions Uplift programme presented its capability impact assessment. The Authority noted that the assessment identified a major change to the Payments capability and moderate changes to two other capabilities. The Authority requested that the programme provide a revised benefits profile within ten working days, and agreed that the programme may continue design work in the meantime. The business architect representing the programme confirmed that the revised profile would draw on figures agreed with Finance. Item 5 was deferred to the next meeting due to time constraints."

#### Question 14 (moderate)

Statement: The Design Authority stopped the Pensions Uplift programme's design work until a revised benefits profile is provided.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The minutes state the Authority "agreed that the programme may continue design work in the meantime". The statement claims design work was stopped, which is a direct contradiction. In governance minutes, the difference between "paused pending X" and "continue while providing X" is enormous for delivery teams, so this item tests exactly the precision stakeholders rely on the business architect for when relaying decisions.

#### Question 15 (hard)

Statement: The revised benefits profile will use figures agreed with Finance.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True)**

**Explanation:** The minutes record that "the business architect representing the programme confirmed that the revised profile would draw on figures agreed with Finance". Within the world of the passage, this confirmation is stated as fact about the intended content of the profile. Answer True. Note that the item asks what follows from the passage; a recorded confirmation in minutes is the passage asserting the plan. Had the statement said "Finance has approved the revised benefits profile", the answer would be Cannot Say, because agreeing figures is not the same as approving the finished profile. Precision about who agreed what, and when, keeps stakeholder relationships healthy.

#### Question 16 (moderate) - Multiple choice comprehension

According to Passage D, why was Item 5 deferred?

- A) The programme failed to attend.
- B) The Authority lacked a quorum.
- C) Time constraints.
- D) The passage does not give a reason.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The final sentence says: "Item 5 was deferred to the next meeting due to time constraints." The reason is stated explicitly, so option C is correct and option D is wrong. Quick factual items like this are easy marks in a timed assessment, provided the candidate scans back to the exact sentence rather than answering from memory of a first read; verifying even "obvious" answers against the text prevents careless errors.

#### Passage E - Consultancy briefing note

Read this extract from a briefing note prepared by a senior business architect:

"The review of the department's operating model options considered three approaches: a functional model organised around specialisms, a service-line model organised around citizen-facing services, and a hybrid model. The review recommends the hybrid model. Under the hybrid model, citizen-facing services would each have a dedicated multidisciplinary team, while scarce specialisms such as security architecture would remain in central pools. The review estimates that the hybrid model would require eighteen months to implement. The functional model was rejected because it scored lowest on responsiveness to user needs. No option was assessed as deliverable in under twelve months."

#### Question 17 (moderate)

Statement: The service-line model was rejected because it scored lowest on responsiveness to user needs.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage attributes that reason to a different option: "The functional model was rejected because it scored lowest on responsiveness to user needs." Since only one option can score lowest, the service-line model cannot also have scored lowest, so the statement contradicts the passage. Swapping an attribute from one named item to another is one of the most frequent tricks in verbal reasoning assessments, and it is defeated by matching each claim to its exact subject in the text. The same discipline protects a business architect at work when summarising options appraisals: attach every finding to the right option, every time.

#### Question 18 (hard)

Statement: The hybrid model can be implemented in under twelve months if additional funding is provided.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states: "No option was assessed as deliverable in under twelve months." The hybrid model is one of the options, and the review's own estimate for it is eighteen months. A claim that it can be implemented in under twelve months contradicts the assessment recorded in the passage. The funding clause is a distraction: the passage says nothing about funding changing any timescale, and adding an unstated condition does not rescue a statement that conflicts with the text as given. When stakeholders float "couldn't we do it faster if...?" questions in real life, the honest answer starts from what the evidence actually says, which is precisely the habit this item rehearses.

#### Question 19 (moderate) - Multiple choice comprehension

According to Passage E, why would scarce specialisms remain in central pools under the hybrid model?

- A) Because central pools scored highest on responsiveness to user needs.
- B) The passage describes this as part of the hybrid model's design but does not state a reason.
- C) Because the spending review required it.
- D) Because the service-line model failed its assessment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says that under the hybrid model "scarce specialisms such as security architecture would remain in central pools", but it never explains why; the word "scarce" hints at a rationale, yet no reason is stated. Option B is therefore correct. Option A misattaches the responsiveness finding, which concerned the rejected functional model. Options C and D invent connections the passage never makes; there is no spending review in Passage E at all, and the service-line model's assessment is not described as failed. This item trains a subtle but important reading skill: noticing the difference between what a document describes and what it justifies. When communicating business architecture frameworks and techniques to others, knowing where the source material is silent is as important as knowing what it says, because those silences are where stakeholders' questions will land.

### Administration tips

- **Decide in advance whether the candidate reads the statement first or the passage first, and apply it consistently.** Under time pressure, scanning for the relevant sentence after reading the statement can be faster than re-reading the whole passage; whichever convention you use, keep it the same for every candidate.
- **Time this section tightly and consistently.** Verbal reasoning items are typically answered in under a minute each; apply the same overall time limit to every candidate.
- **Mark strictly against the text, not the candidate's outside knowledge.** A candidate may know a great deal about government digital work; the assessment specifically tests whether they set that knowledge aside and answer from the passage alone.
- **Watch for absolute-statement traps when reviewing answers.** "All", "never", "only", and "every" in a statement usually signal that the passage contains a qualification the statement ignores; check whether the candidate spotted it.
- **Note whether a candidate reads for the document's world or imports their own.** The strongest candidates keep those separate; if the passage does not say it, it cannot be used to justify an answer, however experienced the candidate is.

### Common pitfalls

- **Crediting an answer built on assumptions rather than the text.** The most experienced candidates are often the most tempted to bring in personal knowledge instead of relying only on the provided text; mark this as incorrect regardless of how plausible the assumption is.
- **Confusing "likely" with "stated" when marking.** A plausible inference (the March version is probably current) is still Cannot Say if the passage does not state it.
- **Confusing False with Cannot Say.** False needs a contradiction; absence of information is Cannot Say. If the candidate cannot point to the contradicting words in a follow-up discussion, the answer should not have been False.
- **Treating an aim or expectation as an accomplished fact.** As in Question 12, this is a specific and common misreading worth checking for explicitly.
- **Allowing insufficient time for scanning versus reading.** A candidate who spends too long reading the whole text in detail before looking at the statements may run out of time; this is a pacing issue, not necessarily a comprehension one, and worth distinguishing when reviewing results.
- **Marking the wrong layer.** When a passage contrasts what a document says with what actually happens (as in Passage B), check carefully which layer the statement, and the candidate's answer, is actually about.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates a candidate's decision-making, interpersonal skills, and professional conduct. Unlike logic or maths tests, it presents realistic workplace dilemmas to see how well a candidate's behavioural choices align with the organisation's values and this role's requirements. For an associate business architect, that means scenarios involving project teams, programme managers, senior stakeholders, subject-matter experts, and the everyday tensions of architecture work: competing priorities, unclear direction, disagreements about approach, and the balance between doing things quickly and doing them well.

Key characteristics of this assessment type:

- **Scenario-based**: each question is a short, hypothetical workplace situation, often a conflict, an ethical wrinkle, or an operational challenge.
- **No single objectively right answer**: several responses may seem reasonable, but some fit the organisation's values and the role's expectations better than others.
- **Response formats**: the candidate is typically asked to pick the Most Effective and Least Effective actions from a set, or to rank or rate all the options from best to worst.
- **Untimed or generously timed**: administer this section without a tight time limit, since it focuses on natural judgement rather than speed.

For UK public sector roles, the values that shape "effective" answers are well known: put user needs first; be open and transparent; collaborate across teams and professions; take accountability at the right level while escalating appropriately; champion accessibility and inclusion; use evidence rather than opinion. At associate level, effective answers also reflect the level: the postholder acts within direction and wider strategy, seeks support when risk and complexity grow, and recommends rather than imposes when decisions exceed their remit. An option in which the candidate unilaterally overrules a senior stakeholder is rarely "effective" at this level; equally, an option of pure passivity ("do nothing and hope") is almost never right either.

### What it measures

Situational judgement maps to this role profile's skills like this:

- **Stakeholder relationship management**: identifying important stakeholders, communicating clearly and regularly, tailoring communication to their needs, building consensus, and using evidence to explain decisions. Many scenarios test whether the candidate keeps stakeholders informed and brings them along rather than surprising them.
- **Communicating information**: working collaboratively, managing stakeholder expectations, being proactive and reactive in communication, and hosting or moderating difficult discussions. Scenarios probe how the candidate handles tension in meetings and workshops.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: making decisions with managed levels of risk and complexity, recommending decisions as risk and complexity increase, and helping resolve disputes considering all views. Scenarios test judgement about when to decide, when to recommend, and when to escalate.
- **Consultancy**: providing advice, guidance, and recommendations based on specialist knowledge, and framing problems so they can be understood. Scenarios test whether the candidate advises honestly even when the message is unwelcome.
- **Business architecture and strategic thinking**: ensuring design work aligns with strategy and highlighting risks and issues. Scenarios test whether the candidate protects strategic alignment without becoming a blocker.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, ask the candidate to give an honest answer before discussing it further. Response formats vary between "most and least effective" and full ranking, matching what a candidate would meet in a real assessment.

#### Question 1 (easy) - The misaligned project

The candidate notices that a project's proposed process design conflicts with the department's target operating model, which the design team seems unaware of. The project's next design review is in two weeks. What should they do?

- A) Say nothing; the design review will probably catch it.
- B) Contact the project's lead designer now, explain the conflict with reference to the target operating model, and offer to walk through it together before the review.
- C) Email the programme director stating the project is non-compliant and should be halted.
- D) Raise it informally with a colleague on their own team and leave it with them.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is proactive, collaborative, and evidence-based: the candidate communicates the issue early, cites the artefact that shows the conflict, and offers help rather than judgement. That is textbook stakeholder relationship management and exactly the "ensure design work aligns with strategy" duty in this role. Option A is the least effective: hoping a later gate catches a known problem wastes two weeks and gambles with delivery; passivity that ignores a root issue is a classic weak answer. Option C escalates disproportionately before talking to the team, damaging relationships and skipping the collaborative step; escalation is for when direct engagement fails or risk is severe. Option D delegates the candidate's insight informally with no ownership, so the issue may simply evaporate.

#### Question 2 (easy) - The jargon problem

After the candidate presents a capability model at a stakeholder meeting, an operations manager says, honestly and a little frustrated: "I did not understand half of that." What should they do?

- A) Suggest they read a business architecture textbook to get up to speed.
- B) Apologise briefly, then re-explain the key points in plain language using an example from their own operational area, and ask them to say where it stops making sense.
- C) Move on quickly to avoid embarrassment and cover the remaining slides.
- D) Repeat the same explanation more slowly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** The role level description calls for the postholder to "explain complex business problems using clear language and artefacts", and the Communicating information skill requires tailoring communication to stakeholders' needs. Option B does both: it adapts language, grounds the model in the listener's world, and invites feedback, turning a stumble into relationship-building. Option A is the least effective because it blames the listener for the presenter's communication choice, which is disrespectful and corrodes the relationship; the obligation to be understood sits with the communicator. Option C ignores the feedback entirely, and option D changes speed but not language, misdiagnosing the problem: the issue is vocabulary and relevance, not pace.

#### Question 3 (moderate) - Conflicting stakeholder demands

Two senior stakeholders give the candidate conflicting instructions about a business design artefact they are producing. The head of operations wants current pain points documented in detail; the programme director wants a future-state-only view and says documenting the current state is a waste of time. Both expect their version this week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Produce the version the programme director wants, since they are more senior in the programme hierarchy.
- B) Produce both versions in secret and show each stakeholder only their own.
- C) Tell both stakeholders about the conflict, propose a short three-way conversation to agree the artefact's purpose and scope, and bring a recommendation with the trade-offs of each approach.
- D) Ask their line manager to decide, and pause work until they respond.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option C is transparent, brings the disagreement into the open, and positions the candidate as a facilitator with a recommendation, which matches the skills of building consensus, managing expectations, and hosting difficult discussions. Bringing "a recommendation with trade-offs" also fits the decision-making level expected here: recommend as complexity increases. Option B is the least effective: maintaining two secret versions is dishonest, unsustainable, and would destroy trust the moment it surfaced; transparency is a core public sector value. Option A resolves the conflict by seniority rather than by purpose, leaving the operations head ignored and the underlying disagreement alive. Option D is not wrong to involve a manager, but pausing all work while delegating the whole problem upward is passive for a conflict the candidate could convene themselves; the stronger pattern is to keep a manager informed rather than hand them the task.

#### Question 4 (moderate) - The optimistic report

A programme manager asks the candidate to soften language in their impact assessment: their draft flags a major risk that the benefits case depends on a system integration that has not been scoped. They say: "Just call it an assumption; a red risk this late looks bad at the board." What should the candidate do?

- A) Make the change; the programme manager owns the report.
- B) Refuse loudly in the team channel so everyone can see the pressure being applied.
- C) Explain why the distinction matters, propose wording that is professionally accurate but constructive (a clearly stated risk with a mitigation plan), and if the disagreement persists, flag it to their senior business architect for guidance.
- D) Make the change but keep the original draft privately for later protection.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A (with D close behind).**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests integrity under pressure, central to consultancy: "provide advice, guidance and recommendations based on your specialist knowledge". Option C holds the professional line while staying constructive: the candidate explains the reasoning, offers wording that is honest and palatable, and escalates for guidance if needed, which is exactly right at associate level where support is part of how the role works. Option A is least effective because mislabelling a known risk as an assumption misleads the board, undermines transparent decision-making, and could compromise the programme; agreeing quietly makes the candidate complicit. Option D performs the same harm while protecting only the candidate, which fails both the organisation and their own integrity. Option B protects the truth but chooses a public, confrontational channel that humiliates a colleague and damages the relationship; the goal is honest reporting, not visible victory.

#### Question 5 (moderate) - The health check finding

During an Agile health check the candidate helps facilitate, a delivery team privately tells them their velocity figures are being inflated before they reach the programme dashboard, because "leadership only wants green". What is the most effective first action?

- A) Report the team to programme leadership immediately, naming the individuals who raised it.
- B) Do nothing; dashboards are not an architecture concern.
- C) Discuss with the team how the inflated figures distort decisions, encourage them to correct the data, and agree how the issue can be raised safely, offering to support them in raising it through the health check findings.
- D) Quietly correct the dashboard figures personally using the numbers the team provided.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** The Communicating information skill explicitly includes identifying issues through Agile health checks "and working with others to address them". Option C does exactly that: it treats the team as partners, protects the trust they showed the candidate, and channels the issue through the legitimate health check route so leadership hears it with context. Option B is least effective: distorted data feeding governance decisions is everyone's concern, and a business architect who brings together information to inform decision making cannot shrug at corrupted information. Option A punishes candour and would teach every team to hide problems from health checks, making the organisation less transparent. Option D fixes one number while leaving the culture that inflated it untouched, exceeds the candidate's authority over another team's reporting, and does it covertly, which compounds the transparency problem it tries to solve.

#### Question 6 (moderate) - Ranking: the workshop that turns hostile

The candidate is moderating a process-mapping workshop. A senior operations stakeholder repeatedly interrupts a junior caseworker who is describing how exceptions are really handled, saying: "That is not our process. We should not document workarounds." The room goes quiet. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Acknowledge both views: explain that documenting actual practice alongside the designed process is standard method, because the gap itself is valuable information, then invite the caseworker to continue.
2. Move to the next agenda item to defuse the tension.
3. Privately decide the senior stakeholder is right and stop capturing the caseworker's input.
4. Ask the senior stakeholder to leave the workshop.

**Correct ranking: 1, 2, 3, 4.**

**Explanation:** Response 1 is the strongest: it moderates the difficult moment (a named behaviour in the Communicating information skill), protects the junior voice, and grounds the resolution in method rather than personality: the documented-versus-actual gap is core business architecture evidence, as Passage B in the verbal section illustrated. Response 2 is weak but not catastrophic: it avoids escalation yet loses the data and signals that interruption works. Response 3 is worse: it silently rewards seniority over evidence and discards exactly the information the workshop existed to capture. Response 4 is the most damaging: publicly ejecting a senior stakeholder humiliates them, poisons a relationship the candidate must maintain for years, and exceeds the facilitator's proportionate options at this level. Notice the pattern in the ranking: engage with the substance first, avoid harm second, and relationship-destroying moves last.

#### Question 7 (moderate) - The deadline versus quality dilemma

A programme needs the candidate's capability impact assessment by Friday for a funding decision. On Wednesday they discover that a whole business area's data is missing from their analysis, and getting it properly would take another week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Deliver on Friday without mentioning the gap; the missing area is probably similar to the others.
- B) Miss the deadline and deliver the complete assessment the following week without warning anyone in advance.
- C) Tell the programme manager now: explain the gap, deliver on Friday with the missing area clearly marked as not yet assessed, state what could change when the data arrives, and agree a date for the completed version.
- D) Work through the weekend to invent reasonable estimates for the missing area so the assessment looks complete.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A (with D equally poor).**

**Explanation:** Option C manages stakeholder expectations proactively (a named element of the Communicating information skill), preserves the funding decision's timeline, and is transparent about confidence levels: decision-makers get a usable product and know exactly where its edges are. Options A and D both present incomplete or fabricated analysis as complete, which corrupts a funding decision; A hides the gap and D fills it with invention dressed as data. In public sector work, decisions must rest on honest evidence, and "clearly marked limitations" is always more professional than false completeness. Option B protects quality but sacrifices the decision itself and blindsides the programme, failing the proactive-communication test; deadlines that cannot be fully met are managed by early conversation, not silent slippage.

#### Question 8 (hard) - The scope-creep request

A well-liked service owner asks the candidate, as a favour, to add a recommendation to their consultancy report endorsing a tool their team has already decided to buy. The analysis did not evaluate that tool. They say: "It would really help us get sign-off, and you would be our hero." What should the candidate do?

- A) Add the endorsement; it maintains a valuable relationship and the tool might be fine.
- B) Decline to endorse what has not been evaluated, explain why the report's credibility depends on that boundary, and offer a genuine alternative: a quick, honest evaluation of the tool against the criteria the report already uses.
- C) Add the endorsement but phrase it vaguely enough to deny later that it meant approval.
- D) Report the service owner to their director for attempting to influence the report.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A (with C close behind).**

**Explanation:** This is a consultancy-integrity scenario. Advice must be "based on your specialist knowledge and experience"; endorsing an unevaluated tool is advice based on friendship, which quietly converts professional judgement into a favour bank. Option B declines the improper part while strengthening the relationship through real help, and keeps the door open for the tool to earn a genuine endorsement: firm on the boundary, generous in the alternative. Option A trades long-term credibility for short-term warmth; the first time an unevaluated endorsement goes wrong, every past and future report the candidate writes is discounted. Option C is worse in character: deliberately ambiguous wording is dishonesty with an escape hatch. Option D treats a common, probably naive request as misconduct, escalating past the person instead of talking to them; proportionality matters, and most boundary-setting happens perfectly well in conversation.

#### Question 9 (hard) - The disputed method

A solution architect and a service designer on the candidate's project are in a standoff. The solution architect insists the future process must follow the constraints of the chosen platform; the service designer insists the process must follow user research findings, platform be damned. Delivery is stalling, and both have asked the candidate to back them. What is the most effective response?

- A) Back the service designer; user needs always win in government.
- B) Back the solution architect; the platform decision was already made and constraints are constraints.
- C) Facilitate a working session in which both constraint sets are laid out against the actual process steps, identify which steps genuinely conflict, and bring any remaining irreconcilable points as a framed recommendation to the project's decision-maker.
- D) Tell them both to sort it out between themselves; it is not the candidate's role to referee.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The Making and guiding decisions skill includes helping resolve technical disputes between peers "considering all views and opinions", and the consultancy skill is about framing problems so they can be understood. Option C does the architect's real job: it converts a personality standoff into an evidence question, usually revealing that only a small subset of steps truly conflict, and it recommends rather than rules on what remains, which is right at associate level. Option A misuses a true principle: user needs are central in government, but "always win, platform be damned" ignores legitimate delivery constraints, and slogans are not analysis. Option B repeats the error in the other direction. Option D is least effective: the dispute is stalling delivery and both parties have invited the candidate in; declining to structure the problem when structuring problems is the profession is passivity at its purest.

#### Question 10 (hard) - The strategy contradiction

While mapping initiatives to strategic goals, the candidate finds that a flagship programme, sponsored by their director's own director, no longer aligns with the refreshed departmental strategy: the goal it served was dropped in the refresh. Nobody seems to have noticed. What should they do?

- A) Leave it out of the mapping; flagging it could embarrass senior people.
- B) Include the finding accurately in the mapping, brief their senior business architect first with the evidence, and let them guide how it is raised through governance.
- C) Email the sponsoring director-general directly to inform them their programme is now unaligned.
- D) Post the finding in the all-staff channel in the interests of transparency.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** The core duty here is "ensure design work from projects and programmes aligns with strategy", and hiding a misalignment because it is politically awkward (option A) is the least effective choice: it falsifies the candidate's artefact by omission and defeats the entire purpose of strategic mapping. Option B is right for this level: the finding stays accurate and evidenced, and the routing goes through a senior business architect, who has the standing and context to raise it well; "recommend as risk and complexity increase" includes political complexity. Option C is honest but reckless about channels: skipping several organisational layers with unwelcome news, alone, as an associate, sets the message up to fail. Option D confuses transparency with broadcast; transparency in governance means the right decision-makers see accurate information, not that sensitive findings bypass governance entirely.

#### Question 11 (hard) - The accessibility trade-off

A project the candidate supports wants to launch a staff-facing tool without completing accessibility fixes, arguing: "Only two people in the pilot group use screen readers, and we will fix it in phase two." The project asks the candidate to reflect this plan in the business design as agreed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reflect the plan as requested; two users is a small impact and phase two is a real commitment.
- B) Advise the project that accessibility is a legal requirement, not a preference to trade off; recommend the fixes be completed before launch or an interim assisted route be provided, and record the risk clearly in the design if the project proceeds anyway.
- C) Refuse to work with the project until they comply.
- D) Reflect the plan but anonymously alert the two affected users so they can complain.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Accessibility in UK public sector services is a legal obligation under equality law and a core value of the profession, and it applies to staff-facing tools, not just citizen services. Option B gives correct specialist advice, offers a practical path (fix first, or interim assisted route), and, crucially, does what architects do when a project proceeds against advice: record the risk clearly and honestly. Option A is least effective because it treats excluding two named colleagues as an acceptable cost and launders that decision through the design document; "we will fix it in phase two" is the most broken promise in software. Option C abandons influence entirely: walking away removes the one person documenting the risk. Option D is passive-aggressive: engineering a complaint instead of giving straight advice corrodes trust in every direction. This item, like most inclusion questions in situational judgement assessments, rewards a candidate who treats accessibility as non-negotiable while remaining constructive about how compliance is achieved.

#### Question 12 (moderate) - The knowledge gap

In a stakeholder meeting, a finance lead asks the candidate a detailed question about the costing methodology behind a target operating model. They do not know the answer; the methodology was built by a senior colleague who is on leave. What should they do?

- A) Improvise a plausible-sounding answer; admitting uncertainty looks weak.
- B) Say they do not know, commit to finding out, give a specific date for the answer, and follow up on time.
- C) Say it is not their area and suggest they wait until the colleague returns from leave in three weeks.
- D) Deflect the question by asking the finance lead what they think.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is honest, keeps ownership of the follow-up, and turns a gap into a demonstration of reliability, which is how trust with stakeholders is actually built: "communicate with them clearly and regularly" includes being clear about what is not yet known. At associate level, a candidate is expected to still be learning; nobody credible penalises "I will find out by Thursday". Option A is least effective and genuinely dangerous: an improvised costing answer to a finance lead can propagate into budgets and decisions, and being caught improvising once discounts everything said afterwards. Option C is honest but abdicates: three weeks of stakeholder limbo for a question that likely has a documented answer fails the proactive test. Option D as a rhetorical dodge fools no one; used sincerely, asking their view can be useful, but not as a substitute for eventually answering.

### Administration tips

- **Do not impose a tight time limit, but keep whatever time allowance you offer consistent across candidates.** Situational judgement items measure natural judgement, not speed.
- **Ask the candidate to explain their ranking or their most/least choice, not just state it.** Recommending a course of action and describing the reasoning behind it is the named skill this section tests, so probe for the "why" as well as the "what".
- **Present every option before asking for a judgement.** Most/least and ranking items are comparative; reading only part of the option set before answering will distort the result.
- **Calibrate to associate level when scoring.** Strong answers at this level usually involve proactive engagement, transparency about problems, a constructive path rather than a bare refusal, and decisions kept at the right level: act, recommend, or escalate according to risk.
- **Watch for a pattern across scenarios, not just one answer.** A single strong or weak answer is less informative than a consistent pattern across several scenarios.

### Common pitfalls

- **Rewarding idealistic-sounding answers over practical ones.** "Convene a full stakeholder summit" sounds noble, but for a two-person disagreement it is overkill; effective means proportionate, and this should be marked accordingly.
- **Under-weighting passive options as merely "safe".** Choices that delay action, pass the problem wholesale to someone else, or ignore the root issue are consistently rated weakest, as in Questions 1, 5, and 9.
- **Discounting the organisation's culture and values.** In UK government digital work, transparency, user needs, accessibility, and collaboration are not decoration; options that sacrifice them for speed or comfort should be marked down, as in Questions 4, 7, and 11.
- **Confusing escalation with communication.** Escalating can be right, but escalating first, publicly, or past the person concerned usually is not; the direct, respectful conversation almost always comes first, and a candidate's answer should reflect that ordering.
- **Rewarding deference to seniority as if it were sound process.** Choosing options that please the most senior person in the scenario, as in Question 3, misreads what is being measured: the assessment rewards sound process, not deference.
- **Forgetting the level being assessed.** At associate level, the postholder acts within direction, recommends as complexity grows, and uses the support around them. An answer in which the candidate unilaterally decides matters beyond their remit is a trap worth marking down, even when their instinct about the substance is right.

## Conclusion

You have now worked through four full practice sections built specifically around associate business architect, exercising the skills the role profile names throughout. In the cognitive section, items trace dependencies, check artefacts for rule breaches, and decompose vague problems into structured ones. In the numeric section, items handle budgets, unit costs, ratios, projections, and the ever-tricky difference between percentages and percentage points. In the verbal section, items practise the discipline of reading standards, emails, strategies, and minutes exactly as written, keeping facts, aims, and assumptions in separate boxes. In the situational judgement section, items rehearse the professional judgement that holds it all together: transparency under pressure, proportionate escalation, accessibility as a non-negotiable, and the associate-level rhythm of act, recommend, escalate.

To score the assessment, work back through each section against the answer key, and note not just whether the candidate reached the correct answer but whether their explanation shows the reasoning each item was built to test - several explanations above flag exactly what a strong versus a weak answer demonstrates. Recording strengths and gaps by skill area is often more useful to a hiring or development decision than a single overall score.

When debriefing the candidate, give an honest and constructive account of how they did and what it suggests about their readiness for the role, without necessarily working through every question and correct answer, since this material may be reused with future candidates. Where useful, connect the debrief to real opportunities: facilitating a workshop, drafting an options appraisal, presenting an impact assessment, so a candidate who is not yet ready leaves with a concrete sense of what would help.

Keep a record of how and when you administered the assessment, and administer it consistently across every candidate for this role level: the same items, the same time limits, the same conditions, and the same standard when scoring. This is what makes a comparison between candidates fair, and what protects your organisation if a decision is ever challenged. Remember throughout that this is one input among several in a hiring or promotion decision, never the sole basis for one - see the advisory above.

For further support, talk to your organisation's human resources or people development colleagues about how this fits into your wider selection process, and consider asking an experienced business architect to sense-check whether the scenarios and standards used here reflect how the role is actually practised locally.
