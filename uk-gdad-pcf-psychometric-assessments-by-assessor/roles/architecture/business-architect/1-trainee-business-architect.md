# Business Architect (Trainee Business Architect) - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor evaluating a candidate for trainee business architect in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are a line manager, a panel member, or a reviewer, this guide gives you everything you need to administer a fair, consistent, job-specific psychometric assessment for this role level.

First, the obvious question: what is a psychometric assessment, and why use one here? Put simply, it is a structured, standardised way of measuring how a candidate thinks, reasons, and makes decisions at work. Rather than asking about qualifications or experience alone, it gives the candidate realistic tasks - a table of numbers to interpret, a passage of policy text to read, a tricky workplace situation to judge - and looks at how they handle them. Used well, alongside interviews, references, and a review of experience, this kind of assessment can add a fair, consistent data point to a hiring or promotion decision.

Why does this matter for this particular role level? A trainee business architect's day-to-day work involves developing their knowledge of business architecture frameworks, supporting senior architects to produce clear outputs, and helping during workshops and meetings with stakeholders. That means the role calls for spotting patterns and errors, working with figures and percentages, reading dense documents carefully, and using good judgement with colleagues and stakeholders. The four assessments in this guide map directly onto those abilities.

This document is organised into four main assessment sections, each following the same structure:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** - problem solving, pattern spotting, error checking, and prioritisation using the kinds of artefacts a trainee business architect really handles, such as capability maps and workshop plans.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** - tables, percentages, ratios, and budgets drawn from realistic public sector business architecture work.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** - careful reading of policies, standards, emails, and reports, with True / False / Cannot Say questions.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** - realistic workplace dilemmas involving senior architects, stakeholders, and multidisciplinary teams.

Each section explains what the assessment measures, why it matters for this role, and then gives a full set of items with worked answers and explanations. Those worked answers and explanations are your answer key: hold them back from the candidate until after the assessment, and use them only when scoring and preparing for the debrief.

Administer this fairly. Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time limit - do not vary the questions, the time allowed, or the level of hinting between candidates, even informally. Brief every candidate the same way before you start, and keep the same conditions (room, equipment, breaks) as far as you reasonably can. After scoring, debrief the candidate: explain broadly how they did and what it showed, without necessarily walking through every question and answer, since some or all of this material may be reused with future candidates.

There is no pass or fail mark built into this guide - that is a judgement for you and your organisation to set, informed by the role's requirements. Use this material as one input among several. It is not a validated instrument, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision - see the advisory below.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes a candidate uses every day: spotting patterns, reasoning logically, checking for errors, prioritising tasks, and solving problems. Unlike a generic intelligence test, a job-specific version uses practical workplace scenarios and data that mimic the actual role. For a trainee business architect, that means questions built around capability maps, process models, workshop plans, stakeholder lists, and strategy documents - the artefacts a trainee business architect genuinely handles.

Administer this section as a timed exercise, typically 15 to 30 minutes for the sample below, with the same limit for every candidate. Score it objectively against the answer key rather than by impression, and consider recording both speed and accuracy separately, since working carefully matters as much as working quickly for this role. If you use an online platform, offer the same short, ungraded warm-up questions to every candidate so nobody is disadvantaged by unfamiliarity with the format.

Reassure the candidate before they start that this is not a school test: it rewards careful reading, checking work, and thinking step by step - exactly the habits a trainee business architect needs to build.

### What it measures

Here is how the dimensions of a cognitive assessment map to the specific skills in this role profile:

- **Business architecture**: awareness of frameworks, principles, and techniques is tested through pattern recognition and classification questions - for example, sorting artefacts into the right framework layer, or spotting an item that does not belong in a capability map.
- **Communicating information**: listening to stakeholder needs and interpreting information is tested through questions where the candidate must extract the essential point from mixed or messy inputs, such as workshop notes.
- **Consultancy**: framing problems that need to be solved is tested through logical deduction questions - working out what must be true, what cannot be true, and what is still unknown.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: recommending decisions and describing the reasoning behind them maps to prioritisation and problem-solving questions, where the candidate must choose the best next step and understand why.
- **Methods and tools**: following advice to apply the most appropriate tools maps to sequencing and process-logic questions - putting steps in the right order and spotting where a process breaks.
- **Strategic thinking**: explaining the strategic context of work maps to questions that ask the candidate to connect a detailed task to a bigger goal, or to check whether a project design aligns with a stated strategy.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate in order, allowing them to work through each one before moving on. Difficulty is labelled so you can gauge progression through the set; keep the labels visible to yourself for scoring purposes, but they need not be shared with the candidate.

**Question 1 (easy) - Pattern recognition in capability codes**

An organisation labels business capabilities with codes. The candidate notices this sequence in the capability model they are helping to update:

CAP-FIN-01, CAP-FIN-02, CAP-FIN-03, CAP-HRM-01, CAP-HRM-02, CAP-HRM-03, CAP-DIG-01, CAP-DIG-02, ?

What is the next code in the sequence?

- A) CAP-DIG-03
- B) CAP-FIN-04
- C) CAP-DIG-04
- D) CAP-HRM-04

**Correct answer: A) CAP-DIG-03**

**Explanation:** The pattern groups capabilities by business area (FIN for finance, HRM for human resources, DIG for digital), and each area runs through numbers 01, 02, 03 before the next area begins. The DIG group has reached 02, so the next code continues that group: CAP-DIG-03. This item tests whether the candidate can spot the structure behind a labelling convention - a real skill when maintaining capability models, because using the wrong code creates confusion for everyone who reads the model later.

**Question 2 (easy) - Odd one out**

A senior business architect asks the candidate to gather examples of business architecture outputs for a training pack. Which one of the following is NOT a typical business architecture output?

- A) A business capability map
- B) A value stream diagram
- C) An operating model description
- D) A database index tuning script

**Correct answer: D) A database index tuning script**

**Explanation:** Capability maps, value stream diagrams, and operating model descriptions are all classic business architecture artefacts - they describe what the organisation does and how it creates value. A database index tuning script is a low-level technical artefact belonging to database administration, not business architecture. This classification question checks a candidate's growing awareness of business architecture frameworks, principles, and techniques - one of the named skills at this level. A trainee who can tell "business layer" artefacts from "technology layer" artefacts is well placed to file, reference, and discuss documents correctly.

**Question 3 (easy) - Simple logical deduction**

A department has a rule: "Every project design document must be reviewed for strategic alignment before it goes to the design authority board." The candidate is told the following facts:

- Project Swift's design document went to the design authority board last week.
- Project Heron's design document has not yet been reviewed for strategic alignment.

Which statement must be true?

- A) Project Heron's design document has already gone to the board.
- B) Project Swift's design document was reviewed for strategic alignment.
- C) Project Heron's design will fail its review.
- D) Project Swift's design is strategically aligned.

**Correct answer: B) Project Swift's design document was reviewed for strategic alignment.**

**Explanation:** The rule says review must happen before the board. Since Swift's document reached the board, the review must have happened first - so B must be true. The traps: A contradicts the rule (Heron cannot have gone to the board without review). C predicts the future, which the facts do not support. D is tempting, but "was reviewed for alignment" is not the same as "is aligned" - a document can be reviewed and found wanting. This distinction between what is stated and what is assumed is central to consultancy-style problem framing, and a candidate who separates the two cleanly is showing exactly that discipline.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Error checking a stakeholder list**

The candidate is supporting a senior architect by checking the stakeholder register for a workshop. The master list says the workshop needs: 2 attendees from Finance, 3 from Operations, 1 from Digital Services, and 2 from Policy. The draft invitation list reads:

1. A. Okafor - Finance
2. B. Singh - Operations
3. C. Murray - Operations
4. D. Evans - Digital Services
5. E. Nowak - Policy
6. F. Ahmed - Finance
7. G. Taylor - Policy
8. H. Li - Digital Services

What is wrong with the draft list?

- A) There is one Finance attendee too many.
- B) There is one Operations attendee missing and one Digital Services attendee too many.
- C) There is one Policy attendee missing.
- D) Nothing is wrong; the list matches the master requirements.

**Correct answer: B) There is one Operations attendee missing and one Digital Services attendee too many.**

**Explanation:** Count each group carefully. Finance: Okafor and Ahmed = 2 (correct). Operations: Singh and Murray = 2, but 3 are required (one missing). Digital Services: Evans and Li = 2, but only 1 is required (one too many). Policy: Nowak and Taylor = 2 (correct). So the list has an Operations shortfall and a Digital Services surplus - answer B. Error checking like this is genuinely part of the role: supporting workshops means making sure the right stakeholders are in the room, and a miscounted invitation list can derail a session before it starts. A candidate who tallies each category methodically, rather than eyeballing the list, is demonstrating the habit this item is built to test.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Sequencing a workshop process**

A senior architect gives the candidate these five workshop preparation steps, out of order:

1. Send the agenda and pre-reading to confirmed attendees.
2. Confirm the workshop objective with the sponsoring stakeholder.
3. Book a room with accessibility provisions and set up the video call link.
4. Draft the agenda based on the confirmed objective.
5. Collect attendee availability and confirm the date.

Which order is most logical?

- A) 2, 4, 5, 3, 1
- B) 5, 2, 4, 1, 3
- C) 2, 5, 4, 3, 1
- D) 4, 2, 5, 1, 3

**Correct answer: A) 2, 4, 5, 3, 1**

**Explanation:** Work through the dependencies. The agenda (4) cannot be drafted until the objective is confirmed (2), so 2 comes before 4 - this eliminates D. The agenda cannot be sent to confirmed attendees (1) until both the agenda exists (4) and the date is confirmed (5). Booking the room (3) needs a confirmed date, so 3 follows 5. Option C is a workable order too - it drafts the agenda after collecting availability, which is not wrong in itself - but option A is stronger because the agenda (4) can be drafted immediately after the objective (2) without waiting for availability, and the room booking (3) rightly follows date confirmation (5), with sending (1) last once everything is fixed. Both A and C place 2 first and 1 last; A is preferred because drafting the agenda before chasing availability means the pre-reading is ready as soon as the date lands, avoiding delay. Sequencing items like this reward thinking about what each step needs as an input - exactly the "methods and tools" discipline of applying the right technique at the right point in planning, and a strong candidate's answer will show that reasoning even without prompting.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Strategic alignment logic**

An organisation's published strategy has three priorities: (P1) improve citizen self-service, (P2) reduce duplicated back-office systems, and (P3) improve data quality for decision making. The candidate is asked to do a first-pass check on four project proposals and flag any that do not clearly support at least one priority:

- Project Alpha: replace three overlapping case-management systems with one shared platform.
- Project Beta: redecorate the ground-floor meeting rooms.
- Project Gamma: build an online portal so citizens can update their own contact details.
- Project Delta: introduce a single data dictionary across departments.

Which project(s) should be flagged?

- A) Beta only
- B) Beta and Delta
- C) Gamma only
- D) Alpha and Delta

**Correct answer: A) Beta only**

**Explanation:** Match each project to the priorities. Alpha consolidates duplicated systems - that is P2. Gamma enables citizen self-service - that is P1. Delta improves consistency and quality of data definitions - that is P3. Beta, redecorating meeting rooms, may be a perfectly reasonable facilities task, but it does not clearly support P1, P2, or P3, so it is the one to flag. A strong candidate's answer will note that flagging does not mean the project is bad - it means it needs a strategic justification or a different funding route. This is a simplified version of a real trainee task: ensuring design work from projects and programmes aligns with strategy is one of the core duties of the profession.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Aggregating messy information**

Three teams describe the same activity in their process documents:

- Team 1 calls it "Verify applicant identity".
- Team 2 calls it "ID checking".
- Team 3 calls it "Customer identity validation (step 4)".

A senior architect asks the candidate to consolidate the three process documents into one capability view. What is the most sensible first move?

- A) Keep all three names so no team is upset.
- B) Treat them as three different capabilities because the names differ.
- C) Confirm with the teams that the three names describe the same activity, then record it once under a single agreed name with the variants noted.
- D) Pick Team 1's name because they wrote their document first.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Aggregating and disaggregating complex information from across the organisation is a core business architect duty, and the first rule is: same thing, one name. Option B creates artificial duplication - exactly what a capability view exists to remove. Option A defeats the purpose of consolidation. Option D guesses instead of verifying, and "first written" is not evidence of "best name". Option C does two important things: it checks the assumption with the people who know (the teams), and it preserves traceability by noting the variant names. At trainee level this would be done under guidance, but the reasoning pattern - verify, unify, keep the audit trail - is one this item is testing early.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Prioritisation under time pressure**

It is 09:15 on Tuesday. The candidate has four tasks:

1. Print and lay out materials for a stakeholder workshop that starts at 10:00 in the building.
2. Reply to an email from a peer asking for last month's workshop notes, needed "sometime this week".
3. Finish the first draft of a capability description due to their senior architect by the end of Thursday.
4. Re-book a meeting room for next Monday's team catch-up because of a double booking.

What is the most sensible order to tackle them?

- A) 3, 1, 4, 2
- B) 1, 4, 3, 2
- C) 1, 3, 4, 2
- D) 2, 1, 3, 4

**Correct answer: C) 1, 3, 4, 2**

**Explanation:** Task 1 is urgent and immovable - the workshop starts in 45 minutes and materials must be ready, so it comes first. After that, compare the rest by deadline and effort. Task 3 has the nearest hard deadline (Thursday) and the largest effort, so it deserves the next block of focused time. Task 4 matters before Monday, but re-booking a room is quick and can slot in as a short break from drafting. Task 2 is explicitly flexible ("sometime this week") and quick, so it can safely come last. Option B puts the quick room re-booking ahead of the substantial Thursday draft, which risks nibbling away focused time; option A misses the workshop; option D answers a low-urgency email while the clock runs down on the workshop. Prioritisation items like this test the judgement behind "recommend decisions and describe the reasoning behind them" - a strong candidate's answer will be ready to say why their order is right, not just state it.

**Question 9 (moderate) - Spotting an inconsistency in a capability model**

The candidate is proofreading a capability model summary before it goes to stakeholders. It contains these four statements:

1. "The organisation has 12 top-level capabilities."
2. "Each top-level capability contains exactly 3 sub-capabilities."
3. "The model contains 38 sub-capabilities in total."
4. "Every sub-capability belongs to exactly one top-level capability."

Which pair of statements cannot both be true?

- A) Statements 1 and 2
- B) Statements 2 and 3
- C) Statements 1 and 4
- D) Statements 3 and 4

**Correct answer: B) Statements 2 and 3**

**Explanation:** If there are 12 top-level capabilities (statement 1) and each contains exactly 3 sub-capabilities (statement 2), and every sub-capability belongs to exactly one parent (statement 4), then the total must be 12 x 3 = 36 sub-capabilities. Statement 3 claims 38, which contradicts that total. Strictly, the contradiction involves statements 1, 2, 3, and 4 together, but among the offered pairs, 2 and 3 is the clash the item targets: "exactly 3 each" cannot produce a total of 38 from 12 parents. Checking internal consistency before documents reach stakeholders is a classic trainee support task - a stakeholder who spots the arithmetic error will doubt the rest of the model too, so this item rewards a candidate who checks the arithmetic rather than skimming the prose.

**Question 10 (hard) - Multi-step logical deduction**

Four programmes (P, Q, R, S) each align to exactly one strategic priority: citizen services, data quality, or system consolidation. The candidate is told:

- Exactly two programmes align to citizen services.
- P and Q align to different priorities.
- R aligns to data quality.
- S aligns to citizen services.

Which of the following must be true?

- A) P aligns to citizen services.
- B) Q aligns to system consolidation.
- C) Exactly one of P and Q aligns to citizen services.
- D) No programme aligns to system consolidation.

**Correct answer: C) Exactly one of P and Q aligns to citizen services.**

**Explanation:** Work it through step by step. R takes data quality and S takes citizen services. Exactly two programmes align to citizen services, and S is one of them - so exactly one more programme from {P, Q} must be citizen services. P and Q align to different priorities, which is consistent with exactly one of them taking citizen services (the other takes data quality or system consolidation). So C must be true. A is only possibly true (it could be Q instead). B is only possibly true (the non-citizen-services one of P and Q could take data quality). D is possibly false for the same reason. The skill here is separating "must be true" from "could be true" - the same discipline needed when aggregating claims from different parts of the organisation and stating only what the evidence supports. A candidate who can articulate that distinction, not just pick C, is showing the strongest form of the skill.

**Question 11 (hard) - Process fault diagnosis**

A team's document approval workflow is: Draft -> Peer review -> Senior architect review -> Publish to the repository. A stakeholder reports that a published operating model description contains a section that the senior architect never saw. Assuming everyone followed the workflow as they understood it, which explanation is most plausible?

- A) The peer reviewer deleted the section before senior review.
- B) The section was added after senior architect review but before publication.
- C) The senior architect reviewed the section but forgot.
- D) The document was never peer reviewed.

**Correct answer: B) The section was added after senior architect review but before publication.**

**Explanation:** Diagnose by matching the symptom to the workflow. The symptom is: published content the senior architect never saw. Option A would result in the section being absent from the published version, not present - it explains the opposite symptom. Option C contradicts the premise that the architect never saw it (and "forgot" is an assumption about memory, not a workflow fault). Option D would not by itself explain the senior architect missing content - senior review happens regardless of whether peer review occurred. Option B fits exactly: an edit inserted between the final review and publication is the only step where unseen content can enter a published document. The practical lesson for this role: version control between sign-off and publication matters, and workflows need a "no edits after final review" rule. Fault-diagnosis items like this reward tracing each option's consequences and comparing them with the observed symptom, and a strong candidate's answer will show that trace rather than jumping to a guess.

**Question 12 (hard) - Connecting detail to strategy**

An organisation's strategy says: "We will reduce the cost of back-office operations so that more funding reaches frontline services." The candidate is helping map current projects to this strategy. A project proposes to automate invoice processing, which will cost 200,000 pounds to build and is forecast to save 80,000 pounds per year in staff time that can be redeployed to frontline casework. A stakeholder argues the project does not support the strategy "because it costs money rather than saving it."

Which is the strongest response to evaluate the stakeholder's claim?

- A) The stakeholder is right: any project with an upfront cost contradicts a cost-reduction strategy.
- B) The stakeholder is wrong: the project supports the strategy because recurring savings exceed the one-off cost within three years, and the savings are redeployed to the frontline, which is the strategy's stated purpose.
- C) The stakeholder is right: 80,000 pounds is less than 200,000 pounds.
- D) The stakeholder is wrong: all automation projects always support all cost-reduction strategies.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strategy has two parts: reduce back-office cost, and shift funding to the frontline. The project's recurring saving of 80,000 pounds per year passes the one-off cost of 200,000 pounds during the third year (80,000 x 3 = 240,000), and the freed staff time goes to frontline casework - the exact outcome the strategy names. Option A confuses one-off investment with ongoing cost; strategies to reduce operating cost routinely require upfront spending. Option C compares a yearly figure with a one-off figure as if they were the same kind of number - a category error. Option D is right by accident but wrong in reasoning: "always" claims are almost never defensible, and this item is testing the candidate's reasoning, not just their conclusion. Explaining the strategic context of a piece of work, clearly and fairly, is a named expectation of the strategic thinking skill at this level, and a strong candidate's answer will show the working, not just select B.

**Question 13 (hard) - Interpreting workshop notes**

During a workshop the candidate took these raw notes: "Ops say handoffs to Finance slow - approx 5 days. Finance say they wait on missing PO numbers from Ops. Digital say new form could capture PO at source. Policy: no objection. Sponsor wants fix before year end."

A senior architect asks: "What is the root cause suggested by the notes, and what is the proposed remedy?" Which summary is most accurate?

- A) Root cause: Finance is slow. Remedy: give Finance more staff before year end.
- B) Root cause: handoffs from Operations arrive without PO numbers, forcing Finance to wait. Remedy: a new digital form capturing the PO number at source, to be delivered before year end.
- C) Root cause: Policy objected to the process. Remedy: escalate to the sponsor.
- D) Root cause: the digital form is broken. Remedy: Operations should stop sending handoffs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Follow the chain in the notes: the delay appears at the Ops-to-Finance handoff; Finance attributes their waiting to missing PO numbers from Ops; Digital proposes capturing the PO number at source via a new form; Policy raises no objection; the sponsor sets the deadline. Option B reproduces that chain faithfully. Option A blames Finance, but the notes say Finance is waiting, not slow - a subtle but crucial difference. Option C inverts the Policy note ("no objection" is the opposite of an objection). Option D invents a broken form; the form does not exist yet. Turning messy, abbreviated notes into an accurate cause-and-remedy summary is one of the most valuable services a trainee provides in workshops, and misreading who is waiting on whom can sour relationships between teams - this item tests exactly that precision.

### Administration tips

- **Have the candidate's own kind of artefact on hand for context, not for the test itself.** If it helps orient a nervous candidate, a brief warm-up conversation about a capability map or process diagram can settle nerves before the timed section begins - but keep the timed items identical for every candidate.
- **Watch for "must be true" versus "could be true" confusion.** Candidates most often lose marks by choosing something that is merely plausible. When reviewing responses, note whether the candidate's stated reasoning tests for a counter-example, which is the hallmark of a stronger answer.
- **Encourage a recount on error-checking items.** For questions like Question 4, a candidate who visibly tallies categories rather than eyeballing the list is demonstrating good practice, and this is worth noting in your assessment record.
- **Run a timed session.** Allow around 20 minutes for a set of eight questions from the pool above, applied identically to every candidate sitting this level's assessment.
- **Set the conditions up for a fair attempt.** Schedule the session at a reasonable time of day, in a quiet space, and let the candidate know in advance roughly how long it will take, so nerves and fatigue do not distort the result.
- **Offer the same warm-up questions to every candidate.** If your platform provides ungraded practice items, use them consistently - they remove interface surprises without affecting the graded result.

### Common pitfalls

- **Scoring as if this were a school test.** Job-specific cognitive items use workplace artefacts, not abstract puzzles. A candidate who reasons from their working knowledge of what a capability model or stakeholder list is for should be credited for using the context as a clue, not penalised for going "beyond" the question.
- **Letting one hard question dominate the session.** If a candidate is visibly stuck, note it and allow them to move on and return later, consistent with how the assessment is designed to be timed; this keeps the session comparable across candidates.
- **Treating speed and accuracy as interchangeable.** Consider recording them separately, since a candidate who works steadily and checks their answers may be a stronger fit for this role than one who rushes and guesses.
- **Misreading adaptivity as failure, if using an adaptive platform.** Rising difficulty usually signals strong performance, not weak performance - do not let a rising difficulty curve bias your impression of the candidate downward.
- **Applying the answer key inconsistently.** Mark strictly against the stated correct answer and explanation for every candidate, so that scoring stays comparable across the pool.


## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates how well a candidate can analyse, interpret, and make sensible decisions using numerical data. It is business maths, not abstract algebra. Questions are built from graphs, tables, budgets, and schedules - the kinds of real-world data a trainee business architect meets when supporting capability assessments, workshop planning, and strategic reporting. The candidate will work with percentages, ratios, averages, simple projections, and cost comparisons.

Most numeric reasoning assessments are calculator friendly - offer an onscreen or basic calculator to every candidate equally - because this role calls for sound interpretation of numbers more than fast mental arithmetic. That said, administer this section under a strict time limit, since quick and accurate data extraction matters: finding the right row in a table, noticing the units, and choosing the right operation are all part of what is being tested.

It may not be obvious why numbers matter in a role that is mostly about frameworks, workshops, and communication. The reason is that business architecture runs on evidence: when a senior architect shows that three systems duplicate each other, or that a process consumes 40 per cent more staff time in one region than another, the argument stands or falls on the numbers behind it. A candidate who is comfortable with basic business maths is a trustworthy pair of hands for this kind of work.

### What it measures

- **Business architecture**: capability assessments often use maturity scores, headcounts mapped to capabilities, and system counts. This assessment tests whether the candidate can read and compare these figures accurately.
- **Communicating information**: stakeholders send data in tables, dashboards, and emails. Questions test whether the candidate can extract the correct figure and interpret what it means before passing it on.
- **Consultancy**: recommendations need evidence. Questions test cost comparisons and simple benefit calculations - the arithmetic behind "we recommend option B".
- **Making and guiding decisions**: several question types present two or three options with costs and benefits, and ask which the numbers support - testing the candidate's ability to describe the reasoning behind a recommendation.
- **Methods and tools**: reading outputs from planning and analysis tools (utilisation figures, schedule data) is tested through table and chart interpretation.
- **Strategic thinking**: percentage-of-total and trend questions test whether the candidate can connect detailed figures to the big picture, such as what share of spend supports each strategic priority.

### Practice questions

Have a calculator and some rough paper available for the candidate. The explanations below show every step of the arithmetic, so you can see exactly where any slip happened when marking.

**Question 1 (easy) - Workshop attendance percentage**

The candidate helped organise a stakeholder workshop. 24 people were invited and 18 attended. What was the attendance rate?

- A) 66%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C) 75%**

**Explanation:** Attendance rate = attendees divided by invitees, times 100. That is 18 / 24 = 0.75, and 0.75 x 100 = 75%. A quick sense-check: 18 out of 24 is the same as 3 out of 4 (divide both by 6), and three quarters is 75%. Reporting attendance rates is a common trainee task after workshops, and a candidate who spots the fraction shortcut rather than reaching for long division is showing efficient number sense.

**Question 2 (easy) - Simple budget remainder**

A trainee's team has a training budget of 3,000 pounds for the year. So far the trainee has spent 450 pounds on a business architecture foundation course and 275 pounds on a workshop facilitation course. How much budget remains?

- A) 2,275 pounds
- B) 2,375 pounds
- C) 2,175 pounds
- D) 2,425 pounds

**Correct answer: A) 2,275 pounds**

**Explanation:** Total spent = 450 + 275 = 725 pounds. Remaining = 3,000 - 725 = 2,275 pounds. The most common slip here is adding 450 + 275 as 700 or 735; check that the candidate's working, where shown, lines up the tens and units correctly. Tracking a small budget, such as team training spend, is a realistic administrative support task at this level, and it is good practice for the larger programme budgets the candidate will meet later in their career.

**Question 3 (easy) - Reading a table accurately**

The table below shows how many business processes each directorate documented this quarter:

| Directorate | Processes documented | Processes still to document |
|---|---|---|
| Finance | 34 | 6 |
| Operations | 28 | 22 |
| Digital | 41 | 9 |
| Policy | 17 | 13 |

Which directorate has documented the highest proportion of its processes?

- A) Finance
- B) Operations
- C) Digital
- D) Policy

**Correct answer: A) Finance**

**Explanation:** The proportion documented = documented / (documented + still to document). Finance: 34 / 40 = 85%. Operations: 28 / 50 = 56%. Digital: 41 / 50 = 82%. Policy: 17 / 30 = 56.7% (rounded). Finance's 85% is highest. The trap is choosing Digital because 41 is the biggest raw number - but the question asks for proportion, not count. Distinguishing "most" from "highest proportion" is one of the most frequently tested skills in numeric reasoning, and it matters in real reporting too: "Digital documented the most processes" and "Finance is furthest through its documentation" are both true statements, and a strong candidate's answer will show awareness of which claim is actually being made.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Percentage change in process time**

A senior architect is showing how a redesigned approvals process has improved. Before the redesign, an approval took an average of 12 working days. After the redesign, it takes an average of 9 working days. What is the percentage reduction in approval time?

- A) 33%
- B) 25%
- C) 3%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B) 25%**

**Explanation:** Percentage reduction = (old - new) / old x 100. That is (12 - 9) / 12 x 100 = 3 / 12 x 100 = 25%. The classic trap is dividing by the new value instead of the old one: 3 / 9 = 33%, which is answer A. Percentage change is always measured against the starting point. In business change reporting this distinction is important and occasionally political - a 25% reduction and a 33% reduction are very different claims, so an assessor should check the candidate's working, not just the final figure, when this item is answered incorrectly.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Capability maturity averages**

An organisation scores capability maturity from 1 (initial) to 5 (optimised). A capability assessment gives these scores:

| Capability | Maturity score |
|---|---|
| Customer case management | 3 |
| Payments processing | 4 |
| Identity verification | 2 |
| Reporting and analytics | 2 |
| Correspondence handling | 4 |

What is the mean maturity score, and how many capabilities score below that mean?

- A) Mean 3.0; two capabilities below
- B) Mean 3.0; three capabilities below
- C) Mean 2.5; two capabilities below
- D) Mean 3.2; three capabilities below

**Correct answer: A) Mean 3.0; two capabilities below**

**Explanation:** Sum the scores: 3 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 4 = 15. Divide by the number of capabilities: 15 / 5 = 3.0. Now count scores strictly below 3.0: Identity verification (2) and Reporting and analytics (2) - that is two capabilities. Customer case management scores exactly 3, which is equal to the mean, not below it, so it does not count. Reading "below" strictly (not "at or below") is exactly the kind of precise interpretation that this item, and real capability reports, both demand. Summaries like "two of our five assessed capabilities fall below average maturity" often go straight into stakeholder packs, so the counting rule matters, and a candidate who gets the boundary case right is showing exactly that care.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Ratios in team composition**

A programme board wants the ratio of business architects to projects kept at 1:4 or better (meaning each architect supports at most 4 projects). The architecture team currently has 3 business architects supporting 14 projects. Next quarter, 4 more projects will start and one architect will retire. How many architects must be recruited to restore the 1:4 ratio next quarter?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) None; the ratio is fine

**Correct answer: C) 3**

**Explanation:** Next quarter there will be 14 + 4 = 18 projects and 3 - 1 = 2 architects. To keep each architect at no more than 4 projects, at least 18 / 4 = 4.5 architects are needed - and since half a person cannot be hired, round up to 5 architects. With 2 remaining, the team must recruit 5 - 2 = 3. The important habit here is rounding up when the quantity is people needed to meet a standard: 4 architects would give 18 / 4 = 4.5 projects each, which breaks the 1:4 rule. Capacity and workload figures like these appear regularly in the strategic planning that a trainee supports in an administrative capacity, and this item is testing whether the candidate rounds in the right direction for the real-world meaning.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Percentage of spend by strategic priority**

The candidate is helping prepare a slide showing how the change budget maps to strategy. The budget is:

| Strategic priority | Spend |
|---|---|
| Citizen self-service | 1.8 million pounds |
| System consolidation | 1.2 million pounds |
| Data quality | 0.6 million pounds |
| Not mapped to strategy | 0.4 million pounds |

What percentage of the total budget is mapped to the "System consolidation" priority?

- A) 25%
- B) 30%
- C) 35%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B) 30%**

**Explanation:** Total budget = 1.8 + 1.2 + 0.6 + 0.4 = 4.0 million pounds. System consolidation share = 1.2 / 4.0 = 0.30 = 30%. Two traps: first, forgetting to include the "not mapped" 0.4 million in the total (giving 1.2 / 3.6 = 33.3%, close to answer C); second, misreading which row the question asks about. Every row belongs in the denominator unless the question says otherwise. Incidentally, the "not mapped to strategy" line is exactly the kind of figure a business architect highlights: 10% of change spend with no strategic justification is a finding worth raising, and an assessor may find it useful to note whether the candidate spots this in their working.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Cost comparison of workshop venues**

A team needs a venue for a full-day stakeholder workshop for 30 people. The candidate gathers these quotes:

- Venue A: room hire 400 pounds, catering 12 pounds per person.
- Venue B: room hire 250 pounds, catering 18 pounds per person.
- Venue C: all-inclusive package at 25 pounds per person.

Which venue is cheapest for 30 people, and by how much compared with the next cheapest?

- A) Venue A, by 30 pounds
- B) Venue B, by 10 pounds
- C) Venue C, by 10 pounds
- D) Venue C, by 40 pounds

**Correct answer: C) Venue C, by 10 pounds**

**Explanation:** Cost each venue in full for 30 people, keeping fixed and per-person costs separate. Venue A: 400 + (12 x 30) = 400 + 360 = 760 pounds. Venue B: 250 + (18 x 30) = 250 + 540 = 790 pounds. Venue C: 25 x 30 = 750 pounds. Venue C is cheapest at 750 pounds; the next cheapest is Venue A at 760 pounds, so the saving is 760 - 750 = 10 pounds. Two lessons this item tests. First, a low room hire (Venue B's 250 pounds) can be swamped by a high per-person cost - the total must be computed, never judged by one component. Second, the margin between options can be small: 10 pounds either way. In a real venue decision, non-price factors would then be weighed too, such as step-free access and hearing-loop provision, which UK public sector events must take seriously - but when the question asks only for the cheapest, the strongest answer trusts the completed arithmetic.

**Question 9 (hard) - Multi-step budget projection**

A programme spends 60,000 pounds per month on contractors. From next month, the programme will reduce contractor spend by 15% and redirect half of the saving into staff training. How much will be redirected into staff training each month?

- A) 9,000 pounds
- B) 4,500 pounds
- C) 6,000 pounds
- D) 3,000 pounds

**Correct answer: B) 4,500 pounds**

**Explanation:** Step 1: calculate the saving. 15% of 60,000 = 0.15 x 60,000 = 9,000 pounds per month. Step 2: half of that saving goes to training: 9,000 / 2 = 4,500 pounds per month. The trap answer is A (stopping after step 1) - multi-step items often list each intermediate result as an option, so this tests whether the candidate re-reads the question to confirm which quantity is actually asked for, since the question asks for the redirected amount, not the saving. When supporting strategic planning administratively, a trainee will often trace money through exactly this kind of two-step logic: a saving is made, then split between destinations.

**Question 10 (hard) - Interpreting a utilisation table**

The architecture practice tracks how architect time is used. Here is last month's breakdown for the team's 4 architects (each works 160 hours per month):

| Activity | Hours (whole team) |
|---|---|
| Project design reviews | 224 |
| Stakeholder workshops | 96 |
| Capability modelling | 160 |
| Administration | 64 |
| Training and development | 96 |

What percentage of total team hours went on stakeholder workshops and capability modelling combined?

- A) 25%
- B) 35%
- C) 40%
- D) 45%

**Correct answer: C) 40%**

**Explanation:** Total team hours = 4 architects x 160 hours = 640 hours. Check the table adds up: 224 + 96 + 160 + 64 + 96 = 640 (it does - always verify). Workshops plus capability modelling = 96 + 160 = 256 hours. Percentage = 256 / 640 = 0.4 = 40%. A useful shortcut: 256 / 640 simplifies by dividing both by 64, giving 4 / 10. Utilisation analysis like this feeds real decisions about where the practice spends its effort, and a trainee may well be the person compiling the figures - so this item also tests whether the candidate checks that a table's rows sum to the stated total before answering, a habit worth noting when marking.

**Question 11 (hard) - Weighted scoring of options**

A senior architect asks the candidate to total a weighted options appraisal for three software tools that support capability modelling. Scores are out of 10; each score is multiplied by the criterion weight, then summed.

| Criterion | Weight | Tool X | Tool Y | Tool Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ease of use | 3 | 8 | 6 | 7 |
| Accessibility compliance | 4 | 6 | 9 | 7 |
| Cost | 2 | 5 | 4 | 8 |
| Integration with existing tools | 1 | 9 | 7 | 5 |

Which tool wins the weighted appraisal?

- A) Tool X
- B) Tool Y
- C) Tool Z
- D) Tools Y and Z tie

**Correct answer: C) Tool Z**

**Explanation:** Compute each weighted total row by row. Tool X: (3 x 8) + (4 x 6) + (2 x 5) + (1 x 9) = 24 + 24 + 10 + 9 = 67. Tool Y: (3 x 6) + (4 x 9) + (2 x 4) + (1 x 7) = 18 + 36 + 8 + 7 = 69. Tool Z: (3 x 7) + (4 x 7) + (2 x 8) + (1 x 5) = 21 + 28 + 16 + 5 = 70. Tool Z wins at 70, narrowly ahead of Tool Y at 69. The result is close, which is exactly why method matters: a single transposed digit would flip the outcome, and a transposed digit feels exactly like a correct answer until the column is re-added. A strong candidate does each row's multiplication once, writes it down, and re-adds the totals at the end rather than trusting a running total in their head. Notice also that Tool Y tops the accessibility criterion but still loses overall - weights change outcomes, which is why appraisal weights are agreed before scoring, never after. Weighted scoring tables are everywhere in public sector options appraisals, and accessibility compliance carrying the highest weight reflects genuine UK government practice - services must be accessible to everyone.

**Question 12 (hard) - Trend interpretation with percentages**

Quarterly counts of duplicate systems identified across the organisation:

| Quarter | Duplicate systems identified (cumulative) | Duplicate systems retired (cumulative) |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 20 | 2 |
| Q2 | 32 | 6 |
| Q3 | 40 | 14 |
| Q4 | 44 | 24 |

Between which two consecutive quarters did the number of systems retired grow by the largest percentage?

- A) Q1 to Q2
- B) Q2 to Q3
- C) Q3 to Q4
- D) The growth rate was the same each quarter

**Correct answer: A) Q1 to Q2**

**Explanation:** Percentage growth = (new - old) / old x 100, applied to the cumulative retired column. Q1 to Q2: (6 - 2) / 2 = 200%. Q2 to Q3: (14 - 6) / 6 = 133% (rounded). Q3 to Q4: (24 - 14) / 14 = 71% (rounded). The largest percentage growth is Q1 to Q2, even though the largest absolute increase (10 systems) came in Q3 to Q4. This is the mirror image of Question 3's lesson: percentage growth and absolute growth can point at different quarters, and both facts can be true at once. When helping draft a consolidation progress report, the strongest candidate's answer will choose the measure that answers the stakeholder's actual question, and never mix the two in one sentence without saying which is which.

**Question 13 (moderate) - Time planning across a programme**

The candidate is helping schedule capability workshops. Each workshop needs: 2 hours of preparation, 3 hours of delivery, and 1.5 hours of write-up. They have been allocated 40 hours of their time this month for workshop support. Travel and admin will take a fixed 7 hours in total for the month. How many complete workshops can they support this month?

- A) 4
- B) 5
- C) 6
- D) 7

**Correct answer: B) 5**

**Explanation:** Hours available for workshops = 40 - 7 = 33 hours. Hours per workshop = 2 + 3 + 1.5 = 6.5 hours. Number of complete workshops = 33 / 6.5 = 5.08, and since only complete workshops count, round down to 5. Note the direction of rounding: in Question 6 the rounding went up (people needed to meet a standard); here it goes down (complete units achievable within a limit). Choosing the rounding direction from the real-world meaning, rather than by habit, is a mark of careful numeric reasoning - and scheduling one's own workshop support time is about as job-specific as it gets for a trainee business architect.

**Question 14 (hard) - Benefits phasing across years**

A business change is forecast to deliver benefits of 50,000 pounds in year 1, rising by 20% in year 2, and rising by a further 25% in year 3 (each rise is on the previous year's figure). The change costs 150,000 pounds, all paid in year 1. By the end of which year do cumulative benefits first exceed the total cost?

- A) End of year 1
- B) End of year 2
- C) End of year 3
- D) Cumulative benefits never exceed the cost in the first three years

**Correct answer: C) End of year 3**

**Explanation:** Build the benefits year by year. Year 1: 50,000 pounds. Year 2: 50,000 x 1.20 = 60,000 pounds. Year 3: 60,000 x 1.25 = 75,000 pounds. Now accumulate: end of year 1 = 50,000 (below 150,000). End of year 2 = 50,000 + 60,000 = 110,000 (still below). End of year 3 = 110,000 + 75,000 = 185,000, which exceeds 150,000 for the first time. Two things this item tests. First, successive percentage rises compound - the 25% rise applies to 60,000, not to the original 50,000, so year 3 is 75,000, not 62,500. Second, the question asks about cumulative benefits versus cost, so a running total must be kept rather than comparing single years. Benefit profiles like this appear in the business cases that business architects help assemble, and "when do we break even?" is one of the first questions any programme board asks.

### Administration tips

- **Give the candidate a note of the key formulas up front, if your organisation allows it.** Three formulas cover most items: part / whole x 100 (what percentage), whole x rate (percentage of), and (new - old) / old x 100 (percentage change). Deciding in advance whether to provide these, and doing so consistently for every candidate, keeps the assessment fair.
- **Provide the same calculator to every candidate.** If the platform offers an onscreen calculator, use it for everyone rather than letting some candidates use their own device.
- **Check table totals when marking, not just final answers.** Several items (Questions 5, 10) hinge on the candidate verifying a table before using it - note in your scoring whether their working shows this check.
- **Watch for units and scales in the candidate's working.** Confusing "in thousands" with raw figures, or per-person with total figures, is a common source of error; when reviewing incorrect answers, check whether this was the cause.
- **Time the session consistently.** Apply the same time limit to every candidate for this role level, and decide in advance whether interruptions (a phone ringing, a fire alarm) warrant extra time, applying that rule consistently if it arises.
- **Score speed and accuracy separately where practical.** A candidate who answers fewer questions but gets them right may be a stronger fit for a role that needs to get numbers right first time.

### Common pitfalls

- **Marking a "most similar-looking" wrong answer as a near miss.** Distractor options are deliberately built from a single wrong operation (dividing by the wrong base, stopping one step early); mark strictly against the correct answer regardless of how close the candidate's working looks.
- **Penalising an estimate-first approach.** A candidate who visibly estimates before calculating precisely ("about three quarters, so around 75%") is demonstrating good numeric judgement, not skipping steps - do not read this as uncertainty.
- **Letting one multi-stage question consume disproportionate session time.** If a candidate is visibly stuck, note it and allow them to move on, consistent with how this section is designed to be timed.
- **Inconsistent rounding expectations.** Confirm before scoring whether your organisation expects rounding up (resources needed to meet a requirement) or down (complete units achievable within a limit) to be shown explicitly, and apply that consistently across candidates.
- **Under-crediting genuine reasoning that reaches a wrong final figure.** Where a candidate's method is sound but a small arithmetic slip changes the final answer, consider whether your scoring approach credits process as well as outcome - decide this in advance and apply it to every candidate.


## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well a candidate understands, analyses, and draws correct conclusions from written business information. It mirrors the reading demands of the actual role: internal standards, governance policies, emails from senior architects, workshop briefs, and programme reports.

The format is distinctive. The candidate is given a passage of text, then a series of statements. For each statement they answer:

- **True** - the statement definitely follows from the passage.
- **False** - the statement definitely contradicts the passage.
- **Cannot Say** - the passage does not give enough information to decide either way.

The golden rule for the candidate to follow: answer based strictly on the text provided, ignoring outside knowledge. Even if the candidate happens to know that something is true in the real world, if the passage does not say it, the answer is Cannot Say. Some assessments also include multiple-choice comprehension questions, which ask the candidate to identify the best summary, the correct application of a rule, or the writer's main point.

Administer verbal reasoning items under time pressure - often less than a minute per question - to simulate workplace reading under deadlines. For a trainee business architect, this assessment is especially relevant: the role profile asks the postholder to listen to the needs of design and business stakeholders and interpret information, and much of that information arrives in writing. Misreading a governance rule or a stakeholder email creates real problems, so this is worth testing carefully.

### What it measures

- **Communicating information**: the heart of this assessment. Interpreting stakeholder needs from emails and documents, and distinguishing what was actually said from what was assumed, is tested in every question.
- **Business architecture**: passages use framework and governance language - capability, operating model, design authority - and test whether the candidate extracts rules from standards documents accurately.
- **Consultancy**: identifying what advice or recommendations might be appropriate starts with reading previous work by others correctly - a named behaviour at this level.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: several questions test whether a conclusion is supported by the evidence in the text - the reading equivalent of describing the reasoning behind a recommendation.
- **Methods and tools**: passages include procedural instructions (how to use a repository, how to run a review), testing whether the candidate can follow written methods precisely.
- **Strategic thinking**: questions ask the candidate to identify the strategic purpose stated in a document, separating the goal from the mechanism.

### Practice questions

Present each passage in full, then work through its questions with the candidate answering from the text alone. The explanations below point back to the exact wording, so you can see precisely why each answer is what it is when marking.

**Passage 1 - Internal standard: design review governance**

"All programmes must submit design documentation to the Design Authority at least ten working days before their scheduled review. Programmes classified as high-risk must additionally submit a business impact statement. The Design Authority meets fortnightly. Where a programme misses its submission deadline, its review will be deferred to the next available meeting. The Design Authority may, at its discretion, grant an expedited review for programmes supporting ministerial priorities."

**Question 1 (easy)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "Programmes must submit design documentation at least ten working days before their scheduled review."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** This is a direct restatement of the first sentence: "All programmes must submit design documentation to the Design Authority at least ten working days before their scheduled review." Easy items like this reward careful, literal matching between statement and text. When marking, check that the candidate has matched every element - "ten working days" (not calendar days), "before their scheduled review".

**Question 2 (easy)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The Design Authority meets every week."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says "The Design Authority meets fortnightly" - that is, every two weeks. "Every week" directly contradicts "fortnightly", so the answer is False, not Cannot Say. A statement is False only when the text contradicts it; here it plainly does. Recognising the word "fortnightly" matters - UK public sector documents use it often, and this item tests basic vocabulary familiarity as well as reading accuracy.

**Question 3 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "A low-risk programme is not required to submit a business impact statement."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that "Programmes classified as high-risk must additionally submit a business impact statement." The word "additionally" tells us this requirement applies on top of the standard documentation, and only to high-risk programmes. The general requirement (first sentence) mentions design documentation only. So a low-risk programme's stated obligations do not include a business impact statement - the statement is True. This relies on the passage listing requirements exhaustively; in verbal reasoning tests, a rule stated as applying to one category is taken as not applying to others unless the text says otherwise, and this item is testing whether the candidate applies that convention correctly.

**Question 4 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "A programme supporting a ministerial priority will receive an expedited review."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says the Design Authority "may, at its discretion, grant an expedited review" for such programmes. "May, at its discretion" means it is possible, not guaranteed. The statement claims it "will" happen - the passage neither confirms this (so not True) nor rules it out (so not False). Cannot Say. This item tests attention to modal verbs: "may", "can", "will", "must" each carry different force, and swapping one for another is the single most common trick in verbal reasoning tests - and a meaningful distinction in governance documents, where discretion and obligation are very different things.

**Question 5 (moderate)** - Multiple choice: A programme missed its submission deadline for a review scheduled at the next Design Authority meeting. According to the passage, what happens?

- A) The programme is cancelled.
- B) The programme's review is deferred to the next available meeting.
- C) The programme must submit a business impact statement.
- D) The programme receives an expedited review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states: "Where a programme misses its submission deadline, its review will be deferred to the next available meeting." Option B repeats this rule. Option A invents a consequence the text never mentions. Option C confuses the missed-deadline rule with the high-risk rule. Option D inverts the situation - expedited review is a discretionary benefit for ministerial priorities, not a remedy for lateness. Applying the right rule to the right situation is exactly what a candidate would do when supporting governance processes in this role.

**Passage 2 - Email from a senior business architect**

"Hi - thanks for offering to help with the operating model pack. Could you pull together the capability heat map and the stakeholder summary by Thursday? The heat map should use the January assessment scores, not the draft February ones, because February hasn't been signed off. Don't worry about the value stream diagrams; Priya is updating those and will send them straight to me. If Finance ask about the pack, please refer them to me rather than sharing anything early. One more thing - the Deputy Director wants the pack to be accessible, so please use the approved template with proper heading styles."

**Question 6 (easy)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The heat map should use the January assessment scores."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The email says directly: "The heat map should use the January assessment scores, not the draft February ones." A literal match. Note the reason given - February "hasn't been signed off" - since understanding why an instruction exists helps a candidate apply it correctly if circumstances change.

**Question 7 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "Priya will send the updated value stream diagrams to the recipient of the email."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The email says Priya "will send them straight to me" - that is, to the senior architect who wrote the email, not to the reader of the note. The statement contradicts the text, so it is False. Misreading who receives what is a small error with real consequences: someone might chase Priya for diagrams that were never coming to them, or worse, tell someone the pack is incomplete when it is not. Pronoun tracking - who "me", "you", and "them" refer to - is a frequent test point and a frequent source of real workplace confusion, which is exactly what this item screens for.

**Question 8 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The February assessment scores are inaccurate."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The email says the February scores are "draft" and "haven't been signed off". Unsigned-off is not the same as inaccurate - a draft may be perfectly accurate but simply awaiting approval. The passage gives no information about the accuracy of the February scores either way, so Cannot Say. This is the "status versus quality" confusion: governance status (signed off, draft, approved) describes where a document is in a process, not how good it is. Business architects live in documents with statuses, so this distinction is tested here because it will serve a successful candidate daily.

**Question 9 (hard)** - Multiple choice: A colleague from Finance stops the candidate in the corridor and asks to see the current draft of the operating model pack "just for a quick look". Based only on the email, what did the senior architect ask the candidate to do in this situation?

- A) Show Finance the pack but ask them to keep it confidential.
- B) Refer the Finance colleague to the senior architect rather than sharing anything early.
- C) Share only the January heat map, since it is signed off.
- D) Tell Finance the pack does not exist.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email states: "If Finance ask about the pack, please refer them to me rather than sharing anything early." Option B matches this instruction exactly. Option A shares the pack, which the instruction forbids ("rather than sharing anything early"). Option C invents a partial-sharing rule the email never gives - the instruction covers "anything", not just unsigned-off content. Option D is dishonest and goes far beyond the instruction. Notice that the correct answer requires the candidate to apply a written instruction to a slightly different situation (a corridor conversation rather than a formal request) - the mark of genuine comprehension rather than keyword matching.

**Passage 3 - Extract from a business change programme report**

"The Casework Modernisation Programme remains on track for its March milestone. Of the six business units in scope, four have completed process mapping and two are scheduled to complete by the end of February. Early findings indicate that duplication between the regional intake teams is greater than originally estimated. The programme board has asked the architecture team to quantify the potential staff-time savings from a single national intake process before any decision is taken. No decision on team restructuring has been made, and staff will be consulted before any changes are proposed."

**Question 10 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "All six business units have completed process mapping."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says "four have completed process mapping and two are scheduled to complete by the end of February". Scheduled to complete is not completed - so the claim that all six are done contradicts the text. Assessments favour this gap between planned and actual; so do programme boards. When candidates draft status summaries in the real role, "done" and "due" need to stay rigorously separate, and this item tests exactly that discipline.

**Question 11 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The programme will merge the regional intake teams into a single national team."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says the board asked the architecture team "to quantify the potential staff-time savings from a single national intake process before any decision is taken" and explicitly adds "No decision on team restructuring has been made". A merger is under investigation, not decided - the passage neither confirms the merger will happen (not True) nor says it will not (not False). Cannot Say. Treating an option under analysis as a decision already taken is one of the most damaging misreadings a trainee can make - if repeated to staff, it could cause genuine alarm about jobs. The passage's careful wording ("before any decision is taken", "will be consulted") is typical of well-written change communications, and this item tests whether the candidate reads with that same precision.

**Question 12 (hard)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "Duplication between the regional intake teams was underestimated at the start of the programme."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states: "Early findings indicate that duplication between the regional intake teams is greater than originally estimated." If actual duplication is greater than the original estimate, then the original estimate was too low - which is what "underestimated" means. This tests whether the candidate can recognise the same fact expressed in different words (paraphrase recognition), rather than hunting for matching keywords. One caution: the passage says "early findings indicate", which signals some provisionality - but the statement under test mirrors that same evidential claim, so True is correct. If the statement had said "duplication is definitely twice the estimate", the answer would be Cannot Say.

**Question 13 (hard)** - Multiple choice: Which of the following best summarises the main point of the passage?

- A) The programme is behind schedule because of duplication in intake teams.
- B) The programme is progressing to plan, an unexpected level of intake duplication has emerged, and evidence is being gathered before any restructuring decision.
- C) The architecture team has recommended a single national intake process.
- D) Staff have been consulted about the proposed team restructuring.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B captures all three strands: on track ("remains on track for its March milestone"), the emerging finding ("duplication... is greater than originally estimated"), and the evidence-before-decision stance ("quantify... before any decision is taken"). Option A contradicts the first sentence - the programme is on track. Option C overstates the architecture team's role: they were asked to quantify savings, not to recommend. Option D reverses the timeline: staff "will be consulted", meaning it has not happened yet. Summary items like this test whether the candidate can hold a whole passage in balance rather than seizing on one vivid detail - the same skill needed when supporting the production of clear business architecture outputs from long source documents.

**Passage 4 - Repository usage guidance**

"All architecture artefacts must be stored in the central repository. Artefacts must be given a status of Draft, In Review, Approved, or Archived. Only Approved artefacts may be shared outside the architecture team. Trainees may create and edit Draft artefacts, and may move an artefact into In Review status once a named senior reviewer has agreed to review it. Only senior architects may set an artefact to Approved. Archived artefacts must not be edited; if an archived artefact needs changing, a new Draft must be created instead."

**Question 14 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "A trainee may set an artefact's status to Approved if a senior architect has verbally agreed the content."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage is explicit: "Only senior architects may set an artefact to Approved." The word "only" excludes everyone else, including a trainee acting with verbal agreement - the mechanism of agreement does not change who may perform the action. The statement contradicts the rule, so it is False. This item tests whether the candidate is drawn in by a plausible-sounding justification ("a senior architect agreed") attached to an action the text forbids; the justification is a distractor. In real governance, this rule protects the candidate: approval authority sits with the accountable person, and the audit trail must show they exercised it themselves.

**Question 15 (hard)** - Multiple choice: An archived process model needs updating to reflect a new legal requirement. According to the passage, what is the correct procedure?

- A) Edit the archived artefact directly, since legal requirements take priority.
- B) Move the artefact back to Draft status and edit it.
- C) Create a new Draft artefact rather than editing the archived one.
- D) Ask a senior architect to edit the archived artefact.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says: "Archived artefacts must not be edited; if an archived artefact needs changing, a new Draft must be created instead." Option C follows this exactly. Option A invents a legal-priority exception the text does not contain - real policies may have such exceptions, but this passage does not, and the candidate must answer from the text alone. Option B invents a status transition the passage never describes. Option D fails because the prohibition on editing archived artefacts is absolute in the text - it does not say "except by senior architects". This item tests the habit of checking what a rule actually permits, rather than what would be convenient, which is core to working with methods and tools under guidance.

**Passage 5 - Workshop briefing note**

"Thursday's capability workshop will run from 09:30 to 12:30 in Conference Room 2, with a hybrid option for remote attendees. The objective is to agree draft definitions for the five customer-facing capabilities; supporting capabilities are out of scope and will be covered in a later session. The facilitator will be the lead business architect. The trainee business architect will capture decisions and actions on the shared board, and will circulate a summary within two working days. Attendees should read the pre-circulated capability definitions before the session. Any attendee who cannot attend should send a delegate with authority to agree definitions on their behalf."

**Question 16 (easy)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "Supporting capabilities will be discussed at Thursday's workshop."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The briefing states that "supporting capabilities are out of scope and will be covered in a later session". The statement directly contradicts this, so it is False. Scope statements - what is in, what is out - are among the most important sentences in any architecture document, and workshops drift badly when nobody holds the scope line. As the note-taker in this scenario, knowing the scope helps a trainee flag out-of-scope discussion in their notes, which is why this item is a good early screen.

**Question 17 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The trainee business architect will circulate the workshop summary within two calendar days."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The briefing says the summary will be circulated "within two working days" - the statement says "calendar days". These are different commitments: two working days from a Thursday workshop means by Monday, whereas two calendar days would mean by Saturday. Because the statement substitutes a materially different deadline for the one in the text, it contradicts the passage and is False. The deciding point is that the passage does state the deadline - it is just a different one, so a candidate hesitating between False and Cannot Say should be marked on whether they spot that distinction. Precision about working versus calendar days matters constantly in public sector service standards and correspondence targets.

**Question 18 (hard)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "If a listed attendee cannot attend, the workshop objective cannot be achieved."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The briefing anticipates absence: anyone who cannot attend "should send a delegate with authority to agree definitions on their behalf". This mechanism suggests the workshop is designed to reach agreement even with substitutions - but the passage never states what happens to the objective if someone is absent, with or without a delegate. It neither confirms the statement (not True) nor denies it (not False) - the outcome depends on facts the passage does not give, such as whether a delegate actually attends. Cannot Say. This item tests whether a well-designed conditional instruction ("should send a delegate") tempts the candidate into inferring an outcome; inference beyond the text is exactly what this format penalises.

### Administration tips

- **Present passages in full before revealing the statements.** Whether the candidate is allowed to read the statement first, then scan the passage, or must read the passage first, keep this order consistent across candidates so the comparison is fair.
- **Time this section tightly and consistently.** Verbal reasoning items are typically answered in under a minute each; apply the same overall time limit to every candidate sitting this level's assessment.
- **When marking, check the exact evidence in the text, not the candidate's outside knowledge.** A candidate who justifies an answer using something true in the real world but absent from the passage has misunderstood the format, even if their real-world knowledge is correct.
- **Note whether the candidate's Cannot Say answers are used appropriately.** A candidate who avoids Cannot Say even when the text is genuinely silent, or who overuses it out of caution, is both worth noting when assessing reading precision.
- **Watch for modal-verb sensitivity.** "May", "must", "will", "should", "can" - the strength of these words decides many answers, and a candidate who explains their reasoning using this vocabulary accurately is demonstrating strong comprehension.

### Common pitfalls

- **Crediting outside knowledge as evidence.** The most common candidate error at every level is bringing in what they know to be true in real life rather than what the passage states; this should be marked as incorrect even if the candidate's external knowledge happens to be accurate.
- **Confusing "likely" with "certain" when marking.** A passage saying findings "indicate" something, or that an outcome "may" occur, does not support a statement that it "will" occur - check that the candidate's answer matches the strength of the text, not just its topic.
- **Rewarding keyword matching over understanding.** Distractor options often reuse exact words from the passage in the wrong relationship (as in Question 13's options), while paraphrase-based correct answers (Question 12) often look less similar to the text than wrong ones do - do not let surface similarity guide your scoring.
- **Letting one long passage dominate the session.** Structure the time allowed so no single passage can consume time needed for the rest of the section, applied identically for every candidate.
- **Treating Cannot Say answers as weaker by default.** Cannot Say is a full-strength verdict when the text does not support a conclusion, and is often the most professionally responsible answer in real analysis too - do not penalise a candidate simply for choosing it more often than True or False.
- **Missing negatives and exceptions when marking.** Words like "not", "unless", "except", and "only" reverse or restrict a rule's meaning; check carefully against the exact wording before marking a candidate's answer wrong.


## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment evaluates a candidate's decision-making, interpersonal skills, and professional conduct. Instead of testing logic or maths, it presents realistic workplace dilemmas - conflicts, ethical grey areas, competing priorities - and asks how the candidate would respond. There is usually no single objectively right answer; several responses may seem reasonable, but some fit the organisation's values and this role better than others.

The common response formats are:

- **Most effective / least effective**: from a set of possible actions, the candidate picks the one they judge most effective and the one they judge least effective.
- **Ranking**: the candidate puts all the options in order from best to worst.
- **Rating**: the candidate rates each option's effectiveness independently.

Unlike the timed tests earlier in this document, administer situational judgement items untimed or generously timed - they measure natural judgement, not speed.

For a trainee business architect in the UK public sector, the scenarios revolve around the candidate's likely working life: supporting senior architects, taking notes in stakeholder workshops, handling requests they are not yet authorised to fulfil, spotting errors in others' work, and balancing learning with delivery. The judging standard reflects UK public sector values: honesty and integrity, putting user needs first, transparency, collaboration across disciplines, accessibility, and inclusion. It also reflects the level - at trainee level, seeking guidance is usually a strength, not a weakness, but passing every problem upwards without thought is not.

### What it measures

- **Communicating information**: scenarios test whether the candidate listens to stakeholders, interprets their needs fairly, and takes part in multidisciplinary discussions constructively - especially when messages conflict.
- **Consultancy**: several dilemmas test whether the candidate understands the purpose of consulting in this role, refers appropriately to previous work by others, and frames problems before jumping to answers.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: the core of the assessment - recommending a course of action and being able to explain the reasoning behind it, including identifying disputes between peers and local stakeholders.
- **Business architecture**: scenarios about applying frameworks under guidance test whether the candidate respects the "under guidance" part while still contributing actively.
- **Methods and tools**: dilemmas about tool choice and following advice test whether the candidate balances initiative with the discipline of agreed methods.
- **Strategic thinking**: options are often stronger or weaker depending on whether they serve the strategic purpose of the work, not just the immediate task.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, ask the candidate to give their honest answer before discussing it further. The explanations below matter more than the "right answer" alone - they show the reasoning patterns to look for when scoring.

**Question 1 (easy) - A mistake in the candidate's own work**

The candidate circulated the summary notes from yesterday's stakeholder workshop. This morning they realise they recorded a decision incorrectly: they wrote that the Finance representative agreed to the new process, when in fact they agreed only to pilot it in one team. Nobody has noticed yet.

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Quietly edit the shared document so the record is correct, without telling anyone.
- B) Send a prompt correction to all recipients, clearly marking what changed, and check the correction with the Finance representative.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone notices; if they do, apologise and correct it.
- D) Mention it to their senior architect at their next scheduled catch-up in four days.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B is honest, fast, and thorough: it corrects the record for everyone who received the wrong version, is transparent about the change, and verifies the correction with the person whose position was misrecorded - which also rebuilds any lost trust. Option A fixes the document but not the misunderstanding: everyone who read the email still believes Finance fully agreed, and a silent edit can look like covering up. Option D is honest but too slow - four days is a long time for a wrong decision record to circulate and be acted on. Option C is least effective because it knowingly leaves a false record in circulation and makes correction conditional on being caught - the opposite of the transparency expected in public service. The general principle this item tests: a candidate who corrects their own error quickly, visibly, and with the affected people is showing exactly the openness assessors should look for. Nobody expects a trainee to be error-free, but everyone should expect them to be straight about it.

**Question 2 (easy) - Asked something beyond the candidate's knowledge**

During a workshop the candidate is supporting, a stakeholder turns to them directly and asks: "So what does the architecture team recommend - should we merge these two teams?" The senior architect facilitating has stepped out for five minutes. The candidate does not know the answer, and team mergers are a sensitive topic.

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Say they will make a note of the question and ensure the senior architect addresses it when they return.
- B) Offer a personal view, making clear it is not the team's position.
- C) Say the architecture team never comments on team structures.
- D) Change the subject until the senior architect returns.

**Best to worst: A, B, D, C.**

**Explanation:** Option A is best: it takes the question seriously, is honest about the right person to answer, and uses the candidate's actual role in the room - capturing questions and actions. It neither overreaches nor dodges. Option B is second: it is honest and appropriately caveated, but a trainee offering a personal view on a sensitive restructuring question in front of stakeholders risks having that view repeated as "the architecture team said..." regardless of the caveat. Option D avoids the overreach but is evasive and slightly disrespectful to the stakeholder - the question deserves acknowledgement, not deflection. Option C is worst because it is a made-up policy statement: inventing an organisational position is worse than admitting uncertainty. The pattern this item is testing: acknowledge, capture, route to the right person - that is what "supporting during workshops and meetings with stakeholders" looks like when done well.

**Question 3 (moderate) - Spotting an error in a senior colleague's work**

The candidate is formatting a capability heat map produced by a senior business architect, due to go to the programme board tomorrow. They notice that two capabilities appear to have swapped scores: "Payments processing" shows the low score that the assessment workshop gave to "Correspondence handling", and vice versa. They were at the workshop and are fairly confident it is a transposition error - but they are the most junior person involved.

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Say nothing; a senior architect is unlikely to be wrong, and it is not the candidate's place to question their work.
- B) Correct the scores personally before sending the pack, being confident about what the workshop agreed.
- C) Contact the senior architect today, explain what was noticed, reference the workshop record, and ask them to check.
- D) Raise it at the programme board meeting itself so the error can be corrected publicly.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option C combines everything this item is testing for: speaking up despite being junior (integrity and courage), doing so privately and promptly (respect and urgency), bringing evidence (the workshop record - referring to previous work is a named consultancy behaviour at this level), and asking the senior architect to check rather than asserting they are wrong (appropriate humility - the candidate might be mistaken). Option A fails the organisation: seniority is not infallibility, and a wrong heat map could misdirect board decisions about real services. Option B has the right instinct about the error but the wrong method - changing a senior colleague's professional judgement without telling them breaks trust and bypasses the approval chain, and the candidate might be wrong. Option D is least effective: it lets a known suspected error reach the board, then corrects it in the most embarrassing possible forum, damaging the team's credibility and the working relationship with the architect. Speaking up early, privately, and with evidence is the professional standard this item rewards.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Conflicting instructions from two seniors**

Two senior business architects both ask for the candidate's support on the same afternoon. Architect One asks them to prepare materials for tomorrow's stakeholder workshop. Architect Two asks them to finish consolidating process documentation "by end of day - the programme office needs it". They cannot do both in the time available. Both colleagues are equally senior.

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Tell both architects about the clash and ask them to agree the priority between them, offering what can realistically be delivered on each.
- B) Work on whichever task was assigned first and let the other architect find out when their task is late.
- C) Stay silent and work late into the evening to attempt both tasks.
- D) Decide yourself which task matters more strategically and do that one, informing the other architect afterwards.

**Best to worst: A, D, C, B.**

**Explanation:** Option A is best because it surfaces the conflict immediately to the people who can resolve it, and comes with a constructive offer ("here is what I can realistically do"). Identifying and articulating disputes - including priority disputes - between peers and stakeholders is literally a named skill behaviour for this role, and it applies to workload conflicts too. Option D is second best: it shows strategic thinking and decisiveness, and it does communicate - but it makes a call that is not a trainee's to make alone, and it informs rather than consults. Option C is third: the willingness is admirable, but silently absorbing an impossible workload hides a resourcing problem from the team, risks two half-finished tasks, and sets an unsustainable pattern that harms wellbeing. Option B is worst: it resolves the conflict by concealment and lets a colleague be surprised by a missed deadline that a single conversation would have prevented. The lesson this item tests: conflicts between legitimate demands are normal; hiding them is the only truly wrong move.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Workshop accessibility**

The candidate is helping organise a hybrid capability workshop. The day before, a remote attendee emails to say they use a screen reader, and asks whether the materials will be accessible. The candidate checks and discovers the pre-read pack is a set of image-heavy slides exported as pictures with no alternative text, prepared by a colleague who has now gone on leave.

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Reply that unfortunately the materials cannot be changed at this notice, and offer to summarise them verbally during the workshop.
- B) Spend the time before the workshop creating an accessible version - a structured document with headings and text descriptions of each diagram - send it to the attendee, and flag the issue to their senior architect so future packs are accessible from the start.
- C) Forward the attendee's email to the colleague on leave, since they made the pack.
- D) Tell the attendee the pack is optional and they can just attend without reading it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B treats accessibility as a need to be met, not a problem to be managed away. It fixes the immediate barrier (the attendee gets usable materials, in time to prepare like everyone else), and it addresses the root cause by flagging the gap so accessible-by-default becomes the team habit. Accessibility is a legal and moral commitment across UK government services and workplaces, and this item weights it heavily. Option A at least offers something, but it accepts an avoidable exclusion: the attendee is denied equal chance to prepare, and a verbal summary during the session is a workaround, not equality. Option D is worse: it quietly downgrades the attendee's participation - "just attend without preparing" is not what other attendees are being asked to do. Option C is least effective: it dumps the problem on someone who cannot act (they are on leave), leaves the attendee without an answer, and treats an organisational responsibility as a personal one. The effective pattern this item rewards, when an inclusion need surfaces: fix it now, fix the system next.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Pressure to skip the method**

The candidate is supporting a capability assessment. The agreed method requires evidence to be gathered from each business unit before scores are assigned. A programme manager, under deadline pressure, says: "Just fill in the scores yourself based on what you reckon - it will be close enough, and we can tidy it up later."

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Explain that scores need evidence to be credible with stakeholders, and offer a practical alternative - such as a clearly-labelled provisional view plus a fast-tracked evidence round - while checking the approach with their senior architect.
- B) Do what the programme manager asks; they are senior and own the deadline.
- C) Refuse outright and tell the programme manager that the method cannot be changed under any circumstances.
- D) Say yes to avoid conflict, but secretly gather what evidence can be found before filling anything in.

**Best to worst: A, C, D, B.**

**Explanation:** Option A is best because it protects the integrity of the work while genuinely engaging with the programme manager's real problem - the deadline. Explaining why the method exists (evidence gives scores their credibility), proposing a compromise that is honest about its own status ("provisional, pending evidence"), and looping in a senior architect - since method changes are not a trainee's call - together show exactly the blend of consultancy, methods discipline, and escalation judgement this role requires. Option C is second: it reaches the right substantive position (do not fabricate scores) but rigidly, without empathy or alternatives, and "under no circumstances" overstates a trainee's authority just as much as caving does. Option D at least tries to preserve some evidence base, but it is built on a false agreement - the programme manager believes one thing is happening while another is - which corrodes trust when discovered. Option B is worst: made-up scores presented as assessment results would mislead every stakeholder who reads them, and "we can tidy it up later" is how provisional guesses become permanent facts. Note the recurring theme this item tests: the strongest options pair honesty about constraints with practical help on the underlying need.

**Question 7 (hard) - Confidential information overheard**

While taking notes in a small meeting, the candidate hears two directors discuss - in passing - a possible reorganisation that would affect a team they work with closely. The information is clearly sensitive and was not meant for wider sharing. Later, a friend from that team says over coffee: "You get into all those senior meetings - is something happening to our team? People are worried."

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Tell the friend what was heard - they deserve to know, and it will leak eventually anyway.
- B) Say that what is discussed in meetings being supported cannot be shared, acknowledge that uncertainty is hard, and suggest they raise their concerns with their line manager; separately, tell the meeting chair that anxiety is building in the team.
- C) Deny that anything is being discussed at all.
- D) Refuse to answer and change the subject abruptly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Trusted access to senior discussions is fundamental to a business architect's ability to work across the organisation - and it survives only if confidences hold. Option B does four things well: it keeps the confidence without lying, it treats the friend's worry with respect rather than brushing it off, it points them to the legitimate channel for their question, and it feeds the human impact back to the chair - who can decide to communicate earlier - without breaching any specifics. Option D keeps the confidence but wastes the chance to respond with empathy or to help the situation; abrupt deflection can also inadvertently confirm suspicions. Option C is a lie: denying a discussion the candidate witnessed is a direct breach of honesty, and when the truth emerges credibility goes with it. Option A is least effective: it breaches confidentiality on the strength of two rationalisations ("they deserve to know", "it will leak anyway") that this item specifically screens for as red flags. If information genuinely should be shared sooner, the effective move is to advocate for earlier official communication - option B's final step - not to leak it directly.

**Question 8 (hard) - Disagreement between stakeholders in a workshop**

In a process-mapping workshop the candidate is supporting, representatives from two teams begin arguing about who owns a handoff step. The discussion becomes circular and heated, consuming time needed for the remaining agenda. The facilitator - their senior architect - is momentarily occupied helping a remote attendee reconnect.

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Politely interject: note the disagreement on the board as an open issue with both positions summarised, and suggest the group park it for the facilitator to pick up in a moment.
- B) Let the argument continue - it is not a trainee's place to intervene between senior stakeholders.
- C) Declare which team is believed to own the step, to settle the matter quickly.
- D) Whisper to the senior architect to interrupt what they are doing and deal with the argument.

**Best to worst: A, D, B, C.**

**Explanation:** Option A uses the legitimate tools available to workshop support staff: the board, the issue log, and the parking technique. Summarising both positions fairly - identifying and articulating a dispute between stakeholders, which is a named behaviour in the "making and guiding decisions" skill - shows each side they have been heard, which usually de-escalates, and it protects the agenda without anyone needing to win on the spot. Option D gets the right person involved but at the cost of interrupting a remote attendee's access - effectively trading one participant's inclusion for conflict management that option A handles without the trade. Option B mistakes passivity for humility: watching the agenda burn while holding the pen is not deference, it is a missed responsibility. Option C is worst: a trainee ruling on contested ownership exceeds their authority, will likely inflame the losing side, and entangles the neutral architecture function in a turf dispute. The deeper lesson this item tests: even at trainee level, a candidate is never "just taking notes" - capturing a dispute accurately and neutrally is a real contribution to resolving it.

**Question 9 (hard) - Learning versus delivery tension**

The candidate is three months into their traineeship. Their development plan says they should attend the fortnightly business architecture community of practice session, where this week's topic - capability modelling techniques - directly supports their learning objectives. An hour before the session, their senior architect asks if they can help collate stakeholder feedback this afternoon: "No pressure - it can wait until tomorrow if needed, but sooner is better."

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Skip the community session; delivery work always comes before personal development.
- B) Attend the session without mentioning the feedback task, and start the collation tomorrow.
- C) Tell their senior architect about the community session this hour, confirm that starting the collation immediately afterwards (or tomorrow morning) meets their need, and do that.
- D) Skip the session but do not tell the senior architect it existed, to appear maximally available.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The scenario deliberately includes the signal many candidates miss: "it can wait until tomorrow if needed". Option C simply takes the senior architect at their word - it is transparent about the clash, confirms rather than assumes, and meets both the delivery need and the development need. Developing knowledge of business architecture frameworks and practice is not a perk; it is a listed duty at this role level, and a trainee who routinely sacrifices structured learning becomes less useful, not more. Option B reaches a similar outcome but silently: the senior architect learns nothing about the candidate's constraints and might reasonably expect the work sooner, so small trust is eroded for no benefit. Option A applies a rule ("delivery always first") that the facts contradict - the task explicitly can wait, so the sacrifice buys nothing. Option D is least effective because it adds concealment to the unnecessary sacrifice and optimises for appearing available rather than being effective - a pattern that leads to burnout and stalled development. This item is testing honest communication and sustainable self-management, not raw willingness.

**Question 10 (moderate) - Referring to previous work**

A senior architect asks the candidate to draft a first-cut stakeholder map for a new programme in the licensing business area. They have never made one before. The team's repository contains an approved stakeholder map from last year's licensing programme, and the team has a standard stakeholder-mapping template.

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Start from the standard template, study the previous licensing map for structure and likely stakeholders, verify which stakeholders remain current, and note the sources and assumptions used for the senior architect's review.
- B) Design a new stakeholder map format from scratch to demonstrate creativity and initiative.
- C) Copy last year's map, change the programme name, and submit it.
- D) Tell the senior architect they have never done one and ask them to do it instead.

**Best to worst: A, D, B, C.**

**Explanation:** Option A is textbook trainee consultancy practice - the role's skill profile calls for working "under guidance to identify what advice, guidance and recommendations might be appropriate, usually by referring to previous work by others", and that is precisely what A does: reuse the approved template and prior map as scaffolding, update rather than assume (stakeholders change in a year), and expose the working so the reviewer can correct it efficiently. Option D is honest and safely avoids a bad output, but it declines the learning opportunity entirely; a stronger version of D is folded into A - attempt the draft, then seek review. Option B ignores an agreed team method for no reason the scenario supports; initiative is valuable, but reinventing a standard artefact format creates inconsistency and extra review work. Option C is worst: it looks like the fastest option but silently imports a year of staleness - departed stakeholders, changed roles, different programme scope - and presents unverified old work as a current deliverable. The ranking this item tests rewards a habit worth screening for early: reuse with verification beats both reinvention and blind copying.

### Administration tips

- **Give the candidate time to think, not to rush.** Situational judgement items measure natural judgement rather than speed; do not impose a tight time limit, but do keep whatever time allowance you offer consistent across candidates.
- **Ask the candidate to explain their reasoning, not just state their ranking.** Recommending a course of action and describing the reasoning behind it is the named skill this section tests, so probe gently for the "why" behind a most/least or ranking answer.
- **Present every option before asking for a judgement.** Most/least and ranking items are comparative - reading only part of the option set before answering will distort the result.
- **Note the verbs in the candidate's own reasoning.** Answers that lean on "quietly", "secretly", "wait and see", or "refuse outright" tend to mark weaker judgement; answers that lean on "explain", "check", "offer", or "flag" tend to mark stronger judgement.
- **Calibrate to the role level.** For trainee-level candidates, strong answers usually involve speaking up promptly, bringing evidence, offering practical help, and looping in a senior colleague at the right moment. Both overstepping (deciding alone) and disappearing (staying silent) should be marked down.

### Common pitfalls

- **Rewarding idealistic-sounding answers over practical ones.** A candidate who says they would "work all night to do both tasks" may sound admirable, but this item is testing what actually works (surfacing the conflict) - do not let apparent selflessness outscore effectiveness.
- **Under-weighting passive options as merely "safe".** Options that delay action, wait to be caught, or pass the problem to someone who cannot act are almost always weak, even when they avoid immediate conflict - mark them accordingly.
- **Missing overreach.** Settling stakeholder disputes personally, changing a senior colleague's work without telling them, or inventing organisational positions all exceed a trainee's authority - note these as weaknesses even if the candidate's intent seems positive.
- **Missing under-reach.** The opposite trap: assuming a trainee candidate should never speak up, never interject, and never question seniors. The strongest answers show courage with humility, and a candidate who never shows initiative should not automatically score well.
- **Ignoring the stated context when marking.** Details like "it can wait until tomorrow" or "the facilitator is momentarily occupied" are placed deliberately in the scenario; an answer that contradicts a stated fact should be marked down even if it otherwise sounds reasonable.
- **Discounting the values dimension.** Accessibility, inclusion, and confidentiality are not side issues in UK public sector scenarios - answers that trade them away for convenience should be marked down heavily.

## Conclusion

You have now worked through the four assessment types most relevant to trainee business architect: cognitive questions using capability models, workshop plans, and strategy alignment checks; numeric questions built on attendance rates, budgets, maturity scores, and benefit projections; verbal questions drawn from governance standards, senior architects' emails, and programme reports; and situational judgement dilemmas about honesty, escalation, accessibility, and balancing learning with delivery. Together, these exercise the skills the role profile names: business architecture awareness, communicating information, consultancy, making and guiding decisions, methods and tools, and strategic thinking.

To score the assessment, work back through each section against the answer key, noting not just whether the candidate reached the correct answer but whether their explanation shows the reasoning this item was built to test - several explanations above flag exactly what a strong versus a weak answer demonstrates. Consider recording strengths and gaps by skill area, since that mapping is often more useful to a hiring or development decision than a single overall score.

When debriefing the candidate, share an honest, constructive summary of how they did and what it suggests about their readiness for the role, without necessarily working through every question and correct answer - this material, or parts of it, may be reused with future candidates. Where the candidate is unsuccessful, focus the debrief on the areas that would most help their development, in the spirit of the profession's emphasis on growth.

Keep a record of how and when you administered the assessment, and administer it consistently across every candidate for this role level: the same items, the same time limits, the same conditions, and the same standard when scoring. Consistency is what makes a comparison between candidates fair, and it is also what protects your organisation if a decision is ever challenged. Remember throughout that this is one input among several in a hiring or promotion decision, never the sole basis for one - see the advisory above.

For further support: consult your organisation's human resources or people development colleagues on how this fits into your wider selection process, and consider asking experienced business architects in your organisation to sense-check whether the scenarios and standards used here reflect how the role is actually practised locally.
