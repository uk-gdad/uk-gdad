The material is job-specific: it presents what an associate performance analyst genuinely does — reading data tables carefully, spotting errors, working out percentages, understanding written guidance, and making sensible judgements when working with colleagues and stakeholders — rather than abstract puzzles.

This document covers four types of assessment, each in its own section:

3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — reading and understanding written material such as service standards, data quality guidance, and stakeholder emails.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes an associate performance analyst uses every day: spotting patterns in data, checking reports for errors, following logical rules, prioritising tasks, and solving practical problems. Unlike a general intelligence test, it uses realistic workplace material — the dashboards, spreadsheets, KPI tables, and analytics reports the role actually handles.

These assessments are usually taken online and typically timed, most often between 15 and 30 minutes, multiple choice, scored against a benchmark group. Many platforms give a few short, ungraded practice questions first, and some are adaptive, so questions may feel like they are getting harder as a candidate does well.

Cognitive ability matters for this role because the core responsibility is the accuracy and quality of data and analysis, and how they are used. Replicating an existing analysis technique means following the logic precisely. Producing a standardised report means spotting the odd number that does not fit the pattern. Receiving a dataset from a senior analyst means checking that it looks right before anyone relies on it.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the skills in the role profile:

- **Analysis and insight**: pattern recognition and logical deduction questions test applying core techniques for analysis and drawing clear, correct conclusions colleagues can understand and use.
- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: error-checking questions test describing and spotting basic data issues, and checking that data and analysis "look right".
- **Technical understanding (performance analyst)**: questions about tool outputs, tracking data, and chart types test awareness of analytical tools and technologies.
- **Performance measurement**: prioritisation and rule-following questions test understanding of how KPIs connect to business objectives.
- **Understanding analysis across the product life cycle**: some questions place data in different service phases (discovery, alpha, beta, live) to test reasoning about how measures vary across the life cycle.
- **User-centred analysis**: problem-solving questions often hinge on remembering that everything focuses on the user of the product or service.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) - Pattern recognition in a weekly report**

A standardised weekly report of completed applications for a licence-renewal service shows the last six weeks: Week 1: 1,200 | Week 2: 1,260 | Week 3: 1,320 | Week 4: 1,380 | Week 5: 1,440 | Week 6: ?

Which figure best fits the pattern for Week 6?

A) 1,470
B) 1,500
C) 1,560
D) 1,620

**Correct answer: B) 1,500**

**Explanation:** The series increases by exactly 60 each week, so Week 6 = 1,440 + 60 = 1,500. This tests recognising a steady trend — which in real reporting helps spot when a new figure breaks the pattern, often the first clue that something has changed.

**Question 2 (easy) - Spotting the data error**

A table of daily visits to a government service's start page, before it goes into a report: the analytics tool never records fractional visits, and the service typically receives between 3,000 and 6,000 visits per day. Monday: 4,512 | Tuesday: 4,388 | Wednesday: 44,102 | Thursday: 4,455 | Friday: 4,610. Which value most likely contains an error, and why?

A) Monday, because it is the highest normal value
B) Tuesday, because it is the lowest value
C) Wednesday, because it is roughly ten times larger than every other day
D) Friday, because visits should fall at the end of the week

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Wednesday's figure is about ten times every other day and far outside the typical range — often a data entry mistake or a tracking fault. Option D relies on an assumption the question does not support. This tests checking whether data "looks right" against a known typical range — a basic quality assurance method.

**Question 3 (easy) - Following a reporting rule**

A team's rule for the monthly KPI report is: "Include a metric in the exceptions section if it moved more than 10% in either direction compared with last month." Completion rate: 82% → 84%. Average session time: 4.0 → 4.6 minutes. Error page views: 900 → 980. Calls to helpline: 2,000 → 1,750. Which metrics belong in the exceptions section?

A) Completion rate and error page views
B) Average session time and calls to helpline
C) Only calls to helpline
D) All four metrics

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Completion rate moves ≈2.4% (under); session time moves 15% (over); error views move ≈8.9% (under); helpline calls fall 12.5% (over, and the rule covers "either direction"). This tests applying a written rule precisely, without letting instinct override it — the discipline needed to replicate an existing reporting technique.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Logical deduction about tracking data**

Three facts about a service's analytics setup: (1) every page with the new tracking tag records the user's journey stage; (2) the "check your answers" page does not record the user's journey stage; (3) all pages in the payment section have the new tracking tag. Which conclusion must be true?

A) The "check your answers" page is in the payment section
B) The "check your answers" page does not have the new tracking tag
C) The payment section does not record journey stages
D) Some pages in the payment section are missing the tracking tag

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** If the page had the tag it would record the journey stage (fact 1); it does not (fact 2); so it cannot have the tag. This tests careful "if-then" reasoning — the same reasoning used when diagnosing why a metric is missing from a dashboard.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Prioritising a morning**

At 9:00, four tasks compete for attention, with a team stand-up at 10:00 (15 minutes): (1) refresh the standard weekly dashboard, 30 minutes, presented at 11:00; (2) reply to a community-of-practice email, 5 minutes, no deadline; (3) investigate why yesterday's data upload shows 0 transactions, 45 minutes, flagged by a senior analyst as blocking today's analysis; (4) tidy the shared folder structure, 60 minutes, no deadline. Which order makes the most sense?

A) 4, 2, 1, 3
B) 3, 1, 2, 4
C) 1, 3, 2, 4
D) 2, 4, 3, 1

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Task 3 is highest priority (a blocking data fault could corrupt the dashboard refresh); starting at 9:00 finishes it by 9:45, in time for stand-up; the dashboard refresh then fits before 11:00. This tests recognising that quality comes before speed — refreshing a dashboard before checking a known data fault risks presenting inaccurate figures.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Which chart looks wrong?**

A standardised monthly report contains four charts: (1) a line chart of weekly digital take-up, 61%–66% over 12 weeks; (2) a bar chart of completions by channel: online 8,200, phone 1,400, post 600, total labelled 10,800; (3) a pie chart of device share: mobile 54%, desktop 38%, tablet 8%; (4) a line chart of average satisfaction (out of 5), 4.1–4.3. Which chart contains an internal inconsistency?

A) Chart 1
B) Chart 2
C) Chart 3
D) Chart 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 8,200 + 1,400 + 600 = 10,200, not the labelled 10,800 — a 600 mismatch. The other charts are internally coherent (the pie chart sums to 100%). This tests re-adding a labelled total rather than trusting it, and being able to demonstrate the specific error rather than saying a chart "looks off".

**Question 7 (moderate) - Odd one out in metric definitions**

Three of these describe measures of user behaviour on a service; one describes something different. Which is the odd one out?

A) The percentage of users who abandon the form at the address page
B) The average number of attempts before a successful sign-in
C) The number of civil servants who have completed data protection training
D) The proportion of sessions that come from mobile devices

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A, B, and D describe how users behave on the digital service; C measures staff training compliance, an internal organisational measure. This tests classifying metrics correctly — mixing user-behaviour KPIs with internal measures leads to muddled dashboards and muddled decisions.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Deducing the cause of a data gap**

A dashboard shows zero page views for 14 and 15 June. Facts: (1) the service was live throughout June (the call centre logged normal demand); (2) a new cookie-consent banner was deployed on the evening of 13 June and rolled back on the morning of 16 June; (3) the analytics tool only records visits from users who accept cookies. What is the most logical explanation for the gap?

A) The service was actually offline on 14 and 15 June
B) Users stopped visiting the service on those days
C) The new banner prevented cookie consent from being recorded, so the analytics tool logged no visits
D) The analytics vendor deleted the data

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The faulty banner's window matches the gap exactly, and fact 1 rules out A and B. This tests recognising that zeros in analytics data usually mean "not measured", not "nothing happened" — understanding the context of the data is a quality assurance skill.

**Question 9 (moderate) - Applying life cycle logic**

A team supports two services: Service X in private beta with 500 invited users; Service Y live for three years with two million users a year. A colleague proposes using exactly the same KPI targets for both. Which is the strongest logical objection?

A) Beta services should not be measured at all
B) Measures and targets should vary by life cycle stage: a beta service's data answers "is this working and improving?", while a live service's data tracks performance at scale against established benchmarks
C) Two million users is too many to measure accurately
D) The services should use different analytics tools

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A small-sample beta and a mature live service answer different questions with their data; identical targets would be unrealistic for one and uninformative for the other. This tests understanding that performance measures vary across the product life cycle.

**Question 10 (hard) - Multi-step problem solving**

Reconciling two sources: the analytics tool reports 12,000 completed online applications in July; the case-management system reports 12,900 applications received in July. Facts: (1) the case-management system includes all channels — online, phone, post; (2) phone applications in July numbered 620; (3) postal applications numbered 310; (4) the analytics tool loses roughly 1–3% of online completions to cookie refusal. Do the two sources reasonably agree?

A) No - there is an unexplained gap of 900 applications
B) Yes - after removing phone and post, the case system implies 11,970 online applications, and the analytics figure of 12,000 is within the expected cookie-loss tolerance of that figure
C) No - the analytics figure should always be higher than the case system figure
D) Cannot tell without data from June

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 12,900 − 620 − 310 = 11,970 online applications from the case system; 12,000 from analytics is a difference of only 0.25%, well within normal noise. This tests breaking a total into comparable parts, comparing like with like, and judging differences against known tolerances rather than expecting perfection.

**Question 11 (hard) - Error checking a calculated column**

A spreadsheet has Started, Completed, and Completion rate (Completed ÷ Started, as a percentage): Row 1: 2,000 | 1,500 | 75%. Row 2: 1,600 | 1,240 | 77.5%. Row 3: 2,400 | 1,850 | 82.4%. Row 4: 3,000 | 2,310 | 77%. Which row contains a calculation error?

A) Row 1
B) Row 2
C) Row 3
D) Row 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 1,850 ÷ 2,400 ≈ 77.1%, not the stated 82.4%. This tests an estimation-first check (1,850 is only a little more than three-quarters of 2,400, so the rate should be barely above 75%) to scan a report quickly before reaching for a calculator.

**Question 12 (hard) - Choosing the right technique**

A service manager asks: "Users seem to drop out somewhere in the application journey - can you find out where?" Which analytical approach best answers the question?

A) A funnel analysis showing the percentage of users progressing through each step of the journey, to locate the step with the largest drop
B) A pie chart of traffic sources, to show where users come from
C) A year-on-year comparison of total visits
D) A word cloud of feedback comments

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The question is about where in the journey users leave, requiring a technique that breaks the journey into steps and measures progression. This tests matching the technique to the question — the heart of analysis and insight.

**Question 13 (hard) - Combining conditions**

A data quality checklist says a dataset may be marked "fit for purpose" only if ALL of these hold: (1) less than 5% of records have missing values; (2) the date range covers the full reporting month; (3) totals reconcile with the source system within 2%. An assessed dataset has: 3% missing values; date range 1st–28th of a 31-day month; totals reconcile within 0.8%. What should be concluded?

A) Fit for purpose - two out of three checks pass, which is a majority
B) Not fit for purpose - condition 2 fails because three days of the month are missing, and the rule requires all conditions to pass
C) Fit for purpose - the missing days probably contain little data
D) Cannot assess without knowing the sample size

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule requires ALL conditions; condition 2 fails since the data stops three days early. Option C guesses rather than checks — precisely what quality assurance processes exist to prevent. This tests applying a unanimity rule correctly rather than a majority rule.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate re-adds a labelled total** rather than trusting it, and can demonstrate the specific error found.
- **Watch for whether a candidate applies a written rule precisely** — "all", "only", "either direction" — rather than judging by instinct.
- **Note whether a candidate estimates before calculating**, to scan a report quickly.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes "not measured" from "did not happen"** in a data-gap item.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and consider offering ungraded practice items first.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that skims the table** rather than reading it properly — misreading a column header can make every subsequent step wrong.
- **Crediting an answer built on an outside assumption** the question does not support.
- **Letting a time-sink question distort the overall picture.**
- **Missing when a candidate's answer is implausible at scale** — an answer ten times too big usually signals a slipped decimal point.
- **Missing when a candidate changes a correct answer under pressure** without being able to point to a specific mistake in their first reasoning.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures a candidate's ability to read, interpret, and make sound decisions from numerical data. It is not a school maths exam: no algebra for its own sake and no formulas to memorise. The questions use the kinds of numbers an associate performance analyst meets — completion rates, digital take-up percentages, cost per transaction, visit counts, satisfaction scores, and simple budget figures.

The role summary for an associate performance analyst asks for "numeracy and some experience in analysis or data handling", so this assessment speaks directly to the foundation of the job. The maths itself is deliberately basic: percentages, ratios, averages, differences, and simple projections. Numeric reasoning tests are usually strictly timed, often around 60 to 90 seconds per question, and most allow or provide an on-screen calculator — the challenge is interpretation, not mental arithmetic.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Analysis and insight**: extracting the correct figures from a table and applying core techniques — percentage change, rates, averages.
- **Performance measurement**: many questions involve KPIs and targets, testing whether a measure is on track against a business objective.
- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: several questions ask for verifying a calculated figure or spotting an implausible one.
- **Communicating analysis and insight**: choosing the correct percentage or rate is the first step to giving tactical recommendations.
- **Understanding analysis across the product life cycle**: questions may compare a small beta cohort with a large live service, testing sensible handling of small and large denominators.
- **Technical understanding**: data is presented the way analytical tools present it — dashboards, exports, summary tables.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded. A calculator is allowed.

**Question 1 (easy) - Percentage completion rate**

In one week, 4,500 users started an online passport-renewal application and 3,600 completed it. What was the completion rate?

A) 75%
B) 80%
C) 85%
D) 90%

**Correct answer: B) 80%**

**Explanation:** 3,600 ÷ 4,500 = 80%. This tests a calculation that should become second nature — completion rate is one of the most common service KPIs.

**Question 2 (easy) - Percentage change**

Visits to a service's guidance page were 15,000 in March and 18,000 in April. What was the percentage increase?

A) 3%
B) 15%
C) 20%
D) 30%

**Correct answer: C) 20%**

**Explanation:** 3,000 ÷ 15,000 = 20%. This tests dividing the increase by the original value, not the new value.

**Question 3 (easy) - Reading a data table**

| Channel | January | February |
|---|---|---|
| Online | 6,200 | 6,800 |
| Phone | 1,500 | 1,300 |
| Post | 800 | 700 |

What percentage of February's applications arrived online (to the nearest whole per cent)?

A) 70%
B) 74%
C) 77%
D) 80%

**Correct answer: C) 77%**

**Explanation:** February total = 8,800; 6,800 ÷ 8,800 ≈ 77%. This tests using the February column throughout rather than mixing months — digital take-up is a headline government service measure worth practising.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Cost per transaction**

A service costs £54,000 a month to run and processed 30,000 transactions this month. Next month, costs stay the same, but transactions are forecast to rise by 20%. What will the cost per transaction be next month?

A) £1.80
B) £1.50
C) £1.44
D) £2.16

**Correct answer: B) £1.50**

**Explanation:** Next month's transactions = 36,000; £54,000 ÷ 36,000 = £1.50. This tests checking the direction of an answer — more transactions for the same cost make each one cheaper, not dearer.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Averages across a week**

Daily completed applications: Monday 820, Tuesday 790, Wednesday 850, Thursday 910, Friday 880, Saturday 310, Sunday 240. What was the average per day, to the nearest whole number?

A) 686
B) 850
C) 600
D) 743

**Correct answer: A) 686**

**Explanation:** Sum = 4,800; ÷7 ≈ 686. This tests including the full week rather than a weekday-only average, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noticing that a single weekly mean hides a large weekday/weekend gap.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Ratios**

A survey of 1,200 service users found 900 "satisfied" and 300 "dissatisfied". What is the ratio of satisfied to dissatisfied users in its simplest form?

A) 4:1
B) 3:1
C) 900:300
D) 1:3

**Correct answer: B) 3:1**

**Explanation:** 900:300 simplifies to 3:1. This tests simplifying a ratio to its lowest terms and keeping the stated order.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Percentage points versus percentages**

A service's digital take-up rose from 60% in Quarter 1 to 69% in Quarter 2. Which statement is accurate?

A) Take-up rose by 9 percentage points, which is a 15% relative increase
B) Take-up rose by 9%, which is 9 percentage points, so the two phrasings are always interchangeable
C) Take-up rose by 15 percentage points
D) Take-up rose by 6.9%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Absolute change = 9 points; relative change = 9 ÷ 60 = 15%. This tests distinguishing percentage points from percentages — one of the most common numerical mistakes in performance reporting.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Projecting from a run rate**

By the end of day 12 of a 30-day month, a new online reporting tool has received 3,000 submissions. If submissions continue at the same daily rate, how many will the tool receive in the full month?

A) 6,000
B) 7,500
C) 9,000
D) 4,500

**Correct answer: B) 7,500**

**Explanation:** 3,000 ÷ 12 = 250 per day; × 30 = 7,500. This tests a run-rate projection, and, for a strong candidate's answer, labelling it as an assumption rather than a certainty, since real demand often has weekly cycles.

**Question 9 (moderate) - Verifying a colleague's figure**

A draft report states: "Error page views fell 25% from 1,600 in May to 1,240 in June." Is the claim right?

A) Yes - the calculation is correct
B) No - the fall is 22.5%, so the claim overstates the improvement
C) No - the fall is 360 views, which is 29%
D) Cannot tell without July's data

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 360 ÷ 1,600 = 22.5%, not 25%. This tests quality-assuring a figure before it is quoted onward, and catching an overstatement before it damages credibility.

**Question 10 (hard) - Multi-step: channel shift savings**

A council service handles 40,000 transactions a year: 70% online at £0.90 each and 30% by phone at £5.10 each. A planned improvement shifts a quarter of the phone transactions to the online channel. How much would the service save per year?

A) £12,600
B) £50,400
C) £15,300
D) £3,000

**Correct answer: A) £12,600**

**Explanation:** Phone transactions = 12,000; shifting = 3,000; saving per shifted transaction = £5.10 − £0.90 = £4.20; total = £12,600. This tests recognising that a shifted transaction still costs something online, so the saving is the difference between channel costs, not the full phone cost.

**Question 11 (hard) - Weighted average satisfaction**

Application step: score 4.2 from 1,000 responses. Payment step: 3.6 from 500. Confirmation step: 4.5 from 500. What is the overall average satisfaction score across all responses?

A) 4.10
B) 4.13
C) 4.30
D) 4.05

**Correct answer: B) 4.13**

**Explanation:** Weighted total = 8,250 ÷ 2,000 = 4.125 ≈ 4.13. This tests weighting by response count rather than taking a simple unweighted average, which would undercount the larger application step.

**Question 12 (hard) - Small samples across the life cycle**

The live service completed 41,500 of 50,000 attempts last month (83%). A private beta of the redesigned journey completed 44 of 50 attempts (88%). A stakeholder says: "The beta journey is clearly better - take-up is 5 percentage points higher." What is the most numerically sound response?

A) Agree - 88% is higher than 83%, so the beta is better
B) Point out that the beta figure comes from only 50 attempts, so a difference of a few completions either way would swing the rate substantially; the comparison is suggestive but not yet reliable
C) Disagree - live services always outperform betas
D) Average the two figures to get a combined rate of 85.5%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With only 50 attempts, each completion is worth 2 percentage points — a small change would move the beta rate substantially. This tests calibrating confidence to sample size, part of understanding analysis across the product life cycle.

**Question 13 (hard) - Reading a percentage table backwards**

35% of a service's 24,000 monthly sessions come from mobile devices, with a completion rate of 65%. Desktop and tablet sessions (the remaining 65%) have a completion rate of 80%. How many sessions completed in total?

A) 17,940
B) 5,460
C) 12,480
D) 18,600

**Correct answer: A) 17,940**

**Explanation:** Mobile completions = 8,400 × 0.65 = 5,460; other completions = 15,600 × 0.80 = 12,480; total = 17,940. This tests re-reading exactly what is asked ("in total") rather than stopping at a partial answer.

**Question 14 (hard) - Budget share and reallocation**

An annual tools budget of £48,000: 50% analytics platform, 25% survey tools, 15% dashboard software, 10% training. Survey tool spending is cut by a third, moved to training. How much will the training budget be after the change?

A) £4,800
B) £8,800
C) £9,600
D) £12,000

**Correct answer: B) £8,800**

**Explanation:** Survey budget = £12,000; a third = £4,000; training = £4,800 + £4,000 = £8,800. This tests writing each intermediate figure down in a multi-step money question, where errors usually occur between steps.

**Question 15 (moderate) - Converting a rate into volumes**

A benefits-enquiry service receives 8,000 calls a month. 45% of calls are about topics fully answered by an existing online guidance page. If better signposting deflects half of those calls, how many calls would the centre still receive each month?

A) 3,600
B) 4,400
C) 6,200
D) 1,800

**Correct answer: C) 6,200**

**Explanation:** Deflectable calls = 3,600; deflected = 1,800; remaining = 8,000 − 1,800 = 6,200. This tests reading the final sentence of a question carefully — "still receives" requires subtracting from the original total, not reporting an intermediate figure.

**Question 16 (hard) - Indexing against a baseline**

A form-error rate index sets January to 100. February was 8% lower than January; March was 10% higher than February. What is the index value for March, to one decimal place?

A) 102.0
B) 101.2
C) 98.0
D) 100.0

**Correct answer: B) 101.2**

**Explanation:** February = 92; March = 92 × 1.10 = 101.2. This tests recognising that sequential percentage changes multiply, not add — adding −8% and +10% to get +2% treats them as applying to the same base, which they do not.

**Question 17 (hard) - Choosing the right denominator**

A service's start page received 20,000 unique visitors; 12,000 clicked "Start now"; 9,000 of those completed. Two draft statements: (1) "The completion rate from start of application was 75%." (2) "45% of start-page visitors went on to complete the application." Which assessment is correct?

A) Both statements are correct, and they measure different things
B) Statement 1 is wrong; the completion rate is 45%
C) Statement 2 is wrong; the figure should be 60%
D) Both statements are wrong

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 9,000 ÷ 12,000 = 75%; 9,000 ÷ 20,000 = 45% — both correct, each with a different, clearly stated denominator. This tests the single most valuable habit in performance measurement: asking "per cent of what?"

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate names the base** before applying a percentage.
- **Watch for whether a candidate writes intermediate steps down** on multi-step problems.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes percentage points from percentages.**
- **Note whether a candidate calibrates confidence to sample size** on small-sample items.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that misreads a chart or table label** — monthly confused with annual, or a missed "in thousands".
- **Crediting a percentage divided by the wrong base.**
- **Rewarding over-calculation** where an estimate would find the answer faster.
- **Letting a time-sink question distort the overall picture.**
- **Missing when a candidate answers with an intermediate result** rather than what was actually asked.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment checks whether a candidate can work out exactly what a piece of writing establishes — not what it implies, and not what they already believe to be true.

For an associate performance analyst the material is the writing that surrounds analysis: a performance measurement framework, a definition of a key performance indicator, a data quality note, guidance on a tool, or a summary of what a service is supposed to achieve.

The usual format gives a passage and then statements to mark True, False or Cannot Say: True — the passage establishes it. False — the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say — the passage settles it neither way. Cannot Say is a confident, correct answer rather than an admission of being stuck, and in a well-built test there will be several.

Two things are worth knowing. First, definitions in performance frameworks are precise, and small words carry them: "completed transactions", "unique users", "per quarter", "excluding resubmissions" each changes what a number means. Second, what a candidate already knows is the main hazard: they may know perfectly well how a metric is usually calculated, but if the passage defines it differently, the passage wins.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading definitions precisely** maps to **Performance measurement**, where KPIs and frameworks are used.
- **Reading data quality notes** maps to **Quality assurance of data and analysis**.
- **Reading tool documentation** maps to **Technical understanding (performance analyst)**.
- **Reading a brief accurately** maps to working under guidance on projects with limited scope.
- **Describing findings accurately** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**.
- **Reading about users** maps to **User-centred analysis**.

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

> "The service's completion rate is defined as the number of applications submitted divided by the number of applications started, expressed as a percentage, measured per calendar month. An application is counted as started when the user reaches the first question page. Applications abandoned and later resumed within 30 days count once, at the point they were first started. Applications from users who begin more than one application in a month are counted separately. Test transactions are excluded. The rate is reported to one decimal place."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: The completion rate is measured per calendar month.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests reading a whole definition before answering the first question about it.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: An application is counted as started when the user lands on the service's home page.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says started is "when the user reaches the first question page", not the home page. This tests catching a precise but easily misread definition — counting from the home page would produce a very different rate.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: A user who starts two applications in the same month is counted once.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Counted separately" contradicts "counted once". This tests noticing the metric counts applications, not users — a common trap since "unique users" is such a familiar measure elsewhere.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: An application started in January, abandoned, and resumed and submitted in February counts towards February's completion rate.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage settles where the start is attributed (January) but is silent on where the submission is counted. This tests recognising a genuine gap in a definition — exactly the kind of question worth taking back to whoever wrote it.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

> "The data quality note records that tracking was not functioning on the mobile version of the service between 3 and 11 March. Desktop tracking was unaffected. Figures for March should therefore be treated as incomplete. The analyst has estimated the missing mobile volume by applying February's mobile-to-desktop ratio to March's desktop figures. The estimate is shown separately in the dashboard and is not included in the published headline figure. Comparisons between March and other months should note the gap."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: Desktop tracking worked normally throughout March.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "Desktop tracking was unaffected" is stated directly. This tests locating a direct confirmation.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: The published headline figure for March includes an estimate of the missing mobile data.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The estimate "is not included in the published headline figure". This tests reading a described good practice correctly — a headline figure missing eight days of mobile traffic changes what a month-to-month comparison actually shows.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: The estimate of missing mobile volume is accurate.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage describes the method, not its accuracy, which nobody can know since the real data was never captured. This tests distinguishing a described method from a validated result.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: Mobile traffic was lower than usual between 3 and 11 March.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Recorded traffic was lower because tracking failed; actual traffic is a different, unaddressed question. This tests the important distinction between "we did not measure it" and "it did not happen".

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

> "The performance framework sets four key performance indicators for the service: completion rate, user satisfaction, cost per transaction, and time to complete. Targets are set annually by the service owner. The framework states that satisfaction is measured by a survey offered at the end of the transaction, with a target of 80% rating the service good or very good. Cost per transaction is calculated quarterly. The framework notes that time to complete excludes time when the application is awaiting information from a third party. Reporting against the framework is monthly, except for cost per transaction."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: The framework sets four key performance indicators.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly and then listed. This tests counting a list against a stated number — a classic error-checking habit.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: Cost per transaction is reported monthly.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reporting is monthly "except for cost per transaction", and that metric is calculated quarterly. This tests reading to the end of a passage before answering — a reader stopping after the first sentence about monthly reporting would answer wrongly.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: The satisfaction figure reflects the views of all users of the service.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The survey is offered "at the end of the transaction", reaching only people who got that far. This tests recognising a limitation the passage's own description of the method establishes.

**Question 12 (harder)**

Statement: A service meeting its satisfaction target has 80% of its users rating it good or very good.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The target concerns survey respondents, not all users, and respondents are a subset who completed and chose to answer. This tests holding a definition from earlier in the passage while reading a claim that quietly substitutes one word.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads a whole definition before answering** the first question about it.
- **Watch for whether a candidate notices small qualifying words** — "started", "submitted", "per calendar month", "excluding".
- **Note whether a candidate checks whether a metric counts people or events.**
- **Note whether a candidate treats Cannot Say as a genuine answer** rather than avoiding it.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer from how a metric is usually defined** rather than the passage's own definition.
- **Missing when a candidate assumes a rate counts unique users** without checking.
- **Crediting an answer that treats a described method as a validated result.**
- **Missing when a candidate reads a tracking outage as a fall in real activity.**
- **Missing an exception clause in the last sentence of a passage.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment gives realistic situations from an associate performance analyst's working life and asks what the candidate would do. The situations are ordinary, and the question is which response an experienced analyst would choose.

The situations involve working under guidance, checking one's own work, being asked for numbers quickly, spotting something that looks wrong, and explaining analysis to people who will act on it.

Candidates usually pick the most effective and least effective response, or rate each. Scoring is against a key built from experienced practitioners, aiming at professional consensus rather than personal style.

Three things explain most of the answer key. First, the role summary states explicitly that this level is responsible for the accuracy of its analysis — unusual for an entry-level role, and it shapes many answers: checking before publishing, flagging a number one is unsure about, and correcting an error already sent are all part of the job rather than signs of inexperience. Second, asking is expected: this role works under the guidance of more senior colleagues, and a question early is the arrangement working properly. Third, a number without context can mislead even when it is correct — much of the craft of this job is making sure the person receiving analysis understands what it does and does not show.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Accuracy and checking** maps to **Quality assurance of data and analysis**.
- **Working under guidance** maps to the role level's description.
- **Explaining findings** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**.
- **Understanding what a metric shows** maps to **Performance measurement**.
- **Tool use** maps to **Technical understanding (performance analyst)**.
- **Community engagement** maps to engaging with the performance analysis community.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — A number that looks wrong**

A routine report is being produced and one figure is much higher than usual. What is the most effective response?

- A) Check it before sending — look at whether the underlying data changed, whether a filter or date range is different, and whether anything happened in the service that would explain it.
- B) Send it; the tool produced it.
- C) Send it with a note saying the figure looks unusual.
- D) Ask a colleague to look at the whole report.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Option A lists the three things that explain most such cases, and ten minutes tells which. Option B is least effective: this role is responsible for accuracy, and "the tool produced it" is not a check. Option C pushes the question to a reader with less context. Option D is reasonable if the candidate cannot resolve it alone.

**Question 2 (easy) — An urgent request**

Someone asks for a figure by the end of the day. It can be produced but not checked properly. What is the most effective response?

- A) Send the figure and say clearly that it is unchecked, what has not been verified, and when it could be confirmed.
- B) Send the figure.
- C) Say it cannot be produced in the time.
- D) Send an approximate figure rounded heavily.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Option A gives the requester what they need to decide how much weight to put on the figure. Option B is least effective: an unlabelled unchecked figure will be treated as verified and quoted onward. Option D disguises uncertainty as false precision. Option C withholds something that could be provided.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Stuck on a tool**

Most of the morning has been spent trying to get a query to produce what is needed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Keep going; the answer will come eventually.
- B) Ask a colleague, saying what is being attempted, what has been tried, and where it is going wrong — because a few minutes of their time saves the rest of the day, and the summary makes it quick for them to help.
- C) Produce the analysis a different way that is known to work, even though it takes longer.
- D) Wait until the next team meeting to raise it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A morning is long enough for a genuine attempt, making this the right moment to ask. The three-part summary — what's being attempted, what's been tried, where it's going wrong — turns a vague interruption into a quickly answerable question. Option D can waste days and is exactly what working under senior guidance is for.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A finding someone will not like**

Analysis shows a change the team made has not improved the metric it was meant to improve. What is the most effective response?

- A) Report what was found, clearly and without softening it, and include what the data does and does not show.
- B) Report it, noting that the sample is small so it may change.
- C) Wait another month to see whether the picture changes.
- D) Report the metrics that did improve.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Reporting the finding is the job, and stating what the data does and does not show is what makes it useful rather than blunt. Option D is least effective: selecting favourable metrics actively misleads and, once noticed, means nobody trusts the analysis again.

**Question 5 (moderate) — An error already sent**

A report sent yesterday is found to contain a mistake. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell the recipients now, explain what was wrong and what the corrected figures are, and say what will be done to avoid it recurring.
- B) Correct it quietly in the next report.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone noticed.
- D) Tell a manager and ask what to do.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** People may already be acting on the wrong number, making speed more important than comfort. Option C is least effective: a mistake found later by someone else is far worse than one corrected proactively, and the delay damages trust more than the error itself.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A request for a metric that will mislead**

A colleague asks for "the number of users last month". The figure available counts sessions, not people. What is the most effective response?

- A) Give the sessions figure, labelled clearly as sessions, and explain the difference — one person visiting four times is four sessions — so they can tell whether it answers their question.
- B) Give the sessions figure as the number of users.
- C) Say the question cannot be answered.
- D) Give the sessions figure and let them ask if they need more.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Sessions and users are commonly muddled, and naming which one is being given prevents a large downstream error. Option B is least effective: a mislabelled figure will be quoted, compared, and planned against incorrectly.

**Question 7 (harder) — Being asked to explain a change that cannot yet be explained**

A service manager asks why a metric moved last week. The movement is visible but the cause is unknown. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say what can be seen, say plainly that the cause is not yet known, and say what will be checked and by when.
- B) Offer the most likely explanation.
- C) Say the cause is unknown.
- D) Say it is probably normal variation.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Option A separates observation from explanation, is honest about the gap, and gives a timescale — reading as competent rather than uncertain. Option D is least effective: it offers an untested, reassuring-sounding explanation that stops anyone investigating.

**Question 8 (harder) — Replicating existing analysis**

Reproducing a colleague's analysis from last quarter, following their method, produces a noticeably different answer. What is the most effective response?

- A) Go through both versions to find where they diverge — usually a date range, a filter, or a changed data source — and raise it with the colleague with the specific difference.
- B) Use the new figure; the method was followed.
- C) Use the colleague's figure; they are more experienced.
- D) Report both figures.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A difference between two runs of the same method has a specific, findable cause. Option C is least effective: deferring to seniority without investigating leaves a possible error in the record unresolved.

**Question 9 (harder) — Feedback on a piece of analysis**

A senior analyst leaves a lot of comments on a piece of work. What is the most effective response?

- A) Work through them, make the changes that are understood, and ask about any that are not — because the reasoning behind a comment is what stops the same thing recurring.
- B) Make all the changes without asking.
- C) Explain why the work was done that way.
- D) Ask a different colleague whether the comments are fair.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A heavily commented review is an investment of time. Option D is least effective: quietly checking whether the reviewer was fair goes around the person trying to help, and would land badly if discovered.

**Question 10 (harder) — A dashboard nobody uses**

Access logs show almost nobody opens a dashboard that is maintained regularly. What is the most effective response?

- A) Mention it to a manager or the team it was built for, and ask whether it is still needed or should show something different.
- B) Keep maintaining it; it was requested.
- C) Stop maintaining it.
- D) Add more charts to make it more useful.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Raising the observation as a question is genuinely valuable. Option C is least effective: quietly stopping means it breaks silently and is discovered at the worst moment.

**Question 11 (harder) — Too much work**

More analysis has been assigned for the week than can be completed to a standard the candidate is happy with. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say so now, explain which pieces are at risk, and ask which matter most.
- B) Do the best that can be managed and see how far it gets.
- C) Complete everything, accepting that the checking will be lighter than usual.
- D) Say something at the end of the week if it is not finished.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Raising it now allows reprioritisation while there is still time. Option C is the tempting trap: quietly reducing checking to fit the volume produces analysis that looks complete and may be wrong — a direct conflict with responsibility for accuracy.

**Question 12 (harder) — Joining the community**

An invitation arrives to a cross-government performance analysis community session, during a busy week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Go, because engaging with the community is named explicitly at this level and these sessions are where other people's solutions to the same problems can be found.
- B) Skip it; delivery work comes first.
- C) Go if there is nothing more urgent.
- D) Ask a colleague to summarise it afterwards.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Community engagement is named explicitly at this level. Option B is least effective: "delivery first" is always available as a reason and, applied consistently, means never going.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate checks an unexpected figure before it leaves their hands.**
- **Watch for whether a candidate labels an unchecked number as unchecked** rather than presenting it as verified.
- **Note whether a candidate separates what was observed from what can be explained.**
- **Note whether a candidate raises a capacity problem at the start of the week**, not the end.
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that sends a figure because the tool produced it**, without checking.
- **Crediting an answer that reports only the metrics that improved.**
- **Missing when a candidate offers an untested explanation because it sounds reassuring.**
- **Missing when a candidate defers to a more experienced colleague instead of finding the difference.**
- **Missing when a candidate cuts their checking to fit the workload** rather than raising the capacity problem.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to an associate performance analyst: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested working carefully from what is in front of the candidate — spotting the pattern in a set of figures, following a definition through several conditions, and checking details rather than trusting a first impression. The numeric section tested ordinary arithmetic done accurately under mild time pressure: percentages and their bases, rates, averages, and units. The verbal section tested reading exactly what a definition says and setting aside how a metric is usually calculated — "started" means what the framework says it means, a tracking outage is not a fall in activity, and a satisfaction target is a percentage of respondents, not of all users. The situational judgement section came back repeatedly to two habits: checking work before it leaves the candidate's hands, and saying things early — asking when stuck, flagging an unchecked figure, correcting a mistake, telling someone the work will not fit.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can work carefully with data, read definitions precisely, and be trusted to check their own work and speak up early — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
