# Performance Analyst (Head of Performance Analysis) - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is a practical resource for the assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for the head of performance analysis role — a panel, a senior manager, or a reviewer running a structured assessment as part of hiring or promotion into this role level.

Psychometric assessments of this kind are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural judgement that underpin effective performance in a role. They are widely used across the Civil Service and the wider public sector because, administered consistently, they are evidence-based, fair, and predictive of on-the-job success. For a leadership role such as this, they are rarely about raw speed of calculation. Instead, they probe the deeper capabilities the role demands: interpreting complex performance data across multiple services, spotting inconsistencies in analytical outputs before they reach ministers or boards, weighing competing priorities when several teams escalate issues at once, and exercising sound judgement in politically sensitive situations. Administering it fairly is the assessor's responsibility: present the same items under the same time limit to every candidate assessed for this role level, and hold the worked answers and explanations back as an answer key until scoring and debrief.

This document covers four assessment types, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** - the candidate's ability to reason, deduce, spot patterns, check for errors, and solve problems using the kinds of artefacts a head of profession handles: KPI frameworks, quality assurance criteria, community capability plans, and escalation logs.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** - the candidate's ability to interpret and manipulate the numbers this role lives with: service performance metrics, completion rates, cost per transaction, capability budgets, and recruitment figures.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** - the candidate's ability to extract precise meaning from dense written material: strategy documents, service standard guidance, stakeholder emails, and quality assurance policies.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** - the candidate's behavioural judgement in realistic dilemmas involving senior stakeholders, the performance analysis community, cross-government collaboration, and the political dimensions of analysis.

Each section explains what the assessment measures, maps it explicitly to the skills in the role profile, provides a substantial set of items with worked answers and explanations forming the answer key, and closes with guidance on administering and scoring it.

How should the assessor use this guide? Present the items under the timing suggested, using the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed at this role level. Keep the worked answers and explanations out of the candidate's sight throughout the session. After scoring, use the explanations as the basis of a debrief with the candidate — many of them model reasoning habits, verifying before trusting, reading exact wording, considering strategic and political context, that distinguish strong heads of profession, and are worth discussing directly rather than only scoring. Keep a record of what was administered and how it was scored, so the process is repeatable and defensible across candidates. This assessment is one input among several into a hiring or promotion decision, never the sole basis for one — see the advisory below.

## Advisory

This assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance colleagues.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes a head of performance analysis relies on daily: logical deduction, pattern recognition, error checking, prioritisation, and structured problem solving. Unlike a generic IQ test, every question is grounded in artefacts a candidate would genuinely handle - performance measurement frameworks, quality assurance checklists, escalation logs, community capability matrices, and analytical outputs from multiple teams.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes, with questions that mimic the everyday data formats of the role. Score objectively, usually benchmarked against a norm group of comparable professionals. Modern platforms may adapt question difficulty based on a candidate's answers; record a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score. Consider offering short, ungraded practice questions first so a candidate can get comfortable with the interface, applied identically to every candidate.

For a head-of-profession role, cognitive assessments matter because the postholder's decisions ripple. When they sign off a quality assurance framework, dozens of analysts apply it. When they deduce which escalation genuinely needs intervention, whole programmes are unblocked or delayed. This assessment is designed to produce evidence that a candidate can reason accurately under pressure with realistic, messy inputs - and notices when something does not add up before anyone else does.

### What it measures for this role

Mapped to the skills in the role profile:

- **Analysis and insight**: questions test whether a candidate can apply a range of techniques to interpret data, draw sound inferences, and spot the innovative reframing that resolves a stuck problem.
- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: error-checking items test the candidate's ability to verify and validate figures, definitions, and logic across outputs produced by multiple teams - exactly the assurance role this position holds.
- **Performance measurement**: pattern and consistency questions use KPI frameworks across multiple projects, testing whether a candidate can see where frameworks are misaligned with business needs or with each other.
- **Understanding analysis across the product life cycle**: deduction questions probe whether a candidate can identify risks that sit between life cycle phases, where they are easiest to miss.
- **Technical understanding**: several items test structured reasoning about analytics tooling choices and their consequences.
- **User-centred analysis**: prioritisation questions require the candidate to keep the end user's outcome at the centre while juggling organisational pressures.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) - Pattern recognition in a KPI framework**

A candidate is reviewing the KPI definitions used by four service teams. Three follow a consistent pattern; one does not.

- Team A: "Digital take-up = digital transactions ÷ total transactions, monthly, GOV.UK analytics"
- Team B: "Completion rate = completed journeys ÷ started journeys, monthly, GOV.UK analytics"
- Team C: "User satisfaction = satisfied responses ÷ total responses, monthly, exit survey"
- Team D: "Cost per transaction = total service cost ÷ digital transactions, monthly, finance system"

Which definition breaks the framework's internal consistency, and why?

A) Team A - digital take-up should be measured weekly
B) Team B - completion rate should use unique users, not journeys
C) Team C - satisfaction uses a survey rather than analytics
D) Team D - the denominator uses digital transactions rather than total transactions

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The first three metrics each divide a subset by its own complete population: digital transactions of all transactions, completed journeys of started journeys, satisfied responses of all responses. Team D divides total service cost by digital transactions only, which inflates apparent efficiency of the digital channel by ignoring assisted-digital and offline transactions that the cost figure includes. Option C is a difference in data source, not a logical inconsistency - the framework can legitimately mix sources. This is the kind of denominator error a head of performance analysis is expected to catch during quality assurance, because cost per transaction is one of the four mandatory KPIs for government services and an inconsistent definition would mislead cross-service comparison.

**Question 2 (easy) - Logical deduction from escalation rules**

An escalation policy states: "An issue is escalated to the head of performance analysis only if it is unresolved after 10 working days AND it affects more than one service, OR it involves a data protection risk of any kind."

Which of the following issues should reach the head of performance analysis?

A) A dashboard outage affecting one service, unresolved for 15 working days
B) A KPI disagreement affecting three services, resolved in 4 working days
C) A one-service analytics implementation that may have captured postcode-level personal data
D) A resource conflict between two services, unresolved for 8 working days

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through the logic precisely. Option A fails the first condition (one service only) and involves no data protection risk. Option B affects multiple services but was resolved, so it never satisfies "unresolved after 10 working days". Option D affects more than one service but has only been open 8 days. Option C involves a potential data protection risk, and the policy says such issues escalate "of any kind" regardless of duration or service count - the OR clause operates independently. Reading compound conditions exactly as written, rather than as one assumes they were intended, is a core assurance habit: analysts across the community will apply these rules literally, so a head of profession must be able to as well.

**Question 3 (moderate) - Error checking across team outputs**

Two teams submit quarterly figures for the same shared service. Team X reports: starts 120,000; completions 96,000; completion rate 80%. Team Y, using the same source, reports: starts 120,000; completions 96,000; completion rate 84%. Both teams insist their arithmetic is right. What is the most likely explanation to investigate first?

A) One team has made a simple division error
B) The teams are using different definitions of "completion", so the 96,000 figures coincidentally match but the rates were calculated on different filtered bases
C) The analytics platform double-counted starts for one team
D) One team rounded to the nearest whole percentage and the other did not

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic: 96,000 ÷ 120,000 = 0.80, so Team X's rate follows from its own numbers and Team Y's does not - 84% of 120,000 would be 100,800 completions. Since both teams "insist their arithmetic is right", the strongest hypothesis is that Team Y computed its rate on a different filtered base (for example, excluding journeys abandoned within 30 seconds as non-genuine starts, giving roughly 114,300 effective starts) and then reported the unfiltered counts alongside it. A rounding difference (D) cannot produce a 4-point gap. Option A is possible but the question asks what to investigate first, and definitional divergence is both more common and more damaging across a multi-team service - it is precisely what a consistent performance measurement framework exists to prevent. A quality assurance framework at this level should require teams to publish the exact base used for every rate.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Prioritisation as an escalation point**

It is Monday morning. Four items are waiting for the head of performance analysis:

1. A lead analyst reports that a departmental board pack going out Thursday contains a KPI figure she believes is wrong.
2. A senior analyst asks for approval of a conference talk abstract due Friday.
3. A service owner emails, copied to the director, complaining that performance analysis "keeps blocking" his release with assurance requests.
4. A recruitment panel needs sift decisions by Wednesday for a campaign that closes candidates' availability windows.

In which order should they be addressed?

A) 3, 1, 4, 2
B) 1, 3, 4, 2
C) 1, 4, 3, 2
D) 4, 1, 2, 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 comes first: a potentially wrong figure heading into board-level decision making is the highest-consequence, time-critical risk, and correcting analysis before it informs decisions is the essence of quality assurance. Item 3 is next: a stakeholder conflict already visible to the director is reputationally live and concerns the core duty to champion performance analysis - left until Wednesday it hardens into a narrative that assurance is a blocker. Item 4 has a hard Wednesday deadline but is a scheduled, bounded task; doing it after the two live risks still meets the deadline. Item 2 has the latest deadline and lowest consequence. Option A puts stakeholder soothing ahead of a possible error reaching a board, which inverts the professional duty this role holds; option C delays the director-visible conflict behind routine sift work. Note the pattern this item tests: consequence and irreversibility outrank mere deadline order.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Deductive reasoning about the product life cycle**

An assurance framework requires: every service in public beta must have a performance framework signed off; every signed-off framework must include all four mandatory KPIs; and any service lacking a signed-off framework must not proceed to a live assessment. A candidate learns that Service Q is booked for a live assessment next month, and that its framework is missing the cost per transaction KPI. What can validly be concluded?

A) Service Q's framework was never signed off
B) Service Q will fail its live assessment
C) Either Service Q's framework was signed off in breach of the rules, or Service Q is booked for assessment in breach of the rules, or both
D) Service Q is not in public beta

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Deduce carefully. If Service Q's framework were validly signed off, it would include all four mandatory KPIs - it does not, so any sign-off that exists breached the rules. If it has no signed-off framework, then booking a live assessment breached the rule that unsigned services must not proceed. One of those two breaches (or both) must have occurred; the evidence does not establish which. Option A overreaches: a non-compliant sign-off may exist. Option B predicts an assessment outcome the rules do not determine. Option D does not follow at all - the rules about beta say nothing that lets a candidate infer Q's phase from a missing KPI. This item rewards the answer that captures exactly what follows, no more. In the real role, this disjunctive conclusion is actionable: the sign-off record should be checked first, because it establishes which control failed and where between-phase risk is leaking through life cycle assurance.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Pattern recognition in performance data**

A service's weekly completion rates over twelve weeks read: 71, 72, 70, 73, 71, 72, 58, 71, 72, 70, 73, 71. A lead analyst proposes reporting the twelve-week average of 70.3% to the board as "stable performance around 70%". What is the most important analytical objection?

A) The average should be weighted by weekly volume
B) A single week at 58 within an otherwise tight 70-73 band is an anomaly that needs investigation and separate narration, not absorption into an average
C) Twelve weeks is too short a period for any conclusion
D) The board should see medians, not means

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eleven of the twelve values sit in a narrow 70-73 band; week seven's 58 is roughly 13 points below the band - a clear outlier, not noise. Averaging it away both slightly depresses the reported figure and, more importantly, buries a signal: something happened that week (an outage, a release, a data collection failure) that the board and the service team may need to know about. "Stable around 70%" is a compelling story, but the skill profile at this level requires communicating stories that are relevant and accurate, and proactively presenting findings that inform wider decisions - an unexplained one-week collapse is such a finding. Options A, C and D are refinements that might each have merit in context, but none addresses the concealment of an anomaly, which is the material issue. This item tests whether a candidate's insight begins with noticing what breaks the pattern.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Problem solving on tooling**

Three teams use three different analytics tools; their figures for equivalent metrics differ by 3-8%, and cross-service reporting is becoming contentious. Consolidating everyone onto a single tool would cost significant money and retraining time, and each tool has features one team depends on. Which option best resolves the problem within the remit to align technology with business goals?

A) Mandate immediate migration to the most widely used tool
B) Let each team keep its tool but publish a caveat on every cross-service report
C) Keep the tools but define and assure a common measurement specification - shared metric definitions, tracking standards, and a reconciliation check - so figures become comparable, then review tooling consolidation as part of business planning
D) Stop producing cross-service reports until the tools agree

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The stated business problem is comparability, not tool multiplicity. Discrepancies of 3-8% between analytics platforms usually stem from definitional and implementation differences - session definitions, bot filtering, tag placement - which a common measurement specification directly addresses at low cost. Option A pays the maximum price immediately and destroys features teams depend on, without first testing whether cheaper alignment suffices; it may still be right eventually, which is why C folds consolidation into business planning rather than discarding it. Option B manages the symptom and trains stakeholders to distrust the numbers. Option D abandons a business need. This is the innovative-but-proportionate problem solving the role profile asks for: resolve the problem, sequence the investment, and keep the analytical capability aligned to business goals — the judgement this item is designed to test.

**Question 8 (hard) - Error checking a quality assurance summary**

An assurance dashboard about to be presented states:

- "42 analytical products reviewed this quarter; 36 passed first time (86%)."
- "Of the 6 that failed, 4 were resubmitted and passed, giving a final pass rate of 40/42 (95%)."
- "First-time pass rates by team: Team A 12/14, Team B 14/16, Team C 9/12."

Which figure is internally inconsistent?

A) The 86% first-time pass rate, because 36/42 does not round to 86%
B) The 95% final pass rate, because 36 + 4 does not equal 40
C) The team breakdown, because the team-level first-time passes sum to 35, not the 36 claimed in the headline
D) Nothing is inconsistent; all figures reconcile

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Verify every figure rather than trusting the summary. Headline rounding: 36 ÷ 42 = 85.7%, which legitimately rounds to 86%, so A fails. Final pass rate: 36 + 4 = 40, and 40 ÷ 42 = 95.2% ≈ 95%, so B fails. Team reviews: 14 + 16 + 12 = 42, matching the headline count of reviews. Team first-time passes: 12 + 14 + 9 = 35, which contradicts the headline claim of 36 first-time passes. So either one team's figures are wrong, or the headline is - and the dashboard cannot be presented until it is known which, because the discrepancy may hide a product that was recorded inconsistently between the team log and the central register. This is exactly the reconciliation habit a quality assurance framework at this level should institutionalise: totals reported at different levels of aggregation must be cross-footed before anything goes to senior stakeholders. A single-unit discrepancy looks trivial, but it destroys confidence in the whole dashboard the moment a board member spots it - and someone eventually will.

**Question 9 (hard) - Deduction about capability planning**

A candidate manages the community's capability through business planning, using these rules of thumb: every service in discovery or alpha needs at least 0.5 of a performance analyst; every beta service needs at least 1.0; every live service needs at least 0.5; and every lead analyst can supervise at most four analysts. Next quarter the community will have 3 discovery, 2 alpha, 4 beta, and 6 live services, plus 2 lead analysts and 8 analysts (leads do not count towards service coverage). Which statement is true?

A) There are enough analysts and enough supervision capacity
B) There are enough analysts but not enough supervision capacity
C) There are not enough analysts, but supervision capacity is sufficient for the analysts available
D) There are neither enough analysts nor enough supervision capacity

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute demand: discovery and alpha are 5 services × 0.5 = 2.5; beta is 4 × 1.0 = 4.0; live is 6 × 0.5 = 3.0. Total demand = 9.5 analyst full-time equivalents against a supply of 8 - a shortfall of 1.5, so there are not enough analysts. Supervision: 2 leads × 4 = capacity for 8 analysts, and there are exactly 8, so supervision suffices for the current staff (though it would not stretch to the 9.5 actually needed - a subtlety worth raising in business planning). Option C is the only statement consistent with both calculations. In the real role this arithmetic becomes the recruitment case: a 1.5 FTE shortfall, plus the observation that hiring 2 more analysts would also require a third lead or an increased supervision ratio, is precisely the evidenced narrative that wins funding — a candidate who reaches for it is showing exactly the reasoning this item tests.

**Question 10 (hard) - Spotting a flawed inference in a team's analysis**

A senior analyst's report concludes: "Users who watched our guidance video completed the application at 91%, against 74% for those who did not. Embedding the video more prominently will therefore raise overall completion by up to 17 percentage points." As the assurance point, what is the strongest objection?

A) The sample sizes are not stated
B) The comparison is observational: motivated users may both watch videos and complete applications, so the 17-point gap cannot be read as the causal effect of the video
C) 91% is suspiciously high for any government service
D) The video should be tested with users before being embedded

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report commits a classic selection-effect error: users self-selected into watching the video, and the trait driving that choice (motivation, digital confidence, time available) plausibly drives completion too. The causal effect of prominence could be far smaller, zero, or even negative for users who find video a barrier. Option A is a fair secondary point but even enormous samples would not fix confounding. Option C is an unfounded prior, and option D is a sensible next step rather than an objection to the inference. The assured recommendation would be to run an A/B test of video prominence and measure the difference experimentally. Coaching analysts to distinguish observed association from causal claim - and to phrase recommendations accordingly - is one of the highest-leverage quality assurance interventions a head of performance analysis can make, because flawed causal claims travel fast once they reach decision makers who like their implications.

**Question 11 (hard) - Multi-constraint prioritisation across the life cycle**

Three risks land on a candidate's desk the same afternoon:

- Risk 1: A beta service's analytics tagging will break when the front-end framework upgrades in six weeks; fixing it needs two weeks of analyst time; without it, the service enters live assessment with no completion data.
- Risk 2: A live service's exit survey response rate has drifted from 12% to 3% over six months, quietly degrading the user satisfaction KPI everyone still quotes.
- Risk 3: A discovery team has commissioned an external agency to define KPIs without consulting the community, creating a framework that will likely need reworking at alpha.

Realistic capacity allows the candidate to intervene properly in only two this week. Which two, and why?

A) Risks 1 and 2 - both threaten the integrity of measurement on services already carrying users, while Risk 3 can be corrected at the alpha gate
B) Risks 1 and 3 - both are upstream and cheap to fix now, while Risk 2 is already six months old and can wait another week
C) Risks 2 and 3 - Risk 1 has a six-week runway so the team can handle it
D) Risks 1 and 2 and 3 - all must be addressed simultaneously by delegating

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weigh consequence, reversibility, and timing. Risk 1 has a hard dependency chain: two weeks of work, six weeks of runway, but analyst scheduling means the window closes fast, and entering a live assessment without completion data is a severe, partly irreversible outcome. Risk 2 is actively corrupting a KPI that senior people currently rely on - every week it persists, decisions rest on a satisfaction figure drawn from a vanishing, increasingly unrepresentative 3% sample. Risk 3 is real but self-correcting through an existing control: a framework review at the alpha transition will catch it, and a brief note to the discovery team costs minutes, not an intervention. Option B undervalues live-service data integrity; option C gambles that a team that has not yet scheduled the fix will do so unprompted. Option D is seductive but dishonest about capacity - the question tests whether a candidate can genuinely prioritise, and "do everything" is the classic evasion. Note that A also respects the life cycle principle in this role's skill set: risks between phases (Risk 3) are best handled by the phase-gate controls designed for them.

**Question 12 (moderate) - User-centred pattern recognition**

Four insights compete for the single slide a candidate gets at the quarterly service board:

1. Page load time improved 22% after the platform upgrade.
2. Assisted-digital phone applications rose 31% in the same quarter that the online journey's drop-out at the identity-verification step rose 19%.
3. Twitter sentiment about the department improved slightly.
4. The team closed 96% of analytics support tickets within SLA.

Which insight should take the slide?

A) Insight 1, because it shows technology investment paying off
B) Insight 2, because the paired movements suggest users are being pushed out of the digital channel by a specific broken step, which has cost, user-experience, and inclusion implications
C) Insight 3, because boards care about reputation
D) Insight 4, because it evidences the community's performance

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The head-of-profession instinct being tested here is pattern recognition across metrics plus user-centred strategic thinking. Insights 1, 3 and 4 are each single, static, mildly positive facts requiring no decision. Insight 2 is a correlation between two movements that tells a story with a probable cause (the identity-verification step), a quantified consequence (31% more expensive phone handling), and an implied action (fix the step, watch both metrics revert). It concerns how well user needs are actually being met - some users abandoning, others diverted to a channel that costs more and may serve them worse or better depending on their needs. The skill profile at this level asks for presenting compelling findings that inform wider decisions and applying strategic thinking to the problem of how to provide the best service for the end user: insight 2 is the only candidate that does both. Boards remember the analyst who brought them a decision, not a status report, and a candidate who selects B is demonstrating exactly that instinct.

**Question 13 (hard) - Logical consistency of a strategy**

A draft performance analysis strategy contains four commitments:

1. "All performance reporting will use the central analytics platform by Q4."
2. "Teams may choose the tools best suited to their service's needs."
3. "No service will report a KPI that cannot be independently reproduced from source data."
4. "We will reduce the community's reporting workload by 20% this year."

A lead analyst says commitments 1 and 2 contradict each other. Do they?

A) Yes - mandating one platform and offering free tool choice are irreconcilable
B) No - commitment 1 governs reporting while commitment 2 governs analysis tooling, so both can hold provided the strategy makes that scope distinction explicit
C) No - commitment 2 is subordinate because it appears second
D) Yes - and commitment 3 also contradicts commitment 4, because reproducibility increases workload

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test for genuine contradiction: two commitments contradict only if no state of the world satisfies both. A world where teams analyse data in whatever tools suit them (commitment 2) but publish their KPIs through the central platform (commitment 1) satisfies both - many organisations run exactly this model. The contradiction is apparent, not real, but the lead analyst's challenge is still valuable: the draft evidently fails to make the reporting/analysis distinction explicit, which is a drafting defect worth fixing before publication, because strategies are read literally by busy people. Option C invents a precedence rule that does not exist. Option D overstates: reproducibility (3) has an upfront cost but often reduces steady-state workload by eliminating rework and disputes, so it is in tension with 4 at worst, not contradiction. Distinguishing contradiction from tension from ambiguity is a genuinely senior skill - strategies routinely contain tensions to be managed, and crying "contradiction" at each one paralyses the community, while missing a real one embarrasses it. This item tests whether a candidate can tell the difference.

### Administering this section

- Give every candidate assessed for this role level the same items under the same time allowance (15 to 30 minutes is typical for the set above), with the same conditions and, if any, the same practice questions beforehand.
- Keep the worked answers and explanations out of the candidate's view throughout the session; they are the scoring key.
- Record both accuracy and time taken. If a candidate is visibly stuck on one item, let the session continue rather than intervening; note it for the debrief instead.
- At debrief, use the explanations to talk through what a strong answer showed and where a weaker one fell short — several items embed governance, trust, and compliance considerations (public dashboards, consent, definition changes) that are worth discussing explicitly as part of the logic, not as background colour.

### Scoring guidance

- Watch for a candidate forcing an inconsistency to exist where the figures actually reconcile (Question 8) — a strong candidate says the figures are sound when checking confirms they are, rather than inventing a fault because the question "feels like" it wants one.
- Note whether a candidate answers from general experience of how government analytics usually works, rather than from the rules and data given in the item — this is a common and instructive failure mode at this level, since the item describes how this particular scenario works.
- Note whether a candidate reads compound conditions (ANDs and ORs) literally in escalation rules or strategy commitments (Questions 2, 5, 13), rather than as they assume they were intended.
- Note whether a candidate defaults to the most sophisticated-sounding option rather than the one the logic actually supports — sophistication is not the criterion here, validity is, and this is a pattern worth flagging at debrief if seen.
- Note where a candidate appeared to spend disproportionate time on one hard item at the expense of others — the assessment rewards steady, checked progress over a stubborn battle on a single question.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment tests whether a candidate can reach a defensible conclusion from data at speed. As head of performance analysis the data is community capability and capacity, portfolio-level service performance, business cases attached to analytical investment, and the figures the postholder's own analysts produce and that others summarise.

The format is typically fifteen to twenty-five minutes with a calculator, mixing computation with interpretation. Interpretation dominates at this level, because the role is rarely the person doing the calculation and frequently the last person able to notice that it is wrong.

Three habits are worth naming and watching for when scoring.

The first is that a head of profession is usually assessing someone else's arithmetic, and the errors are rarely in the sums. They are in the base, the period, the assumption of linearity, or a benefit that would have arrived anyway.

The second is that portfolio and community figures conceal enormous variation, so a mean across services or across analysts is generally the least informative statistic available.

The third is that the profession's credibility rests on its numbers being right, and this role sets the framework that determines whether they are. An arithmetic error let through at this level is a different kind of problem from one an analyst makes.

### What it measures for this role

- **Capability and capacity arithmetic** maps to managing capability through business planning, development, and recruitment.
- **Portfolio interpretation** maps to **Analysis and insight** and presenting findings that inform wider decisions.
- **Quality framework arithmetic** maps to defining a framework for quality assurance.
- **Business case scrutiny** maps to setting the performance analysis strategy.
- **Measurement design** maps to **Performance measurement**.
- **Presenting figures** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**.

**Question 1 (easy) — Vacancy rate**

What is the overall vacancy rate across the community?

- A) About 12%
- B) About 21%
- C) About 25%
- D) About 17%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total posts: 10 + 22 + 12 + 4 = 48. Filled: 6 + 20 + 8 + 4 = 38. Vacancies: 10, which is 10/48 = 20.8%. More than one post in five is unfilled — and the distribution matters more than the total, which the next question addresses.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reports per filled post**

Which level produces the most reports per filled post per quarter?

- A) Associate
- B) Performance analyst
- C) Senior
- D) Lead

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Associate 90/6 = 15.0; performance analyst 340/20 = 17.0; senior 210/8 = 26.25; lead 60/4 = 15.0. Senior is highest at 26.25, well ahead of the rest. The intuitive answer is that junior staff produce more routine output, and the figures say otherwise — which is why computing every row before choosing is worth the twenty seconds. Note too that a report is not a standardised unit, so this measure identifies where to look rather than what is true: seniors may be producing shorter pieces, or may simply be counted against work several people contributed to.

**Question 3 (moderate) — The shape of the community**

What is the strongest interpretation of the vacancy distribution?

- A) Recruitment is failing across the board.
- B) The hollows are at associate (40% vacant) and senior (33% vacant) while the analyst tier is nearly full — a shape consistent with weak entry-level recruitment at one end and, at the other, analysts either not progressing or leaving before they do, which the 19-month mean tenure at analyst level supports.
- C) The community has too many posts.
- D) Senior recruitment should be prioritised.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The two vacancy concentrations have different causes and the full middle tier connects them. A nearly-full analyst tier alongside a third of senior posts vacant means either that analysts are not being promoted or that they leave before they are, and 19 months' mean tenure against 31 at senior points at the second. Option B reads the whole shape. Option A treats it as uniform when it plainly is not. Option D would recruit externally into posts existing analysts could fill, which makes the tenure problem worse.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Cost of a quality assurance requirement**

A candidate is considering requiring a second-analyst review of every report. There are 700 reports a quarter and a review takes about 45 minutes. What is the approximate annual cost in analyst-days?

- A) About 175 days
- B) About 280 days
- C) About 70 days
- D) About 350 days

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 700 reports × 4 quarters = 2,800 reviews a year. At 45 minutes each that is 2,800 × 0.75 = 2,100 hours, and at a 7.5-hour day, 2,100 / 7.5 = 280 analyst-days — roughly 1.5 full-time equivalents, permanently. That is the figure nobody computes before proposing a universal review requirement, and it is why quality frameworks usually scope review to publication risk rather than applying it to everything.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Scoping the requirement**

If review were required only for the 180 reports a quarter that inform external publications, what is the approximate annual cost?

- A) About 72 analyst-days
- B) About 140 analyst-days
- C) About 36 analyst-days
- D) About 200 analyst-days

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 180 × 4 = 720 reviews a year × 0.75 hours = 540 hours, divided by 7.5 = 72 analyst-days. Roughly a quarter of the universal figure, for the reports where an error would be most damaging. This pair of calculations is the substance of designing a quality assurance framework at this level: the question is not whether review is good but where the benefit exceeds a cost that has actually been computed.

**Question 6 (harder) — A benefits claim**

A business case for an analytics platform claims £900,000 of annual savings from removing 20 full-time equivalent roles. Fully loaded cost per role is £48,000. Is the claim internally consistent?

- A) Yes; 20 × £48,000 = £960,000, close enough.
- B) No — 20 roles at £48,000 is £960,000, which exceeds the claim, so the case understates rather than overstates and the difference needs explaining before either figure is relied on.
- C) Yes, and the difference is immaterial.
- D) There is not enough information.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 20 × £48,000 = £960,000 against a claimed £900,000 — a discrepancy of £60,000 in the conservative direction, which is unusual and therefore worth asking about. It may reflect a partial-year assumption, a lower loading for some roles, or a residual cost retained. The habit worth building at this level is checking the internal arithmetic of any business case before engaging with its conclusions; it takes under a minute and it establishes whether the author has been careful.

**Question 7 (harder) — Benefits that would arrive anyway**

The same case notes that 7 of the 20 roles are already vacant and were due to be removed in a planned restructure. What is the strongest assessment?

- A) The saving is unaffected; the roles still go.
- B) Those 7 would be removed regardless, so they are not attributable to this investment — the attributable saving is 13 roles at £48,000, about £624,000, which is roughly two-thirds of the claim.
- C) Only the vacant roles should be excluded.
- D) The case should be rejected.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A benefit arriving without the investment is not a benefit of the investment. 13 × £48,000 = £624,000 against a claimed £900,000. This is rarely bad faith — the author sees the roles going and counts them — and it is the commonest inflation in an analytical business case. Option D rejects a case that may well still be worth doing at £624,000, depending on cost.

**Question 8 (harder) — Recruitment arithmetic**

To fill the 4 senior vacancies externally, historical data shows an average of 0.6 appointments per campaign. Roughly how many campaigns are needed, and what does that suggest?

- A) About 7 campaigns, which is likely to take more than a year and makes internal development a serious alternative rather than an aspiration.
- B) About 4 campaigns.
- C) About 2 campaigns.
- D) About 10 campaigns.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 4 / 0.6 = 6.7, so about 7 campaigns. Each consumes panel time, advertising and weeks of elapsed time, so filling four senior posts externally is likely to run beyond a year. The value of doing this calculation is that it makes the alternative concrete: promoting four analysts with structured support costs far less than seven campaigns and simultaneously addresses the 19-month analyst tenure that is generating the vacancies.

**Question 9 (harder) — Portfolio interpretation**

Across eight services, completion rates are 91%, 88%, 63%, 90%, 87%, 41%, 89%, and 86%. A director asks for the department's average completion rate. What is the strongest response?

- A) 79.4%, the mean of the eight.
- B) Give the mean with the distribution, because six services sit between 86% and 91% and two sit far below at 63% and 41% — the average describes none of them, and the useful finding is that two services need attention rather than that the department averages 79%.
- C) 87.5%, the median.
- D) The figures cannot be averaged.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean of the eight is 79.4% and it describes no service in the set. The distribution is bimodal — a tight cluster around 88% and two clear outliers — and that structure is the finding. Option B gives the director the number they asked for and the information they actually need. Option C is a better single statistic and still hides the outliers. Option D declines an answer that can be given carefully. Note also that an unweighted mean across services ignores their sizes entirely, which is a further reason to lead with the distribution.

**Question 10 (harder) — Trend against variation**

Quarterly community output over two years: 640, 690, 660, 720, 670, 710, 680, 730 reports. A director asks whether productivity is improving. What is the strongest answer?

- A) Yes; output rose from 640 to 730.
- B) The series moves between 640 and 730 with a slight upward drift — the first four average 677.5 and the last four 697.5, about 3% — but report counts depend on what was asked for, so the series may reflect demand rather than productivity.
- C) No; the variation is too large.
- D) There is insufficient data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two problems and option B names both. The statistical one is that a 3% difference between halves sits inside the quarter-to-quarter variation. The measurement one is more serious: report count is driven by how much analysis people request, so the series is at least as much a measure of demand as of productivity — which makes it a poor productivity metric regardless of the trend. Flagging that before it becomes a departmental target is worth doing, and a candidate who does so unprompted is demonstrating exactly the judgement this item tests.

**Question 11 (harder) — Capacity for a strategic initiative**

A candidate's community has 38 filled posts averaging 190 productive days a year. Standing reporting consumes 55% of capacity. How many analyst-days remain for strategic and improvement work?

- A) About 3,250
- B) About 7,220
- C) About 3,970
- D) About 1,800

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total capacity: 38 × 190 = 7,220 analyst-days. Standing reporting takes 55%, which is 3,971 days, leaving 3,249 for everything else — so about 3,250. The 3,971 days locked into recurring reporting is the number worth interrogating: retiring or automating even 20% of it releases roughly 800 analyst-days, which is more than any realistic recruitment would deliver and considerably faster.

**Question 12 (harder) — Presenting to a board**

A candidate has ten minutes with a departmental board to make the case for investment in analytical capability. Which figure leads best?

- A) The community's vacancy rate.
- B) The number of analyst-days consumed by recurring reporting that nobody has re-examined, alongside one concrete example of a decision the department made without analysis it could have had.
- C) The number of reports produced per quarter.
- D) The cost of the current analytical function.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B pairs a number that establishes scale with an example that establishes consequence, and boards need both — a capacity figure without an example is abstract, and an example without a figure is anecdote. It also frames the ask around released capacity rather than new money, which is a much easier proposition. Option A is an internal problem the board will read as a recruitment matter. Option C measures activity rather than value. Option D leads with cost, which invites a conversation about reducing it.

### Administering this section

- Decide in advance whether a calculator is permitted, and apply that decision identically to every candidate assessed for this role level.
- Hold every candidate to the same time limit (around fifteen to twenty-five minutes is typical), and use the same table and items for every candidate compared for a given decision.
- Keep the worked answers out of the candidate's sight until scoring.
- At debrief, focus on the "what does the data support" items (Questions 3, 9, 10, 12), since interpretation dominates at this level far more than raw computation, and a candidate's reasoning on these is a stronger signal than the arithmetic alone.

### Scoring guidance

- Note whether a candidate reports a mean across a distribution that is plainly bimodal (Questions 3 and 9), rather than leading with the shape of the distribution.
- Note whether a candidate would set a universal review requirement without first pricing its annual cost (Questions 4 and 5), and whether they check the internal arithmetic of a business case before engaging with its conclusions (Question 6).
- Note whether a candidate strips out benefits that would arrive without the investment being assessed (Question 7) — a common way business cases are inflated, and a strong signal of financial scrutiny.
- Note whether a candidate questions whether an output metric measures effort, demand, or productivity (Question 10) before answering a question about improvement.
- Note whether a candidate expresses capacity in analyst-days and interrogates what recurring work consumes (Question 11) — this speaks directly to how a candidate would operate as head of profession managing capability through business planning.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment tests whether a candidate can determine exactly what a piece of written material establishes. As head of performance analysis the material is governance and quality frameworks, business cases, employment and recruitment guidance, cross-government agreements, and the reports and summaries the community produces.

The commonest format presents a passage followed by statements to be marked True, False or Cannot Say. True means the passage establishes it, False means it contradicts it, and Cannot Say means it settles neither — regardless of what the candidate knows professionally.

Long experience is the main hazard here: a candidate will know how these arrangements usually work while the passage describes this one, and at this level a candidate's reading tends to become the community's reading.

Three specifics matter particularly at this level, worth bearing in mind when scoring.

The first is that governance and employment guidance allocate authority in language that looks interchangeable and is not — approves, endorses, is consulted, may direct, must consult. This role manages capability through business planning, development and recruitment, where acting outside remit is a serious matter rather than a technical one.

The second is that business cases are written persuasively, and separating what is asserted from what is established is a routine part of the job. The head of profession is frequently the most senior person in the room able to do it and the one expected to.

The third is that a document naming what it did not examine is telling the reader where its evidence stops, and the unexamined question is often the one that decides whether its recommendation holds.

### What it measures for this role

- **Reading governance and quality frameworks** maps to defining a framework for quality assurance.
- **Reading employment and recruitment guidance** maps to managing capability through recruitment and development.
- **Reading business cases** maps to setting the performance analysis strategy.
- **Reading cross-government agreements** maps to working with colleagues across government.
- **Distinguishing evidence from claim** maps to **Analysis and insight**.
- **Reading and summarising accurately** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Where a post has been advertised twice without a suitable appointment, the recruiting manager may propose a revised specification, an increased pay band subject to approval by the pay committee, or engagement of an interim resource for a period not exceeding twelve months. Interim engagements beyond twelve months require the approval of the director of resources. An interim engagement should not be used where the underlying need is permanent, unless a plan to establish permanent capability accompanies the request. Recruiting managers should consult the head of the relevant profession before advertising a post requiring specialist skills."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: A recruiting manager may propose a revised specification after two unsuccessful advertisements.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly as the first of three options available. Note this is the one route in the list with no approval condition attached, which the next question turns on.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: A recruiting manager can increase the pay band for such a post.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the manager "may propose" an increased band "subject to approval by the pay committee". Proposing is not deciding, and the approval sits elsewhere. The statement asserts a power the passage allocates to another body, which makes it False rather than Cannot Say — the passage does more than omit the manager's power, it assigns the decision away.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: A recruiting manager must consult the head of profession before advertising a specialist post.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage uses "should consult", not "must". In a document that says "require the approval of" and "subject to approval by" elsewhere, the choice of "should" is deliberate. The statement's "must" overstates it — and for the head of profession being consulted, this tells you something practically useful: a manager who did not consult departed from good practice rather than breaching a rule, which changes how the head of profession raises it with them.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: An eighteen-month interim engagement would require both the director of resources' approval and a plan to establish permanent capability.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The first limb is established: engagements beyond twelve months require the director of resources' approval, and eighteen exceeds twelve. The second is not, because the capability plan is required only where the underlying need is permanent, and the statement says nothing about whether it is — an eighteen-month engagement could cover a time-limited programme. A compound statement is True only if every part is established, so one unestablished conjunct makes the whole Cannot Say.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The department's analytical quality framework requires that analysis informing an external publication is reviewed by a second analyst before release. Analysis informing internal decisions should be reviewed where the method is novel to the data source. Reviewers must record the review in the analysis log and may not review analysis they contributed to. Heads of analytical professions are responsible for assuring compliance within their community and must report annually on compliance rates. The framework anticipates that all professions will reach 95% compliance on external publications within two years. The framework is reviewed every three years."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: Heads of analytical professions must report annually on compliance rates.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly with "must", and it is an obligation that falls on the postholder personally. Note it is paired with a responsibility for assuring compliance, which is a broader duty than reporting on it — the framework makes the head of profession accountable for the number as well as for publishing it.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: All professions will reach 95% compliance on external publications within two years.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The framework "anticipates" this. An anticipation is a forecast, and the passage establishes that the forecast exists rather than that the outcome will occur. Marking True treats a document's expectation as a fact about the world, which is the specific error framework documents reliably produce — and for the person who will be reporting against it, the distinction between a target and a prediction is one they will need to make in front of other people.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: An analyst who gave advice on a piece of analysis may review it provided they did not write it.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The framework says reviewers "may not review analysis they contributed to". Contribution is the test, not authorship, so someone who advised on the method is excluded. The passage contradicts the statement. This is a real constraint in small communities, where the only other person who understands a piece of analysis is often the person consulted about it — and the framework has clearly anticipated that.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: The framework requires review of internal analysis using a method novel to the data source.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The framework says such analysis "should be reviewed", not "must". The statement's "requires" overstates it — and the contrast is sharp, since the immediately preceding sentence uses "is reviewed" as a requirement for external publications. For the person defining quality assurance for their own community, this is exactly the space in which a higher local standard than the departmental framework can be set.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"An internal review examined how performance analysis is used across twelve services. It found that analysts were involved in decision-making from the start in four services, consulted after decisions in six, and not involved in two. Services where analysts were involved from the start reported higher confidence in their performance measures. The review did not assess whether decisions in those services were better. Analyst tenure was longer in the four early-involvement services. The review recommends embedding analysts in service teams from inception."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: Analysts were involved from the start in a third of the services examined.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Four services out of twelve is exactly one third. The passage supplies both figures, so this is established by arithmetic. Worth doing the division rather than eyeballing it — and worth noting the shape of the rest: half the services consulted analysts only after decisions had been made, which is the finding with the most direct implication for how the profession is used.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: Involving analysts from the start causes higher confidence in performance measures.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and does not establish direction. It is at least as plausible that services which already valued measurement involved analysts early, and that the confidence and the involvement share a common cause. The passage also notes longer analyst tenure in those same services, which is a third variable confounded with both. Marking True is the inference the review's own recommendation invites.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: Decisions were better in the services where analysts were involved from the start.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states explicitly that the review "did not assess whether decisions in those services were better". Nothing either way — and this is the question that actually matters. Confidence in measures is a proxy; whether the decisions improved is the outcome the recommendation implicitly promises, and the review has left it unexamined while recommending a change.

**Question 12 (harder)**

Statement: Embedding analysts in service teams from inception would improve confidence in performance measures.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is the review's recommendation, and a recommendation is not a finding. Whether embedding produces the effect depends on the causal question the review could not answer: if early involvement is a symptom of a service that already values measurement rather than a cause of confidence, embedding analysts into services that do not will not reproduce it. Distinguishing a proposed remedy from a demonstrated one is exactly the judgement required when a candidate is asked to endorse a recommendation about their own profession — and the temptation to endorse it is real, because it is a recommendation most heads of profession would probably want anyway. A candidate who marks C despite that pull is showing real discipline.

### Administering this section

- Use the same three passages and twelve items for every candidate assessed at this role level, and hold every candidate to the same time limit.
- Keep the worked answers out of the candidate's sight until scoring.
- At debrief, focus discussion on the "Cannot Say" items (Questions 4, 6, 10, 11, 12) — these are where a candidate's long experience and, in Question 12 particularly, their own professional preferences, are most likely to substitute for what the passage actually establishes.

### Scoring guidance

- Note whether a candidate distinguishes proposing from deciding (Question 2), and reads "should" against "must" within the same document (Questions 3 and 8) — both bear directly on how a candidate would read their own authority correctly in post.
- Note whether a candidate checks each limb of a compound statement separately (Question 4), and whether they read "anticipates" as a forecast rather than a commitment (Question 6).
- Note whether a candidate looks for a third variable when two things are associated (Question 10), and whether they catch the sentence naming what a review did not assess (Question 11).
- Note whether a candidate treats a recommendation as a proposal rather than an established remedy, especially where it aligns with what they would likely want anyway (Question 12) — this is one of the more revealing items in the section for judgement and self-awareness at this level.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment presents realistic scenarios and asks a candidate to identify effective and ineffective responses. As head of performance analysis the scenarios involve the people in the community, the standards set for them, the relationship with the business, and the recurring problem that analysis is wanted most when it confirms and needed most when it does not.

The commonest formats ask for the most and least effective response, or for ratings. Score against a key derived from experienced practitioners at comparable levels — the key below.

Three patterns run through the strong answers, worth bearing in mind when scoring and at debrief.

The first is that this role is the point of contact between the community and the business, which means absorbing pressure that would otherwise land on individual analysts. An analyst who produced an unwelcome finding should not be the person defending it to a director.

The second is that a quality framework is paid for by every analyst, on every piece of work, for as long as it stands. Whether it describes good practice is the easy question; whether the benefit exceeds the distributed cost is the one that decides it, and the head of profession is the person most likely to be able to compute that.

The third is that the community watches how a head of profession handles the hard cases. Whether they back an analyst whose correct finding was unpopular, whether they withdraw their own standard when the evidence turns, whether they tell a board something it does not want to hear — these set whether anyone brings them a problem early.

### What it measures for this role

- **Championing the community** maps to championing performance analysis and performance analysts.
- **Setting standards** maps to defining a framework for quality assurance.
- **Strategy** maps to setting the performance analysis strategy with input from leads and seniors.
- **Capability management** maps to business planning, development, and recruitment.
- **Escalation** maps to removing blockers and acting as an escalation point.
- **Influence** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and presenting compelling findings.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — An analyst under pressure**

A director is pressing one of a candidate's analysts to change a finding that reflects badly on their programme. What is the most effective response?

- A) Ask the analyst to review their method.
- B) Take the conversation with the director personally, having first checked the analysis is sound — because a head of profession can have a robust exchange with a director that an analyst reporting into a delivery team cannot, and an analyst left to defend a correct finding alone will produce a more comfortable one next time.
- C) Advise the analyst to hold their position.
- D) Suggest the finding be presented with additional caveats.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This role names being the point of contact between the community and the business, and absorbing this pressure is what that means in practice. The check comes first — analysis that has not been verified should not be defended. Option D is least effective: adding caveats in response to pressure changes the finding by another route, and once a community sees that pressure produces caveats, findings soften pre-emptively. Option C leaves the analyst exposed to a conversation they cannot win. Option A implies the method is the problem, which is what the director wants implied.

**Question 2 (easy) — A metric proposed as a target**

A directorate proposes a target on the number of cases closed per caseworker per week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Name how the target would be met — the cheapest way to close more cases is to close them without resolving them, which raises reopened cases while the number improves — and propose pairing it with reopened case rate and outcome quality.
- B) Agree; throughput is a reasonable measure.
- C) Advise against targets on caseworker throughput.
- D) Agree, and monitor for unintended effects.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Option A names the mechanism rather than objecting in principle, which is what persuades people who need a throughput measure for legitimate reasons — and the paired measures are the remedy, since they are hard to move without genuine improvement. Option B is least effective: agreeing without asking how the target will be met is exactly the question this profession exists to ask, and a target starts changing behaviour immediately. Option D detects the damage after real cases have been affected. Option C is a defensible position offered without a route forward.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Setting a quality framework**

A candidate is defining the quality assurance framework for their community. What is the strongest basis?

- A) Require second-analyst review of all analysis.
- B) Scope review by the consequence of an error — universal review of anything informing an external publication or a ministerial submission, and a lighter, targeted requirement elsewhere — because a universal requirement costs a large permanent share of community capacity and analysts will find ways around it.
- C) Require review where the analyst requests it.
- D) Require review of anything a lead analyst judges significant.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Option B applies the right test — where would an error do most damage — and prices the alternative honestly. Option A is least effective: a universal requirement consumes well over a full-time equivalent of community capacity, and a framework people cannot follow gets followed nominally, which loses visibility of who is actually complying. Option C gets review where it is least needed, since the analysts who request it are the careful ones. Option D is workable and puts an unbounded judgement call on leads without a criterion.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A vacancy that will not fill**

A candidate is asked to recruit a senior analyst into a specialism where the market is thin and the pay band is below market rate. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say plainly that the role as specified is unlikely to fill, and offer the alternatives — reshaping it for a strong analyst who could grow into it, an interim arrangement while capability is built, or a case for the band.
- B) Run the campaign and see.
- C) Make the case for a higher band.
- D) Recommend a contractor.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** The failure mode is slow and invisible: a role that will not fill stays open for months, the work does not happen, and nobody records the cost. Option A names it immediately and offers three routes — of which reshaping the role for someone who can grow into it is the most overlooked and often the best, since scarce specialisms are frequently more reliably built than bought, and it addresses the progression problem at the same time. Option B is least effective: it spends a campaign learning what is already known.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Analysis arriving after the decision**

A service repeatedly makes decisions and then asks a candidate's team to produce supporting analysis. What is the most effective response?

- A) Find a decision they are about to make, produce the analysis that would inform it, and bring it before the decision — because the pattern usually reflects analysis having always arrived too late to be useful, and one timely piece changes more than any conversation about it.
- B) Explain to the service that analysis should inform decisions rather than justify them.
- C) Decline to produce post-hoc analysis.
- D) Raise it with the service owner.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Teams stop using analysis when it consistently arrives after the decision or answers a question nobody asked, and option A breaks the cycle by demonstration. Option B is least effective: telling a service they are using analysis wrongly is heard as a criticism, changes nothing about the timing, and makes the next request less likely. Option C withholds something they will simply obtain elsewhere, probably worse. Option D escalates a relationship problem the head of profession is better placed to solve directly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A lead analyst's judgement a candidate doubts**

A lead analyst managed by a candidate has taken an approach to a community-wide issue that the candidate thinks is wrong, and their reasoning is coherent. What is the most effective response?

- A) Ask what they weighed and what would change their mind, then offer the concern with reasons — because they may hold context the candidate does not, and if not, the reasoning is what transfers rather than the conclusion.
- B) Tell them to take a different approach.
- C) Accept it; it is their area.
- D) Take the decision back personally.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** "Coherent but different from mine" is the expected outcome of delegating to a lead, and option A tests it without overriding it — the "what would change your mind" question locates the assumption actually in dispute. Option D is least effective: taking a decision back from a lead removes their authority visibly and tells the whole community that delegation here is provisional. Option B instructs rather than engages. Option C declines the judgement the role holds.

**Question 7 (harder) — Pressure to present selectively**

A director preparing a submission asks a candidate to include the measures that improved and omit the one that did not. What is the most effective response?

- A) Include everything relevant, and offer to help present the unfavourable measure accurately — what it shows, why it may have moved, and what is being done — because a submission omitting a known adverse result is one neither of them could defend.
- B) Include what they asked; it is their submission.
- C) Refuse to contribute.
- D) Include everything without discussion.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** The director's underlying concern — not wanting the submission undermined — is legitimate and addressable through framing rather than omission. Option A holds the line on content while helping with presentation, which is both correct and far more likely to be accepted than a refusal. Option B is least effective: a submission omitting an adverse measure misleads whoever reads it, and the profession's name is on the analysis. Option D reaches the right result while turning a solvable conversation into a unilateral act. Option C forgoes the chance to help.

**Question 8 (harder) — Evidence against a candidate's own framework**

Data shows the quality assurance framework a candidate introduced consumes substantial capacity and has not measurably reduced errors. What is the most effective response?

- A) Improve the measurement; prevented errors are hard to count.
- B) Publish the finding, including that it is the candidate's own framework, and narrow it to where the benefit is demonstrable or withdraw it — because a community paying weekly for an unmeasurable benefit is a real cost, and withdrawing a framework on evidence makes every remaining standard more credible.
- C) Retain it; some benefits do not show in data.
- D) Reduce its scope quietly.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The cost is certain and distributed; the benefit is unmeasured. Option B allows the right resolution — narrowing rather than abandoning — and gets the credibility that only comes from doing it openly. Option C is least effective because it is unfalsifiable, which makes it the argument that keeps every unproductive standard in place indefinitely. Option D reaches a defensible outcome by a route that looks like concealment once noticed. Option A is legitimate in principle and indistinguishable from motivated reasoning when the person improving the measurement owns the framework.

**Question 9 (harder) — A strong analyst wants to leave**

An excellent senior analyst tells a candidate privately they are considering leaving because there is no progression route. What is the most effective response?

- A) Offer a promotion if one is available.
- B) Be honest about what is and is not available, and work the routes that exist — stretching scope, a role elsewhere in the department, a cross-government move — because keeping them in government is a far better outcome than losing them, and a head who helps people move internally hears about approaches early.
- C) Explain that progression takes time.
- D) Accept it; people move on.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** They have given information at some cost, and honesty — including unwelcome honesty — is what keeps them longest and keeps the channel open. Option B also widens the frame beyond the postholder's own establishment, which is where the opportunities usually are. Option C is least effective: it is the standard deflection, it confirms what they already suspect, and it converts a conversation into a decision. Option A is the reflex and is right only if it is real and deliverable. Option D wastes an opening they created.

**Question 10 (harder) — Presenting an unwelcome portfolio picture**

A candidate is asked to present service performance to a board. Six services perform well and two are performing very poorly. What is the most effective response?

- A) Present the distribution rather than the average, name the two services, and say what would be needed to understand why they differ — because an average of 79% describes none of the twelve and conceals the only finding that matters.
- B) Present the departmental average.
- C) Present the average and mention the two if asked.
- D) Present the six strong services as evidence the approach works.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A bimodal distribution has a finding in its shape, and averaging it away is a choice to conceal the useful information. Option A gives the board something they can act on. Option D is least effective: presenting only the strong services is selective in a way that will be discovered, and it forfeits the head of profession's standing as the person whose numbers can be trusted — which is the profession's only real asset. Option B hides the finding behind a statistic that describes nothing. Option C is the same omission with a fallback.

**Question 11 (harder) — Championing across government**

A candidate is asked to speak at a cross-government event about their department's performance analysis. Some work is strong; a major measurement programme was abandoned. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Present all of it, and be specific about why the abandoned programme failed, because that is what the audience can learn from and the candidate's own analysts will hear whether the year they actually had was described accurately.
- B) Present the strong work.
- C) Present the successes and refer generally to challenges.
- D) Decline; the picture is mixed.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Two audiences are listening and option A is the only response serving both — the external one gets the transferable material, and the presenter's own community hears whether their head of profession told the truth. Option C is least effective: vague reference to "challenges" in front of practitioners who know the specifics reads as evasion and costs more credibility than the abandonment itself. Option B omits the useful part. Option D forgoes influence indefinitely, since the picture is always mixed.

**Question 12 (harder) — A defensible finding that turned out wrong**

An analyst produced a well-reasoned finding that later proved incorrect because of a data issue nobody could have known about. Stakeholders are looking for accountability. What is the most effective response?

- A) Take responsibility publicly as head of profession, be clear about what has been learned and what will change in the quality framework, and handle any individual development need privately.
- B) Explain that the analyst followed the correct process.
- C) Acknowledge the error and commit to preventing recurrence.
- D) Review whether the analysis was in fact defensible.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Option A separates the two things that must not be conflated: the quality of the work, judged on what was knowable, and the outcome, which was not. Taking it publicly protects an analyst who will already feel exposed, and naming a framework change gives stakeholders the accountability they are looking for without it landing on a person. Option B is least effective: it is true, it sounds like a deflection, and it leaves the analyst identifiable as the one whose work was wrong. Option C accepts a framing the facts do not support. Option D second-guesses a premise already established.

### Administering this section

- Present the same twelve scenarios to every candidate assessed at this role level, using the same instructions (most and least effective response, or ratings) consistently.
- Keep the answer key out of the candidate's sight until scoring, and score against it rather than against personal judgement, since the key reflects experienced practitioners at comparable levels.
- At debrief, discuss not just which options a candidate chose but the reasoning they gave — several of these scenarios are defensible from more than one angle, and at this level the political and organisational judgement in the reasoning is where the real signal lies.

### Scoring guidance

- Note whether a candidate would absorb pressure that would otherwise land on an individual analyst, and whether they would check the work first before defending it (Question 1) — this speaks directly to the role's function as the point of contact between the community and the business.
- Note whether a candidate would price a quality requirement before imposing it (Question 3), and whether they would name early when a role as specified is unlikely to fill (Question 4) rather than run a campaign expected to fail.
- Note whether a candidate would withdraw their own standard openly when the evidence turns against it (Question 8), and whether they would present a distribution honestly rather than selectively (Question 10) — both are strong indicators of the self-scrutiny this level requires.
- Note whether a candidate would take public responsibility for a team's error while handling individual development privately (Question 12), and whether they would be candid about an abandoned programme when representing the department externally (Question 11) — both speak to the credibility this role depends on.
- Note whether a candidate's reasoning, not just their selected options, shows awareness that this profession's only real asset is that its numbers turn out to be right — this thread should be visible across most of the twelve scenarios, not just the ones that name it explicitly.

## Conclusion

This document has given the assessor four assessments pitched at head of performance analysis, and once scoring is complete it is worth drawing the threads together.

The cognitive section tests reasoning across a portfolio and a community rather than a single service — reading a pattern for the cause that generates it, distinguishing a measurement artefact from a change in the world, and asking what a figure was designed to show before asking whether it moved.

The numeric section tests the arithmetic of running a profession: vacancy distributions read as shapes rather than totals, quality requirements priced across the annual volume that bears them, business cases stripped of benefits that would have arrived anyway, and a mean of 79% across a bimodal distribution that describes none of the twelve services in it.

The verbal section tests reading the documents that define this role's obligations and discretion. Proposing is not deciding. "Should consult" is not "must". "Anticipates" is a forecast. Contribution rather than authorship is what disqualifies a reviewer. And a review that names the question it did not examine has told the reader why its recommendation cannot yet be relied on — including when the recommendation is one a candidate would have wanted anyway.

The situational judgement section tests the position between a community and a business that mostly wants confirmation. The strong answers absorbed pressure that would otherwise fall on an analyst, priced a quality framework before imposing it, changed a service's behaviour by demonstration rather than advocacy, and took public responsibility for work that turned out wrong while handling the individual privately.

When scoring, weigh the pattern across all four sections rather than any single item, and record where a candidate's answers diverged from the key and why — that record is what makes the debrief useful and the decision defensible if it is later questioned. Use the same items, time limits and scoring key for every candidate assessed at this role level, and treat this assessment as one input among several: pair it with interview, references, or evidence of past work rather than deciding on it alone. If one thread runs through all four sections, it is that this profession has exactly one asset, which is that its numbers turn out to be right — everything else follows from that and is spent by a single figure softened under pressure or a submission that quietly omitted the measure nobody wanted to see. A strong candidate's answers, across all four sections, should show an understanding that they would be the person deciding how that asset is spent.
