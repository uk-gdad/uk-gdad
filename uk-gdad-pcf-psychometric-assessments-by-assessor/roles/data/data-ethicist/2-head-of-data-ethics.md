# Data Ethicist (Head of Data Ethics) - Psychometric Assessment Resources

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of data ethics, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. The material is job-specific: it presents what a head of data ethics genuinely handles — ethics review registers, algorithmic impact assessments, framework documents, and escalation queues — and the judgements the role turns on, rather than abstract puzzles.

This is a leadership role that sits at the intersection of data science, law, social science, policy, and organisational culture. It demands rapid synthesis of complex documents, sound quantitative judgement about risk and evidence, precise reading of policy and legislation, and mature situational judgement when mediating between stakeholders on high-stakes ethical questions.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment is a targeted psychometric test that measures the exact mental processes needed for this role, rather than abstract intelligence in general. It uses practical workplace scenarios and data drawn from the everyday tasks and artefacts of the job: ethics review registers, algorithmic impact assessment summaries, escalation queues, framework version histories, and cross-departmental governance charts.

The typical format is an online, timed assessment lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with objective scoring against a benchmark or norm group appropriate to senior professional and leadership roles. Many platforms offer short, ungraded practice questions before the real test begins, and some adapt question difficulty based on previous answers. A breakdown of speed versus accuracy is usually more informative than a single raw score.

Cognitive assessment matters at this level because the intellectual core of leading a data ethics function is synthesis under pressure: quickly reading and interpreting complex documents from a range of sources, distilling what is relevant, spotting inconsistencies between what a team claims and what its evidence shows, and prioritising a portfolio of consequential risks with imperfect information.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the skills in the role profile:

- **Analysis and synthesis (data ethics)**: drawing together qualitative and quantitative evidence, reading complex documents quickly, and distilling them to what is relevant. Cognitive items test this through error checking, logical deduction from documentary evidence, and identifying which findings actually follow from stated data.
- **Managing decisions and risks**: working with consequential or complex risks and applying different risk methodologies in proportion to the risk. Prioritisation and problem-solving items test whether a candidate can rank issues rationally when criteria conflict.
- **Problem management**: ensuring the right actions are taken to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems. Fault-diagnosis items test tracing a reported symptom back to its most plausible cause in a process or governance chain.
- **Data ethics and privacy**: overseeing compliance with standards and legislation. Error-checking items test the precision needed to spot where a register, framework, or assessment record contradicts the rules it claims to follow.
- **Applied social sciences** and **Analysis and synthesis** together: several items ask whether a stated conclusion is warranted by the study design described — the daily work of challenging assumptions made in data science projects.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; roughly two to three minutes per question is a reasonable guide.

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking an ethics review register.**

An ethics secretariat maintains a register of algorithmic impact assessments (AIAs). An extract reads:

- Project Alpha: AIA submitted 03 March; risk rating High; board review required within 20 working days; board review held 02 April.
- Project Bravo: AIA submitted 10 March; risk rating Medium; board review required within 40 working days; board review held 29 April.
- Project Charlie: AIA submitted 17 March; risk rating High; board review required within 20 working days; board review held 22 May.
- Project Delta: AIA submitted 24 March; risk rating Low; board review not required; board review held 01 April.

Which entries are inconsistent with the stated rules?

A) Alpha only
B) Charlie only
C) Charlie and Delta
D) Bravo and Charlie

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Alpha and Bravo both comply within their respective windows. Charlie is High risk, due within 20 working days of 17 March, but the review happened on 22 May — well outside the window. Delta is rated Low and marked "board review not required", yet a board review was held — an inconsistency between the rule and the record. The trap is option B: a candidate who spots the obvious lateness of Charlie and stops checking misses Delta. This tests exhaustive verification of every row, exactly as a function assuring compliance records across an organisation must.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from framework rules.**

An organisational data ethics framework states: "Every project that processes special category data must complete a full AIA. Every project with a full AIA must be reviewed by the ethics board. Some projects reviewed by the ethics board are escalated to the executive risk committee."

Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

A) Every project reviewed by the ethics board processes special category data.
B) Every project that processes special category data is reviewed by the ethics board.
C) Some projects that process special category data are escalated to the executive risk committee.
D) No project without a full AIA is reviewed by the ethics board.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Chain the first two rules: special category data leads to full AIA, and full AIA leads to ethics board review, so every special-category project reaches the board. Option A reverses the implication. Option C is tempting but not guaranteed. Option D also reverses the second rule. This tests discipline about the direction of implications — the same discipline applied when checking whether a policy actually mandates what a team believes it mandates.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Prioritising an escalation queue.**

Five items sit in a Monday-morning queue. The framework prioritises, in order: (1) live harm to the public, (2) legal or regulatory breach, (3) reputational exposure, (4) internal process failure. Items of the same priority are handled in order of scale of impact.

- Item V: A journalist has emailed asking about the fairness testing of a benefits triage model; publication expected Thursday.
- Item W: A data science team reports that a deployed fraud model is disproportionately flagging claimants from one postcode cluster, and flagged claimants face payment suspensions this week.
- Item X: An internal audit finds that six of forty project teams skipped the AIA sign-off step last quarter.
- Item Y: A legal adviser notes that a live data-sharing pilot may lack a lawful basis under data protection legislation, affecting around 2,000 records.
- Item Z: A training session scheduled for Friday has no facilitator after a resignation.

Which is the correct order to address them?

A) Y, W, V, X, Z
B) W, Y, V, X, Z
C) W, V, Y, Z, X
D) Y, V, W, Z, X

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Item W is live harm — payment suspensions this week — so it takes first place under criterion (1). Item Y is a probable legal breach in a live pilot, criterion (2), second. Item V is reputational exposure with a Thursday deadline, criterion (3), third. Item X, an internal process failure, is fourth; Item Z, smaller in scale, comes last. The common error is placing Y first because "legal breach" sounds most severe, but the stated framework ranks live public harm above legal breach. This tests applying stated criteria rather than instinct — while a real head of data ethics might well challenge the framework's ordering, on the test the framework governs.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Spotting the flaw in an evaluation claim.**

A team pilots a new "ethics self-assessment tool" with ten volunteer project teams. Nine of the ten report that the tool improved their ethical awareness, and the team concludes: "The tool improves ethical awareness across the organisation and should be mandated for all 120 project teams."

Which is the strongest single objection to this conclusion?

A) Ten teams is too small a sample for any conclusion whatsoever.
B) Volunteer teams are likely to differ systematically from teams in general, so the result may not generalise to mandated use.
C) Self-reported awareness is not a meaningful measure under any circumstances.
D) The tool should have been piloted for longer before any survey was run.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The decisive flaw is selection bias: teams that volunteer for an ethics pilot are plausibly more engaged with ethics than average, so a tool that works for enthusiasts may fail when mandated for the indifferent. Options A and C are too absolute. Option D is a procedural preference, not a logical objection. This tests identifying the specific inferential gap in a claim, rather than a generic complaint — the same skill exercised when evaluating and challenging assumptions in data science projects.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in incident data.**

A quarterly log of data ethics incidents by category shows:

- Q1: bias-related 4, consent-related 9, transparency-related 6
- Q2: bias-related 6, consent-related 7, transparency-related 6
- Q3: bias-related 9, consent-related 5, transparency-related 6
- Q4: bias-related 13, consent-related 3, transparency-related 6

If the underlying patterns continue, what is the most reasonable expectation for next quarter?

A) Bias-related 18, consent-related 1, transparency-related 6
B) Bias-related 15, consent-related 2, transparency-related 9
C) Bias-related 16, consent-related 2, transparency-related 6
D) Bias-related 13, consent-related 3, transparency-related 3

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Bias-related incidents increase by a growing increment (+2, +3, +4, so next +5 = 18). Consent-related incidents fall by 2 each quarter (9, 7, 5, 3, so next is 1). Transparency-related incidents are flat at 6. Only option A matches all three patterns; option C is the near-miss for a candidate who assumes the bias series grows linearly. This tests detecting and extending a structure quickly — a component of scanning dashboards for emerging risk signals.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about committee composition.**

A data ethics board must include: at least one legal adviser, at least one data scientist, at least two independent external members, and no more than six members in total. The chair must be an external member. Current confirmed members: Priya (legal adviser), Tomasz (data scientist), Elena (external), and Marcus (external, chair). Two candidates remain for the final places: Ruth (data scientist) and Owen (legal adviser). The board wants maximum disciplinary diversity, defined as the greatest number of distinct categories with more than one holder being avoided.

If both Ruth and Owen are appointed, which requirement or preference is compromised?

A) The maximum size limit is breached.
B) The external membership minimum is breached.
C) The board's diversity preference is compromised, because two categories become duplicated.
D) No requirement or preference is affected.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Six members meets the size limit exactly, so A is wrong. Two externals satisfy the minimum, so B is wrong. But appointing both duplicates two categories (data scientist and legal adviser), working against the diversity preference. This tests careful bookkeeping across multiple simultaneous constraints — the same operation as checking that a proposed governance structure satisfies its terms of reference.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a framework version history.**

The version history of an organisational data ethics framework reads:

- v1.0 published January 2023; review due every 12 months.
- v1.1 published July 2023 (minor amendment; does not reset review clock).
- v2.0 published March 2024 (major revision; resets review clock).
- v2.1 published November 2024 (minor amendment).
- Register note: "Framework fully in date; next review due March 2026."

Today is August 2025. Which statement about the register note is correct?

A) The note is correct.
B) The note is wrong: the review was due January 2025 because minor amendments never affect the clock.
C) The note is wrong: the review was due March 2025, twelve months after v2.0, and the framework is currently overdue.
D) The note is wrong: the review is due November 2025, twelve months after v2.1.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The clock resets only on major revisions. v2.0 (March 2024) is the last major revision, so review fell due March 2025. v2.1 was minor and did not reset the clock, ruling out D; option B miscalculates by ignoring the v2.0 reset. As of August 2025, the framework is around five months overdue, so the register note is wrong. This tests applying a rule mechanically and distrusting a summary line — the cognitive skill behind overseeing compliance with data ethics standards.

**Question 8 (hard) — Diagnosing the root cause of a governance failure.**

Six project teams skipped AIA sign-off last quarter. Facts: (i) all six teams used the new fast-track delivery pathway introduced in April; (ii) the fast-track pathway checklist was copied from the standard pathway but the AIA sign-off line was dropped in editing; (iii) two of the six teams say they knew about the AIA requirement but assumed fast-track projects were exempt; (iv) training records show all six teams completed data ethics induction; (v) teams on the standard pathway had 100% sign-off compliance.

What is the most plausible root cause?

A) Inadequate ethics training across the organisation.
B) A defective process artefact: the fast-track checklist omits the AIA step, so compliant behaviour was never prompted.
C) Deliberate evasion by delivery teams under time pressure.
D) The AIA requirement is disproportionate for fast-track projects.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Perfect compliance on the standard pathway (v) plus universal training completion (iv) eliminates option A. The failure correlates exactly with the pathway whose checklist lost the AIA line, and even the teams who knew the requirement inferred an exemption — a reasonable reading of an official checklist omitting the step, undermining option C's assumption of bad faith. Option D is a policy opinion, not a cause. This tests locating the defect in the system rather than in the people — classic problem management.

**Question 9 (hard) — Evaluating competing explanations for a model disparity.**

A recruitment-screening model shortlists 18% of applicants from group P and 11% from group Q. Four explanations are offered: (1) the model uses postcode, which correlates with group membership; (2) group Q applicants apply to more oversubscribed roles; (3) the training data reflects historically biased human shortlisting decisions; (4) random noise, since the sample is only 60 applicants in total.

Which explanations could, if true, each independently account for the disparity, and which is easiest to check first?

A) All four could account for it; explanation 4 is easiest to check first.
B) Only 1 and 3 could account for it; explanation 1 is easiest to check first.
C) Only 2 and 4 could account for it; explanation 2 is easiest to check first.
D) All four could account for it; explanation 3 is easiest to check first.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Each mechanism is independently sufficient, so options B and C, which rule mechanisms out, are wrong. Checking sample size takes minutes with data already in hand, whereas auditing training data provenance (3) is the slowest, most expensive check. This tests sequencing diagnostic effort by cost and discriminating power — applying risk methodologies in proportion to the risk.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling of ethics reviews.**

A board meets monthly and can review at most three AIAs per meeting. Pending reviews: four High-risk AIAs (due within one meeting cycle), three Medium-risk (due within two cycles), and two Low-risk (due within three cycles). The framework forbids deferring a High-risk AIA and requires at least one Medium-risk review per meeting once any Medium is pending.

Which schedule is compliant?

A) Meeting 1: three High. Meeting 2: one High, two Medium. Meeting 3: one Medium, two Low.
B) Meeting 1: two High, one Medium. Meeting 2: two High, one Medium. Meeting 3: one Medium, two Low.
C) Meeting 1: three High. Meeting 2: three Medium. Meeting 3: one High, two Low.
D) Meeting 1: two High, one Medium. Meeting 2: one High, two Medium. Meeting 3: two Low.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Option A defers a High to meeting 2 while violating the Medium rule at meeting 1. Option C violates the Medium rule at meeting 1 and defers a High to meeting 3. Option D leaves the fourth High unscheduled. Option B clears all four Highs across meetings 1 and 2, includes a Medium at every meeting, and completes the Lows within three cycles — every count reconciles. This tests finding the schedule that satisfies the binding rules and breaches none, and counting every item across the whole schedule since near-miss options usually drop one quietly.

**Question 11 (hard) — Inference from mixed qualitative and quantitative evidence.**

Evidence about a chatbot deployed in a public-facing service: (i) analytics show 92% of sessions complete without human handover; (ii) user research interviews with twelve participants found four who abandoned the chatbot and phoned instead, all with limited English proficiency; (iii) a complaints log shows chatbot complaints doubled month-on-month, from 5 to 10 to 21; (iv) the supplier's report states the model was evaluated "on a representative sample of UK English speakers".

Which synthesis is best supported?

A) The chatbot works well for most users, and the complaint growth is explained by rising usage.
B) The chatbot likely underserves users with limited English proficiency, a group plausibly excluded by the evaluation design; the completion metric may mask abandonment through channel-switching, and complaints are growing at a rate that warrants investigation.
C) The chatbot is failing and should be withdrawn immediately pending a full equality impact assessment.
D) The evidence is contradictory and no conclusion can be drawn until a larger user research study is completed.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The interview evidence and the supplier's evaluation scope converge on a specific, mechanistically plausible gap. The 92% completion metric does not contradict this — users who give up and phone instead may not register as handovers. Option C overshoots proportionality; option D undervalues mixed evidence that triangulates. This tests integrating evidence without exceeding it — turning research data into clear findings without overreaching or deferring indefinitely.

**Question 12 (hard) — Detecting an inconsistency across documents.**

Three documents concern the same automated triage project. The AIA states: "No special category data is processed; ethnicity is neither collected nor inferred." The model card states: "Input features: age band, postcode sector, service usage history, and interpreter-language flag." The DPIA states: "The interpreter-language flag records the language requested for interpreter support and is used to route cases to appropriately skilled caseworkers."

What is the most important issue to raise?

A) Nothing — no document lists ethnicity, so the AIA statement stands.
B) The interpreter-language flag is a plausible proxy from which ethnicity or national origin may be inferred, so the AIA's claim that such data is "neither collected nor inferred" needs re-examination, and the model's use of the flag needs bias assessment.
C) The DPIA and model card contradict each other about the purpose of the flag.
D) Postcode sector is special category data and the AIA is therefore false.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The documents are individually accurate but jointly problematic: an interpreter-language flag correlates strongly with national origin, so a model using it as an input feature may effectively infer protected characteristics even though none is collected by name. Option C is wrong — the two documents are consistent, which is precisely what confirms the flag feeds the model. Option D is legally incorrect. This tests drawing together documents from a range of sources and distilling the one issue that matters, then converting it into an actionable finding.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate checks every row or clause**, rather than stopping at the first fault found.
- **Watch for whether a candidate reads the direction of an implication correctly** — "all A are B" is not "all B are A".
- **Note whether a candidate applies stated criteria in their stated order**, even where the framework's ordering might itself be debatable.
- **Note whether a candidate sequences diagnostic effort by cost and discriminating power** rather than checking everything at once.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and consider offering ungraded practice items first.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer drawn from professional knowledge instead of the material given** — where a stated framework rule conflicts with best practice, the item wants the rule applied.
- **Missing when a candidate stops at the first error found** in a register or record-checking item.
- **Crediting an answer that confuses plausibility with necessity** — a "could be true" option chosen over the one that "must be true".
- **Letting a time-sink question distort the overall picture.**
- **Missing when a candidate second-guesses a correct answer** because a question feels harder than expected.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates a candidate's ability to analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical data presented in the formats this role genuinely produces and consumes. It is not a mental arithmetic contest: most assessments allow or provide an onscreen calculator, because the skill being measured is interpretation — extracting the right figures from a table, choosing the right operation, and sense-checking the result — under a strict time limit.

For a head of data ethics, the numbers that matter are bias audit outputs, selection-rate comparisons, training programme completion figures, ethics board capacity plans, incident trend series, survey results, and the budget stewarded for the function. Holding meaningful conversations with data scientists about minimising bias is a quantitative conversation, and a single misread percentage can undermine an otherwise strong ethical argument presented to a board.

Typical format: 15 to 25 minutes, 12 to 20 questions, each anchored to a table, chart, or short numerical scenario.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Analysis and synthesis (data ethics)**: expertly drawing together and evaluating quantitative data, and turning it into clear findings that inform decisions. Nearly every item below ends with a decision, not just a number.
- **Data ethics and privacy**: overseeing compliance across the organisation requires reading compliance dashboards, completion rates, and audit metrics correctly.
- **Managing decisions and risks**: applying risk methodologies in proportion to the risk means quantifying likelihood, impact, and cost of mitigation.
- **Communication (data ethics)**: holding one's own with data science experts on false positive rates, base rates, and disparity ratios.
- **Product ownership (data ethics)**: analysing survey and adoption metrics for the tools a candidate creates.
- **Problem management**: trend extrapolation and capacity planning items test the ability to anticipate problems before they arrive.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded. A calculator is assumed.

**Question 1 (easy) — Allocating the data ethics programme budget.**

An annual programme budget is £240,000. The allocation is: 35% training delivery, 25% research partnerships, 20% ethics tooling, and the remainder for staff engagement events. How much is allocated to staff engagement events?

A) £36,000
B) £42,000
C) £48,000
D) £60,000

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Named allocations total 80%, leaving 20% for engagement: 0.20 × £240,000 = £48,000. Sense check: 84,000 + 60,000 + 48,000 + 48,000 = £240,000. This tests correctly identifying "the remainder" rather than confusing it with one of the stated percentages.

**Question 2 (easy) — Selection rates in a bias audit.**

A screening model processed 15,000 applications from group A and flagged 1,200 for rejection. It processed 4,500 applications from group B and flagged 540 for rejection. What are the two flag rates, and what is the ratio of group A's rate to group B's rate?

A) 8% and 12%; ratio 0.67
B) 8% and 12%; ratio 1.50
C) 12% and 8%; ratio 1.50
D) 8% and 8.3%; ratio 0.96

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Group A: 8%. Group B: 12%. Ratio A:B = 8 ÷ 12 = 0.67. Option B computes the inverse ratio. This tests reading a ratio in the direction the question asks — the same care needed in a published disparate-impact comparison.

**Question 3 (easy) — Training completion gap.**

4,800 staff are in scope for mandatory data ethics training. At the end of Q2, 62% have completed it. The target is 85% completion by the end of Q4. How many additional staff must complete the training in the second half of the year?

A) 960
B) 1,056
C) 1,104
D) 1,150

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Gap = 23 percentage points; 0.23 × 4,800 = 1,104. This tests working with the percentage-point gap directly, faster and less error-prone than computing two headcounts separately.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Absolute versus relative change in incidents.**

Reported data ethics incidents were 40 in year one, 52 in year two, and 39 in year three. Which statement is correct?

A) Incidents rose 30% then fell 25%, a net fall of 2.5% over the two years.
B) Incidents rose 30% then fell 25%, a net rise of 5% over the two years.
C) Incidents rose 12% then fell 13%, a net fall of 1% over the two years.
D) Incidents rose 30% then fell 33%, a net fall of 3% over the two years.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Year one to two: +30%. Year two to three: −25%. Net (year one to three): (39−40)÷40 = −2.5%. Option B commits the classic error of adding percentage changes; option C confuses absolute changes with percentages. This tests recognising that percentage changes compound on shifting bases rather than adding.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Ethics board capacity planning.**

A board meets 11 times a year and can review at most 3 AIAs per meeting. Next year, 120 projects are expected, of which 30% will require board review. What is the capacity position?

A) Capacity 33, demand 36: a shortfall of 3 reviews.
B) Capacity 33, demand 40: a shortfall of 7 reviews.
C) Capacity 36, demand 36: exactly balanced.
D) Capacity 33, demand 30: headroom of 3 reviews.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Capacity = 11 × 3 = 33. Demand = 0.30 × 120 = 36. Shortfall = 3. This tests anchoring each figure to its source before calculating, and, for a strong candidate's answer, naming a proportionate response — an extra meeting, a higher per-meeting maximum, or risk-based triage.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Risk scoring with an escalation rule.**

A risk methodology scores each risk as likelihood (1–5) multiplied by impact (1–5), and additionally requires that any risk with impact 5 is escalated to the executive committee regardless of score. Four risks: A (likelihood 4, impact 4), B (likelihood 3, impact 5), C (likelihood 5, impact 3), D (likelihood 2, impact 5).

Which risk has the highest score, and which risks are escalated?

A) Highest: A (16); escalated: B and D only.
B) Highest: A (16); escalated: A, B and D.
C) Highest: B (15); escalated: B and D only.
D) Highest: A (16); escalated: A only.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Scores: A=16, B=15, C=15, D=10. A is highest. Escalation triggers on impact 5, applying to B and D — D escalates despite the lowest score, and A does not despite the highest. This tests applying two independent rules rather than assuming the highest score drives every consequence — reflecting why severity-based escalation exists alongside multiplicative scoring.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Evaluating a training programme.**

Before a training programme, staff scored a mean of 3.2 on a 5-point ethical-awareness scale; afterwards the mean was 3.8. What is the percentage increase in the mean score?

A) 12%
B) 15.8%
C) 18.75%
D) 60%

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Increase = 0.6; relative to baseline: 0.6 ÷ 3.2 = 18.75%. Option B divides by the post-training score instead of the baseline. This tests measuring percentage change against the starting value.

**Question 8 (hard) — Balancing the function's staffing budget.**

A data ethics function has 6 full-time staff at an average annual cost of £68,500 each. Non-staff costs are: training materials £36,000, an external research partnership £24,000, and tooling licences £18,000. Total budget is £480,000. What is the position, expressed both in pounds and as a percentage of budget?

A) £9,000 underspend; 1.875% under budget.
B) £9,000 overspend; 1.875% over budget.
C) £21,000 overspend; 4.375% over budget.
D) £3,000 overspend; 0.625% over budget.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Staff = 6 × £68,500 = £411,000. Non-staff = £78,000. Total = £489,000, an overspend of £9,000 (1.875%). This tests careful multi-step multiplication and a sense-check (roughly 6 × £70k ≈ £420k confirms the right region).

**Question 9 (hard) — Base rates and the precision of a fraud model.**

A fraud-detection model screens 10,000 claims. In reality, 2% of claims are fraudulent. The model correctly flags 90% of fraudulent claims, and incorrectly flags 5% of legitimate claims. Of all the claims the model flags, what percentage are actually fraudulent?

A) 90%
B) 47%
C) 27%
D) 18%

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Fraudulent = 200; true positives = 180. Legitimate = 9,800; false positives = 490. Total flagged = 670. Precision = 180 ÷ 670 ≈ 27%. Even with a 90% detection rate, roughly 73% of flagged claims are innocent because the low base rate lets false positives from the large legitimate population swamp true positives. This tests base-rate reasoning — arguably the single most important calculation in this role's professional toolkit, since a candidate who can reproduce it in a meeting changes the conversation about proportionality and human review.

**Question 10 (hard) — Comparing disparity across regions.**

A shortlisting algorithm's success rates by region and group:

| Region | Group X rate | Group Y rate |
|--------|--------------|--------------|
| North  | 20%          | 15%          |
| South  | 24%          | 21%          |
| East   | 18%          | 12%          |
| West   | 30%          | 27%          |

Using the ratio of the lower rate to the higher rate in each region, which region shows the greatest disparity, and which regions fall below a 0.80 ratio threshold?

A) North greatest; North and East below 0.80.
B) East greatest; East only below 0.80.
C) East greatest; North and East below 0.80.
D) North greatest; East only below 0.80.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Ratios: North 0.75, South 0.875, East 0.667, West 0.90. East has the greatest disparity; North and East fall below 0.80. This tests choosing the metric the question specifies (ratio, not percentage-point gap) — a methodological judgement exercised when reviewing a fairness audit.

**Question 11 (hard) — Cost-effectiveness of two interventions.**

A refreshed training programme costs £90,000 per year and is expected to prevent 24 incidents per year. An automated ethics-checklist tool costs £54,000 per year and is expected to prevent 15 incidents per year. The average cost of an incident to the organisation is £5,200. Which statements are correct?

A) The training is more cost-effective per incident prevented; only the training has benefits exceeding its cost.
B) The tool is more cost-effective per incident prevented; both interventions have benefits exceeding their costs.
C) The tool is more cost-effective per incident prevented; only the tool has benefits exceeding its cost.
D) They are equally cost-effective; both have benefits exceeding their costs.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Cost per incident: training £3,750; tool £3,600 — the tool is slightly more cost-effective. Benefit-cost: training nets +£34,800; tool nets +£24,000 — both clear the bar. This tests holding two distinct comparisons — cost-effectiveness and benefit-cost — simultaneously.

**Question 12 (hard) — Projecting complaint growth.**

Complaints about an automated service stood at 21 this quarter and have been growing at approximately 15% per quarter. If that growth rate continues, roughly how many complaints should be expected four quarters from now?

A) 30
B) 34
C) 37
D) 42

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** 21 × 1.15⁴ ≈ 36.7 ≈ 37. Option B applies simple rather than compound growth. This tests recognising and executing a compounding structure cleanly.

**Question 13 (moderate) — Reading a stacked completion table.**

| Directorate | Projects | AIAs completed | AIAs in progress | AIAs not started |
|-------------|----------|----------------|------------------|------------------|
| Operations  | 40       | 28             | 8                | 4                |
| Policy      | 25       | 20             | 3                | 2                |
| Digital     | 55       | 33             | 11               | 11               |

Which directorate has the lowest completion rate, and what is the organisation-wide completion rate?

A) Digital lowest at 60%; organisation-wide 67.5%.
B) Digital lowest at 60%; organisation-wide 66.7%.
C) Operations lowest at 70%; organisation-wide 67.5%.
D) Digital lowest at 55%; organisation-wide 65%.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Rates: Operations 70%, Policy 80%, Digital 60%. Organisation-wide: 81 ÷ 120 = 67.5%, not the unweighted average of the three rates (70%). This tests recognising that a pooled rate is not the average of component rates when group sizes differ.

**Question 14 (hard) — Sampling for a compliance assurance exercise.**

320 completed AIAs are on the register, of which 20% are High-risk. A spot-check exercise samples 15% of the register in total, stratified so that High-risk AIAs are sampled at twice the rate of the others. How many High-risk and how many other AIAs will be checked?

A) 24 High-risk and 24 others.
B) 20 High-risk and 28 others.
C) 16 High-risk and 32 others.
D) 12 High-risk and 36 others.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Total sample = 48. Register = 64 High-risk, 256 others. Solving 128r + 256r = 48 gives r = 0.125, so 32 others and 16 High-risk (verify: 16÷64=25%, 32÷256=12.5%, a 2:1 ratio). This tests defining the base rate as an unknown and solving for stratified sampling — the quantitative expression of risk-proportionate assurance design.

**Question 15 (moderate) — Interpreting survey response bias.**

A "speak up about data ethics" survey went to 4,800 staff; 1,440 responded. Among respondents, 78% agreed that "I feel able to raise ethical concerns". A board member says: "78% of staff feel able to raise concerns." What is the response rate, and what is the correct upper and lower bound for the true organisation-wide agreement figure if every view were known?

A) Response rate 30%; true agreement is between 23.4% and 93.4%.
B) Response rate 30%; true agreement is exactly 78%.
C) Response rate 33%; true agreement is between 30% and 78%.
D) Response rate 30%; true agreement is between 78% and 100%.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Response rate = 30%. Respondents agreeing ≈ 1,123. Lower bound (all non-respondents disagree): ≈23.4%. Upper bound (all agree): ≈93.4%. This tests catching the silent conversion of "respondents" into "all staff" — a slip worth correcting gently in a senior meeting, and one whose bounds discipline everyone's language even when not presented as likely outcomes.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate anchors every percentage to its base** before computing.
- **Watch for whether a candidate writes intermediate results down** on multi-step problems.
- **Note whether a candidate estimates before computing precisely**, catching order-of-magnitude errors early.
- **Note whether a candidate reproduces base-rate arithmetic (precision versus sensitivity) fluently.**
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that misreads scale qualifiers or labels.**
- **Crediting an answer that adds percentage changes across periods** rather than compounding them.
- **Missing when a candidate divides by the wrong base** — percentage change uses the starting value; precision uses the flagged population.
- **Rewarding an unweighted average of rates** across differently sized groups.
- **Letting a time-sink question distort the overall picture.**

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well a candidate understands, analyses, and draws correct conclusions from written material of the kind this role turns over daily: policy documents, legislative guidance, board papers, supplier assurances, research summaries, and delicate stakeholder correspondence. The defining discipline of the format is fact-based logic: answer based strictly on the text provided, ignoring outside knowledge — even where a candidate's outside knowledge is extensive and probably correct.

The classic question format asks whether a statement is True (the passage logically entails it), False (the passage logically contradicts it), or Cannot Say (insufficient information). Some assessments add multiple-choice comprehension items. Timing is tight — often less than a minute per question.

This assessment is unusually authentic for this role. Quickly reading and interpreting complex documents from a range of sources and distilling what is relevant is a named element of the analysis and synthesis skill; overseeing compliance with standards and legislation demands exact reading of what a rule does and does not require; and mediating between technical and non-technical stakeholders often turns on catching precisely what a sentence commits its author to.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Analysis and synthesis (data ethics)**: distilling complex documents to what is relevant, refusing to let a summary drift beyond its evidence.
- **Data ethics and privacy**: reading policy and legislative text with precision — what is mandated, what is merely encouraged, what is conditional.
- **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: several passages are written communications to or from stakeholders; the questions probe tone, commitment, and implication.
- **Communication (data ethics)**: understanding technical jargon well enough to catch when a technically-worded assurance says less than it appears to.
- **Applied social sciences**: one passage is a research summary; the questions test distinguishing findings from interpretations.
- **Stakeholder relationship management**: correspondence items test the careful reading that precedes influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Passage A — extract from a draft organisational data ethics policy:**

> "All projects that process personal data must complete a data ethics screening questionnaire at the discovery phase. Projects whose screening indicates elevated ethical risk must additionally complete a full algorithmic impact assessment before public beta. The ethics board may, at its discretion, require a full assessment from any project regardless of screening outcome. Completed assessments must be reviewed by the ethics board within twenty working days of submission, and a summary of each reviewed assessment must be published, except where publication would prejudice law enforcement activity or national security. The head of data ethics is accountable for the operation of this policy, and each director-general remains accountable for compliance within their own directorate."

**Question 1 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Every project that processes personal data must complete a full algorithmic impact assessment.

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** Every personal-data project must complete a screening questionnaire; the full assessment is required only where screening indicates elevated risk (or at board discretion). This tests catching an over-generalised obligation against a two-tier structure.

**Question 2 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The ethics board can require a full assessment from a project whose screening did not indicate elevated risk.

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** "Any project regardless of screening outcome" logically includes those with no elevated risk finding. This tests a direct entailment carried by a single phrase.

**Question 3 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Summaries of reviewed assessments relating to immigration enforcement are never published.

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The publication exception applies "where publication would prejudice law enforcement activity or national security" — a case-by-case test the passage does not resolve for any specific topic. This tests resisting a blanket "never" claim the text neither asserts nor denies.

**Question 4 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: If a directorate fails to comply with the policy, the head of data ethics is accountable for that failure.

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The final sentence assigns compliance accountability to directors-general, distinct from the head of data ethics' accountability for the policy's operation. This tests reading a carefully drawn accountability distinction as written, not as commonly misremembered.

**Passage B — extract from published guidance on an algorithmic transparency standard:**

> "Public sector organisations in scope are expected to publish a transparency record for algorithmic tools that either engage directly with the public or have a significant influence on decisions affecting individuals. Publication is expected before the tool is used to inform decisions, or as soon as practicable afterwards where earlier publication was not possible. A record comprises two tiers: the first tier provides a short, plain-language description of the tool and its purpose; the second tier provides more detailed technical information, including data sources and oversight arrangements. The guidance does not itself create statutory obligations; however, organisations should note that existing data protection legislation continues to apply independently of this standard."

**Question 5 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: An in-scope organisation that deploys a significant algorithmic tool without publishing a transparency record is breaking the law.

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The guidance "does not itself create statutory obligations", so non-compliance with it cannot, on this text, constitute breaking the law by virtue of the guidance. This tests separating "expected to" from "legally required to" — normative registers a policy professional must keep razor-sharp.

**Question 6 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: A tool that never interacts with the public directly can still be in scope for a transparency record.

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** Scope is defined disjunctively — "either... or" — so a back-office tool with significant influence over decisions about individuals satisfies the second limb alone. This tests processing a disjunction correctly.

**Question 7 (hard).** Which one of the following best describes the relationship between the two tiers of a transparency record, according to the passage?

A) The first tier is mandatory and the second tier is optional.
B) The first tier is for the public and the second tier is for regulators only.
C) The first tier gives an accessible overview while the second tier gives fuller technical detail, and both together comprise the record.
D) The second tier replaces the first tier once detailed information becomes available.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** "A record comprises two tiers" makes both constituents of one record, ruling out D's replacement model; nothing differentiates audiences (eliminating B) or obligatory status (eliminating A). This tests matching each option against specific wording rather than plausibility.

**Passage C — email from a director of operations to the head of data ethics:**

> "Thanks for your team's review of the caseworker triage model. I accept the finding that the model's error rate is higher for claimants over 65, and we will fund the retraining work you recommended, though I cannot commit to your proposed timescale until I have the supplier's estimate. I do want to push back on one point: pausing the model entirely while retraining happens would, on our figures, add six weeks to average waiting times, and I am not persuaded the interim risk justifies that harm. I would rather we discuss enhanced human review of over-65 cases as a bridge. Could your team and mine meet this week to agree an interim safeguard? I should add that the minister's office has asked for a note on this by Friday."

**Question 8 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The director has agreed to fund the retraining work.

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** An unqualified commitment; the timescale qualification attaches to a different noun. This tests separating what was conceded from what was reserved.

**Question 9 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The director believes the interim risk to over-65 claimants is acceptable.

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** "Not persuaded the interim risk justifies that harm" is a comparative judgement about a full pause, not a statement that the risk is acceptable — and the proposal of enhanced human review suggests the opposite. This tests a tone-sensitive reading that a loose paraphrase would miss.

**Question 10 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The minister's office has requested a note on this matter by Friday.

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** Stated directly with matching force and content. This tests matching both the content and the modal force of a paraphrase — a stronger claim (e.g. "legally required") would be Cannot Say.

**Question 11 (hard).** Which one of the following is an assumption the director makes but does not defend?

A) That retraining the model is necessary.
B) That the six-week waiting time estimate produced by their own team is reliable.
C) That the model's error rate is higher for claimants over 65.
D) That the minister's office is aware of the model's error rate.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The six-week figure is asserted, not defended, while options A and C are explicit acceptances rather than assumptions. This tests identifying the load-bearing but undefended premise in a stakeholder's argument — the analytical heart of mediating a difficult discussion.

**Passage D — summary of a commissioned research study:**

> "The study examined attitudes to data sharing among 2,400 adults across the UK, using a quota sample matched to national demographics. Sixty-one per cent of participants said they would support their health data being used for planning public services, provided the data were anonymised. Support fell to 28 per cent where data would be shared with commercial organisations, even with anonymisation. Younger participants expressed higher trust in government data handling than older participants, reversing the pattern reported in the research team's 2019 study. The authors caution that stated attitudes in surveys may diverge from behaviour, and recommend deliberative workshops before any policy change."

**Question 12 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: A majority of UK adults support their health data being used for planning public services without anonymisation.

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The 61% figure is explicitly conditional on anonymisation; the passage says nothing about support without it. This tests not mistaking the falsity of the stated condition's applicability for evidence about the unconditioned figure.

**Question 13 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The research team's 2019 study found that older participants expressed higher trust in government data handling than younger participants.

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** If the current pattern is a reversal of 2019, the 2019 pattern ran the other way. This tests catching a one-word logical hinge ("reversing") that a research synthesiser must catch at reading speed.

**Question 14 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The authors believe the survey results should not influence policy.

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** Recommending deliberative workshops before policy change presupposes the evidence is policy-relevant, just insufficient alone — methodological caution, not dismissal. This tests distinguishing caution from dismissal when synthesising research for an organisation.

**Passage E — extract from a supplier's assurance letter about a procured risk-scoring model:**

> "Our model was evaluated for fairness prior to delivery. Bias testing was conducted against gender and age using the supplier's standard evaluation dataset, and no statistically significant disparities were identified at the 95 per cent confidence level. Ethnicity testing was not performed, as ethnicity data was not available in the evaluation dataset. The model is refreshed quarterly using data supplied by the client, and responsibility for monitoring performance drift between refreshes rests with the client under clause 9 of the services agreement. We remain confident that the model, when operated in accordance with our published guidance, supports fair decision-making."

**Question 15 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The supplier found no evidence of gender bias in the model.

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** Testing occurred and returned no statistically significant disparity — matching "no evidence of gender bias" exactly as worded; a stronger claim ("the model is free of gender bias") would be Cannot Say. This tests staying within what the passage asserts rather than reaching for the nearby stronger claim.

**Question 16 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The model has been shown to make fair decisions across ethnic groups.

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** "Ethnicity testing was not performed" directly contradicts a claim that fairness across ethnic groups "has been shown". This tests the asymmetry with Question 15: a claimed demonstration the passage denies ever happened is False, not Cannot Say.

**Question 17 (hard).** According to the passage, which one of the following is the client responsible for?

A) Conducting quarterly refreshes of the model.
B) Monitoring performance drift between refreshes.
C) Supplying ethnicity data for future bias testing.
D) Publishing guidance on fair operation of the model.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Stated directly under clause 9. Option A fails on exact wording — the client supplies data, but the passage does not say the client conducts the refresh. This tests allocating obligations to parties exactly as the text does.

**Question 18 (hard).** Which one of the following is the best summary of Passage E for a briefing to an executive committee?

A) The supplier has fully assured the model's fairness and the client's only duty is routine monitoring.
B) The supplier reports clean bias results for gender and age on its own evaluation dataset, but ethnicity was untested for lack of data, and drift monitoring between quarterly refreshes is contractually the client's responsibility.
C) The supplier has refused to test the model for bias and has transferred all assurance responsibilities to the client.
D) The model is biased against groups the supplier chose not to test.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Option B preserves the passage's findings, scope limits, and allocation of responsibility without adding a verdict the text does not contain. This tests neutral summarising — letting a decision-maker reach their own judgement rather than smuggling one in.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate distinguishes False (contradicted) from Cannot Say (silent).**
- **Watch for whether a candidate catches logical hinge words** — must/should/may, "either... or", "unless", "regardless of", "provided that", "reversing".
- **Note whether a candidate quarantines outside expertise**, pointing to the exact phrase supporting an answer.
- **Note whether a candidate matches modality precisely in a paraphrase**, not just topic.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer drawn from expert outside knowledge** rather than the passage — the most common failure mode for expert candidates.
- **Missing when a candidate confuses "likely" with "certain".**
- **Crediting Cannot Say used as a refuge for uncertainty** rather than a precise verdict the candidate can articulate a reason for.
- **Missing loose paraphrase** — "accountable for the operation of the policy" is not "accountable for compliance".
- **Missing when a candidate misattaches a qualifier to the wrong noun.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to evaluate possible responses. There is no formula to apply: several options may seem reasonable, and the task is to identify which behaviour best fits the role's demands and the organisation's values — and which fits worst. Response formats vary: most-effective and least-effective selection, full ranking, or independent rating. Timing is usually generous or absent.

For a head of data ethics, this is arguably the most authentic assessment of the four. The role profile is dense with judgement-laden work: mediating between people and strengthening relationships; moderating difficult discussions about high-risk topics within constrained timescales; influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders; building consensus between independent stakeholders; helping people ask questions and express concerns; and developing a data ethics culture that people act on rather than merely comply with.

The scenarios below are calibrated to head-of-profession level: they involve ministers' offices, directors-general, supplier executives, civil society, the media, and the candidate's own team — and they frequently pit two goods against each other. The scoring convention in UK public sector assessments rewards options that align with public sector values: putting user needs and the public interest first, transparency and honesty, collaboration across boundaries, accessibility and inclusion, proportionality, and taking ownership rather than deflecting.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: several scenarios test mediating, choosing the right channel and register, and moderating difficult discussions under time pressure.
- **Stakeholder relationship management**: scenarios involving directors, ministers' offices, and suppliers test influencing, negotiating, and preserving relationships while holding a line.
- **Managing decisions and risks**: dilemmas require weighing consequential risks and acting proportionately rather than maximally.
- **Data ethics and privacy** and **Product ownership (data ethics)**: scenarios test whether culture and capability are built rather than a bottleneck approval gate.
- **Empathy and inclusivity**: scenarios test whether underrepresented voices are genuinely incorporated, not consulted cosmetically.
- **Problem management**: incident scenarios test driving investigation, resolution, and prevention in the right order.
- **Communication (data ethics)** and **Analysis and synthesis**: scenarios test candour about evidence, and support for data scientists implementing ethics in practice.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (moderate) — The minister's deadline versus the unfinished assessment.**

A minister's office asks a department to launch a public-facing eligibility checker in ten days, to coincide with a policy announcement. The algorithmic impact assessment is half-complete, and early findings suggest the tool may struggle with users who have non-standard work histories — disproportionately carers, most of whom are women. A director asks: "Can we live with launching and finishing the assessment afterwards?"

A) Agree to launch: the tool is only a checker, not a decision-maker, and the assessment can conclude in parallel.
B) Refuse to support any launch until the assessment is fully complete, and say escalation will follow if overruled.
C) Propose a risk-based path: complete the carers-related analysis before launch as the known material risk, launch with clear signposting to non-digital routes and enhanced monitoring, and finish the remaining assessment to an agreed date.
D) Tell the director the decision is theirs alone, and note concerns by email for the record.
E) Ask the team to work over the weekend to finish the entire assessment before launch, whatever it takes.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option C isolates the identified material risk, insists on resolving it before public exposure, and offers a credible path to the deadline with mitigations. Option B is principled but rigid, treating a two-tier instrument as all-or-nothing and spending escalation capital before negotiation is tried. Option A accepts an identified equality risk on the strength of a label rather than analysis. Option E gold-plates beyond what risk requires. Option D is least effective: passive deflection that technically covers the candidate while abandoning both the users at risk and the director who asked for professional judgement.

**Question 2 (moderate) — The data scientist who found bias and is scared to say so.**

A mid-level data scientist contacts the head of data ethics privately: her team's deployed model shows a disparity affecting a minority ethnic group, but her team lead told her the finding is "statistical noise, don't distract the sprint" and she fears being labelled difficult. She asks for her name to be kept out of it.

A) Thank her, promise anonymity, and quietly commission an independent re-analysis of the model's outputs through the standard assurance programme.
B) Raise the issue immediately with the team lead's head of profession, naming the data scientist so the report is credible.
C) Tell her to raise it through her own management chain first, since bypassing it would undermine her team lead.
D) Thank her, promise anonymity, commission an independent look at the model through routine assurance, and separately work on why her team treats bias findings as sprint distractions — since the culture that silenced her is itself a finding.
E) Ask her to send the analysis first, to judge whether it is worth pursuing before doing anything.

**Most effective: D. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option D does everything option A does and adds the leadership-level insight that a team dismissing a bias finding as sprint noise is a culture failure that will recur regardless of this model's outcome. Option B is least effective: breaking an explicit confidence within hours teaches the whole organisation that speaking to the ethics function is unsafe. Option C ignores that her chain has already suppressed the finding once. Option E makes support conditional and risks identifying her before any commitment is made.

**Question 3 (hard) — The journalist with a partially accurate story.**

A national journalist emails a department's press office, copying the head of data ethics: they will publish in 48 hours a story claiming the department's fraud model "discriminates against ethnic minorities". The last bias audit found a small, monitored disparity judged proportionate with mitigation in place; the story's central claim overstates this, but the underlying figures cited are broadly right. The press office drafts: "The department does not comment on operational algorithms."

A) Support the press office line: engaging will amplify the story.
B) Push for a factual, plain-language response acknowledging the audited disparity, explaining the mitigation and monitoring, and offering the transparency record and audit summary — and use the moment to accelerate their publication.
C) Push for a robust rebuttal stating the model has been audited and found fair.
D) Contact the journalist directly to correct the figures off the record.
E) Advise saying nothing now but commissioning a fresh independent audit, announcing that instead.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B contextualises rather than stonewalls, converting a defensive moment into evidence that governance works. Option A cedes the entire frame to the story. Option C is least effective because it is false — the audit did find a disparity, and "found fair" would be a public misstatement the journalist's own figures could demolish. Option D bypasses the press office and creates an unminuted account. Option E delays truth-telling that could happen today.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two directors, one dataset, no consensus.**

The operations directorate wants to reuse an existing dataset of service users to train a demand-forecasting model; it would cut waiting times measurably. The legal team is comfortable, but analysis finds the original collection notice never anticipated this reuse, and user researchers report that affected users — many from low-income groups — would find the reuse surprising. The operations director says: "Legal signed it off; ethics is not a second legal team." The policy director backs the ethics position. Both directors ask for the deadlock to be resolved.

A) Concede: legality is the authoritative test, and waiting-time reductions are a genuine user benefit.
B) Hold the objection and escalate the deadlock to the executive committee for a decision.
C) Convene both directors around the actual evidence — the collection notice, the user research — and propose a path that separates what blocks from what does not: proceed with the forecasting work using data with an updated notice and a light-touch public engagement exercise on reuse, with the surprise finding tested rather than asserted.
D) Run a full public consultation before any decision, whatever the delay to the programme.
E) Agree to the reuse provided the model's outputs are never used for individual-level decisions.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C re-anchors a stalled disagreement in evidence, distinguishes ethics' distinctive contribution (legitimate expectations, not legality) without disparaging legal's role, and converts a binary fight into a sequenced plan. Option A is least effective: it accepts the category error that legality exhausts ethics — the precise distinction the role exists to hold — and abandons a documented user-expectation problem. Option B is premature. Option D overshoots the risk. Option E addresses a harm nobody established while leaving the actual issue untouched.

**Question 5 (hard) — The framework nobody follows.**

Twelve months after publishing an organisational data ethics framework, uptake is poor: teams describe it as "a 40-page PDF written for philosophers", the screening questionnaire is completed late and minimally, and one delivery team has built its own informal checklist instead — which is actually rather good. The candidate's own team is demoralised and wants mandatory compliance backed by spend controls.

A) Seek the mandate: make framework compliance a condition of funding approval, since culture follows structure.
B) Treat the informal checklist as user research: work with that delivery team to co-design a shorter, workflow-native version of the framework, pilot it, and publicise the collaborating team's work as exemplary practice.
C) Commission a communications campaign to explain the existing framework better.
D) Rewrite the framework alone over the next quarter to be shorter and clearer, then relaunch it.
E) Keep the framework as the reference standard but focus the team on embedding advisers within high-risk programmes only.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is product ownership done properly: a team spontaneously building its own checklist is the loudest user need available, and co-designing with them turns the biggest compliance failure into the best adoption asset. Option A is least effective at this moment: mandating an artefact users have shown to be unusable enforces resentment and converts the function from enabler to police before the product deserves enforcement. Option C explains harder instead of listening. Option D repeats the original error of authoring alone. Option E is defensible but abandons the organisation-wide ambition on its own.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The consultation that excluded the affected.**

A programme team presents "extensive engagement" for a new data-sharing policy: a public survey (2,000 responses), two industry roundtables, and an academic workshop. The survey was online-only and English-only, and the policy will most affect people with limited digital access and limited English proficiency. Launch is six weeks away and the engagement budget is 90% spent.

A) Note the limitation in the ethics review and let the programme proceed: engagement was genuinely extensive by normal standards.
B) Require the programme to repeat its engagement exercise with a representative sample before launch.
C) Work with the team to commission rapid, targeted engagement with the excluded groups — through community organisations, interpreters, and existing outreach channels — using the remaining budget, and make the policy's launch plan explicitly provisional on what it hears.
D) Ask the programme to add a paragraph to the policy acknowledging the gap and committing to post-launch review.
E) Escalate to the senior responsible owner that the engagement was discriminatory and the launch must be paused.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C operationalises empathy and inclusivity through in-depth outreach strategies targeted at the most affected groups. Option A is least effective: it mistakes volume for representativeness and waves through a consultation that structurally excluded the policy's primary population. Option B overcorrects by junking all engagement. Option D converts a fixable exclusion into a documented-but-accepted one. Option E reaches for loaded language before offering a constructive path.

**Question 7 (hard) — The supplier who will not show their working.**

A critical supplier's risk-scoring model is central to a live service. An assurance review requests feature-level documentation and bias-testing evidence. The supplier's account director declines, citing intellectual property, offering instead a summary attestation "consistent with what other government clients accept". The contract, agreed before the current head of data ethics' appointment, is ambiguous on audit rights. The service is performing well operationally, and renewal negotiations start next month.

A) Accept the attestation for now, noting the limitation in the assurance report.
B) Threaten to advise termination of the contract unless full documentation is provided within 30 days.
C) Brief the commercial team so that explicit audit, documentation, and bias-testing rights become non-negotiable clauses in the renewal, and meanwhile negotiate a middle path for the live period — such as supervised access under a non-disclosure agreement, or independent third-party evaluation against agreed fairness metrics.
D) Ask the data science team to reverse-engineer the model's behaviour from its inputs and outputs instead.
E) Escalate to the department's board that the service is operating without adequate ethical assurance.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option C plays the hand that actually exists — the renewal is the moment of maximum leverage — and offers an interim path respecting the supplier's IP concern while refusing the false choice between full disclosure and blind trust. Option B is least effective: an empty threat (the service will not be terminated over documentation on this advice alone) destroys negotiating position weeks before the real negotiation. Option A concedes at exactly the wrong moment. Option D has value as a supplement but not a substitute. Option E may become necessary but arrives at the board without a recommendation if attempted first.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The incident that is also a pattern.**

A confirmed data ethics incident: an analytics team matched two datasets in a way that re-identified around 300 individuals, and a small number of records were viewed by staff without a need to know. Containment is done, the data protection officer is handling the statutory side, and the analytics team is mortified and cooperative. This is the third re-identification near-miss or incident involving dataset matching this year, each from a different team.

A) Focus effort on the current incident: a thorough investigation and a strong lessons-learned report for this team.
B) Ask each directorate to remind staff of the data-matching policy.
C) Treat the recurrence as the primary problem: alongside the incident response, initiate a cross-team problem investigation into why matching risks keep arising — tooling defaults, absent re-identification checks, training gaps — and put a preventive fix (such as a mandatory pre-matching risk step with support tooling) to the governance board.
D) Recommend disciplinary referrals for the staff who viewed the records, to signal seriousness.
E) Suspend all dataset-matching activity organisation-wide pending a review.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: E.**

**Explanation:** Option C is textbook problem management: three similar events from three different teams is a system signal, and the right unit of analysis is the pattern. Option E is least effective: an organisation-wide suspension over a contained incident is wildly disproportionate and teaches the organisation that reporting incidents to ethics triggers paralysis. Option A does honest work on the wrong scope. Option B is an empty gesture. Option D punishes individuals while leaving the system intact and risks chilling the cooperation that enabled rapid containment.

**Question 9 (hard) — Public criticism of the candidate's own tool.**

At a sector conference, a respected academic presents research arguing that the ethics self-assessment tool the candidate's team built and rolled out "produces false assurance": teams score themselves highly while their outputs show unaddressed risks. Colleagues are watching the reaction; the academic has a point, though the study covers only the tool's early version. The candidate is due on the same stage tomorrow.

A) Use the slot to defend the tool: cite adoption figures, note the study used the outdated version, and reassure the audience it works.
B) Use the slot to engage the critique on its merits: acknowledge the false-assurance risk as real and inherent to self-assessment, describe what the current version changed and what remains open, and invite the academic to collaborate on evaluating it against outcome data.
C) Skip the topic in the slot and speak to the academic privately afterwards.
D) Ask the team overnight to produce a rebuttal analysis showing the tool's scores correlate with better outcomes.
E) Announce from the stage that the tool is being withdrawn pending an independent review.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B models non-defensive engagement with challenge in public — where culture is actually made — and converts a critic into a potential evaluator. Option A is least effective: defending with adoption figures answers a validity critique with a popularity statistic, and the audience learns that ethics leadership gets defensive under peer review. Option C wastes the platform. Option D asks for confirmatory analysis overnight, methodologically unserious. Option E overcorrects theatrically.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Building the pipeline behind the function.**

Funding exists for one senior hire. The team is stretched: the AIA review queue is growing, and directors increasingly ask for personal involvement in their programmes. Two strong internal candidates emerge: a brilliant analyst who would clear the review queue fastest, and a capable practitioner from an underrepresented background whose strengths are training, community-building, and translating ethics into delivery language — weaker on review throughput. Several directors lobby for the analyst.

A) Hire the analyst: the queue is the immediate operational risk, and the directors supporting the function matters.
B) Hire the practitioner: representation matters more than throughput.
C) Decide against the assessment of the function's strategic need: if the bottleneck is that ethics scales only through the team's own reviews, the capability-building practitioner changes that curve — so hire them, address the queue through triage and trained reviewers in directorates, and explain the reasoning to the lobbying directors.
D) Delay the hire and ask for funding for both.
E) Hire the analyst now and promise the practitioner the next post.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option C makes the strategic reading this level demands: a growing central queue plus rising demand for personal presence signals a function scaling linearly while its remit grows exponentially, and enabling others to implement data ethics best practice is the durable fix. Option D is least effective: the queue and demand are live now, and deferring a decision the candidate has the resources and evidence to make is the passive option in strategic clothing. Option A entrenches the cause of the queue. Option B reaches a defensible hire by an indefensible route. Option E mortgages trust with a promise that may not be kept.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate's response holds an ethical line while offering stakeholders a workable path**, rather than blocking outright or conceding outright.
- **Watch for whether a candidate reads for the systemic signal** — a repeat pattern, a cultural signal, an approaching negotiation — rather than only the presenting incident.
- **Note what a candidate's action teaches**: does it make it safer or more dangerous to raise concerns, report incidents, or invite scrutiny?
- **Note whether a candidate attempts direct, constructive resolution before escalating**, absent live harm or genuine misconduct.
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding the idealistic answer over the effective one** — "refuse until perfect" feels principled but often abandons the negotiation the role exists to conduct.
- **Crediting a passive option** — noting concerns for the record, waiting for unnecessary information, deferring a decision the candidate is equipped to make.
- **Missing when a candidate escalates before attempting direct resolution.**
- **Missing when a candidate ignores the stated organisational values** — transparency, inclusion, and user needs generally outweigh commercial confidence and reputational defensiveness in this context.
- **Rewarding a bold, decisive-sounding option that is actually disproportionate** — suspending everything, terminating a contract, announcing a withdrawal on stage.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a head of data ethics: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested synthesis under pressure: error-checking registers, chaining deductions correctly, and diagnosing root causes from a system rather than blaming individuals. The numeric section tested the arithmetic behind bias audits and base rates — the calculation that changes a conversation about a "90% accurate" model. The verbal section tested reading policy, research, and supplier assurances at clause-level precision, and resisting the pull of extensive outside expertise. The situational judgement section tested mediating dilemmas that pit two goods against each other, holding an ethical line while offering stakeholders a workable path, and protecting the people and culture that let concerns be raised safely.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can reason precisely under pressure, read dense material for exactly what it does and does not establish, and lead a data ethics function through mediation, proportionality, and culture-building — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
