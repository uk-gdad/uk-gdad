# Digital Evaluator (Junior Digital Evaluator) - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide gives you, the assessor, a structured psychometric assessment to administer to a candidate for the junior digital evaluator role level. It is written for a line manager, a panel member, or another reviewer running a recruitment or promotion process - not for the candidate to work through alone.

A psychometric assessment is a structured, standardised way of measuring how a candidate thinks, reasons, and makes decisions. "Psycho" refers to the mind, and "metric" refers to measurement. These items are not designed to catch a candidate out, and they do not test memorised facts. They measure skills a junior digital evaluator uses every day: spotting patterns in data, reading a report carefully, working out a percentage, and choosing a sensible course of action when a situation is tricky.

Why does this matter for this role in particular? A junior digital evaluator supports experienced evaluators in assessing whether digital products and services deliver real value for the public. They help develop theories of change, collect and analyse quantitative and qualitative data, check that data looks right, and communicate findings clearly. Every one of those duties depends on the mental skills this assessment measures. Administering it well tells you whether a candidate can reason carefully with evidence - the heart of evaluation work.

This document is organised into four main assessment sections, each matching a common type of workplace psychometric test:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** - the candidate's general thinking, logic, and problem-solving skills.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** - the candidate's ability to work with numbers, tables, and percentages.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** - the candidate's ability to read carefully and draw accurate conclusions from text.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** - the candidate's judgement in realistic workplace situations.

Each section explains what the assessment measures, how it connects to the specific skills in the role, and then gives a generous set of items with worked answers and explanations - your answer key. Every item is set in the world a junior digital evaluator actually works in: evaluation plans, theories of change, survey data, performance indicators, and UK government digital services.

**Administer it fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time limit. Keep this document - including every worked answer and explanation - away from the candidate until after they have completed the assessment; it is your answer key, not their study material. Each section notes a rough time per item, useful for planning a session, and you may want to offer a few short, ungraded practice items first so a candidate can get used to the format before the clock starts.

**Debrief afterwards.** Once you have scored the assessment, talk it through with the candidate using the explanations below - this gives them useful feedback and gives you a fuller picture than the score alone.

**One input among several.** Use this assessment alongside a structured interview, a review of the candidate's experience, and any other evidence your process gathers. See the advisory below for what this assessment is not.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment is a timed test that measures the core mental processes a candidate needs for this particular job. Unlike a generic intelligence test, it uses practical workplace scenarios, documents, and data that look like the real things a junior digital evaluator handles: evaluation plans, theories of change, survey responses, data tables, monitoring dashboards, and government guidance documents.

Plan for this to take 15 to 30 minutes to complete online. Items are objectively scored, and results are best interpreted against a benchmark group of candidates at a similar level, or against the answer key below. Consider giving candidates a few short, ungraded practice items first so they can get used to the interface before the clock starts. Some platforms are adaptive, meaning difficulty adjusts based on a candidate's previous answers - if you use one, it is worth telling candidates beforehand that questions feeling harder as the session goes on can be a sign they are doing well, not a sign of struggle.

Why does this matter for the role? Evaluation is fundamentally a thinking discipline. Developing a theory of change means reasoning about logical links between activities, outputs, and outcomes. Checking whether data looks right is systematic error checking. Supporting an impact evaluation means applying logical deduction: if the intervention caused the outcome, what patterns should we see in the data, and what patterns would make us doubt that conclusion? A cognitive assessment samples exactly these processes, which is why employers in analytical professions rely on them.

Because a junior digital evaluator uses these thinking skills constantly in day-to-day work, this section gives you a genuine read on job-ready reasoning, not just test-taking ability.

### What it measures for this role

Here is how the main cognitive dimensions map onto the specific skills in the junior digital evaluator role:

- **Logical deduction** maps to the **Evaluation delivery** skill. Evaluating interventions using techniques such as process evaluation, impact evaluation, theory of change, experimentation, and economic evaluation requires reasoning from evidence to conclusions, and spotting when a conclusion does not follow from the evidence.
- **Pattern recognition** maps to the **Evaluation delivery** and **Product and service monitoring** skills. Analysing and interpreting data - including descriptive and inferential analysis - means noticing trends, seasonality, and anomalies in performance indicators.
- **Error checking** maps directly to the **Quality assurance of data and analysis** skill. Describing basic data issues, checking that data and analysis look right, and understanding whether data is fit for purpose are all forms of structured error detection.
- **Prioritisation and problem solving** map to the **Evaluation planning and strategy** skill. Translating stakeholder needs into research questions, and choosing appropriate monitoring and evaluation methods, requires weighing options under constraints.
- **Sequencing and process logic** map to the **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle** skill. Understanding how evaluation activities vary across discovery, alpha, beta, and live phases requires reasoning about order, dependency, and timing.

### Practice questions

Present each question in order and hold back the answer until scoring. Budget roughly one to two minutes per question at this level.

**Question 1 (easy) - Theory of change logic**

A theory of change for a new online benefits application service reads, in part:

- Activity: Build a simpler online application form.
- Output: More citizens complete the application without needing telephone support.
- Intended outcome: Citizens receive their benefit decisions faster.

Which of the following, if true, would most directly weaken the logical link between the output and the intended outcome?

A) Some citizens do not have internet access.
B) The decision-processing team has a backlog, so completed applications wait three weeks before anyone reads them.
C) The new form took longer to build than planned.
D) Telephone support staff feel their roles may change.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question asks about the link between the output (more completed applications without phone support) and the outcome (faster decisions). Option B shows that even if applications are completed more easily, decisions will not necessarily arrive faster, because the bottleneck is downstream in decision processing. That directly breaks the output-to-outcome link. Option A weakens the activity-to-output link (some people cannot use the form at all), not the link the question asks about. Options C and D are delivery and people concerns that do not affect the logical chain. In theory-of-change work, being precise about which link an assumption supports is a core junior evaluator skill.

**Question 2 (easy) - Error checking in a dataset**

A candidate is checking a spreadsheet of user satisfaction survey responses before analysis. Satisfaction is recorded on a scale of 1 to 5. Which row contains a value that should be flagged?

| Row | Respondent ID | Date | Satisfaction score |
|-----|---------------|------------|--------------------|
| 1 | U-1041 | 2026-03-02 | 4 |
| 2 | U-1042 | 2026-03-02 | 5 |
| 3 | U-1043 | 2026-03-03 | 55 |
| 4 | U-1044 | 2026-03-03 | 1 |

A) Row 1
B) Row 2
C) Row 3
D) Row 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The satisfaction scale runs from 1 to 5, so the value 55 in row 3 is outside the valid range. It is most likely a data entry error - perhaps the true answer was 5 and the key was pressed twice. This is exactly the kind of basic data issue the candidate's quality assurance skill covers: a range check. Note that row 4's score of 1 is a valid value even though it is unhappy feedback; low values are not errors. A strong candidate will check the minimum and maximum of every numeric column before analysing it.

**Question 3 (easy) - Sequencing evaluation activities**

A team is planning evaluation activities for a digital service moving through its life cycle. Place these activities in the most logical order:

1. Analyse live performance data against key indicators to assess whether intended outcomes are being achieved.
2. Develop a theory of change with stakeholders to agree what the service should achieve.
3. Define measurable performance indicators based on the theory of change.
4. Set up data collection so the indicators can actually be measured.

A) 2, 3, 4, 1
B) 3, 2, 1, 4
C) 2, 4, 3, 1
D) 1, 2, 3, 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The logical dependency chain is: meaningful indicators (3) cannot be defined until it is known what the service is trying to achieve (2); indicator data (4) cannot be collected until the needed indicators are known (3); and outcomes (1) cannot be analysed until data has been collected (4). So the order is 2, 3, 4, 1. This mirrors real practice: the theory of change comes early and drives everything downstream. Recognising dependency chains like this is a form of process logic that cognitive assessments test frequently.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Logical deduction from monitoring rules**

A team follows these monitoring rules for a live digital service:

- If weekly completion rate falls below 70%, an alert is raised.
- If an alert is raised, a senior evaluator reviews the funnel data.
- If the senior evaluator finds a specific failing step, a usability investigation is commissioned.

This week, a usability investigation was commissioned. Which of the following must be true?

A) The completion rate fell below 70%.
B) A senior evaluator reviewed the funnel data and found a specific failing step.
C) The completion rate fell below 70% and the service will be taken offline.
D) Nothing can be concluded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rules say an investigation is commissioned if the senior evaluator finds a failing step. Since an investigation happened, we can reasonably work back one step: the senior evaluator reviewed the data and found a failing step - that is the stated trigger, so B follows. Be careful with A: the rules describe one route to a review (an alert), but strictly they do not say a review can *only* happen after an alert; the investigation is triggered by the review's finding, not directly by the completion rate. Option C invents a consequence (taking the service offline) that appears nowhere in the rules. In logical deduction questions, only conclude what the stated rules force to be true - the same discipline a strong evaluator applies when interpreting evaluation evidence.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Pattern recognition in performance indicators**

A digital service reports the following weekly figures for completed transactions:

Week 1: 1,200 | Week 2: 1,260 | Week 3: 1,323 | Week 4: 1,389 | Week 5: ?

Which value best continues the pattern?

A) 1,425
B) 1,458
C) 1,500
D) 1,389

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the relationship between consecutive weeks. 1,260 ÷ 1,200 = 1.05; 1,323 ÷ 1,260 = 1.05; 1,389 ÷ 1,323 ≈ 1.05. The series grows by about 5% each week, not by a fixed amount. So Week 5 ≈ 1,389 × 1.05 ≈ 1,458. A common mistake is to compute the first difference (60) and add it repeatedly, which would suggest around 1,449 - close, but option B fits the multiplicative pattern exactly. Distinguishing steady percentage growth from steady absolute growth matters in real monitoring, because the two patterns lead to very different forecasts.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Spotting the flaw in an evaluation claim**

A delivery team writes: "After we redesigned the confirmation page, support calls fell by 30% in the following month. This proves the redesign caused the reduction in calls."

As a junior evaluator, which is the strongest single reason to be cautious about this claim?

A) Thirty per cent is too small a change to matter.
B) Support calls are not worth measuring.
C) Other things also changed over the same period, so the comparison cannot isolate the redesign's effect.
D) The team should have measured for a week, not a month.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a before-and-after comparison with no comparison group. Many things could explain the drop: seasonal patterns in demand, a policy change, an unrelated fix, or normal fluctuation. Without a way to isolate the redesign's contribution - for example an A/B test, a phased rollout, or at least a comparison with a similar service - "proves" is far too strong. This reasoning sits at the heart of impact evaluation: correlation in time is not causation. Options A and B dismiss the measurement rather than critiquing the logic, and D gets the direction wrong - a shorter window would usually be less reliable, not more.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Categorisation of evaluation types**

Match the question to the type of evaluation that best answers it. Which pairing is correct?

A) "Was the service delivered as intended, and did users experience it as planned?" - Impact evaluation
B) "Did the service cause the improvement in outcomes we observed?" - Process evaluation
C) "Do the benefits of the service justify its costs?" - Economic evaluation
D) "What is our theory of change?" - Experimentation

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Economic evaluation compares the value of benefits against costs, so C is correctly paired. The others are swapped or mismatched: whether a service was delivered as intended is a *process* evaluation question (so A is wrong); whether the service *caused* outcomes is an *impact* evaluation question (so B is wrong); and a theory of change is a planning artefact that underpins evaluations rather than being answered by experimentation (so D is wrong). Keeping these categories straight helps a candidate choose the right method when a stakeholder brings them a question - a core part of the evaluation delivery skill.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Prioritisation under time pressure**

It is Thursday afternoon. A candidate's senior evaluator is presenting evaluation findings to a policy team on Friday morning. The candidate has four tasks left and time to do only three well:

1. Re-check the arithmetic in the summary table that will appear on the presentation's key slide.
2. Reformat the appendix tables so fonts are consistent.
3. Confirm with the data owner that the latest extract includes this month's figures, as the slide claims.
4. Draft a survey question bank for a different project starting in three weeks.

Which task should be dropped?

A) Task 1
B) Task 2
C) Task 3
D) Task 4

**Correct answer: D** (with B as the clearly second-lowest priority)

**Explanation:** Prioritise by impact and urgency. Tasks 1 and 3 protect the accuracy of tomorrow's presentation - errors in a key slide or a false claim about data coverage would damage trust in the whole evaluation, so both are essential. Task 2 affects appearance only, and task 4 is important but not urgent: three weeks of slack means it can safely wait. Between B and D, D is the one to drop entirely because task 2 might take only minutes; but if forced to choose exactly three tasks "done well", the correct set is 1, 3, and 2, dropping 4. This item tests whether a candidate can separate urgency (deadline-driven) from importance (impact-driven) - and whether they protect accuracy above cosmetics.

**Question 9 (moderate) - Data fit for purpose**

A candidate is asked whether a dataset of website analytics is fit for purpose for measuring "how many distinct citizens used the service last month". They learn the following facts. Which one most seriously undermines fitness for this specific purpose?

A) The analytics tool counts devices, and many citizens use both a phone and a laptop.
B) The dashboard's colour scheme is hard to read.
C) The data is stored in a cloud service.
D) The figures update with a 24-hour delay.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Fitness for purpose means the data can validly answer the specific question being asked. The question is about distinct *citizens*, but the tool counts distinct *devices*. One citizen with two devices is counted twice, so the measure systematically overstates the true number - a mismatch between the concept wanted and the concept the data captures. Option D matters for real-time monitoring but not for a monthly figure. Options B and C are presentation and storage issues that do not affect validity. The quality assurance skill explicitly includes explaining fitness for purpose and the context of the data: a strong candidate will ask "what does this number actually count?"

**Question 10 (challenging) - Logical consistency across documents**

An evaluation plan states: "All user interviews will be conducted with informed consent, and no interviews will be conducted with users under 18." A fieldwork log records: "12 interviews completed; participants aged 17-64; all consent forms collected." A summary report states: "All fieldwork complied fully with the evaluation plan."

Which statement is best supported?

A) The summary report is correct, because all consent forms were collected.
B) There is an inconsistency: the log's age range includes at least one participant under 18, which the plan prohibits.
C) The fieldwork log must be wrong.
D) The evaluation plan was too strict.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The plan sets two conditions: informed consent AND no participants under 18. The log shows consent was collected (first condition met) but records an age range starting at 17 - meaning at least one participant was under 18, breaching the second condition. Therefore the summary report's claim of full compliance is contradicted by the log. Option A checks only one of the two conditions - a classic trap. Option C jumps to a conclusion: we know the documents conflict, but not which one is in error; the right action is to investigate. Option D is an opinion, not a deduction. Cross-checking documents for consistency is exactly what quality-assuring evaluation outputs requires, and it also touches the data ethics awareness skill: safeguarding rules around minors exist for good reasons.

**Question 11 (challenging) - Interpreting a funnel anomaly**

A four-step application funnel usually shows this pattern of users reaching each step: Step 1: 10,000 → Step 2: 8,000 → Step 3: 6,400 → Step 4: 5,100. This week it shows: Step 1: 10,000 → Step 2: 8,100 → Step 3: 2,900 → Step 4: 2,850.

Which is the most logical first hypothesis to investigate?

A) Users have suddenly lost interest in the service overall.
B) Something changed at or just after Step 2 - for example a broken page element or a new question - causing users to drop out before Step 3.
C) Step 4 has a technical problem.
D) The Step 1 figure is wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Locate where the pattern breaks. Steps 1 and 2 look normal (8,100 vs the usual 8,000). The collapse happens between Step 2 and Step 3: retention drops from the usual 80% (6,400/8,000) to about 36% (2,900/8,100). Step 3 to Step 4 actually looks healthier than usual (2,850/2,900 ≈ 98%), which often happens when only the most determined users get through a broken step. Option A cannot explain why Step 1 and Step 2 are normal. Options C and D point at steps whose behaviour matches expectations. This is pattern recognition plus fault localisation: find the first place the data deviates, and hypothesise there - the same reasoning a candidate would use when monitoring a live service's performance indicators.

**Question 12 (challenging) - Deduction about experiment design**

A team runs an A/B test: half of users see the current guidance page (A), half see a rewritten page (B), assigned at random. After two weeks, completion is 62% for A and 68% for B. A colleague says: "B is better for every type of user."

Which conclusion is actually justified by the evidence described?

A) B produced a higher overall completion rate than A during the test period.
B) B is better for every type of user.
C) B will always outperform A in future.
D) The rewrite should be rolled out to all government services.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Random assignment and a like-for-like comparison mean the overall difference (68% vs 62%) is a fair estimate of the rewrite's overall effect during the test. But an overall average can hide variation between groups: B could be worse for, say, users of assistive technology while still winning on average - so the "every type of user" claim (B) goes beyond the evidence. Option C assumes future conditions will match the test period, and option D generalises to entirely different services and contexts. Choosing the strongest conclusion that the evidence *actually supports* - no more, no less - is the essence of inferential thinking, and it is a habit that will serve a candidate well throughout an evaluation career.

**Question 13 (challenging) - Odd one out in evaluation reasoning**

Three of these statements describe *descriptive* analysis; one describes *inferential* analysis. Which one is the inferential statement?

A) "The mean satisfaction score last month was 4.1 out of 5."
B) "Completion rates were highest on Mondays and lowest on Saturdays."
C) "Based on our sample of 400 users, we estimate that between 71% and 79% of all users found the service easy to use."
D) "The service received 12,406 applications in March."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Descriptive analysis summarises the data actually held: means, counts, and observed patterns (A, B, and D all do this). Inferential analysis goes further - it uses a sample to draw conclusions about a wider population, with an expression of uncertainty. Option C generalises from 400 sampled users to *all* users and gives a confidence interval (71% to 79%), which is the hallmark of inference. The skill description explicitly includes using descriptive and inferential approaches, and assessments like this check whether a candidate can tell the two apart. A simple test: does the statement talk only about the observed data (descriptive), or about a wider group it is meant to represent (inferential)?

**Question 14 (challenging) - Prioritising evaluation questions**

A stakeholder workshop generates six candidate evaluation questions, but the evaluation has resources to answer three well. A senior evaluator suggests scoring each question on two criteria: "How important is the answer for upcoming decisions?" and "How feasible is it to answer robustly with available data?" The scores (out of 5) are:

| Question | Importance | Feasibility |
|----------|------------|-------------|
| Q-A | 5 | 4 |
| Q-B | 5 | 1 |
| Q-C | 2 | 5 |
| Q-D | 4 | 4 |
| Q-E | 3 | 3 |
| Q-F | 1 | 5 |

Which three questions should the evaluation prioritise, following the logic of the scoring approach?

A) Q-A, Q-B, Q-D (the three most important)
B) Q-C, Q-F, Q-A (the three most feasible)
C) Q-A, Q-D, Q-E (the best combinations of importance and feasibility)
D) Q-B, Q-C, Q-F

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scoring approach deliberately balances two criteria, so the right picks are the questions strong on *both*. Q-A (5,4) and Q-D (4,4) are clear winners. The interesting choice is third place: Q-B is maximally important (5) but barely feasible (1) - an evaluation that cannot be answered robustly wastes resources however important the question, so it should be flagged for future work once better data exists, not chosen now. Q-C and Q-F are easy but unimportant - answering them is activity without value. Q-E (3,3) is moderate on both, making it the best remaining balanced choice. Option C reflects this reasoning. This is a genuine evaluation planning task: translating stakeholder needs into a prioritised, achievable set of research questions - and the underlying cognitive skill is multi-criteria reasoning, which assessments frequently test with exactly this kind of table.

### Administration and scoring notes

- **Look for the underlying habits, not just correct answers.** A strong candidate will treat theories of change, funnel charts, and data tables as familiar territory, and should be able to explain *why* an answer is right, not just state it - worth probing in debrief.
- **The "which link?" habit is a good marker.** Candidates who can name exactly which logical step a claim relies on tend to do well on the deduction items; it is worth asking about directly in debrief.
- **Consider offering untimed practice before the timed session**, then apply the stated time limit consistently for every candidate at this level. Entry-level items reward steady accuracy over raw speed, so avoid over-tightening the clock.
- **Give every candidate the same guidance on process**, such as permission to eliminate clearly wrong options - consistency in what candidates are told matters as much as consistency in the items themselves.
- **Schedule the assessment for a quiet time and place**, and avoid running it immediately after another demanding activity - cognitive performance is measurably affected by fatigue, and this should be controlled for across candidates rather than left to chance.
- **Calibrate expectations to career stage.** These items are built for someone at the start of their evaluation career; benchmark candidates against others at a similar level, not against senior evaluators.

### Common candidate errors to watch for when scoring

- **Reading too fast.** Cognitive items often hinge on one word ("must", "could", "only", "all"). Misreading a qualifier is the most common avoidable error, and it is worth flagging in debrief when you see it.
- **Bringing outside assumptions.** A strong candidate answers from the rules and data given, not from what usually happens in their own workplace. Where a candidate's answer relies on outside assumptions rather than the stated rules, that is worth probing.
- **Getting stuck on one question.** Watch for candidates who visibly spend disproportionate time on a single item; a single time-sink question can cost three easier ones, and this is fair to mention in feedback on exam technique, separately from the score itself.
- **Confusing plausible with proven.** Several options may be sensible real-world ideas; the correct one is the option the evidence forces. Score against what the evidence supports, not against what sounds reasonable.
- **Reacting to adaptive difficulty.** If you use an adaptive platform, remind candidates beforehand that harder-feeling questions later on can be a sign they are doing well, so they are not thrown off by it.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures a candidate's ability to read, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical information presented the way it appears in this job. For a junior digital evaluator, that means tables of performance indicators, survey results, completion rates, sample sizes, evaluation budgets, and simple cost-benefit figures - not abstract algebra or school-style trigonometry.

The maths itself is deliberately basic: percentages, ratios, differences, averages, and simple proportional reasoning. What this assessment really tests is whether a candidate can extract the right numbers from a table or chart, apply the right operation, and interpret the result sensibly. Consider allowing or providing an on-screen calculator, because the emphasis is on interpretation rather than mental arithmetic. Time limits are usually strict - budget around one minute per item - so quick, accurate data extraction matters.

Why is this important for the role? Numbers are the raw material of monitoring and evaluation. A junior digital evaluator collects quantitative data, runs descriptive analysis, checks that figures look right, and helps communicate findings that often boil down to "the indicator moved by this much, and here is what that means". If a percentage is calculated wrongly in a published evaluation report, the error is public and can mislead policy decisions. This assessment checks for the careful, accurate number-handling the job demands, at a level appropriate for someone at the start of their evaluation career.

None of this requires advanced mathematics. Every item in this section can be solved with addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and percentages.

### What it measures for this role

- **Percentages and rates** map to the **Product and service monitoring** skill. Completion rates, digital take-up, satisfaction percentages, and month-on-month changes are the everyday language of performance indicators, and this skill includes describing common issues when measuring them.
- **Reading tables and charts accurately** maps to the **Evaluation delivery** skill (collecting and analysing quantitative data) and the **Quality assurance of data and analysis** skill (checking that data and analysis look right).
- **Averages, distributions, and sampling figures** map to the **Evaluation delivery** skill, especially the use of descriptive and inferential analysis. A candidate needs to understand what a mean does and does not tell you, and how sample size affects confidence.
- **Simple cost and value calculations** map to the **Evaluation delivery** skill in economic evaluation - comparing the costs of an intervention with the value of its benefits.
- **Sense-checking results** maps to the **Quality assurance of data and analysis** skill: the habit of asking "is this figure even plausible?" before accepting a calculation.

### Practice questions

A calculator may be provided, as in most real assessments. The explanations below show the working.

**Question 1 (easy) - Completion rate**

Last week, 4,800 users started an online application and 3,600 completed it. What was the completion rate?

A) 65%
B) 70%
C) 75%
D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Completion rate = completed ÷ started × 100 = 3,600 ÷ 4,800 × 100. First, 3,600 ÷ 4,800 = 0.75. Then 0.75 × 100 = 75%. A quick sense-check: 3,600 is three quarters of 4,800 (because 4,800 ÷ 4 = 1,200 and 1,200 × 3 = 3,600), and three quarters is 75%. Completion rate is one of the most common performance indicators this role monitors, so this calculation should become automatic for a strong candidate.

**Question 2 (easy) - Percentage change in an indicator**

A service's monthly digital take-up rose from 15,000 transactions in April to 16,800 in May. What was the percentage increase?

A) 10%
B) 12%
C) 15%
D) 18%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. The increase is 16,800 − 15,000 = 1,800. Then 1,800 ÷ 15,000 = 0.12, and 0.12 × 100 = 12%. The classic trap is dividing by the *new* value: 1,800 ÷ 16,800 ≈ 10.7%, which would tempt a candidate towards option A. The original (old) value is always the denominator when calculating change. In monitoring reports, month-on-month percentage changes are everywhere, so this habit protects against a very common published error.

**Question 3 (easy) - Reading a survey results table**

A user survey produced these results:

| Response | Count |
|---------------------------|-------|
| Very satisfied | 180 |
| Satisfied | 240 |
| Neither | 60 |
| Dissatisfied | 90 |
| Very dissatisfied | 30 |

What percentage of respondents were satisfied or very satisfied?

A) 60%
B) 66%
C) 70%
D) 72%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** First find the total: 180 + 240 + 60 + 90 + 30 = 600 respondents. The satisfied group is 180 + 240 = 420. Then 420 ÷ 600 = 0.70 = 70%. Two care points worth watching for: a strong candidate computes the denominator rather than assuming a round number, and reads the question carefully - it asks for two categories combined. This "top two box" satisfaction measure is a standard way of communicating survey findings, which links directly to the communicating analysis and insight skill.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Mean versus the middle**

Task completion times (in minutes) for nine observed users were: 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 32.

The mean completion time is 8 minutes. Why could reporting only the mean mislead stakeholders?

A) The mean is calculated incorrectly.
B) One extreme value (32) pulls the mean above what eight of the nine users experienced; a typical user took about 5 minutes.
C) Nine users is always enough to report a mean.
D) Minutes are the wrong unit.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the mean: 3 + 4 + 4 + 5 + 5 + 6 + 6 + 7 + 32 = 72, and 72 ÷ 9 = 8, so the arithmetic is right - the problem is interpretation. Eight of nine users finished in 7 minutes or less, but the single outlier (32 minutes) drags the mean up to 8, a figure higher than almost everyone's actual experience. The median (the middle value, 5) describes the typical user far better here. When analysing and interpreting data, choosing a summary statistic that honestly represents the data is part of quality assurance - and outliers like this are often worth investigating in their own right (was the user interrupted? did the tool log the time wrongly?).

**Question 5 (moderate) - Sample proportions**

In an evaluation, a candidate interviews a sample of 250 service users, and 90 of them report needing help from another person to complete the service. Roughly what proportion is this, and what is a correct way to describe it?

A) 36%; "about 36% of our sample needed help, which suggests a substantial minority of users may need assisted routes."
B) 36%; "exactly 36% of all users in the country need help."
C) 27%; "about a quarter of the sample needed help."
D) 90%; "most users need help."

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The proportion is 90 ÷ 250 = 0.36 = 36%, so options C and D miscalculate. The difference between A and B is about inference: a sample statistic (36% of *the sample*) is an estimate for the wider population, not an exact fact about it. Option A both calculates correctly and communicates honestly, flagging the implication (assisted routes) without overclaiming. This mirrors the role's skills exactly: descriptive analysis to get the number, inferential thinking to generalise carefully, and clear communication of what the finding means.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Evaluation fieldwork budget**

A team's evaluation has a fieldwork budget of £24,000. Each user interview costs £120 to conduct and each focus group costs £900. The plan is to run 8 focus groups. How many interviews can the remaining budget fund?

A) 120
B) 140
C) 160
D) 200

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cost of focus groups: 8 × £900 = £7,200. Remaining budget: £24,000 − £7,200 = £16,800. Interviews affordable: £16,800 ÷ £120 = 140. So the answer is 140 interviews. Multi-step budget questions like this reward writing down each stage rather than doing it all mentally - the same discipline a candidate would use when helping prepare a real evaluation plan with costed data collection.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Comparing indicators across channels**

A service is available through three channels. Last month's figures:

| Channel | Transactions | Errors reported |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|
| Online | 42,000 | 1,260 |
| Telephone | 9,000 | 405 |
| Post | 4,000 | 240 |

Which channel had the highest error *rate*?

A) Online
B) Telephone
C) Post
D) They are equal

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute each rate: Online = 1,260 ÷ 42,000 = 0.03 = 3%. Telephone = 405 ÷ 9,000 = 0.045 = 4.5%. Post = 240 ÷ 4,000 = 0.06 = 6%. Post has the highest rate even though it has the *fewest* errors in absolute terms. This is one of the most important habits in performance monitoring: never compare raw counts when the underlying volumes differ - convert to rates first. The product and service monitoring skill includes describing common issues when measuring performance indicators, and confusing counts with rates is issue number one.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Weighted average satisfaction**

Two survey waves measured satisfaction with a digital service. Wave 1 had 400 respondents with a mean score of 4.0. Wave 2 had 100 respondents with a mean score of 3.0. What is the overall mean across both waves?

A) 3.5
B) 3.6
C) 3.8
D) 4.0

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Simply averaging 4.0 and 3.0 to get 3.5 is wrong, because the waves have different sizes. Compute total score points: Wave 1 contributes 400 × 4.0 = 1,600; Wave 2 contributes 100 × 3.0 = 300. Total = 1,900 points across 400 + 100 = 500 respondents. Overall mean = 1,900 ÷ 500 = 3.8. The larger wave pulls the overall figure towards its own mean. Unweighted averaging of group means is a classic analysis error that quality assurance reviews frequently catch.

**Question 9 (challenging) - Cost per outcome (economic evaluation)**

A digital support programme cost £180,000 to run this year. An impact evaluation estimates it caused 1,200 additional people to complete their applications successfully who otherwise would not have. A comparable programme achieved similar outcomes at £175 per additional completion. What is this programme's cost per additional completion, and how does it compare?

A) £150, cheaper than the comparator
B) £150, more expensive than the comparator
C) £216, more expensive than the comparator
D) £120, cheaper than the comparator

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Cost per additional completion = £180,000 ÷ 1,200 = £150. Compare with the benchmark of £175: £150 is lower, so this programme delivered each additional outcome more cheaply. Two interpretation notes worth remembering for real economic evaluation: the denominator must be *additional* (caused) outcomes from the impact evaluation, not all completions; and cost per outcome is only comparable when the outcomes are genuinely similar. This is a simplified version of the cost-effectiveness reasoning the evaluation delivery skill introduces.

**Question 10 (challenging) - Ratios in sampling**

An evaluation plan requires interviewing users in the ratio 3:2:1 across three groups: frequent users, occasional users, and non-digital users. There is funding for 48 interviews in total. How many non-digital users should be interviewed?

A) 6
B) 8
C) 12
D) 16

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The ratio 3:2:1 has 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 parts. Each part is 48 ÷ 6 = 8 interviews. Non-digital users are 1 part, so 8 interviews (frequent users get 3 × 8 = 24, occasional users 2 × 8 = 16; check: 24 + 16 + 8 = 48). Ratio-based quotas appear often in qualitative research design, where evaluators deliberately include groups - such as non-digital users - whose experiences might otherwise be missed. Getting quota arithmetic right matters for inclusion as well as accuracy.

**Question 11 (challenging) - Percentage points versus percentages**

A performance indicator shows that assisted-digital support usage fell from 20% of all transactions to 15%. A colleague drafts: "Assisted support usage fell by 5%." What is the most accurate correction?

A) No correction needed; the sentence is accurate.
B) "Usage fell by 5 percentage points, which is a 25% relative reduction."
C) "Usage fell by 15 percentage points."
D) "Usage fell by 20%."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The gap between 20% and 15% is 5 *percentage points*. As a relative change, the fall is 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25 = 25%. Saying "fell by 5%" is ambiguous at best and understates the relative change: a 5% relative fall from 20% would be 20% × 0.95 = 19%, not 15%. Precision here is not pedantry - published evaluation reports are read by journalists and policymakers, and the difference between percentage points and per cent regularly causes public confusion. Clear, accurate communication of analysis is one of this role's named skills, and this distinction is a flagship example.

**Question 12 (challenging) - Multi-step dashboard interpretation**

A quarterly monitoring dashboard shows:

| Metric | Q1 | Q2 |
|---------------------------------|--------|--------|
| Applications started | 60,000 | 66,000 |
| Applications completed | 45,000 | 47,520 |
| Of completed: needed no support | 36,000 | 39,600 |

A stakeholder asks: "Did the completion rate improve in Q2, and did the share of completers needing no support improve too?"

A) Both improved.
B) Completion rate worsened; no-support share improved.
C) Completion rate improved; no-support share worsened.
D) Both worsened.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work each rate. Completion rate: Q1 = 45,000 ÷ 60,000 = 75%; Q2 = 47,520 ÷ 66,000 = 72%. So completion *worsened* by 3 percentage points, even though the raw number of completions rose - rising volumes hid a falling rate. No-support share of completers: Q1 = 36,000 ÷ 45,000 = 80%; Q2 = 39,600 ÷ 47,520 = 83.3%. So that share *improved*. The correct reading is option B. This question bundles the two most valuable habits in this whole section: convert counts to rates before comparing, and make sure each rate uses the right denominator (completions are divided by starts; no-support cases are divided by completions).

**Question 13 (challenging) - Sense-checking a published figure**

A draft report states: "The service saved users 250,000 hours last year. With 500,000 transactions and an average time saving of 3 minutes per transaction, this demonstrates substantial value."

What should be flagged?

A) Nothing; the arithmetic is consistent.
B) The stated figures imply 25,000 hours, not 250,000 - the claim is ten times too high.
C) The stated figures imply 2,500 hours.
D) Time savings cannot be estimated.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the claim from its own inputs: 500,000 transactions × 3 minutes = 1,500,000 minutes. Convert to hours: 1,500,000 ÷ 60 = 25,000 hours. The report claims 250,000 - exactly ten times too high, which suggests a slipped decimal point or an extra zero. This is precisely what the quality assurance skill means by "checking that data and analysis look right": recompute headline figures from their stated components before anything is published. Catching a tenfold error before publication is the kind of contribution that gets a junior evaluator noticed - in the best way.

**Question 14 (moderate) - Response rate and what it implies**

A candidate emails a survey to 5,000 service users; 850 respond. Of respondents, 68% say the service is easy to use. What is the response rate, and which caution is most appropriate when reporting the 68% figure?

A) 17% response rate; note that non-respondents may differ from respondents, so 68% may not represent all users.
B) 17% response rate; the 68% can be reported as true of all 5,000 users.
C) 68% response rate; no caution needed.
D) 5.9% response rate; the survey is invalid.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response rate = respondents ÷ invited = 850 ÷ 5,000 = 0.17 = 17%. That rules out C (which confuses the satisfaction figure with the response rate) and D (which miscalculates - 5,000 ÷ 850 inverted would give 5.9, but that is not a rate of anything meaningful here). The choice between A and B is about inference: with 83% of invited users not responding, the respondents may systematically differ from non-respondents - happier users, more digitally confident users, or users with strong opinions are often more likely to reply. So the 68% describes respondents, and generalising to all users needs caution. A low response rate does not make a survey worthless (D overshoots), but it does shape honest reporting. This blends three of the role's skills: descriptive analysis, inferential care, and clear communication of findings.

**Question 15 (challenging) - Reading a two-way table**

An evaluation team cross-tabulates application outcomes by channel for 2,000 applications:

| | Successful | Unsuccessful | Total |
|-----------|------------|--------------|-------|
| Online | 1,140 | 360 | 1,500 |
| Assisted | 380 | 120 | 500 |
| Total | 1,520 | 480 | 2,000 |

A colleague says: "Online applications are more successful - 1,140 successes versus only 380 for assisted." What do the *rates* show?

A) Online has a higher success rate than assisted.
B) Assisted has a higher success rate than online.
C) The success rates are equal at 76%.
D) The table cannot be used to compare rates.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute each channel's success rate. Online: 1,140 ÷ 1,500 = 0.76 = 76%. Assisted: 380 ÷ 500 = 0.76 = 76%. The rates are identical - the colleague's comparison of raw counts (1,140 versus 380) reflects nothing more than the fact that three times as many applications came through the online channel. This is the counts-versus-rates trap in its purest form, hiding inside a perfectly ordinary cross-tabulation. Overall success rate confirms the picture: 1,520 ÷ 2,000 = 76% across both channels. When quality-assuring analysis that compares groups, a careful evaluator recomputes the within-group rates before accepting any claim about which group does "better".

**Question 16 (challenging) - Projecting with two moving parts**

A service currently handles 40,000 transactions per quarter, and 60% are completed through the digital channel. The programme's target is to grow total transactions by 10% next quarter while raising digital take-up to 66%. If both targets are met, how many *digital* transactions will there be next quarter?

A) 26,400
B) 29,040
C) 27,600
D) 24,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two steps, in either order. New total transactions: 40,000 × 1.10 = 44,000. Digital share of those: 44,000 × 0.66 = 29,040. Now look at how the wrong options are built. Option D, 24,000, is the current digital figure (40,000 × 0.60) - the "no change" answer. Option A, 26,400, is what results from applying only *one* of the two changes: either growing the current digital figure by 10% (24,000 × 1.10 = 26,400) or applying the new 66% share to the *old* total (40,000 × 0.66 = 26,400) - both half-done calculations land on the same trap answer, which is why it is such an effective distractor. Option C, 27,600, sits plausibly between the half-done answer and the right one, to catch candidates who sense 26,400 is too low and guess upwards. The lesson: when two factors change at once, apply each explicitly and in full - multiply the new total by the new share. Wrong options in numeric tests are usually the answers produced by doing only half the work, which is exactly why writing down both steps protects against the error.

**Question 17 (moderate) - Estimation under time pressure**

An evaluation report needs a quick sense-check: a service received 118,742 applications last year, and 88,912 were completed digitally. Roughly what digital take-up percentage should the report show, using estimation rather than exact calculation?

A) About 55%
B) About 65%
C) About 75%
D) About 85%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Round both numbers to friendly values: 118,742 ≈ 120,000 and 88,912 ≈ 90,000. Then 90,000 ÷ 120,000 = 0.75 = 75%. The exact answer (88,912 ÷ 118,742 ≈ 74.9%) confirms the estimate. Estimation is not a lesser skill - it is often the *right* skill: when answer options are far apart, rounding gets a candidate there in seconds and frees time for harder questions. It is also a first line of quality assurance in real work: before trusting any calculated percentage in a report, a ten-second estimate shows whether it is even in the right neighbourhood. If the report had claimed 85% digital take-up from these figures, that rounding habit would have caught the error immediately.

### Administration and scoring notes

- **Five operations cover most items.** Percentages, percentage change, rates, weighted averages, and ratios cover the vast majority of numeric items at this level - useful to know when interpreting where a candidate's answer went wrong.
- **The denominator is the most common failure point.** Most numeric errors come from dividing by the wrong total; if a candidate's wrong answers cluster around this, it is worth naming in debrief.
- **Encourage candidates to show working, even with a calculator available.** Multi-step items punish mental shortcuts, and visible working also helps you diagnose exactly where an answer went wrong.
- **A sense-check habit is a strong signal.** A candidate whose working shows they questioned an implausible result (a completion rate over 100%, an impossible score) is demonstrating exactly the quality-assurance habit this role needs.
- **These items reflect real public data conventions.** GOV.UK service performance dashboards and published statistics use tables in the same style, useful context if you extend or vary this material.
- **These items test careful basic arithmetic and interpretation, not advanced statistics** - score and calibrate accordingly for an entry-level candidate.
- **Keep a record of common errors across candidates.** A pattern of errors across sittings (dividing by the new value, missing a scale qualifier) is useful both for refining this assessment and for understanding what to cover in onboarding.
- **If an on-screen calculator is used, check candidates are familiar with a similar interface beforehand** - an unfamiliar calculator under time pressure can cost accuracy on items a candidate could otherwise answer easily.

### Common candidate errors to watch for when scoring

- **Misreading scale qualifiers.** Confusing "in thousands" with raw numbers, or monthly with quarterly figures, silently multiplies the error - watch for this in wrong answers that are out by a consistent factor.
- **Dividing by the new value in percentage change.** Percentage change always uses the *original* value as the denominator; this is one of the most common and diagnostic errors to look for.
- **Confusing percentage points with per cent.** A fall from 20% to 15% is 5 percentage points but a 25% relative fall - these items test this distinction deliberately.
- **Over-calculating instead of estimating.** Some candidates do not use estimation even when it would answer the question faster; this is a matter of technique rather than ability, worth noting separately from accuracy.
- **Time-sink items.** One long multi-stage calculation can cost three easier items; note whether a candidate manages time well across the section, not only whether each answer is correct.
- **Comparing counts instead of rates.** When volumes differ across groups, channels, or time periods, raw counts mislead; converting to rates with the correct denominator is one of the clearest markers of a strong candidate.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate understands, analyses, and draws conclusions from written material of the kind this role really involves. For a junior digital evaluator, that means passages drawn from evaluation guidance, monitoring policies, research ethics documents, stakeholder emails, published evaluation reports, and data-sharing agreements.

The most common format gives a short, dense passage followed by statements the candidate must judge as **True** (the passage says so), **False** (the passage contradicts it), or **Cannot Say** (the passage gives insufficient information either way). Some items instead use multiple-choice comprehension: "Which of the following best summarises...?" or "According to the passage, which action is required?". The rule in all cases is to answer using *only* the passage - outside knowledge, even when correct, should be set aside. Timing is usually tight, often under a minute per item, to simulate reading under workplace deadlines.

Why does this matter for the role? Evaluation work is saturated with careful reading. A junior digital evaluator follows established monitoring and evaluation methods and government guidance, which means reading guidance precisely and noticing what it does and does not require. They produce evaluation plans that others will scrutinise word by word, help publish reports where a single overstated sentence can misrepresent findings to the public, and routinely need to distinguish what the evidence *says* from what a stakeholder *wishes* it said. The True/False/Cannot Say discipline - concluding only what the text supports - is the same discipline a strong evaluator applies when interpreting evaluation data.

For an entry-level evaluator, this assessment is also a good test of one of the hardest professional habits: resisting the urge to fill gaps with assumptions. A candidate who answers "Cannot Say" appropriately is showing professional judgement, in tests and in evaluation practice alike.

### What it measures for this role

- **Precise comprehension of guidance and policy** maps to the duty to follow established monitoring and evaluation methods and government guidance, and to the **Evaluation planning and strategy** skill - understanding what stakeholders need and translating written requirements accurately.
- **Distinguishing stated facts from assumptions** maps to the **Evaluation delivery** skill: interpreting data and findings without overclaiming, and to inferential thinking generally.
- **Reading dense material about data handling** maps to the **Data ethics and privacy** skill - understanding consent, confidentiality, and appropriate data use as written in agreements and policies.
- **Identifying the main message of a report** maps to the **Communicating analysis and insight** skill: summarising findings clearly requires first identifying precisely what they are.
- **Spotting contradictions and unsupported claims** maps to the **Quality assurance of data and analysis** skill, applied to words instead of numbers - checking that a report's conclusions match its own evidence.

### Practice questions

For each passage, judge the statements strictly on the text provided. Passages are fictional but realistic.

**Passage A - Evaluation guidance extract**

*"Evaluation plans for major digital programmes must be drafted before the public beta phase begins. Each plan must identify the evaluation questions, the methods to be used, and the data required. Where personal data will be collected, the plan must state the lawful basis for collection and name the information asset owner. Plans should be proportionate: small, low-risk services may combine their evaluation plan with their performance framework in a single document. All plans must be reviewed by the departmental evaluation lead, although approval authority may be delegated in writing."*

**Question 1 (easy) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "Evaluation plans for major digital programmes must be drafted before public beta begins."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A - True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly and without qualification: "must be drafted before the public beta phase begins." When a statement closely paraphrases an explicit sentence in the passage, the answer is True. This is a straightforward mark for a candidate who checks the wording rather than skimming. Note the statement says "drafted", matching the passage; if it had said "approved before public beta", the passage does not say that, and the answer would change.

**Question 2 (easy) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "Small, low-risk services are exempt from evaluation planning."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B - False**

**Explanation:** The passage says small, low-risk services "may combine their evaluation plan with their performance framework in a single document". That is a lighter-touch format, not an exemption - they still need a plan. The statement contradicts the passage, so it is False. Watch for this pattern: a passage grants a *flexibility*, and the statement inflates it into an *exemption*. Precision about what a rule actually permits is exactly the skill needed when following government evaluation guidance in real work.

**Question 3 (moderate) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "The departmental evaluation lead personally approves every evaluation plan."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B - False**

**Explanation:** The passage says all plans must be *reviewed* by the departmental evaluation lead, but that "approval authority may be delegated in writing". So approval is not necessarily personal to the lead - the passage explicitly allows delegation, which contradicts "personally approves every". Notice the two distinct verbs, "reviewed" and "approval": the passage attaches different rules to each. Dense governance text often does this, and reading each clause's exact scope is what the assessment - and the job - requires.

**Question 4 (moderate) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "Most departmental evaluation plans name an information asset owner."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C - Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage requires naming an information asset owner only "where personal data will be collected". It gives no information about how many plans actually collect personal data, or how many plans comply. "Most" is a claim about frequency in the real world that the passage neither supports nor contradicts. This is the heart of Cannot Say: the statement might well be true in reality, but *the text does not say*. Separating "the rule exists" from "we know how often it applies" is the same distinction a strong evaluator draws when interpreting evaluation evidence.

**Passage B - Email from a policy stakeholder**

*"Thanks for sharing the interim findings. I note completion has improved since the redesign, which is encouraging. Before we publish anything, I want to be confident the improvement is not just seasonal - January is always our quietest month, and quieter periods have historically shown higher completion. Could your team compare against the same month last year, and also check whether the improvement holds for users who need assisted support? If both checks are reassuring, I am content for the summary to go to the publications team, provided the evaluation lead signs it off first."*

**Question 5 (moderate) - Multiple choice**

What is the stakeholder's main concern about the interim findings?

A) The redesign was too expensive.
B) The observed improvement might be explained by seasonal patterns rather than the redesign.
C) The findings should not be published under any circumstances.
D) Assisted support users were excluded from the analysis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder explicitly says: "I want to be confident the improvement is not just seasonal - January is always our quietest month." That is a concern about an alternative explanation for the improvement, which option B captures. Option C overstates: publication is conditional ("If both checks are reassuring... I am content"), not blocked. Option D misreads - the stakeholder asks to *check* assisted support users, not claims they were excluded. Option A appears nowhere. Identifying a stakeholder's actual concern from their written words is the first step of the evaluation planning and strategy skill: understanding what they need and why.

**Question 6 (moderate) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "The stakeholder will allow the summary to be published if the two checks are reassuring and the evaluation lead signs it off."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A - True**

**Explanation:** The final sentence sets out exactly these conditions: "If both checks are reassuring, I am content for the summary to go to the publications team, provided the evaluation lead signs it off first." The statement matches both conditions - the checks and the sign-off - so it is True. Compound conditions like this are common in workplace correspondence; verbal reasoning tests whether a candidate registers *all* the conditions, not just the first one read.

**Question 7 (challenging) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "Completion rates were higher last January than in the months around it."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C - Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** Tempting to say True - the stakeholder notes "quieter periods have historically shown higher completion" and that January is the quietest month. But look carefully: that is a general historical pattern plus a general claim about January's volume. The passage never states what actually happened *last January specifically*. The general pattern makes the statement plausible, but plausibility is not textual support. This is the subtlest and most common Cannot Say trap: a reasonable inference from a generalisation is treated as a stated fact. In evaluation work, the same discipline stops an evaluator writing "completion was higher last January" in a report when the evidence is only a general trend.

**Passage C - Data ethics and consent extract**

*"Research participants must be told the purpose of data collection, how long their data will be retained, and with whom it will be shared, before any data is collected. Consent must be recorded, and participants may withdraw consent at any time; on withdrawal, their identifiable data must be deleted unless the data has already been anonymised and aggregated into published findings. Interview recordings are classified as identifiable data. Anonymised transcripts may be retained for up to five years for secondary analysis, provided this retention was described in the original consent materials."*

**Question 8 (moderate) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "If a participant withdraws consent, their interview recording must be deleted."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A - True**

**Explanation:** Chain two stated facts: on withdrawal, "identifiable data must be deleted unless the data has already been anonymised and aggregated into published findings", and "interview recordings are classified as identifiable data". A recording cannot be an anonymised, aggregated published finding - it remains identifiable by the passage's own classification - so the exception cannot apply to the recording itself, and deletion is required. Two-step deductions inside a passage are a hallmark of harder verbal items: each link is stated, but a candidate must connect them. Handling consent and withdrawal correctly is also core to the data ethics and privacy skill.

**Question 9 (challenging) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "Anonymised transcripts may always be kept for five years."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B - False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches a condition: retention for up to five years is allowed "provided this retention was described in the original consent materials". The word "always" removes that condition, so the statement goes beyond - and thereby contradicts - the rule as written. Also note "up to five years" is a maximum, not an entitlement. When a statement takes a conditional permission and restates it as unconditional, the answer is False. Spotting dropped conditions is vital when summarising ethics requirements for colleagues: a summary that loses the condition changes the rule.

**Question 10 (challenging) - Multiple choice**

According to the passage, which of the following must happen *before* any data is collected?

A) Participants are told the purpose of collection, the retention period, and who the data will be shared with.
B) The data is anonymised.
C) Transcripts are prepared for secondary analysis.
D) Published findings are aggregated.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The first sentence lists exactly three things participants must be told "before any data is collected": purpose, retention, and sharing. The other options describe activities that happen during or after research, and none is tied to the "before collection" requirement. This item rewards locating the sentence with the matching time anchor ("before") and checking the list against the options item by item - the same careful reading applied when following research ethics procedures with support from senior evaluators.

**Passage D - Extract from a published evaluation report**

*"The process evaluation found that the service was largely delivered as designed, although two of the seven planned outreach events did not take place. Interviews suggested that awareness of the service remained low among older users. The impact evaluation, based on a matched comparison design, estimated that the service increased successful applications by eight per cent, with the effect concentrated among first-time applicants. **Question 11 (moderate) - Multiple choice**

Which statement best summarises the report's overall message?

A) The service failed to deliver and should be closed.
B) The service worked broadly as intended and produced a measurable positive impact, but awareness among older users needs targeted attention.
C) The service's only problem was cost.
D) The impact evaluation was inconclusive.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report says delivery was largely as designed (with a minor shortfall in outreach events), impact was positive (an estimated eight per cent increase), costs were acceptable, and the recommendation is continued funding plus awareness work for older users. Option B captures all of that faithfully. Option A contradicts the recommendation; option C contradicts the cost finding; option D contradicts the stated estimate. Summarising a report's overall message accurately - neither rosier nor gloomier than the text - is precisely the communicating analysis and insight skill in action.

**Question 12 (challenging) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "The service increased successful applications by eight per cent."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A - True**

**Explanation:** This one is fair game as True because the passage states the impact evaluation "estimated that the service increased successful applications by eight per cent" - the statement is a direct report of the passage's content. There is a nuance worth noting, though: in stricter test variants, a statement phrased as absolute fact ("increased by eight per cent") versus the passage's "estimated" can be marked Cannot Say; here, the statement mirrors the passage's core claim and True is the intended answer. In report writing, careful phrasing preserves the passage's own caution: keeping the word "estimated" attached to estimates. The habit of preserving uncertainty language is a mark of a trustworthy evaluator.

**Question 13 (challenging) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "Older users were less likely than younger users to apply successfully."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C - Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says awareness of the service "remained low among older users" and that the impact was "concentrated among first-time applicants". Neither statement compares older and younger users' *success rates* in applying. Low awareness might plausibly lead to fewer applications, but the passage says nothing about the success of those who do apply, and "first-time applicants" is not an age group. The statement introduces a comparison the text never makes - Cannot Say. Guarding against this drift, where a related finding morphs into a different claim, is one of the most valuable quality assurance habits an evaluator can build.

**Passage E - Monitoring and performance framework note**

**Question 14 (easy) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "Performance indicators must be reviewed every month."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B - False**

**Explanation:** The passage says indicators "should be reviewed at least quarterly". Monthly review would satisfy "at least quarterly", but the statement claims monthly review is *required*, which the passage does not say - the stated minimum is quarterly. A requirement of monthly review is therefore contradicted by the text's actual rule. Take care with frequency words: "at least quarterly" sets a floor, and the statement invents a stricter rule. Test writers love converting floors into stricter requirements, and careful readers catch them every time.

**Question 15 (moderate) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement: "The evaluation lead can retire an indicator on their own authority."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B - False**

**Explanation:** The passage requires that "the service owner and the evaluation lead both agree" before an indicator may be retired. "Both agree" means neither can act alone, so unilateral retirement by the evaluation lead contradicts the rule. This mirrors Question 3's lesson about reading exactly who holds which authority - governance text distributes powers precisely, and so must a careful reading. In real monitoring work, knowing who must agree to changes in a performance framework prevents well-intentioned but improper shortcuts.

**Question 16 (challenging) - Multiple choice**

According to the passage, what is the difference between digital performance analysis and broader monitoring?

A) There is no difference; the passage treats them as interchangeable.
B) Digital performance analysis measures how well the service performs as a product, while broader monitoring assesses contribution to strategic objectives - and the passage says they should not be treated as interchangeable.
C) Digital performance analysis is done monthly, broader monitoring quarterly.
D) Broader monitoring only applies when indicators miss their targets.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states the distinction directly: digital performance analysis "measures how well the service performs as a product" (completion rates, error rates), while broader monitoring "assesses whether the service contributes to strategic objectives", and "the two should not be treated as interchangeable". Option B restates all of this faithfully. Option A directly contradicts the text; option C invents frequencies the passage never assigns to these activities; option D wrongly attaches broader monitoring to the missed-target rule, which is actually about recording explanations and responses. This distinction is word-for-word one of this role's named skill outcomes - explaining the difference between analysis of digital performance and broader monitoring of a product against strategic objectives - so this item is doubly useful for scoring: it earns a mark and reflects the job directly.

**Question 17 (moderate) - True/False/Cannot Say**

Statement (about Passage E): "If an indicator misses its target for two consecutive quarters, the service must be redesigned."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B - False**

**Explanation:** The passage does attach a consequence to two consecutive missed quarters - but the consequence is that "the service owner must record an explanation and a planned response". Recording an explanation and planning a response is a much weaker obligation than redesigning the service; the planned response could be anything from a minor fix to a decision that the target itself was unrealistic. The statement takes the passage's genuine trigger (two missed quarters) and attaches a consequence the passage never states, which contradicts the rule as written. This trigger-plus-wrong-consequence pattern is a favourite in verbal items: the first half of the statement checks out, lulling a candidate into approving the second half. Verify both halves independently - the same habit that stops a real monitoring report from overstating what a governance framework actually requires.

### Administration and scoring notes

- **Encourage the three-way discipline in debrief.** Taking any paragraph and identifying one True, one False, and one Cannot Say statement about it is a good way to help a candidate see the traps after the assessment, even though it is not something to rehearse beforehand.
- **A strong candidate anchors every answer to exact words.** In debrief, ask the candidate to point to the sentence that supports their judgement; if they cannot, that is diagnostic.
- **Qualifier words carry the meaning.** "Must", "may", "all", "some", "always", "unless", "provided that" - most wrong answers come from missing one of these, and this is worth naming specifically when giving feedback.
- **"Cannot Say" is a legitimate, professional answer**, not a sign of weak knowledge - score it as such, and say so in debrief if a candidate seems to regard it as a failure.
- **Reading stamina matters.** Government guidance is long and dense; a candidate who reads real evaluation and monitoring guidance regularly will generally perform better here.
- **Compound statements are a deliberate test.** They often pair a true trigger with an invented consequence, or a real rule with a dropped condition; each clause needs checking separately, and this is a good discriminator between candidates.
- **A one-sentence summary test is a useful debrief exercise.** Asking a candidate to summarise a passage's overall message in one sentence, after the assessment, gives insight into their communication skill beyond what the scored items show.

### Common candidate errors to watch for when scoring

- **Using outside knowledge.** A candidate may know how consent rules work in their own organisation, but the item asks about *this passage*. Where an answer draws on outside knowledge rather than the text, mark against the passage.
- **Confusing plausible with stated.** A likely inference from a general pattern is not textual support. If the specific claim is not in the text, the correct answer is Cannot Say, however sensible the claim sounds.
- **Missing dropped conditions.** Statements that restate a conditional rule without its condition ("provided that...", "unless...") are False, not True.
- **Inflating flexibilities into exemptions.** "May combine documents" does not mean "need not produce one" - watch for candidates who stretch a permission into an exemption.
- **Poor time allocation.** A candidate who spends too long memorising a passage before looking at the statements will run short of time; this is a technique issue worth separating from comprehension ability in feedback.
- **Treating "reviewed" and "approved" (and similar near-synonyms) as identical.** Governance text assigns different obligations to different verbs, and so do these items.
- **Losing uncertainty language.** When a passage says a finding was "estimated" or "suggested", a strong candidate's answer preserves that caution rather than restating it as established fact.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to judge how effective different responses would be. Unlike the cognitive, numeric, and verbal assessments, there is usually no single objectively "right" answer computed from data - instead, some responses fit professional values and the demands of the role better than others. Common formats ask the candidate to pick the **most effective** and **least effective** actions from a set, to **rank** all the options from best to worst, or to **rate** each option's effectiveness independently. These items are usually untimed or generously timed, because they measure judgement rather than speed.

For a junior digital evaluator, the dilemmas involve the people and pressures of the real working world: delivery teams who want good news, policy stakeholders with deadlines, senior evaluators who guide the candidate's work, research participants who trust the evaluator with their data, and members of the public who ultimately rely on the services being evaluated. Scenarios probe tensions specific to evaluation: honesty about findings versus pressure to soften them, admitting the limits of one's knowledge versus appearing capable, raising ethical concerns versus not wanting to make a fuss.

The judgement being assessed reflects UK public sector values: putting user needs first, transparency and honesty about evidence, collaboration across disciplines, accessibility and inclusion, and careful stewardship of public money and personal data. At this level, look for two junior-specific qualities in a candidate's answers: knowing when to seek guidance (a strength, not a weakness, at entry level) and taking ownership of their own learning and mistakes.

A useful lens when scoring: the strongest options in each scenario usually combine honesty, proportionate action, and appropriate involvement of others, rather than heroic solo fixes or silent avoidance.

### What it measures for this role

- **Integrity in analysis and reporting** maps to the **Communicating analysis and insight** skill and the duty to publish findings: scenarios test whether findings stay honest under pressure.
- **Ethical awareness and escalation** maps to the **Data ethics and privacy** skill, which explicitly includes identifying appropriate channels to discuss ethical issues, with support.
- **Working with stakeholders** maps to the **Evaluation planning and strategy** skill - understanding what stakeholders need, and managing situations where needs conflict.
- **Quality and accuracy under pressure** maps to the **Quality assurance of data and analysis** skill: scenarios test what a candidate does when they find an error, especially their own.
- **Knowing your level** maps to the role-level expectations: supporting experienced evaluators, developing through training, and receiving guidance - scenarios test whether a candidate seeks help at the right moments without either hiding problems or delegating everything upwards.

### Practice questions

Present each scenario and let the candidate answer before offering the commentary. Formats vary, as in real assessments.

**Question 1 (easy) - The inconvenient finding**

A candidate has analysed survey data for an evaluation. The findings show user satisfaction has fallen since the last release. The delivery team's product manager, who is friendly and well liked, says: "That dip is probably just noise - could you leave it out of the summary? It will distract from the good news."

Rate the options: which is MOST effective and which is LEAST effective?

A) Leave the dip out; the product manager knows the service better than the candidate does.
B) Include the finding, and discuss with a senior evaluator how to present it fairly - for example alongside context about uncertainty and possible causes.
C) Include the finding but bury it in an appendix footnote so it technically appears.
D) Tell the product manager it will be thought about, then quietly do nothing until the deadline forces a decision.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A** (with D a close second-worst)

**Explanation:** Evaluation exists to give an honest account of whether services deliver value for the public - omitting an inconvenient finding at a stakeholder's request undermines the entire purpose, so A is least effective; it is not collaboration, it is capture. B is most effective because it keeps the finding in, treats the product manager's "noise" point seriously (uncertainty context is a fair request), and involves a senior evaluator - exactly right at this level. C technically preserves the finding but is designed to hide it, which fails the transparency test. D is passive avoidance: it resolves nothing and forfeits the chance to handle the situation well. Notice that the best option is neither combative nor compliant - it is honest, constructive, and appropriately supported.

**Question 2 (easy) - Out of a candidate's depth**

In a meeting, a policy stakeholder asks a candidate directly: "What does the regression analysis say about which user groups benefited most?" The candidate supported the data collection, but a senior evaluator ran the regression and they do not fully understand its details.

Which response is MOST effective?

A) Improvise a plausible-sounding answer based on what is remembered.
B) Say the analysis is confidential.
C) Say: "That analysis was led by my colleague - I don't want to misrepresent it, so let me take that away and get you an accurate answer today."
D) Stay silent and hope someone changes the subject.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C is honest about the limits of a candidate's knowledge, protects the accuracy of what stakeholders hear, commits to a concrete follow-up with a timescale, and preserves credibility - the model behaviour for an entry-level professional. Option A is the most dangerous: improvised statistics can travel into policy decisions, and being caught guessing destroys trust in a team's whole evidence base. Option B is untrue and blocks a legitimate question, harming transparency. Option D avoids harm but also avoids responsibility; C shows the candidate can hold the space professionally without overreaching. Assessors at junior level should actively reward comfortable, prompt "I'll find out" responses.

**Question 3 (moderate) - The data ethics worry**

While preparing interview data for analysis, a candidate notices a spreadsheet on the shared drive containing participants' names and contact details alongside their full interview answers - accessible to the whole programme team, although the consent form promised that only the evaluation team would see identifiable responses.

Rank these options from most to least effective:

A) Do nothing; the candidate might be wrong about what the consent form said, and does not want to cause a fuss.
B) Immediately raise it with a senior evaluator (or the named information asset owner), describe what was found, and ask about restricting access while it is checked.
C) Delete the spreadsheet personally, right away, so the problem disappears.
D) Mention it casually to a teammate at lunch and leave it at that.

**Correct ranking: B, D, A, C** (some assessments would score A below C - see below)

**Explanation:** B is clearly best: it uses an appropriate channel (the data ethics and privacy skill explicitly includes identifying channels to discuss ethical issues, with support), it is prompt, factual, and proposes a proportionate immediate safeguard (restricting access) while leaving decisions to those with authority. D at least surfaces the issue but to the wrong audience with no follow-through. A and C compete for last place. A leaves a potential breach of participant trust running - and note the trap in its wording: uncertainty about the consent form is a reason to *check*, not to stay silent. C acts unilaterally and destructively: deleting data may compound the problem (it may be the only copy, it may be needed to assess the breach, and it hides the incident from those obliged to record it). Many scorers would rank C last for that reason; either way, the lesson is the same - escalate promptly through proper channels, do not freelance.

**Question 4 (moderate) - A candidate's own mistake**

A week after a team circulated interim findings, a candidate discovers they made an error preparing the data: test transactions were accidentally included, inflating completion figures by around four percentage points. The interim note has already been read by stakeholders. No one else has noticed.

Which is MOST effective and which is LEAST effective?

A) Tell a senior evaluator immediately, explain what happened, and offer a corrected analysis with a note of what changed.
B) Quietly fix the data for the final report and never mention the interim error.
C) Wait to see if anyone notices; if they do, correct it then.
D) Correct the figures in the final report with a footnote saying "figures have been revised", without telling the team the cause.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Everyone makes data errors - including experienced evaluators. What distinguishes professionals is what they do next. A is most effective: prompt, honest disclosure lets the team correct the record before decisions are made on inflated figures, and it builds exactly the trust that makes senior colleagues give juniors more responsibility. It also demonstrates the quality assurance skill in the most meaningful way: catching and fixing an error, including one's own. C is least effective because it gambles the team's credibility on no one noticing, and every day the error stands, decisions may be based on it. B and D both correct the numbers but conceal the process; concealment corrodes team learning (how will the team add a "check for test transactions" step if no one knows it happened?) and risks worse embarrassment if discovered. Honesty plus a proposed fix beats every alternative.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Conflicting instructions**

A candidate's senior evaluator asked them to spend this week quality-assuring a dataset for an impact evaluation. Midweek, a delivery manager from another team asks the candidate to spend two days building them a quick dashboard, saying: "It's urgent, and it'll be great experience for you."

Which response is MOST effective?

A) Say yes; helping other teams is collaborative and the experience sounds useful.
B) Say no outright; it is not the candidate's job.
C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing commitment and deadline, and offer to check with the senior evaluator whether the dashboard can be scheduled or reassigned.
D) Try to do both by working late all week without telling anyone.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C respects the existing commitment, is honest with the delivery manager, and routes the prioritisation decision to the person with visibility of the whole workload - the senior evaluator. That is not "passing the buck": at this level, priority calls between teams genuinely belong with those coordinating the work. A silently deprioritises an agreed task, and quality assurance of an impact evaluation is exactly the kind of work whose delay causes downstream slips. B damages a relationship unnecessarily and misses a chance to help through proper channels. D looks heroic but is unsustainable, hides a resourcing conflict that managers need to know about, and risks both tasks being done tiredly and badly. Collaboration means transparent coordination, not saying yes to everything.

**Question 6 (moderate) - The struggling participant**

A candidate is supporting user interviews for an evaluation. Midway through an interview, an elderly participant becomes visibly confused and distressed, saying they thought this meeting was about fixing their benefits problem.

Rank these options from most to least effective:

A) Pause the interview, gently clarify what the session is and is not, check whether they are happy to continue, and afterwards ask a senior evaluator about signposting the participant to the right support channel.
B) Press on with the interview script; the schedule is tight and the data is needed.
C) End the session abruptly and remove their data without explanation.
D) Explain the session's purpose, and if they still wish to stop, end the interview respectfully, thank them, and record that consent was withdrawn.

**Correct ranking: A, D, C, B**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests participant welfare and informed consent - foundations of ethical research. A is best: it prioritises the participant's understanding and comfort, re-confirms consent (consent must be informed and ongoing, not a signature collected at the start), and adds the humane extra step of signposting help through the right channel. D is also good practice - respectful withdrawal is always available to participants - but it moves to ending the session before first checking understanding and comfort, so it edges below A. C protects the participant's data rights but handles the human being poorly; abruptness can compound distress. B is clearly worst: continuing to extract data from a confused, distressed participant is a serious ethical failure, whatever the schedule pressure. User needs come before data needs - in evaluation as in service design.

**Question 7 (moderate) - The theory of change workshop**

A candidate is helping organise a workshop to develop a theory of change for a new digital service. Reviewing the invitation list, they notice it includes only the delivery team and its programme manager - no policy colleagues, no operational (call centre and casework) staff, and no one representing users.

Which is the MOST effective action?

A) Say nothing; the delivery team knows the service best, and the invitation list is not the candidate's call.
B) Suggest to the workshop organiser that policy, operational, and user-representative voices be included, briefly explaining that a theory of change is stronger when the people who see different parts of the service help build it.
C) Cancel the workshop until the list is fixed.
D) Attend, and afterwards write a critical email about who was missing.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The product and service monitoring skill includes identifying appropriate stakeholders to involve in developing a theory of change - and this scenario is that skill in action. A theory of change built only by the delivery team tends to encode the delivery team's assumptions: operational staff know where processes really fail, policy colleagues know the intended outcomes, and user representatives keep the chain anchored to real needs. B raises the gap constructively, at the right time (before the workshop), with a reason attached - a model junior intervention: helpful, evidence-based, and respectful of the organiser's authority. A wrongly assumes juniors should not speak up; assessors should consistently reward well-judged input from any level. C is disproportionate - this is a suggestion, not a crisis. D raises the right issue at the wrong time, after the opportunity to fix it has passed.

**Question 8 (challenging) - Pressure to overstate**

A team's evaluation found a modest, statistically uncertain improvement attributable to a programme. A senior policy official, preparing a ministerial briefing, drafts the line: "The evaluation proves the programme works" and asks the team to confirm it by 16:00 today. The senior evaluator is in meetings and unreachable until 17:00.

Which is MOST effective and which is LEAST effective?

A) Confirm the line; the official is senior and the deadline is real.
B) Reply before 16:00: explain that wording cannot be confirmed, offer accurate alternative wording (for example, "the evaluation found early signs of improvement, though findings are not yet conclusive"), and flag that the senior evaluator will confirm at 17:00.
C) Ignore the request since the senior evaluator is unavailable.
D) Confirm the line but keep a private note recording disagreement.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A** (D is barely better than A)

**Explanation:** "Proves" misrepresents a modest, uncertain finding, and confirming it would put the team's name behind an overstatement that could reach a minister - the credibility cost of that dwarfs any deadline. So A is least effective, and D is scarcely better: a private note does not stop the public overstatement; it only documents that the candidate knew. B does everything right within the candidate's authority: it responds by the deadline (respecting the official's genuine constraint), refuses only the inaccurate wording, *offers a usable accurate alternative* (which is what busy officials actually need), and creates a clear path to senior confirmation. C fails the stakeholder entirely - silence at 16:00 may lead the official to assume confirmation or to escalate a complaint. The deep lesson for the debrief: honesty about uncertainty is not obstruction; pairing it with constructive alternatives is what makes it collaborative.

**Question 9 (challenging) - The fudged benchmark**

While quality-assuring a colleague's analysis, a candidate notices the "industry benchmark" used for comparison has no source, and the figure makes the service look unusually good. When asked, the colleague - who is more experienced than the candidate - shrugs: "It's roughly what it was last time someone checked. Don't worry about it."

Rank these options from most to least effective:

A) Accept the reassurance; they are more experienced, and it is only a benchmark.
B) Ask the colleague to help find and document the source, explaining it is needed for the quality assurance record; if it cannot be sourced, suggest the comparison be caveated or removed.
C) Go straight to the head of profession to report the colleague's conduct.
D) Remove the benchmark from the report personally, without telling the colleague.

**Correct ranking: B, D, A, C**

**Explanation:** B is best because it pursues the substantive issue (an unsourced figure in an evaluation output) through the least confrontational effective route: making the request procedural ("the QA record needs it") rather than personal, giving the colleague the chance to resolve it, and defining what happens if they cannot (caveat or remove). This is quality assurance done with emotional intelligence. D fixes the document but bypasses the colleague - it damages the relationship, and unilateral edits to someone else's analysis are poor practice, though the output at least ends up honest. A fails the core duty: "roughly what it was last time someone checked" is not a source, and experience does not exempt anyone from evidencing claims - deference is not quality assurance. C is worst because it escalates a resolvable sourcing question into a conduct complaint without first attempting the obvious direct step; escalation is right when direct routes fail or the issue is serious misconduct, and this (so far) is neither.

**Question 10 (challenging) - Inclusion in research design**

A team is designing data collection for evaluating a digital service. The plan is an online-only survey, because it is cheap and fast. A candidate recalls that a key question for the evaluation is whether the service works for people with low digital confidence.

Which is the MOST effective contribution the candidate can make?

A) Raise the concern in the design discussion: an online-only survey will under-represent exactly the users the evaluation most needs to hear from, so suggest complementing it with phone or face-to-face methods for low-digital-confidence users.
B) Say nothing; the method was chosen by more experienced colleagues.
C) Privately decide the findings will be flawed and mention it only if the report is criticised later.
D) Insist the survey be scrapped entirely.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** This is a sampling-bias problem with an inclusion dimension: people with low digital confidence are the least likely to respond to an online survey, so their absence would systematically bias the evaluation on its own key question. Option A names the problem precisely, links it to the evaluation's purpose, and proposes a proportionate fix (mixed-mode collection) rather than demanding perfection - textbook constructive challenge. It draws directly on the evaluation planning and strategy skill (translating stakeholder needs into research approaches) and understanding of quantitative and qualitative design. B repeats the deference trap: seniority chose the method, but a junior evaluator's job is to contribute observations, and good teams want them. C is quiet cynicism - the least professional stance available - and D overcorrects: the online survey is fine for most users; the fix is addition, not demolition.

**Question 11 (challenging) - The life cycle mismatch**

A programme manager asks a team to run a full impact evaluation, with a matched comparison group, on a service that entered private beta three weeks ago with 200 users. The senior evaluator asks a candidate on the team for their thoughts before the response meeting.

Which is the MOST effective input to offer?

A) "We should do it - impact evaluation is the gold standard, and it's what the stakeholder asked for."
B) "It's too early for a robust impact evaluation - at this life cycle phase, evaluation effort is better spent on process evaluation, early user feedback, and building the monitoring foundations (theory of change, indicators, data collection) that will make a later impact evaluation possible. We could propose that as a roadmap."
C) "Impact evaluations are too difficult; we should decline."
D) "Let's run the impact evaluation but keep expectations low."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests the monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle skill: evaluation needs vary by phase. Three weeks into private beta, with 200 users, an impact evaluation with matched comparison would be premature - effects have had no time to emerge, samples are tiny, and the service itself is still changing weekly, so any measured "impact" would describe a version of the service that no longer exists. B says this honestly and then does the crucial extra thing: it converts "no" into a roadmap - what the stakeholder *can* have now (process evaluation, user feedback) and what will make their request answerable later (theory of change, indicators, data collection). A is stakeholder-pleasing at the cost of methodological honesty. C declines without explanation or alternative, and misstates the problem (timing, not difficulty). D wastes public money on an evaluation everyone expects to be uninformative - "keeping expectations low" is not a methodology. Matching the method to the moment is what turns evaluators into trusted advisers, and is a strong marker to look for in a candidate's answer.

**Question 12 (challenging) - Credit where it isn't due**

At a show-and-tell, a senior colleague presents analysis a candidate largely produced, describes it as their own work, and receives warm praise. The candidate feels stung. The findings themselves were presented accurately.

Which is MOST effective and which is LEAST effective?

A) Interrupt the presentation to correct the record publicly.
B) Say nothing ever; juniors should expect this.
C) Raise it privately and calmly with the colleague afterwards ("I'd appreciate being credited for the parts I produced"); if it recurs, mention it to a line manager or senior evaluator.
D) Complain about the colleague in the team chat channel.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A** (D is close behind A)

**Explanation:** The findings were accurate, so the public interest is not at stake - this is a fairness and relationships issue, which changes the calculus compared with earlier questions. C handles it with proportion: a direct, private, specific conversation first (most people correct course immediately), with a measured escalation path if the behaviour repeats. That protects both the candidate's interests and the working relationship. A creates a public confrontation over a private grievance, embarrassing everyone and making the candidate - unfairly but predictably - look like the difficult one. D is public criticism without even the courage of A's directness; written complaints in team channels escalate conflicts and cannot be unsaid. B mistakes self-suppression for professionalism: credit matters for a junior's development and visibility, and calm self-advocacy is a career skill worth practising early. Note the contrast with Question 8: when *public accuracy* is at stake, a strong candidate acts immediately even under pressure; when the issue is *personal fairness*, they act privately and proportionately.

### Administration and scoring notes

- **Score against public sector values, not a scripted answer.** UK public sector scenarios reward honesty about evidence, user needs first, transparent collaboration, inclusion, and proportionate escalation - use these as the yardstick when a candidate's ranking differs from the key.
- **Apply the "role level" lens consistently.** At junior level, the best answers usually involve doing what is in the candidate's power well, being honest about limits, and involving a senior evaluator at the right moment - neither hiding problems nor delegating everything upwards. Score with this in mind.
- **Look for options that pair honesty with helpfulness.** The strongest responses rarely just refuse or just comply - they offer an accurate alternative, a next step, or a proportionate fix. This is a useful discriminator to explain in debrief.
- **Use the debrief to explore fluency with hard conversations.** Ask the candidate how they would actually phrase "I don't know, I'll find out" or "I made an error, here's the correction" - fluency here is a good signal beyond the ranking itself.
- **Read each scenario's specifics before scoring.** Details such as "the findings were presented accurately" or "the senior evaluator is unreachable until 17:00" change which option is best. Score against the exact scenario, not a generic rule.
- **Ask about real dilemmas in debrief.** A candidate who can describe a real tricky moment, what they did, and what they would do differently is showing the reflective habit this assessment is trying to measure.

### Common candidate errors to watch for when scoring

- **The idealistic answer over the effective one.** "Cancel the workshop until it's perfect" sounds principled but is disproportionate; watch for candidates favouring idealistic over practical, proportionate responses.
- **Passive options.** Waiting to see if anyone notices, staying silent, or hoping the subject changes almost always rank poorly; candidates who consistently choose passive options are worth probing further in debrief.
- **Confusing escalation with avoidance.** Escalating well (promptly, to the right person, with facts and a proposed step) is a strength; escalating instead of attempting reasonable direct action - or to avoid an awkward conversation - is a weakness. The distinction is worth drawing out in feedback.
- **Excessive deference.** "They are more senior, so I said nothing" is a recurring wrong answer at junior level; seniority deserves respect, not silence about errors, ethics, or evidence.
- **Solving people problems with unilateral action.** Deleting files, editing colleagues' work secretly, or working hidden overtime all remove symptoms while hiding the real issue from those who need to manage it - worth flagging explicitly if a candidate consistently favours these.
- **Ignoring the participant or user in the scenario.** When a member of the public appears in a dilemma, their welfare and trust usually outrank internal schedules and convenience; check whether a candidate's ranking reflects this.

## Conclusion

This guide has taken you through all four assessment sections for the junior digital evaluator role level: cognitive items that exercise the logical core of the job - theory-of-change reasoning, error spotting, and drawing only the conclusions the evidence supports; numeric items built from the completion rates, survey tables, budgets, and cost-per-outcome figures that fill this role's working week; verbal reasoning passages that apply the True/False/Cannot Say discipline to guidance extracts, stakeholder emails, ethics documents, and report summaries; and situational judgement scenarios about inconvenient findings, data ethics, mistakes, and pressure to overstate findings.

**Scoring.** Mark each section against the worked answers and explanations above. For the cognitive, numeric, and verbal sections, this is largely objective. For the situational judgement section, use the stated most/least effective answers and rankings as your key, but apply judgement to close calls - the explanations set out the reasoning so you can weigh an answer that is defensible but not the top-ranked one.

**Debrief.** Go through the candidate's performance with them after scoring, section by section. Use the explanations above to show why an answer was strong or weak - this gives useful feedback to the candidate regardless of the outcome, and it is fairer than a bare score. Be specific about patterns you noticed, such as a tendency to compare counts rather than rates, or to choose passive responses in situational judgement scenarios.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a record of the items used, the time given, and the candidate's answers and score, alongside any other evidence gathered in your process. Administer the same items and the same time limit to every candidate for this role level, so that results are comparable across your process. Remember the advisory above: this assessment is one input among several, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance, or disciplinary decision.
