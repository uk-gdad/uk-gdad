# Digital Evaluator (Head of Digital Evaluation) - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of digital evaluation, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. The material is job-specific: it presents what a head of digital evaluation genuinely handles — theories of change, evaluation plans, quality assurance frameworks, and logic models — and the judgements the role turns on, rather than abstract puzzles. The same faculties tested here — spotting flaws in a theory of change, interpreting an evaluation dataset under time pressure, extracting the precise meaning of Magenta Book guidance, and choosing the wisest course of action when a minister's office wants findings before quality assurance is complete — are the faculties exercised daily as leader of an organisation's digital evaluation community.

This document is organised into four assessment areas, each tailored specifically to this role:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, and prioritisation using the artefacts a head of digital evaluation genuinely handles.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — interpreting evaluation budgets, effect sizes, response rates, benefit-cost ratios, and performance indicators, with full worked arithmetic.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — dense passages drawn from evaluation strategies, government guidance, data ethics frameworks, and stakeholder correspondence, followed by True / False / Cannot Say and comprehension questions.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — realistic leadership dilemmas involving delivery teams, policy colleagues, senior stakeholders, analytical professions, and the evaluators being led.

Each section explains what the assessment measures, maps it explicitly to the skills in the capability framework, offers substantial items with worked answers for use as an answer key, and closes with administration tips and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Let the candidate work through each section without interruption, and note anything unusual about the conditions so the record is honest. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief — a candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Every question mimics the everyday artefacts and decisions of a head of digital evaluation: logic models with missing links, evaluation plans containing internal contradictions, quality assurance registers that need error checking, and competing demands on limited capacity that must be prioritised on defensible grounds.

Typical delivery is online and timed — commonly 15 to 30 minutes for 20 to 30 questions — with objective scoring against a norm group of comparable professionals. Modern platforms may adapt question difficulty and report speed and accuracy separately.

Cognitive rigour is the engine of everything this role does. Assuring the evaluation work of digital evaluator teams is error checking. Scrutinising a theory of change is testing logical deduction. Acting as an escalation point and removing blockers is prioritising under uncertainty. Setting the digital evaluation strategy is recognising patterns across a portfolio and abstracting them into a coherent direction.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the skills in the role summary:

- **Logical deduction → Product and service monitoring and Evaluation delivery.** Deduction questions test tracing causal chains, spotting non sequiturs, and identifying assumptions that break a logic model.
- **Error checking → Quality assurance of data and analysis.** Error-checking questions present registers, tables, and specifications containing deliberate inconsistencies to find quickly.
- **Prioritisation and problem solving → Evaluation planning and strategy and head-of-level duties.** Prioritisation questions give more demands than capacity and ask which allocation best serves quality standards and strategic goals.
- **Pattern recognition → Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle.** Pattern questions ask candidates to detect trends, anomalies, and structural similarities across evaluation portfolios.
- **Abstract and strategic reasoning → Communicating analysis and insight.** Complex recommendations at tactical and strategic level require moving fluently between the detail of a finding and the shape of the argument it supports.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Logic model sequencing.** A delivery team drafts a theory of change for a new digital licensing service with these elements, presented out of order: (1) fewer incomplete applications are submitted; (2) applicants receive real-time validation messages while completing the online form; (3) build inline validation into the application form; (4) caseworker time spent chasing missing information falls, reducing average decision time from 20 days to 12 days; (5) applicants trust the service more and channel shift from paper increases. Which ordering correctly runs activity → output → intermediate outcome → outcome → longer-term outcome?

- A. 3, 2, 1, 4, 5
- B. 2, 3, 1, 5, 4
- C. 3, 1, 2, 4, 5
- D. 3, 2, 4, 1, 5

**Correct answer: A.** The activity is what the team does (3, build validation). The output is what that directly produces for users (2, real-time messages). The intermediate outcome is the first behavioural change (1, fewer incomplete applications), driving the operational outcome (4, faster decisions), which supports the longer-term outcome (5, trust and channel shift). Options C and D place a behavioural change before its cause; option B begins with an output before any activity generates it. This tests the sequencing discipline exercised when providing guidance on developing theories of change.

**Question 2 (easy) — Error checking a QA register.** A quality assurance framework requires every published evaluation to record: peer reviewer named, data sources logged, code review completed, and limitations section present. Four entries in the assurance register: Report Alpha — reviewer J. Achebe; 3 data sources; code review "complete"; limitations "present". Report Beta — reviewer "TBC"; 5 data sources; code review "complete"; limitations "present". Report Gamma — reviewer M. Osei; 2 data sources; code review "complete"; limitations "present". Report Delta — reviewer L. Fraser; 4 data sources; code review "n/a — qualitative study"; limitations "present".

Which report clearly breaches the framework as written?

- A. Alpha
- B. Beta
- C. Gamma
- D. Delta

**Correct answer: B.** "TBC" means no peer reviewer is named — a direct breach. Delta is the tempting distractor: "n/a" for code review may legitimately reflect a qualitative study with no code, a judgement call rather than an unambiguous breach. This tests distinguishing definite non-compliance from items needing a policy ruling.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Logical deduction from assurance rules.** Evaluation standards state: "Every impact evaluation must include a counterfactual. Every evaluation with a counterfactual must be peer reviewed by an analyst outside the commissioning team. Process evaluations are not required to include a counterfactual." A passport renewal evaluation was peer reviewed by an analyst inside the commissioning team. Which conclusion follows logically?

- A. The evaluation is a process evaluation.
- B. The evaluation did not include a counterfactual, or it breaches the standards.
- C. The evaluation breaches the standards.
- D. The evaluation is not an impact evaluation.

**Correct answer: B.** If it included a counterfactual, it needed external review and got internal only — a breach. If it lacked a counterfactual, the review rule never triggered, but it cannot be a compliant impact evaluation. The only certainty is the disjunction in B. This tests identifying what a set of facts proves versus merely suggests — the reasoning behind assuring evaluation work.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across a portfolio.** Reviewing the year's completed evaluations: the benefits calculator underestimated uptake because it ignored word-of-mouth referral; the appointment booking service underestimated demand because it ignored referrals from charities; the grants portal underestimated volume because it ignored signposting from local authorities. What is the generalisable pattern to feed into best practice?

- A. Teams overestimate demand; guidance should mandate conservative forecasting.
- B. Teams systematically omit indirect and third-party referral routes from their theories of change; guidance should require mapping all entry channels, including intermediaries, at the planning stage.
- C. Teams lack quantitative skills; recruitment should prioritise statisticians.
- D. The three services are unusually popular; no general lesson exists.

**Correct answer: B.** Each case shares an unmodelled indirect entry route causing underestimation. This tests abstracting a structural cause from three instances and converting it into a reusable planning requirement — leading and championing best practice.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Prioritisation under capacity constraint.** Capacity exists for exactly two major assurance reviews this quarter. Four requests: (i) a politically high-profile evaluation publishing in five weeks with known methodological disputes; (ii) a routine process evaluation publishing next quarter; (iii) an impact evaluation whose findings will decide whether a £40 million programme is scaled, publishing in eight weeks; (iv) a retrospective review of a service decommissioned last year. Which pair should be prioritised?

- A. (i) and (ii)
- B. (i) and (iii)
- C. (iii) and (iv)
- D. (ii) and (iv)

**Correct answer: B.** (i) combines imminent publication, methodological risk, and political exposure; (iii) carries the largest decision consequence. (ii) can wait until next quarter; (iv) has no live decision depending on it. This tests a defensible framework of risk, consequence, and timing rather than "loudest stakeholder wins".

**Question 6 (moderate) — Spotting the flawed inference.** A report states: "Following the redesign of the appeals service, user satisfaction rose from 61% to 79%. The redesign therefore caused an 18 percentage point improvement in satisfaction." Which single weakness most undermines the causal claim?

- A. Percentages should never be subtracted.
- B. The before-and-after comparison has no counterfactual, so other concurrent changes (seasonality, case mix, a media story, survey method changes) could explain some or all of the rise.
- C. Satisfaction is not worth measuring.
- D. The sample sizes are not stated, so the result is certainly wrong.

**Correct answer: B.** The claim leaps from correlation across time to causation without controlling for what would have happened anyway. This tests precise diagnosis of the core weakness a counterfactual addresses — coaching is only as credible as the accuracy of the critique behind it.

**Question 7 (hard) — Deduction with interacting rules.** A data ethics framework: (R1) any evaluation using linked administrative data must complete an ethics review before data access; (R2) any ethics review involving children's data must go to the full ethics board, not the fast-track panel; (R3) fast-track decisions take two weeks; full board decisions take six weeks, and the board meets only in the first week of each month. On 1 September, a team asks to start analysis on 1 November for an evaluation linking school attendance records with a digital tutoring service's usage logs. Can they meet their date, and what is the binding constraint?

- A. Yes, comfortably — fast-track takes two weeks.
- B. Yes, but only if the full board reviews the case at its early-September or early-October meeting, because children's data mandates the full board and its six-week decision means only the September meeting guarantees a decision before 1 November, making the September board the binding constraint.
- C. No — six weeks from 1 November is mid-December.
- D. Yes — ethics review can run in parallel with analysis.

**Correct answer: B.** School attendance records are children's data, mandating the full board (eliminating A). A September board decides by mid-October (in time); an October board decides by mid-November (too late). This tests multi-rule scheduling deduction — finding the one step that actually determines whether a team's date survives, and rejecting the parallel-running option D, which violates R1.

**Question 8 (hard) — Error checking interlocking figures.** An evaluation plan states: "We will survey 1,200 users, expecting a 40% response rate, giving about 480 responses. We will interview 5% of respondents, approximately 30 interviews. Fieldwork costs £25 per survey response and £150 per interview, so the fieldwork budget is £15,600." Which element is internally inconsistent?

- A. The 480 responses figure.
- B. The 30 interviews figure.
- C. The £15,600 budget figure.
- D. All figures are consistent.

**Correct answer: B.** 5% of 480 = 24, not "approximately 30". The budget line (480 × £25 + 24 × £150 = £15,600) is consistent with the correct figure of 24, confirming "30" is the error. This tests reconciling a document against itself — the elegant diagnostic where one figure silently reveals the intended value of another.

**Question 9 (hard) — Prioritisation with strategic trade-offs.** A business plan can fund three of five initiatives: (i) a coaching programme teaching delivery teams to build their own theories of change; (ii) a central tool that automates quality assurance checks on analytical code across all teams; (iii) a high-visibility summative evaluation of a flagship digital service; (iv) a data ethics training refresh; (v) a conference stand to advocate for digital evaluation across government. The strategy's stated priorities, in order: raise quality, build capability, grow influence. Which three best fit the strategy?

- A. (iii), (v), (i)
- B. (ii), (i), (iv)
- C. (ii), (i), (v)
- D. (i), (iii), (iv)

**Correct answer: C.** (ii) scales assurance across every team — the strongest quality lever; (i) is the clearest capability multiplier; (v) serves the advocacy priority. This tests reading a priority ordering as a decision rule, not a mood, and recognising that (iii) is a delivery output that could be resourced through normal channels rather than a strategic investment in the function.

**Question 10 (hard) — Detecting a subtle metric artefact.** After a service moved from monthly to weekly release cycles, "defects per release" fell from 12 to 4, and the product manager claims quality has tripled. Weekly releases are roughly a quarter the size of monthly ones. What should be concluded?

- A. Quality tripled, as claimed.
- B. Normalising by release size, defects per unit of change went from 12 per month-sized release to approximately 16 (4 × 4) per equivalent volume — so defect density may actually have worsened, and the metric needs re-basing before any quality claim is made.
- C. Weekly releases are always better.
- D. The metric is fine because fewer defects is always good.

**Correct answer: B.** Four defects in a quarter-sized release is equivalent to ~16 per month-sized volume, a possible worsening masked by re-slicing. This tests recognising that whenever a process changes shape, every rate metric spanning the change must be re-based.

**Question 11 (moderate) — Life cycle risk identification.** A digital service is about to move from public beta to live. User research is extensive; a process evaluation ran during beta; baseline data for the key outcome (reduced fraud) was never collected before beta launched; the monitoring dashboard tracks uptake and satisfaction but not fraud outcomes. What is the most important evaluation risk to raise at the live assessment?

- A. Satisfaction may dip after launch.
- B. Without a pre-launch baseline and outcome monitoring, the service will never be able to demonstrate its impact on fraud — the core benefit in its business case — so an urgent decision is needed on constructing a retrospective baseline or comparison group while that is still possible.
- C. The dashboard has too many metrics.
- D. Process evaluations are unnecessary.

**Correct answer: B.** The service's central claim is currently unmeasurable, and every month that passes makes retrospective baseline construction harder. This tests severity ranking: many things are imperfect, but only one gap destroys the ability to ever answer the business-critical question.

**Question 12 (hard) — Deductive reasoning about missing evidence.** Team X reports on all five committed indicators. Team Y reports on four of five, omitting its economic outcome indicator, noting "data supplier delay". Team Z reports on three of five, omitting both indicators that showed decline at mid-year, with no explanation. Applying the principle that omissions correlated with unfavourable results are a stronger assurance concern than omissions with documented external causes, which team warrants the most urgent assurance attention, and why?

- A. Team X, because complete reporting may hide manipulation.
- B. Team Y, because economic outcomes matter most.
- C. Team Z, because the pattern of omissions — precisely the two declining indicators, unexplained — is consistent with selective reporting, which threatens the integrity of the evidence base itself.
- D. All three equally.

**Correct answer: C.** Team Z's omissions select exactly the unfavourable indicators with no cause offered — the signature of selective reporting. This tests recognising that completeness is not itself suspicious (against option A) and that an escalation point cannot afford to refuse prioritisation (against option D).

**Question 13 (moderate) — Problem solving a coaching bottleneck.** Personal coaching demand has grown to twelve teams with time for three. Coaching quality drops sharply above eight participants. Four lead evaluators can each coach two teams to a good standard after a short train-the-trainer investment. What allocation solves the bottleneck this quarter while protecting quality?

- A. Coach all twelve teams in one large group.
- B. Coach three teams personally and tell the other nine to wait.
- C. Invest time training the four lead evaluators (who then coach eight teams), coach the three most complex or highest-risk teams personally, and schedule the one remaining team for next quarter.
- D. Send all teams a written guide instead of coaching.

**Correct answer: C.** Using scarce time to create coaches yields 4 × 2 = 8 coached teams plus 3 personally = 11 of 12, with only one deferred, and builds durable capability. This tests recognising that the highest-leverage use of expert time is usually creating more experts.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate verbalises a decision rule** for prioritisation items ("consequence times urgency, tie-break on reversibility") rather than judging case by case.
- **Watch for whether a candidate checks arithmetic relationships across a document**, not just individual values, to spot which figure is the error.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes definite non-compliance from a judgement call** in an error-checking item.
- **Note whether a candidate reads a priority ordering as a decision rule** rather than choosing on instinct.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and consider offering ungraded practice items first.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer drawn from experience instead of the stated rules and data.**
- **Crediting the plausible over the proven** — a sensible-sounding professional judgement that does not actually follow from the premises.
- **Missing a changed denominator** in a rate or ratio artefact.
- **Rewarding over-flagging** — marking an ambiguous entry as a definite breach in an error-checking item.
- **Letting a time-sink question distort the overall picture.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates how a candidate analyses, interprets, and makes sound decisions from numerical data presented in the formats this role really uses. The arithmetic rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, weighted averages, and rates; what it tests is whether a candidate can extract the right numbers from a table, transform them correctly, and judge what they do and do not support.

Expect real-world inputs: evaluation budgets, survey response tables, benefit-cost calculations, uptake dashboards, capacity plans, and performance indicator time series. Most platforms allow or provide an on-screen calculator, but impose strict time limits.

Numeric fluency is a leadership credential in this role, not a technicality. Influencing senior stakeholders to secure resources means constructing and defending budgets and benefit-cost arguments in numbers. Defining best practice for measuring indicators, including economic outcomes, means personally modelling impeccable quantitative hygiene. Assuring the data and analysis of multiple teams means catching numerical errors that survived the teams' own checks.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interpreting performance metrics and indicators → Product and service monitoring.** Questions use uptake rates, completion rates, unit costs, and trend data from digital services.
- **Budgets, resourcing, and capacity arithmetic → Evaluation planning and strategy and business-planning duties.** Questions cover evaluation project budgets, day rates, team capacity, and recruitment costs.
- **Verifying and validating figures → Quality assurance of data and analysis.** Questions ask for reconciling totals, checking percentages, and spotting implausible values.
- **Quantitative research design arithmetic → Evaluation delivery.** Sample sizes, response rates, confidence in subgroup estimates, and effect sizes appear throughout.
- **Presenting numbers honestly → Communicating analysis and insight and Data ethics and privacy.** Several questions test whether a numerical claim is a fair representation of the data.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Response rates.** A survey of digital benefits calculator users sends 8,500 invitations and receives 2,210 complete responses. What is the response rate?

- A. 22%
- B. 26%
- C. 30%
- D. 34%

**Correct answer: B.** 2,210 ÷ 8,500 = 26%. This tests dividing correctly and a quick sanity check (25% of 8,500 is 2,125; the result is slightly more).

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change in an indicator.** A digital licensing service's median transaction time falls from 14 minutes to 10.5 minutes after a redesign. What is the percentage reduction?

- A. 20%
- B. 25%
- C. 30%
- D. 35%

**Correct answer: B.** (14 − 10.5) ÷ 14 = 25%. Dividing by the new value instead (3.5 ÷ 10.5 ≈ 33%) overstates improvement. This tests the correct base for a percentage reduction — exactly the kind of flattering slip an assurance system exists to catch.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Evaluation budget composition.** An impact evaluation budget totals £240,000: staff costs 55%, data acquisition 20%, fieldwork 15%, dissemination the remainder. Dissemination must fund a published report (£9,000) plus stakeholder events. How much remains for stakeholder events?

- A. £12,000
- B. £15,000
- C. £24,000
- D. £33,000

**Correct answer: B.** Dissemination = 10% × £240,000 = £24,000; minus the report = £15,000. This tests a two-step problem where the distractors catch a candidate who stops early or adds instead of subtracting.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Weighted average across services.** Service A: 82% satisfied from 12,000 respondents; Service B: 74% from 6,000; Service C: 90% from 2,000. What is the overall satisfaction rate across all respondents?

- A. 79.8%
- B. 82.0%
- C. 80.4%
- D. 84.7%

**Correct answer: C.** Weighted total = 16,080 ÷ 20,000 = 80.4%. The unweighted mean (82.0%) is the trap. This tests insisting on denominators — an average of averages is only valid when groups are the same size.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Benefit-cost ratio for a resourcing case.** A programme costs £1.8 million over two years. Evaluation evidence projects annual savings of £1.35 million once implemented, starting in year two, with year-two savings at 60% of the full rate. What is the simple benefit-cost ratio over the first three years (ignoring discounting)?

- A. 0.75
- B. 1.20
- C. 1.50
- D. 2.25

**Correct answer: B.** Benefits = £0 + £0.81m + £1.35m = £2.16m; BCR = 2.16 ÷ 1.8 = 1.20. This tests building a ratio from correctly phased flows rather than crediting full savings too early.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reconciling a data table.** A monitoring return reports quarterly digital uptake: Q1 18,400 digital / 9,600 paper / 65.7% share; Q2 21,150 / 8,850 / 70.5%; Q3 23,400 / 7,800 / 75.0%; Q4 26,000 / 6,500 / 78.0%. Which quarter's share is misreported?

- A. Q1
- B. Q2
- C. Q3
- D. Q4

**Correct answer: D.** Q4's correct share is 26,000 ÷ 32,500 = 80.0%, not 78.0%. This tests verifying every row rather than stopping at the first plausible suspect.

**Question 7 (hard) — Sample size and subgroup precision.** A survey of 1,600 users reports results for four nations: England 1,120, Scotland 240, Wales 160, Northern Ireland 80. The margin of error at 95% confidence is roughly 1 ÷ √n. Approximately what is the margin of error for the Northern Ireland estimate, and what should guidance say?

- A. About ±2.5%; subgroup reporting is fine.
- B. About ±11%; the plan should either boost the Northern Ireland sample, combine reporting periods, or clearly flag the estimate as indicative only.
- C. About ±5%; identical to the whole sample.
- D. Margins of error do not apply to subgroups.

**Correct answer: B.** For n = 80: 1 ÷ √80 ≈ ±11 points. This tests catching a design that promises subgroup findings the sample cannot honestly deliver, and offering the standard remedies: boost, pool, or caveat.

**Question 8 (hard) — Unit cost with phased volumes.** A digital service costs £4.2 million per year, fixed. Transaction volumes were 1.2 million in year one and are forecast to grow 25% in year two and a further 20% in year three. What is the forecast cost per transaction in year three, to the nearest penny?

- A. £2.33
- B. £2.80
- C. £2.92
- D. £3.50

**Correct answer: A.** Year three volume = 1.2m × 1.25 × 1.20 = 1.8m; £4,200,000 ÷ 1,800,000 = £2.33. This tests compounding sequential growth multiplicatively, never additively.

**Question 9 (hard) — Capacity planning for the community.** Each lead evaluator delivers 1.5 assurance reviews per month and each senior evaluator 1.0 per month, with 20% of all time reserved for coaching and community activities. There are 3 leads and 6 seniors. The portfolio forecast requires 108 assurance reviews in the year. What is the capacity position?

- A. Surplus of about 6 reviews.
- B. Exactly at capacity.
- C. Shortfall of about 6 reviews.
- D. Shortfall of about 18 reviews.

**Correct answer: C.** Net monthly capacity = (4.5 + 6.0) × 0.80 = 8.4; annual = 100.8, a shortfall of ~7 against 108. This tests applying the utilisation haircut rather than forgetting it entirely.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Recruitment funnel arithmetic.** Historical funnel rates for senior digital evaluators: 40% of applicants pass sift, 50% of sifted candidates pass interview, 75% of passers accept an offer. 6 hires are needed. Approximately how many applicants must the campaign attract?

- A. 20
- B. 30
- C. 40
- D. 60

**Correct answer: C.** 6 ÷ (0.40 × 0.50 × 0.75) = 40. This tests reversing a chained-rate funnel correctly.

**Question 11 (moderate) — Interpreting an effect size honestly.** A trial finds a digital reminder increases form completion from 64.0% (control, n=5,000) to 66.5% (treatment, n=5,000). A colleague drafts: "Reminders boost completion by 4%." Which statement is the most accurate and honest headline figure?

- A. "Completion rose by 2.5 percentage points (from 64.0% to 66.5%), a relative increase of about 3.9%."
- B. "Completion rose 4%."
- C. "Completion rose 2.5%."
- D. "Two-thirds of users now complete."

**Correct answer: A.** The absolute change is 2.5 points; the relative change is ≈3.9%. This tests giving both figures, correctly labelled — where numeracy meets the data ethics duty to avoid predictably misread claims.

**Question 12 (hard) — Two-variable trade-off from a dashboard.** A £600,000 improvement budget for a digital grants service: Option X, each £100,000 cuts failed applications by 3,000, each failed application costing £45 in reprocessing. Option Y, each £100,000 attracts 5,000 additional digital applications, each saving £22 versus paper. Which option yields the larger annual saving, and by how much?

- A. X, by £150,000
- B. Y, by £150,000
- C. X, by £270,000
- D. They are equal

**Correct answer: A.** X saves 18,000 × £45 = £810,000; Y saves 30,000 × £22 = £660,000; X wins by £150,000. This tests multiplying volume by value before comparing, rather than letting the larger raw count dominate.

**Question 13 (moderate) — Data quality rate with unequal batches.** Team P submits 12,000 records with a 2.5% error rate; Team Q submits 4,000 records with a 6% error rate. What is the overall error rate, and which single intervention cuts total errors most: halving P's rate or halving Q's rate?

- A. 4.25% overall; halving Q's rate helps most.
- B. 3.375% overall; halving P's rate helps most.
- C. 3.375% overall; halving Q's rate helps most.
- D. 4.25% overall; halving P's rate helps most.

**Correct answer: B.** Total errors = 540 ÷ 16,000 = 3.375%. Halving P removes 150 errors; halving Q removes 120. This tests targeting by absolute error contribution rather than headline rate.

**Question 14 (easy) — Reading scale qualifiers.** A spending table headed "Evaluation programme costs (£ thousands)" shows the digital evaluation line as 1,240. A briefing draft states the programme costs "£1,240". What is the correct figure and the error type?

- A. £1,240; no error.
- B. £124,000; decimal slip.
- C. £1,240,000; ignored scale qualifier.
- D. £12,400; transcription error.

**Correct answer: C.** The header means 1,240 = £1,240,000. This tests catching an ignored scale qualifier — among the most damaging real-world errors when it survives into a ministerial briefing.

**Question 15 (hard) — Phased evaluation spend against a fixed envelope.** A £1.5 million evaluation strategy is split 20% year one, 45% year two, 35% year three. At the end of year two, actual cumulative spend is £1,040,000, with year one exactly on plan. By how much did year two overspend or underspend against its allocation?

- A. Overspend of £65,000
- B. Underspend of £65,000
- C. Overspend of £140,000
- D. On plan exactly

**Correct answer: A.** Planned cumulative by end of year two = £975,000; actual = £1,040,000; overspend = £65,000. This tests a multi-step budget reconciliation and, for a strong candidate's answer, flagging the resulting squeeze on year three's analysis and reporting phase.

**Question 16 (moderate) — Completion-rate funnel on a live service.** 40,000 users start an online appeals form; 85% reach evidence-upload, 90% of those complete upload, 95% of those submit. The service standard requires an overall start-to-submit completion rate of at least 75%. Does the service meet the standard, and what is the rate?

- A. Yes — 76.5%
- B. No — 72.7%
- C. Yes — 90.0%
- D. No — 70.3%

**Correct answer: B.** 0.85 × 0.90 × 0.95 ≈ 72.7%, below standard. This tests chaining stage rates correctly, and, for a strong candidate's answer, identifying the biggest drop (evidence upload) as the priority for improvement.

**Question 17 (easy) — Ratio for a coaching plan.** A coaching plan targets 1 evaluator to 4 teams. There are 52 delivery teams and 11 evaluators available. How many additional evaluators are needed to meet the target ratio exactly?

- A. 1
- B. 2
- C. 3
- D. 4

**Correct answer: B.** Required = 52 ÷ 4 = 13; gap = 2. This tests computing the exact staffing gap rather than judging the current ratio as "close enough".

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads a table's scale qualifiers** before extracting numbers.
- **Watch for whether a candidate writes intermediate figures down** on multi-step problems.
- **Note whether a candidate weights an average by volume** rather than averaging averages.
- **Note whether a candidate compounds sequential growth multiplicatively.**
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that misreads a chart or table label** — monthly versus annual, or a missed "in thousands".
- **Crediting an unweighted average of subgroup percentages.**
- **Missing when a candidate adds sequential growth rates** instead of multiplying them.
- **Rewarding over-calculation** where estimation and elimination would settle the answer faster.
- **Missing when a candidate trusts a flattering figure** without checking it twice.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate understands, analyses, and draws conclusions from written material of the kind this role turns on: evaluation strategies, government guidance, quality assurance frameworks, data ethics policies, stakeholder emails, and draft report text. The defining discipline is fact-based logic — answering strictly from the passage, setting aside outside knowledge, however expert.

The dominant format is a short, dense passage followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say. Multiple-choice comprehension questions round out the set. Timing is tight, often under a minute per question.

Words are the instruments of governance in this role. A quality assurance framework binds teams only through what its sentences actually require. Advice to senior stakeholders is quoted, forwarded, and acted on. When a report is assured, its conclusions are tested against its own text and evidence — literally the True/False/Cannot Say judgement at professional scale.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise reading of standards and guidance → Evaluation delivery and Quality assurance of data and analysis.** Questions test extracting exactly what a framework mandates, permits, and is silent on.
- **Distinguishing evidence from assertion → Communicating analysis and insight.** True/False/Cannot Say is the discipline of knowing precisely what evidence licenses saying, in miniature.
- **Interpreting policy and legal text → Data ethics and privacy.** Questions use ethics framework and consent-wording passages where a single qualifier changes the obligation.
- **Reading stakeholder communications accurately → Evaluation planning and strategy and the escalation-point duty.** Questions test extraction of who has asked for what, by when, under which conditions.
- **Synthesising long documents → strategy-setting duties.** Best-summary questions mirror reducing a forty-page report to the three sentences a director general will read.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Passage A — from a draft organisational evaluation strategy.** "All major digital programmes must produce an evaluation plan before their beta phase begins. Plans must identify a named senior responsible analyst. Programmes classified as high-risk must additionally commission an independent peer review of their evaluation design; for other programmes, independent review is recommended but not mandatory. Where a programme processes special category personal data, the evaluation plan must be approved by the data ethics board regardless of risk classification. The head of digital evaluation may grant a deferral of the beta deadline in exceptional circumstances, provided the deferral and its rationale are recorded in the assurance register."

**Question 1 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Every major digital programme must commission an independent peer review of its evaluation design."

**Correct answer: False.** The mandatory review is restricted to high-risk programmes; for others it is "recommended but not mandatory". This tests answering only what the passage requires, not whether universal review would be good practice.

**Question 2 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A medium-risk programme processing special category personal data needs data ethics board approval for its evaluation plan."

**Correct answer: True.** Approval is required "regardless of risk classification". This tests recognising a one-word hinge ("regardless of") that overrides classification.

**Question 3 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Deferrals of the beta deadline have been granted more than once in the past year."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.** The passage establishes a power and a recording requirement but says nothing about whether the power has been exercised. This tests distinguishing what a framework permits from what has actually happened.

**Question 4 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "An evaluation plan that names a senior responsible analyst satisfies all requirements for a low-risk programme that processes no special category data."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.** The plan must also be produced "before their beta phase begins" — timing the statement says nothing about. This tests checking a claim of complete compliance against every clause, not just the clauses the statement mentions.

**Passage B — from a data ethics framework.** "Personal data collected for service monitoring may be reused for evaluation purposes only where users have been informed of this possibility at the point of collection. Anonymised data, from which individuals can no longer be identified by any reasonably available means, falls outside this restriction. Where evaluators are uncertain whether data meets the anonymisation threshold, they must consult the data protection team before access is granted. Consultation outcomes must be documented. Nothing in this framework removes obligations arising under data protection legislation."

**Question 5 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Evaluators may reuse fully anonymised monitoring data for evaluation without users having been informed at the point of collection."

**Correct answer: True.** Anonymised data is expressly placed "outside this restriction". This tests tracking the passage's own definition rather than importing scepticism about real-world anonymisation claims.

**Question 6 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "If an evaluator is confident that data is anonymised, the framework does not require them to consult the data protection team."

**Correct answer: True.** The consultation duty triggers only on uncertainty; a confident evaluator is not uncertain. This tests reading a trigger condition exactly as drafted, rather than substituting a broader duty the passage does not impose.

**Question 7 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The framework overrides data protection legislation where the two conflict."

**Correct answer: False.** The final sentence states the opposite. This tests catching a savings clause easy to skim past at the end of a passage.

**Passage C — email from a director of policy.** "Thanks for the interim findings on the digital identity pilot. I appreciate the caveats, but the programme board meets Thursday and the Minister's office has asked for a one-page summary before then. I'd like the summary to lead with the 30% reduction in processing time. I understand the fraud outcome data isn't mature yet — fine to omit that, since it's inconclusive anyway. Could your team also confirm that the evaluation supports national rollout? We can revisit the detail in the full report next quarter."

**Question 8 (moderate).** Which of the following does the director explicitly request? Select the best answer.

- A. A one-page summary before Thursday, leading with the processing-time reduction, omitting the immature fraud data, and confirmation that the evaluation supports national rollout.
- B. A full report before Thursday.
- C. Deletion of the fraud outcome data from the evaluation.
- D. A meeting with the Minister's office.

**Correct answer: A.** Every element is stated directly. This tests accurate extraction, kept separate from any judgement about whether to comply.

**Question 9 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The director believes the fraud outcome data will eventually support national rollout."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.** "Inconclusive anyway" describes current state, not an eventual prediction. This tests not importing a suspected motive or an optimistic reading the text does not state.

**Passage D — from published cross-government evaluation guidance.** "Departments should proportionately evaluate all significant programmes. Proportionality means the scale and rigour of evaluation should reflect the programme's cost, risk, novelty, and the decisions the evidence will inform. Untested, novel interventions warrant more rigorous designs, including experimental methods where feasible. Evaluation findings should be published within twelve months of completion unless there are compelling reasons not to, such as national security. Departments are encouraged, but not required, to pre-register evaluation protocols publicly."

**Question 10 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Departments are required to pre-register evaluation protocols publicly."

**Correct answer: False.** "Encouraged, but not required" directly contradicts the statement. This tests the encouraged/required distinction that runs throughout guidance interpretation.

**Question 11 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A low-cost, low-risk programme using well-established methods requires an experimental evaluation design."

**Correct answer: False.** Rigour is tied to cost, risk, and novelty, and experimental methods are reserved for "untested, novel interventions". This tests applying the passage's own stated rule to a case at the opposite pole of every criterion.

**Question 12 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Evaluation findings must always be published within twelve months of completion."

**Correct answer: False.** The exception for compelling reasons contradicts an absolute "always". This tests scanning for absolute words before anything else.

**Question 13 (hard) — Best summary.** A 60-word extract reads: "Uptake of the digital service exceeded forecast among users aged under 50 but fell materially short among older users, who cited difficulty with identity verification. Overall uptake met target only because the under-50 group over-performed. Unless verification is simplified, projected savings — which assume uniform uptake growth — are unlikely to be realised in full." Which one-sentence summary best serves a senior stakeholder?

- A. "The service met its uptake target."
- B. "Older users dislike the service."
- C. "Overall uptake met target, but only through over-performance among under-50s; the verification barrier for older users puts the assumed savings at risk unless it is fixed."
- D. "Savings will not be realised."

**Correct answer: C.** Option C preserves finding, mechanism, and consequence. This tests compression that loses no decision-relevant truth, rather than compression that hides the compositional fragility a passage exists to flag.

**Question 14 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say (Passage D again): "A department that delays publication of findings for fourteen months without any compelling reason is acting inconsistently with the guidance."

**Correct answer: True.** Fourteen months exceeds twelve, and the stipulated absence of a compelling reason rules out the exception. This tests combining a stated rule with the facts given in the question itself.

**Passage E — from the terms of reference for a cross-government digital evaluation network.** "The network exists to share evaluation methods, findings, and standards across departmental digital evaluation communities. Membership is open to any government analyst or digital professional with evaluation responsibilities. The network is chaired on a rotating annual basis by a head of digital evaluation from a member department. The chair may convene working groups on specific methodological topics; working group outputs are advisory and become binding on a department only if adopted through that department's own governance. The network does not itself commission evaluations, and it holds no budget beyond secretariat costs, which are met by the chairing department in the year of its chairing."

**Question 15 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Working group outputs are binding on all member departments once published by the network."

**Correct answer: False.** Outputs "become binding on a department only if adopted through that department's own governance". This tests the advisory-until-adopted pattern common to cross-government communities.

**Question 16 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A department chairing the network will incur costs that other member departments do not incur that year."

**Correct answer: True.** Secretariat costs are borne by the chairing department alone. This tests synthesising two clauses (cost scope and cost bearer) rather than single-sentence matching.

**Question 17 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A head of digital evaluation whose department is not currently a member of the network is eligible to chair it."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.** The passage uses "membership" in two subtly different ways (individual and departmental) and never defines departmental membership. This tests recognising a deliberately drafted ambiguity the text cannot resolve, rather than forcing a verdict.

**Question 18 (moderate) — Comprehension.** According to Passage E, which of the following can the network itself do?

- A. Commission an evaluation of a cross-departmental digital service.
- B. Convene a working group on quasi-experimental methods.
- C. Compel a department to adopt a shared quality standard.
- D. Fund a departmental evaluation from its central budget.

**Correct answer: B.** A, C, and D are each explicitly excluded by the passage. This tests elimination against explicit exclusions.

**Question 19 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say (Passage E again): "The current chair of the network is a head of digital evaluation."

**Correct answer: True.** The rule defines the office, so whoever holds it fits the definition. This tests distinguishing rules that constrain what must be true (this question) from rules that merely enable what might happen (Question 3).

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate distinguishes False (contradicted) from Cannot Say (silent).**
- **Watch for whether a candidate underlines trigger words** — regardless, unless, only, must, should, may, all, always, never, encouraged, required.
- **Note whether a candidate articulates why a passage is silent** rather than treating Cannot Say as a shrug.
- **Note whether a candidate checks every clause of a full-compliance claim**, not just the ones a statement mentions.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer drawn from outside knowledge** rather than the passage.
- **Missing when a candidate confuses "likely" with "certain"** — converting a probabilistic claim into an absolute.
- **Crediting an answer that verifies only the clauses a statement mentions**, missing a clause it doesn't.
- **Missing a savings clause or exception** at the end of a passage.
- **Rewarding an inferred motive** from correspondence rather than what is written.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment evaluates decision-making, interpersonal effectiveness, and professional integrity by presenting realistic workplace dilemmas and asking which responses are most and least effective. There is often no single provably correct answer; responses are scored against what experienced practitioners and the organisation's values identify as stronger or weaker courses of action. Formats include selecting the most and least effective options, or ranking or rating all options. Timing is usually generous or absent.

This is arguably the assessment closest to the actual job of a head of digital evaluation. Reasoning skills are exercised in private; judgement is exercised in public, in rooms where a policy director wants a caveat softened, a delivery team wants a risk waved through, an evaluator wants backing in a methodological dispute, and a finance colleague wants an assurance budget halved. The scenarios below are built from real duties: acting as an escalation point, defending quality standards, overseeing data ethics, influencing senior stakeholders, advocating across government, and developing the community led. Scoring aligns with UK public sector values: honesty, objectivity, integrity, and impartiality; user needs and accessibility; transparency; collaboration; and inclusion.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Escalation handling and blocker removal → head-of-level duties.** Scenarios test acting at the right altitude — neither absorbing every problem personally nor bouncing everything back down.
- **Integrity under pressure → Data ethics and privacy and Quality assurance of data and analysis.** Scenarios probe the line between being helpful to stakeholders and protecting the objectivity of evidence.
- **Influence and advocacy → Evaluation planning and strategy and Communicating analysis and insight.** Scenarios test securing resources and buy-in without overclaiming what evaluation can deliver.
- **Developing people → Evaluation delivery and coaching duties.** Scenarios test how evaluators and teams are corrected, stretched, and backed.
- **Cross-government collaboration → the advocacy duty.** Scenarios involve other GDAD communities and the analytical professions.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (moderate) — The pressured summary.** The director of policy from Verbal Passage C emails again: the one-page summary for Thursday's programme board should "lead with the 30% processing-time reduction, leave out the immature fraud data, and confirm the evaluation supports national rollout". The interim findings support the 30% figure with caveats, the fraud data is genuinely immature, and the evidence so far supports rollout to two further regions, not national rollout.

A. Produce the summary as requested; the full report next quarter can correct the record.
B. Refuse to provide any summary until the full report is complete.
C. Provide the one-pager on time: lead with the 30% figure and its key caveat, note that fraud outcomes are not yet mature enough to report, and state that current evidence supports a staged extension to two regions, with national rollout contingent on the next evidence point. Offer to brief the director before Thursday.
D. Escalate the director's email to their director general as attempted interference.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.** Option C serves the stakeholder's legitimate need for a timely, usable summary while protecting evidential integrity, with a pre-briefing that treats the director as a partner. Option A is worst because it publishes an overclaim into a ministerial process; "correcting the record next quarter" after decisions are taken is the textbook failure of evaluation integrity. Option B fails a reasonable timeliness need. Option D is disproportionate as a first move. This tests finding a fast, honest alternative rather than either compliance or refusal.

**Question 2 (moderate) — The struggling evaluator.** A senior digital evaluator submits an assurance review that a delivery team immediately disputes, correctly identifying two clear analytical errors. The evaluator is usually reliable, has been carrying a heavy workload, and is visibly embarrassed. The product manager, copied in, asks that the review be "withdrawn and redone by someone competent".

A. Publicly defend the review to maintain the community's authority.
B. Acknowledge the errors promptly to the product manager, commit to a corrected review within a stated time, and separately talk with the evaluator about workload, support, and what allowed the errors through — also asking what the assurance-of-assurance process should have caught.
C. Withdraw the review, reassign it to another evaluator, and quietly reduce the original evaluator's responsibilities.
D. Reply that everyone makes mistakes and the review largely stands.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.** Option B pairs honesty about errors with a concrete remedy, care for the person, and a systemic question. Option A is worst: defending known errors to preserve authority is the fastest way to lose it permanently. Option C fixes the artefact but quietly punishes a reliable person without development. Option D concedes nothing specific. This tests treating the incident as information about the system, not just the individual.

**Question 3 (hard) — The ethics shortcut.** A high-priority evaluation needs linked data urgently. An analyst confides that a colleague has suggested proceeding with the data linkage now and completing the ethics review paperwork retrospectively, "since the board always approves this kind of thing anyway". The programme's senior responsible owner is pressing for results and is aware of the suggestion.

A. Allow it this once, given the precedent that similar linkages are always approved.
B. Stop the linkage proceeding before review; tell the analyst they were right to raise it; contact the SRO directly to explain why retrospective ethics review is not review at all, and offer practical help — an expedited board submission and interim analysis on already-approved data — so the programme keeps moving.
C. Stop the linkage and report the analyst's colleague and the SRO to the permanent secretary for attempted misconduct.
D. Ask the ethics board to meet retrospectively and backdate the approval to keep the paperwork tidy.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.** Option B upholds the non-negotiable (review precedes access), protects and affirms the person who spoke up, and offers a legitimate faster path. Option D is worst: backdating an approval falsifies a governance record. Option A substitutes a prediction of approval for actual approval. Option C leaps to the gravest escalation before any conversation. This tests upholding a control while reducing the incentive to cut corners.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The budget challenge.** A finance director proposes cutting the evaluation programme budget by 30%, remarking that "evaluation is a nice-to-have — the services already have analytics dashboards". There are fifteen minutes tomorrow.

A. Accept the cut and spread the reduction evenly across all planned evaluations.
B. Bring one page showing: the decisions next year that depend on evaluation evidence, with the money at stake in each; two concrete past cases where evaluation findings changed course and saved money; the specific decisions that would be taken blind under a 30% cut; and a proposal for a smaller, prioritised cut that could be absorbed by deferring the lowest-consequence evaluations.
C. Warn that the cut will be raised with the departmental board as a breach of government evaluation expectations.
D. Explain that dashboards only monitor and cannot attribute impact, so the premise of the cut is analytically mistaken.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.** Option B reframes evaluation from cost to decision-insurance, quantifies consequence, and concedes something real. Option D is analytically correct but tactically weak alone. Option C substitutes threat for persuasion as an opening move. Option A is worst: an even spread abdicates exactly the prioritisation judgement the role exists to provide. This tests influencing through evidence rather than assertion or threat.

**Question 5 (hard) — The inconvenient finding.** An evaluation of a flagship accessibility feature finds it is barely used, and that the users who most need it largely cannot find it. The service's director — a strong ally who secured much of the funding — asks to "hold the finding back until we've fixed discoverability, so the feature isn't killed off unfairly". The finding is due for publication under the organisation's transparency commitment within the month.

A. Publish on schedule with no engagement, since transparency rules are rules.
B. Agree to hold the finding back indefinitely to protect the feature and the relationship.
C. Publish on schedule, but work with the director beforehand so publication lands alongside their discoverability improvement plan — presenting the finding accurately with the response, and framing low uptake as a fixable design issue, which is what the evidence shows.
D. Delay publication quietly for six months and hope the fix works by then.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.** Option C honours the transparency commitment and the accuracy of the finding while giving the ally legitimate context. Option B is worst: indefinite suppression at a sponsor's request converts the function from evaluator to protector. Option D is B with a timer and added concealment. Option A honours the rule but squanders the relationship needlessly. This tests protecting both the evidence and the relationship rather than trading one for the other.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The methodological turf war.** A lead digital evaluator and a principal statistician from the analytical profession are in escalating disagreement over the evaluation design for a shared programme: the evaluator favours a rapid quasi-experimental design aligned to the delivery timetable; the statistician insists a randomised design is the only defensible option and has said the evaluation community "lacks statistical depth". Both have escalated.

A. Back the evaluator publicly — community standing is at stake.
B. Convene the two of them with a neutral structure: agree the decision criteria first (what decision the evidence must support, by when, at what confidence), then assess both designs against those criteria together, and record the trade-offs whichever way it lands.
C. Defer entirely to the statistician, since randomisation is the stronger method.
D. Take the design decision without meeting them, and inform both professions of the outcome.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.** Option B converts a status contest into a criteria problem. Option A is worst because it makes the dispute tribal. Option C capitulates on method-worship grounds when proportionality is the actual value. Option D resolves the object-level question while teaching both communities that escalation means exclusion from the reasoning. This tests converting professional status contests into evidence-based decisions.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The coaching shortcut.** A delivery team entering private beta asks for their theory of change to be written for them: "You'd do it in an afternoon, and honestly yours will be better than anything we'd produce." The diary could just about absorb it, and demand for exactly this kind of help is rising across the organisation.

A. Write it for them — it is efficient and the quality will be high.
B. Decline and send them the guidance documents.
C. Run a two-hour facilitated session where the team drafts their own theory of change, challenging and shaping it, then have a lead evaluator review the result — and note the recurring demand as a case for a standing coaching offer in next year's business plan.
D. State that theories of change are their responsibility and evaluation gets involved after beta.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.** Option C reflects coaching rather than ghost-writing, since a theory of change works only when the team owns the causal claims. Option A produces a beautiful artefact nobody owns. Option B is technically correct and practically useless. Option D is worst: it is factually wrong about the framework itself and slams a door on a team actively asking to do the right thing. This tests coaching for capability transfer rather than performing the work.

**Question 8 (hard) — The community in decline.** Exit interviews show the digital evaluation community's morale is sagging: two strong senior evaluators have left, citing "no visible career path"; recruitment to a vacant lead role failed twice; and delivery teams increasingly bypass evaluators, citing slow turnaround. A director asks for a plan.

A. Request an immediate pay-flexibility case for evaluators to stem departures.
B. Present a capability plan that addresses the system: a published career pathway with named development moves; turnaround-time standards with a triage tier for rapid requests (fixing the bypass incentive); re-running the lead recruitment with a revised, realistic role profile and active search across the cross-government community; and retention conversations with current seniors now — with measurable indicators for each strand.
C. Commission a staff survey to gather more evidence before acting.
D. Accept the shrinkage and re-scope the function around a smaller core.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.** Option B treats three symptoms as one system, pairing every diagnosis with an intervention and an indicator. Option A addresses one lever for one symptom, when pay was not the stated reason for departure. Option C defers action the existing evidence already supports. Option D is worst as a first response: it converts a recoverable dip into a strategic retreat without testing any intervention. This tests systemic diagnosis over single-lever or deferred responses.

**Question 9 (moderate) — The cross-government ask.** A head of digital evaluation in another department asks to share a quality assurance framework and coaching materials a team spent a year developing. A colleague objects: "We should keep the advantage — we're competing with them for the same analysts."

A. Share the materials openly, offer a walkthrough session, and invite the other department to contribute improvements back — while raising the recruitment-competition concern honestly inside internal retention planning.
B. Decline; the materials are the organisation's asset.
C. Share an older, redacted version to appear collaborative at minimal cost.
D. Agree to share only if the other department formally credits the team in all uses.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C.** Option A recognises that a stronger evaluation profession anywhere raises the standard everywhere. Option B mistakes the public sector for a market competitor. Option D converts collegiality into a licensing negotiation. Option C is worst because it is dishonest collaboration — taking the reputational benefit of sharing while deliberately delivering less than it appears to. This tests genuine cross-government collaboration over territorial instincts.

**Question 10 (hard) — The minister's deadline.** A director says the Minister's office wants headline findings from a major evaluation two weeks before quality assurance completes. The analysis is done; the assurance review — which in the past has changed headline numbers in roughly one case in five — has not started. The director says: "Give me your best numbers with a health warning. Refusing isn't an option."

A. Provide the numbers with a standard caveat line in the footer.
B. Provide a briefing that gives the current headline range rather than point estimates, states plainly that one in five assurance reviews changes headline findings, identifies which specific findings are most and least likely to move, and commits to a confirmed set by a named date — and start the assurance review immediately on the highest-consequence findings first.
C. Refuse, citing the quality assurance framework, and offer the confirmed findings in two weeks.
D. Quietly compress the assurance review into three days by pulling evaluators off other work, and present the result as fully assured.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.** Option B answers legitimate political reality with honest uncertainty rather than false precision. Option A technically discloses but practically misleads. Option C is principled but politically naive given the stated constraint. Option D is worst: it degrades the control while claiming its authority. This tests honest disclosure under uncertainty rather than false precision or non-negotiable refusal.

**Question 11 (moderate) — The accessibility blind spot.** Evaluation plans across the portfolio rely on sample frames drawn from completed digital journeys, systematically excluding users who abandoned or never started them — including many disabled users. Delivery timelines are tight, and adding recruitment strands would delay two evaluations by six weeks.

A. Note the limitation clearly in each evaluation's report and proceed to timetable.
B. Require the two highest-impact evaluations to add inclusive recruitment strands despite the six-week delay; for the rest, mandate the limitation be stated prominently, and change the evaluation planning guidance so sample-frame inclusion is checked at plan approval, before timelines are set.
C. Delay all evaluations until every design includes disabled users.
D. Commission a separate, single study of disabled users' experience across all services next year.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.** Option B fixes it fully where the decisions are biggest, is loudly honest where it cannot be fixed this cycle, and repairs the system so the gap cannot silently recur. Option A treats a systematic exclusion of disabled users as a documentation issue. Option C honours the principle at a blanket cost no portfolio can bear. Option D is useful but a year late and detached from decisions. This tests proportionate inclusion — fixing what can be fixed now, and repairing the system for the future.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate finds a "third way"** — a fast, honest alternative that meets a legitimate need — rather than simple compliance or refusal.
- **Watch for whether a candidate's least-effective choice is passive or integrity-corroding** — waiting, caveat-and-carry-on, backdating, suppressing, overclaiming.
- **Note who a candidate's chosen option informs, consults, or bypasses** — bypassing a person before talking to them is usually a weaker answer; protecting someone who spoke up is usually a stronger one.
- **Note whether a candidate protects systems, not just artefacts** — fixing the one flawed review, plan, or finding without asking what let it through.
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding the idealistic option over the effective one** — a "refuse on principle" answer that abandons the outcome to less careful hands.
- **Crediting a passive option** — waiting for more evidence, sending documents instead of coaching, spreading cuts evenly, delaying quietly.
- **Missing when a candidate ignores the organisation's values** — transparency, user needs, accessibility, inclusion, collaboration across professions.
- **Rewarding an answer that escalates to formal process before any conversation.**
- **Missing when a candidate fixes an artefact without asking what let it through** — the head-of-level layer most of these scenarios contain.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a head of digital evaluation: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested sequencing theories of change, deducing what interlocking rules actually require, spotting internal inconsistencies a document hides from its own authors, and prioritising a portfolio when everything cannot be first. The numeric section tested the fluency that carries credibility with finance directors and programme boards: percentage points versus percentages, weighted averages, compounding, capacity plans, and benefit-cost cases built from correctly phased flows. The verbal section tested the precision on which frameworks, guidance, and stakeholder correspondence all turn — including the evaluator's signature skill of saying "Cannot Say" with confidence. The situational judgement section tested judgement under pressure: protecting evidence and relationships together, refusing false choices, backing the people who speak up, and repairing systems rather than just artefacts.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can reason precisely under pressure, read frameworks and guidance for exactly what they establish, and lead a digital evaluation community whose entire value rests on being right, honest, and useful at the same time — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
