# Lead Service Transition Manager - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for lead service transition manager, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. The material is job-specific: it presents what a lead service transition manager actually handles — portfolio-level transition plans, supplier commitments across multiple contracts, release policies and their exceptions, capacity trajectories, benefits narratives, and the organisational dynamics of teams held accountable — and it samples the judgements the role turns on: sequencing resources across competing programmes, managing high-impact and complex change, intervening in team dynamics before they become delivery failures, and championing user outcomes when every institutional incentive points elsewhere.

A lead service transition manager is solely responsible for co-ordination activities across projects, suppliers, and service teams. That word "solely" carries weight: when transitions collide, when a supplier's confidence outruns its evidence, when capacity trends toward a ceiling nobody has budgeted for, the synthesis — and the accountability — sits with this role. The four assessment types map cleanly onto that reality. Cognitive assessment samples the multi-constraint reasoning behind portfolio sequencing and release policy. Numeric reasoning samples the arithmetic of capacity, availability, cost, and trend on which remedial actions rest. Verbal reasoning samples the precision with which contracts, policies, and carefully worded supplier positions are read — and the clarity with which the documents others are held to are written. Situational judgement samples the leadership behaviours: strategic escalation, team repair, feedback that lands and lasts, and the championing of service and user focus across organisational boundaries.

This document is organised into four assessment sections, each with the same structure: what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, a mapping to the named skills in the role summary, a substantial set of items with worked explanations for use as an answer key, administration tips, and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring. The questions progress from easy through moderate to genuinely hard, and the hard ones are hard the way the job is hard — through ambiguity, interlocking constraints, and competing legitimate interests rather than through obscure knowledge.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Let the candidate work through each section without interruption, and note anything unusual about the conditions so the record is honest. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief — a candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental operations this role demands, using realistic material rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level, the emphasis shifts from item-level checking toward systems reasoning: multi-constraint scheduling across a portfolio, policy application with exceptions and precedence, detection of structural inconsistencies across documents produced by different teams, and diagnosis of patterns that span suppliers, projects, and time.

The format is typically an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 multiple-choice questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group — for senior roles, often a norm group of comparable seniority. Adaptive difficulty is common.

Cognitive assessment matters at this level because co-ordination at scale is applied reasoning under load. Sequencing three programmes through one operations calendar means solving a constraint problem whose errors surface months later as collisions. Applying release policy to a high-impact change means executing conditional logic where a misread exception becomes an unauthorised deployment. Noticing that three unrelated transition failures share a structural cause is pattern-matching across noisy data — the seed of every process improvement the role will ever initiate. And because the role carries sole responsibility for cross-portfolio co-ordination, there is often no second reader for that reasoning: the assessment's simulation of accurate-thinking-under-time-pressure is unusually faithful to the role's working conditions.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in the role summary:

- **Multi-constraint reasoning and prioritisation** map to the core accountability — planning and co-ordination of resources across projects, suppliers, and service teams — where sequencing errors are the expensive ones, and to the **Change management** skill of managing high-impact, complex change requests.
- **Logical deduction with exceptions and precedence** maps to the **Change management** duty to ensure release policies, procedures, and processes are applied: policy application is conditional logic, and the exceptions are where it fails.
- **Pattern recognition across noisy, multi-source data** maps to the **Problem management** skill — initiating and monitoring actions to investigate patterns and trends — and to the **Ownership and topic** skill of proactively searching for potential problems before they occur.
- **Error checking at the structural level** maps to the **Asset and configuration management** skill (maintaining secure configuration and accurate information across areas) and the **Availability and capacity management** duty to ensure standards are correctly implemented — both are verification applied to systems rather than items.
- **Applied problem solving across the life cycle** maps to the **Service focus** skill: maintaining focus on the entire life cycle of the service means reasoning about how a decision at transition echoes through operation, improvement, and retirement.
- **Capacity and remediation logic** maps to the duty to identify capacity issues, implement required changes, and initiate remedial action — diagnosis plus intervention, which is problem solving with authority attached.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a release calendar**

A release calendar shows the last eight major releases and their outcomes: releases deployed in the first week of the month — four releases, zero rollbacks; releases deployed in the last week of the month — four releases, three rollbacks. All eight followed the same release process. A colleague concludes the process is unreliable. What is the more precise observation?

- A) The process fails 37.5% of the time, confirming unreliability.
- B) Failures cluster in end-of-month deployments; something that varies with month-end — freeze pressure, batch processing, staff leave patterns — deserves investigation before the process itself is blamed.
- C) The first-week releases were smaller, which explains everything.
- D) Eight releases is too few to observe anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The aggregate failure rate (3 of 8) is real but unilluminating; the structure inside it is stark — all three rollbacks sit in one calendar position. When outcomes cluster on a variable, the variable is the lead, and month-end is rich in candidate mechanisms: change freezes compressing schedules, month-end batch loads colliding with deployments, thinner staffing. Option A averages away the signal. Option C proposes a plausible confounder as though it were established — release size is worth checking, but the stem gives no size data. Option D overcorrects: eight data points cannot prove a cause, but a 0-versus-3 split across a clean partition is exactly enough to justify investigation, which is all B claims. This tests reading failure data for structure before rate, and sizing a conclusion to "worth investigating" rather than "proven" — the **Problem management** skill operating at the correct evidential temperature.

**Question 2 (easy) — Release policy deduction**

A release policy states: "High-impact changes require a full Change Advisory Board (CAB) review. Changes to services in their protected early-life period require CAB review regardless of impact classification. Low-impact changes to services outside early-life may use the standard pre-approved route." A team proposes a low-impact configuration change to a service that entered live service twelve days ago, with a protected early-life period of thirty days. Which route applies?

- A) Standard pre-approved route — the change is low-impact.
- B) CAB review — the service is inside its protected early-life period, which overrides impact classification.
- C) Either route, at the team's discretion.
- D) No change is permitted during early-life.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Parse the policy's precedence. The early-life rule is explicitly unconditional on impact ("regardless of impact classification"), and the pre-approved route is explicitly scoped to services "outside early-life". Twelve days into a thirty-day protected period, the service is inside; the low-impact classification, which would otherwise open the pre-approved route, is overridden. Option A applies the general rule while ignoring the specific one — and specific-over-general is the default precedence in well-drafted policy. Option C invents discretion the policy nowhere grants. Option D over-reads protection as prohibition. This tests reading policy for precedence, catching exactly the error of a candidate applying the rule that suits them rather than the rule that governs them.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a portfolio status summary**

A programme office produces a portfolio summary: "Twelve transitions in flight: seven green, three amber, one red. Green transitions require no lead review this cycle." What is wrong with the summary before examining any individual transition?

- A) Twelve transitions is too many to run concurrently.
- B) Seven plus three plus one is eleven — one transition is unaccounted for, and the unaccounted one could be anywhere from unstarted to failing.
- C) Green transitions should still be reviewed.
- D) Red should be listed before green.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reconcile the parts against the stated whole: 7 + 3 + 1 = 11, against "twelve in flight". One transition has fallen out of the classification entirely — and unclassified is more concerning than red, because red is at least being watched. The most dangerous items in any portfolio are the ones that vanish from summaries: a transition nobody is rating is a transition nobody is escalating. Option C is a defensible governance opinion but not an arithmetic defect; options A and D are style judgements. This tests the lead-level reflex of verifying a report's accounting — every item in exactly one category, categories summing to the total — before engaging with its content, because status processes report what is on their list, never what has slipped off it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across the portfolio**

Monday, 8:30. Four items compete for the first hours of the day:

1. A supplier on the largest transition has emailed overnight invoking a contract clause to delay their deliverable by three weeks; the commercial team asks for an impact analysis by Wednesday.
2. The go/no-go review for a mid-sized transition is at 14:00 today; a delegate prepared the recommendation, and it has not yet been read.
3. An operations manager reports that two transition managers gave their teams contradictory instructions about the shared release calendar, and the teams have stopped booking slots pending clarity.
4. A quarterly portfolio report to the digital board is due Friday.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 3, 2, 1, 4
- B) 2, 3, 1, 4
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4
- D) 3, 2, 4, 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 3 first: a contradiction between the candidate's own managers has operationally frozen a shared resource — teams have stopped booking slots — and it is likely resolvable in minutes with a single clarifying message; nothing else on the list is both this quick and this actively blocking other people. Item 2 next: a 14:00 governance gate at which a delegate's recommendation carries the lead's authority — reading it that morning is due diligence on a same-day hard deadline, and delegation without review at a decision gate is abdication, not empowerment. Item 1 has a Wednesday deadline for substantial analysis: it owns the afternoon and the following day. Item 4 is Friday. Option B reverses the first two, leaving the calendar frozen for hours; option C mistakes the biggest item for the most urgent; option D relegates the contract analysis behind a Friday report. This tests sequencing by unblock-others-fast first, then same-day gates, then near deadlines by size, then far deadlines.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction across supplier commitments**

Three suppliers feed one cutover. Supplier X commits to deliver their component "within ten working days of contract variation signature". Supplier Y commits to start integration "within five working days of receiving X's component". Supplier Z commits to complete security verification "within five working days of integration completing", and integration takes Y exactly ten working days. The variation was signed on day 0. Assuming every supplier hits the latest date its commitment permits, on which working day does security verification complete?

- A) Day 25
- B) Day 30
- C) Day 20
- D) Day 35

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the worst-case commitments. X delivers by day 10. Y starts integration by day 15 (10 + 5) and, taking ten days, completes by day 25. Z completes verification by day 30 (25 + 5). Option A forgets Y's five-day start window; option C compounds that omission; option D double-counts somewhere in the chain. Two observations travel with the arithmetic: "within N days of [event]" clauses chain multiplicatively across suppliers — three modest-sounding commitments quietly produce a thirty-day worst case; and planning against anything better than the worst case is planning on goodwill, since suppliers are entitled to their full windows. This tests chaining supplier commitments at their contractual limits — the sole responsibility for cross-supplier co-ordination makes this arithmetic specifically the role's own.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capacity logic with a remedial decision**

A shared integration platform serves four in-flight transitions. Its sustained utilisation is 71%; the platform team's standard requires remedial action when sustained utilisation exceeds 75%. The four transitions will each add load at go-live: transition 1 adds 3 points (going live in 4 weeks), transition 2 adds 4 points (6 weeks), transition 3 adds 2 points (8 weeks), transition 4 adds 5 points (12 weeks). Additional capacity takes ten weeks to provision. When does utilisation first breach the standard, and is the ten-week provisioning lead time compatible with waiting for the breach?

- A) Breach at week 6 (71+3+4 = 78); no — provisioning must start now, since waiting for the breach means capacity arrives at week 16, ten weeks late.
- B) Breach at week 12 when all transitions are live; yes — provision at week 2.
- C) Breach at week 4 (74); provisioning is already too late.
- D) No breach — each individual addition is small enough to keep utilisation under 75%.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Accumulate the load against the calendar. Week 4: 71 + 3 = 74 — under the standard (option C misreads 74 as a breach). Week 6: 74 + 4 = 78 — first breach. Weeks 8 and 12 worsen it (80, 85). Option D commits the incremental fallacy: each addition is individually modest, but utilisation is cumulative, which is precisely why shared platforms fail. Now the planning half: capacity takes ten weeks; the breach is at week 6; starting provisioning at the breach delivers capacity at week 16, ten weeks after it was needed. Compatibility requires starting now. This tests the **Availability and capacity management** duty in full: identify the issue by projection, initiate remedial action while the lead time still fits, and co-ordinate go-live sequencing with infrastructure reality.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Structural inconsistency across documents**

Three documents describe the same transition. The project plan says: "Cutover weekend: 14–15 November; operations acceptance sign-off due 7 November." The operations readiness checklist says: "Sign-off will be granted only after the final disaster-recovery test, scheduled 11 November." The supplier's statement of work says: "Disaster-recovery test environment available from 10 November." Which is the structural problem?

- A) The supplier's environment comes too late for the DR test.
- B) The DR test (11 November) postdates the sign-off deadline (7 November) that depends on it — the plan's milestone is impossible as scheduled, regardless of how well each team performs.
- C) Cutover should not be on a weekend.
- D) There is no problem — all dates precede the cutover.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build the dependency chain across the documents: sign-off requires the DR test (checklist); the DR test is on 11 November and cannot be earlier than the environment's 10 November availability (statement of work — so the test date itself is internally fine, eliminating A); but the plan demands sign-off by 7 November, four days before its own prerequisite can occur. No team is failing; the documents jointly specify an impossibility. Option D checks each date against cutover and misses that the inconsistency is between intermediate milestones. This error class — each document locally coherent, the set globally infeasible — is invisible to every individual document owner and is exactly why this role exists: the project reads its plan, operations reads its checklist, the supplier reads its statement of work, and only the person solely responsible for co-ordination reads all three against each other.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Sequencing three transitions through one freeze**

An organisation-wide change freeze runs weeks 9–10 (inclusive). Three transitions need go-live slots, at most one go-live per week: transition A needs two consecutive non-freeze weeks for go-live plus stabilisation, and its earliest readiness is week 6. Transition B needs one week, is ready from week 8, and must go live before week 12 for contractual reasons. Transition C needs one week, is ready from week 5, but reuses A's engineers, so it can go live no earlier than two clear weeks after A's go-live week (A live in week 6 means C in week 9 at the earliest). Freeze weeks cannot host any go-live or stabilisation week. What is the earliest feasible schedule?

- A) A: weeks 6–7; B: week 8; C: week 9
- B) A: weeks 6–7; B: week 8; C: week 11
- C) A: weeks 7–8; B: week 11; C: week 12
- D) A: weeks 6–7; B: week 11; C: week 8

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Place the largest footprint first. A needs two consecutive non-freeze weeks from week 6 onward: weeks 6–7 work. With A live in week 6, C's earliest permitted week is week 9 — but week 9 is frozen, and so is week 10, pushing C to week 11. B is ready from week 8 and must precede week 12; week 8 is free and beats week 11 — B takes week 8, C takes week 11. That is option B. Option A ignores the freeze (C in week 9); option D violates C's engineer constraint (week 8 is not two clear weeks after week 6); option C delays A a week for no reason and then pays for it twice. This tests placing the most constrained item first, propagating its consequences through the resource-sharing constraints, then slotting the flexible items into what remains — and testing the winning schedule against every constraint once assembled, since pairwise-feasible placements can still collide in the aggregate.

**Question 9 (hard) — Diagnosing a cross-supplier pattern**

Over six months, transitions involving supplier P show these features: deliverables arrive on the contractual due date 94% of the time; post-delivery defect rates run three times higher than other suppliers; P's staff turnover on the account has been 40% in the period; and P's project managers consistently decline offers of early informal design reviews, citing "efficiency". Which hypothesis best integrates all four observations, and what intervention does it suggest?

- A) P is contractually compliant and simply less skilled; replace P at the next procurement.
- B) P is optimising to the letter of its contract — hitting dates by compressing quality, with churned staff and no early feedback loops making defects structurally likely; the intervention is to renegotiate the incentive structure (quality gates and defect liability alongside date compliance) and make early reviews contractually expected rather than informally offered.
- C) P's turnover is the sole cause; require named-individual continuity clauses.
- D) Acceptance testing is too strict for P's deliverables.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Integrate, don't cherry-pick. The 94% date compliance alongside triple defect rates is the signature of letter-optimisation: when only dates are contractually sharp, dates absorb all the pressure and quality becomes the release valve. High turnover contributes (knowledge walks out) and declined early reviews complete the mechanism. Option B explains all four observations through one mechanism — incentives — and intervenes on the mechanism rather than its symptoms. Option A treats a structural behaviour as a capability deficit and reaches for the most expensive, slowest remedy first. Option C elevates one contributing factor to sole cause. Option D has no support in the stem — other suppliers pass the same testing. This tests the lead-level principle that suppliers behave as their contracts incentivise, and that the **Ownership and topic** duty to proactively search for problems extends to commercial structures.

**Question 10 (hard) — Life-cycle reasoning about a transition decision**

A programme proposes transitioning a new case system in two phases: phase 1 migrates active cases only (280,000 records, clean data, three months); phase 2 migrates historical cases (1.9 million records, poor data quality, timeline unknown) "after phase 1 stabilises". The legacy system costs £40,000 per month to run and cannot be decommissioned until phase 2 completes. The programme's benefits case claims legacy decommissioning savings from month 4. Which is the most important structural observation?

- A) Phase 1's three months is optimistic for 280,000 records.
- B) The benefits case books decommissioning savings from month 4, but decommissioning depends on phase 2, which has no committed timeline — the savings are tied to the phase the plan leaves unbounded, so the benefits case is structurally unsupported as written, and the legacy run-cost continues indefinitely at £40,000/month.
- C) Historical data should never be migrated.
- D) Two-phase migrations are inherently riskier than single-phase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Follow the money through the dependency chain. Savings require decommissioning; decommissioning requires phase 2 complete; phase 2 has "timeline unknown". The benefits case therefore claims, from month 4, savings whose enabling event is unscheduled — and every month of phase-2 drift costs £40,000 against those booked savings. This is not an argument against phasing (phased migration of clean-first data is often the right engineering call, so C and D overreach into generic assertions) — it is an argument that the plan and the benefits case describe different projects. Option A is a fair challenge but a judgement call the stem gives no evidence to settle. This tests the **Service focus** skill spanning the entire life cycle including decommissioning, and insisting that an unbounded later phase acquire a committed, resourced timeline before its benefits case is accepted.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reasoning about a decision under uncertainty**

A flagship cutover is Saturday. Wednesday evening, the supplier reports their final performance fix reduced response times in test — but their test environment has just been found to run on newer hardware than production, overstating all performance results by an unknown factor. A production-representative re-test needs four days that are not available. Postponement costs a six-week delay (the next available window) and significant credibility; proceeding risks a degraded service for 40,000 daily users. Monitoring can detect degradation within an hour of go-live, and rollback is tested and takes two hours. Which reasoning is soundest?

- A) Proceed — the fix probably works, and six weeks is too costly.
- B) Postpone — never go live with unquantified performance risk.
- C) Reframe the decision around the tested rollback: the effective worst case of proceeding is roughly three hours of degraded service before recovery completes, against a certain six-week delay for postponement; verify the rollback path and monitoring thresholds are genuinely ready, define the rollback trigger criteria in advance, brief the service owner on this as the actual risk trade — and let the go/no-go be made on that basis.
- D) Proceed, but only if the supplier accepts contractual liability for any degradation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A and B share a defect: they weigh "unquantified risk" against "certain cost" as though the risk's consequence were unbounded — but the scenario's own facts bound it. Tested rollback plus one-hour detection converts "the fix may not work in production" from an open-ended catastrophe into an approximately three-hour worst case, which is a comparable, priceable alternative to a six-week delay. That reframing — from "how likely is failure?" to "how expensive is failure, given the recovery position?" — is the soundest available reasoning, and it is why transition disciplines invest in rollback: reversibility is what buys decision freedom under uncertainty. Note C's conditions, which do the real work: verify the rollback and monitoring are genuinely ready, pre-commit the trigger criteria, and brief the decision owner on the true trade. Option D manages the department's money, not its users' Saturday. This tests the principle that when uncertainty cannot be shrunk in the time available, the cost of being wrong should be shrunk instead — and the decision made explicitly about that cost.

**Question 12 (hard) — Portfolio triage under a sudden constraint**

A central directive lands: for the next eight weeks, the operations team can support at most one go-live per fortnight (four slots total) due to a major infrastructure programme. Six transitions expect go-live in the window: (i) a statutory service with a legally mandated start date in week 3; (ii) a replacement for a system whose supplier support expires in week 6; (iii) a high-profile ministerial commitment with "expectation" of week 4; (iv) an internal efficiency service saving £25,000/month, ready now; (v) a service whose delay pushes a dependent local-authority programme into its own freeze, costing that programme three months; (vi) a well-progressed service with flexible timing. Which allocation principle produces the most defensible schedule?

- A) First-ready, first-scheduled: reward the teams that hit their dates.
- B) Seniority of sponsor: the ministerial commitment takes slot one.
- C) Constraint-type triage: hard external constraints first in deadline order — statutory (week 3), supplier-support expiry (week 6), then the dependency that cascades three months of cost onto another organisation (week 5 area); the ministerial expectation is negotiated against evidence, and the efficiency service and flexible service queue behind, with the displaced go-lives given committed post-window slots.
- D) Equal shares: give each transition two-thirds of a slot by splitting weekends.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Four slots, six claimants: the allocation principle matters more than any single allocation, because it is the principle that must be defended to five disappointed sponsors. Option C triages by constraint type: legal mandates and support expiries are hard walls with dates; the cross-organisational cascade is next because its cost is large, external, and borne by a partner who did nothing wrong; a ministerial "expectation" is a strong preference from a powerful stakeholder — real, but negotiable with evidence in a way statute is not. The efficiency saving and the flexible service queue with committed follow-on slots, which converts "displaced" from grievance into schedule. Option A rewards readiness while ignoring consequence. Option B converts hierarchy into priority, which fails precisely when the statutory deadline and the ministerial preference collide. Option D is arithmetic satire, though its instinct survives in C's committed follow-on slots. This tests the reusable principle that under sudden scarcity, the triage principle is published before the schedule, ordered by hardness of constraint then size and externality of consequence, with every displacement converted into a commitment.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads failure data for structure before rate** — partitioning by time, place, team, or supplier before averaging.
- **Watch for whether a candidate applies policy by precedence** — specific over general, unconditional over conditional — rather than by convenience.
- **Note whether a candidate verifies a report's accounting** before engaging with its content.
- **Note whether a candidate computes worst-case chains** for multi-party commitments, rather than expected-case ones.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and consider offering ungraded practice items first.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that averages away a structural signal** — a stable rate and a sharp cluster can be the same data.
- **Crediting an answer that applies the convenient rule rather than the governing one.**
- **Missing when a candidate trusts a report whose parts don't sum to its stated total.**
- **Accepting an answer that plans on suppliers' goodwill** rather than their contractual limits.
- **Missing when a candidate falls for the incremental fallacy on shared resources** — each addition individually reasonable, the aggregate not checked.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures the accuracy and confidence with which a candidate handles quantitative information in realistic working contexts. The mathematical toolkit is deliberately modest — percentages, ratios, averages, rates, compound change, and disciplined table-and-chart reading — because the difficulty in professional numeracy is rarely the operations; it is choosing the right operation, the right base, and the right level of scepticism for the numbers in front of you.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 multiple-choice questions built around small data sets. Calculators are almost universally permitted; senior-role variants are normed against senior comparison groups and lean toward multi-step questions where the challenge is structuring the calculation rather than performing it.

At lead level, the case for numeric assessment is straightforward: the role presides over numbers produced by other people. Suppliers present pass rates; programmes present benefits projections; platform teams present capacity forecasts; managers present portfolio dashboards. Every one of those numbers arrives with an agenda attached — usually benign, occasionally not, always shaped by what its producer wanted to show. The role's value is the ability to recompute the headline from the raw counts, spot the averaged-away cluster, notice the denominator that changed between two slides, and project the trend the deck stopped one quarter short of.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Capacity and utilisation arithmetic** maps directly to the **Availability and capacity management** duties: identifying capacity issues means projecting utilisation against triggers, and initiating remedial action means computing whether provisioning lead times fit inside breach timelines.
- **Multi-source aggregation** maps to sole responsibility for co-ordination across projects, suppliers, and service teams: portfolio truth is assembled from unequal-sized parts, and correct aggregation (counts before percentages) is what makes dashboards honest.
- **Trend analysis and projection** maps to the **Problem management** skill — investigating patterns and trends quantitatively — and the **Ownership and topic** duty to search proactively for problems before they occur: a projected breach is a found problem.
- **Cost and benefits arithmetic** maps to the **Service focus** skill across the entire life cycle: run-costs, decommissioning savings, and delay costs are the currency in which life-cycle decisions are argued, and to the duty to communicate service benefits credibly.
- **Change and release metrics** map to the **Change management** skill: managing high-impact change includes reading failure rates, and ensuring release policies are applied includes measuring whether they are working.
- **User-impact quantification** maps to the **User focus** skill: representing users internally is most effective when their experience is expressed in numbers decision-makers cannot wave away.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Recomputing a portfolio headline**

A dashboard claims "91% of portfolio milestones met this quarter". The underlying data: programme A met 34 of 36 milestones; programme B met 22 of 26; programme C met 17 of 18. Is the headline accurate?

- A) Yes — the rate is 91.25%
- B) Yes, almost exactly — the rate is 91.3%
- C) No — the rate is 87.5%
- D) No — the rate is 94%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combine counts, then divide: met = 34 + 22 + 17 = 73; total = 36 + 26 + 18 = 80; 73 ÷ 80 = 0.9125 = 91.25%, which rounds to 91.3% — the headline's "91%" is fair. Confirming a correct headline matters as much as catching a false one: a candidate's scepticism should be calibrated, not performative. Note the method discipline: the per-programme rates averaged naively give 91.1% — coincidentally close here because the groups are similar sizes, but the counts-first method is the one that stays correct when they are not. This tests recomputing an aggregate from raw counts and recognising when a headline survives scrutiny.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cost of delay**

A transition's delay keeps a legacy system running at £38,000 per month and defers efficiency savings of £22,000 per month. What is the total cost of a five-month delay?

- A) £190,000
- B) £110,000
- C) £300,000
- D) £60,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The monthly cost of delay is both components: £38,000 continuing run-cost + £22,000 deferred savings = £60,000 per month. Over five months: £60,000 × 5 = £300,000. Option A prices only the legacy run-cost; option B only the lost savings; option D is one month, not five. The run-cost is cash out of the door and the deferred saving is cash that fails to arrive, and both are real relative to the on-time counterfactual. This tests combining a cost-of-delay figure from its components — one of the strongest single numbers in portfolio negotiation.

**Question 3 (easy) — Weighted portfolio risk score**

A portfolio risk model weights transitions by user base: transition X (500,000 users) has risk score 4; transition Y (250,000 users) has risk score 8; transition Z (250,000 users) has risk score 2. Using user-base weights, what is the portfolio's weighted average risk score?

- A) 4.67
- B) 4.5
- C) 5.0
- D) 3.5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weights: X = 500k of 1,000k total = 0.5; Y = 0.25; Z = 0.25. Weighted average = (4 × 0.5) + (8 × 0.25) + (2 × 0.25) = 2 + 2 + 0.5 = 4.5. Option A is the unweighted mean, which treats a half-million-user transition as equal to a quarter-million-user one. Sense-check: the answer must lie between the extremes (2 and 8), pulled toward the heaviest-weighted score (X's 4) — 4.5 fits. This tests weighted-average calculation and appreciating that weighting by user base encodes a judgement — risk to more people counting for more — that is the **User focus** skill built into the measurement itself.

**Question 4 (easy) — Release failure rate against a policy threshold**

A release policy triggers a process review if the rolling change failure rate exceeds 8%. The last 150 changes include 13 failures. Is the review triggered?

- A) No — the rate is 7.8%
- B) Yes — the rate is about 8.7%
- C) No — the rate is exactly 8%
- D) Yes — the rate is 13%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 13 ÷ 150 = 0.0867 ≈ 8.7%, which exceeds 8% — the review is triggered. A quick route: 8% of 150 = 12, so the threshold sits at 12 failures; 13 breaches it by one. Option D mistakes the count for the rate; options A and C require arithmetic errors. This tests converting a percentage threshold into a concrete count — a candidate who computes the tripwire (11 failures is one incident from a mandated review) demonstrates management-grade numeracy, not just correct arithmetic.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Compound growth against a capacity ceiling**

A platform processes 2.0 million transactions monthly, growing 8% per month compound. Its tested ceiling is 2.9 million transactions monthly. After how many complete months does demand first exceed the ceiling?

- A) 4 months
- B) 5 months
- C) 6 months
- D) 9 months

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compound forward: month 1: 2.0 × 1.08 = 2.16m; month 2: 2.333m; month 3: 2.519m; month 4: 2.721m; month 5: 2.939m — first breach of 2.9m. Option A stops a month early; option C is the simple-growth answer (adding 0.16m/month flat, breaching during month 6). The compound-versus-simple gap is small at month two and decisive by month five, which is why demand forecasts understate late-period load when they assume linearity. This tests compound projection, and — the number that matters for a remedial-action duty — computing backward from the breach to the last responsible decision moment given a provisioning lead time.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Aggregating supplier quality across contracts**

Supplier M delivered 12 releases with a 25% defect-escape rate on a small contract, and 4 releases with a 5% rate on the flagship contract. Supplier N delivered 8 releases at 15% across all work. Counting each release equally, which supplier has the better (lower) overall defect-escape rate, and by how much?

- A) N, by 5 percentage points
- B) M, by 5 percentage points
- C) They are equal at 15%
- D) N, by 2 percentage points

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weight M's rates by releases: (12 × 25% + 4 × 5%) ÷ 16 = (300 + 20) ÷ 16 = 320 ÷ 16 = 20%. N's rate is 15% across its 8 releases. So N is better by 5 percentage points — option A. The trap is option C: averaging M's two contract-level rates (25% and 5%) gives 15%, apparently equal to N — but that treats a 12-release contract and a 4-release contract as equal evidence. M's small contract dominates its true average because it carries three-quarters of M's releases. This tests recognising that the weighting choice depends on the question being asked, and that a supplier's account manager is likely to prefer the weighting that flatters them.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Availability across a chain of dependent services**

A citizen journey traverses three services in sequence; the journey succeeds only if all three are up. Each service independently achieves 99.0% availability. What is the availability of the end-to-end journey, and what does it mean at 100,000 journeys per month?

- A) 99.0% — about 1,000 failed journeys
- B) 97.0% — about 3,000 failed journeys
- C) 98.0% — about 2,000 failed journeys
- D) 99.7% — about 300 failed journeys

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chained availability multiplies: 0.99 × 0.99 × 0.99 = 0.9703 ≈ 97.0%. Failure rate ≈ 3%, so roughly 3,000 of 100,000 monthly journeys fail. Option A commits the commonest availability error — assuming the chain inherits the components' figure; option C adds the two extra services' shortfalls but misses that all three contribute; option D bears no relation to the arithmetic. This tests recognising that three services each meeting a respectable 99.0% SLA can jointly deliver an end-to-end experience that would breach a 98% journey-level target — the **Service focus** skill in numeric form, since per-service metrics structurally overstate what users experience.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Benefits realisation shortfall**

A transition's benefits case promised £480,000 of annual savings: £300,000 from decommissioning, £120,000 from reduced manual processing, £60,000 from lower incident volumes. One year on: decommissioning delivered in full; manual-processing savings achieved 65%; incident-related savings achieved 40%. What percentage of the promised total was realised?

- A) 68%
- B) 84%
- C) 79%
- D) 88%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each stream: decommissioning £300,000 (100%); manual processing £120,000 × 0.65 = £78,000; incidents £60,000 × 0.40 = £24,000. Total realised: 300 + 78 + 24 = £402,000. Against the promise: 402 ÷ 480 = 0.8375 ≈ 84%. Option A averages the three percentages, ignoring that the fully delivered stream is also the largest — the unweighted average understates performance here by sixteen points. This tests weighted benefits arithmetic, and a strong candidate's answer notes the pattern the composition reveals: hard-edged savings deliver fully while behaviour-dependent savings underperform — worth scrutinising hardest at business-case stage.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reading a multi-series trend table**

| Month | Changes implemented | Emergency changes | Failed changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| April | 80 | 6 | 5 |
| May | 88 | 9 | 6 |
| June | 96 | 14 | 7 |
| July | 104 | 22 | 8 |

Which statement best captures the most decision-relevant trend?

- A) Failure rates are stable at 6–8%, so the change process is healthy.
- B) Total change volume is growing 10% monthly, requiring more CAB capacity.
- C) Emergency changes are growing far faster than volume — from 7.5% to 21% of all changes in four months — signalling that work is increasingly bypassing normal planning; the failure rate's stability (6.3%, 6.8%, 7.3%, 7.7% — gently rising) is reassurance the emergency trend will eventually erode.
- D) All three series are rising, which is expected in a growing organisation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Normalise each series against volume before judging. Failures: 5/80 = 6.3%, 6/88 = 6.8%, 7/96 = 7.3%, 8/104 = 7.7% — gently rising, not yet alarming. Emergencies: 6/80 = 7.5%, 9/88 = 10.2%, 14/96 = 14.6%, 22/104 = 21.2% — near-tripling as a share in four months. That is the decision-relevant signal: emergency routes exist for genuine urgency, and a fifth of all changes travelling through them means planning is failing upstream. Option A reads only the series that looks fine; option D commits the growth-excuses-everything fallacy — proportional growth would hold the emergency share at 7.5%. This tests decomposing an aggregate that looks healthy into shares that do not, and recognising that a stable failure rate can be partly borrowed against tomorrow's, since emergency changes fail more often once volume normalises.

**Question 10 (hard) — The denominator shift**

A supplier's two consecutive monthly reports state: March: "94% of incidents resolved within SLA (of 380 incidents)". April: "96% of incidents resolved within SLA (of 310 incidents, excluding the 45 incidents reclassified as service requests under the new categorisation agreed with the service desk)". The report does not say how the 45 reclassified items would have performed against SLA under March rules. What can honestly be concluded about April's performance on a March-comparable basis?

- A) 96% — the reclassification was agreed, so April's figure stands.
- B) Between 83.9% (if all 45 would have breached) and 96.6% (if none would have), so the reported improvement from 94% is unverifiable without knowing the reclassified items' outcomes — the comparison basis changed mid-series.
- C) 90% exactly.
- D) 85.5% exactly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** April on its own terms: 96% of 310 = 297.6 ≈ 298 resolved within SLA. On a March-comparable basis the denominator is 310 + 45 = 355, and the numerator depends on how the 45 would have fared: if all would have breached, 298 ÷ 355 ≈ 83.9%; if all would have met SLA, 343 ÷ 355 ≈ 96.6%. The honest conclusion is that April's "improvement" over March's 94% cannot be verified from the report: the comparison spans a definitional change, and the reclassified population's outcomes are precisely the data the new report no longer shows. Options C and D manufacture false precision; option A accepts that an agreed reclassification also legitimises a broken time series, which it does not. This tests recognising a mid-series denominator shift and demanding the metric restated on both bases for the transition period.

**Question 11 (hard) — Provisioning economics under uncertainty**

Storage for a growing service can be expanded now for £90,000, or expanded in an emergency later for £150,000 plus an expected £60,000 of incident costs if growth continues and capacity is breached. Analysts estimate a 70% probability that growth continues at the current rate (breach in roughly five months) and 30% that a planned archiving feature (due in three months) flattens growth and no breach occurs. Comparing expected costs, what is the case for expanding now versus waiting?

- A) Expand now: £90,000 certain beats the alternative's expected cost of £147,000 (0.7 × £210,000).
- B) Wait: there is a 30% chance of spending nothing, and £90,000 is certain if action is taken now.
- C) The options are equal in expectation.
- D) Expand now, but only because emergencies are always worse.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Price both branches. Expand now: £90,000, certain. Wait: with probability 0.7, growth continues and the cost is £150,000 + £60,000 = £210,000; with probability 0.3, the archiving feature lands and the cost is £0. Expected cost of waiting: 0.7 × 210,000 + 0.3 × 0 = £147,000 — against £90,000 for acting now, a £57,000 expected saving for early action. Option B correctly describes the branch probabilities and then reasons from hope: a 30% chance of zero does not outweigh a 70% chance of £210,000. Option D reaches the right act by superstition — always-expand is wrong whenever the probabilities reverse. This tests pricing both branches of a wait-versus-act decision, and a strong candidate's answer also notes that expected-value reasoning assumes the bad branch is absorbable — a judgement, not an equation.

**Question 12 (hard) — The portfolio dashboard that disagrees with itself**

A monthly dashboard reports: "Portfolio SLA attainment: 92.4%. Component services: service 1: 96% (12,000 measured interactions); service 2: 89% (30,000); service 3: 95% (8,000)." Is the portfolio figure consistent with its components?

- A) Yes — 92.4% is the correct weighted result.
- B) No — the weighted figure is about 91.6%; the reported 92.4% overstates attainment by 0.8 points, and the discrepancy should be queried before the dashboard circulates.
- C) No — the correct figure is the simple average, 93.3%.
- D) It cannot be checked without the raw data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weight by interactions: service 1: 0.96 × 12,000 = 11,520; service 2: 0.89 × 30,000 = 26,700; service 3: 0.95 × 8,000 = 7,600. Total within SLA: 11,520 + 26,700 + 7,600 = 45,820, of 50,000 interactions: 45,820 ÷ 50,000 = 0.9164 ≈ 91.6%. The dashboard says 92.4% — a 0.8-point overstatement, most plausibly from under-weighting service 2, the largest and worst-performing component. Option C endorses the simple average, which is wrong in principle at these unequal volumes. Option D is defeatist: the components as published are sufficient to check the aggregate's consistency. This tests verifying an aggregate from its own published components — a check that takes under a minute and one a lead-level candidate should treat as routine before circulating a figure upward.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate recomputes an aggregate from its published components** rather than accepting it.
- **Watch for whether a candidate normalises a series against volume** before judging a trend as stable or healthy.
- **Note whether a candidate spots a mid-series denominator or definitional shift** and demands comparability.
- **Note whether a candidate prices both branches of a wait-versus-act decision** rather than reasoning from the favourable branch alone.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an average of percentages across unequal groups.**
- **Missing when a candidate reads a stable aggregate as a healthy system** without decomposing it into shares.
- **Crediting an answer that accepts a mid-series redefinition at face value.**
- **Accepting a simple-growth projection for a phenomenon that compounds.**
- **Missing when a candidate treats a chain's component figures as its overall figure** — three good components can still fail a chained target.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures precision in reading, interpretation, and inference from written material. At lead level, the material mirrors what crosses this role's desk: contract schedules and their carefully engineered caveats, policy documents with interacting clauses, board papers whose framing does quiet work on their readers, supplier correspondence written with one eye on future disputes, and the assurance reports on which recommendations to senior decision-makers rest.

The core format remains true / false / cannot say — judging statements as stated-or-entailed, contradicted, or unaddressed by a passage — supplemented by best-summary, logical-completion, and clear-writing questions. A typical test runs 15 to 25 minutes over 20 to 30 questions, normed for senior roles against senior comparison groups. The differentiating skill at this level is not vocabulary but discipline under plausibility: senior readers carry deep contextual knowledge, and the test rewards those who can hold that knowledge apart from what a given document actually establishes.

This role operates at the altitude where words are commitments. A supplier's "we anticipate", a policy's "should" versus "must", a board paper's "on track" defined nowhere — each is a load-bearing ambiguity, and the lead who reads past it inherits the dispute it was built to create. Equally, this role is a principal producer of consequential text: transition strategies, portfolio recommendations, escalation letters to supplier executives, and the acceptance frameworks other people are held to. There is also a leadership layer worth naming: reading errors at this level propagate, because the correction mechanisms above a lead thin out.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise comprehension of commitments** maps to sole responsibility for co-ordination across suppliers and projects: cross-supplier plans are built from contractual language, and the plan is only as sound as the reading of each clause.
- **True / false / cannot say discipline** maps to the **Ownership and topic** skill — proactively searching for potential problems means cataloguing precisely what is established, what is contradicted, and what remains unknown in every assurance artefact.
- **Reading policy with interacting clauses** maps to the **Change management** duty to ensure release policies and procedures are applied: application begins with correct parsing, especially of precedence and exception structure.
- **Inference control under expertise** maps to the **Problem management** skill: pattern investigation generates hypotheses, and senior credibility depends on labelling them as hypotheses until documents or data confirm them.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to the **Service focus** and **User focus** skills: communicating service benefits and representing users internally are exercises in saying exactly what is meant, and to the **Community collaboration** skill of adapting feedback so it is effective and lasting.
- **Reading framing critically** maps to board-facing duties: recognising what a paper's structure emphasises, omits, or predecides is the reading skill that keeps governance honest.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

For Questions 1 to 5, the candidate reads this extract from a supplier contract schedule.

> "The Supplier shall deliver the Transition Deliverables by the milestone dates in Annex 4, provided that the Authority has fulfilled its dependencies in Annex 5 by their respective due dates. Where an Authority dependency is delayed, affected milestone dates shall be extended day-for-day, and the Supplier shall notify the Authority of the affected milestones within five working days of the dependency's original due date. Failure to notify within this period shall constitute waiver of the extension for the affected milestones. Liquidated damages apply to Supplier delay only where the milestone date, as extended under this clause, is missed."

**Question 1 (easy) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "If the Authority delays a dependency, affected milestone dates are extended day-for-day."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly: "Where an Authority dependency is delayed, affected milestone dates shall be extended day-for-day." The statement tracks the clause without addition or omission — true. Even at the easy tier, note what precise contract reading involves: the extension is automatic in form but, as the next sentence reveals, conditional in practice on the supplier's timely notification. This tests locating a directly stated mechanism while noticing that a statement quoting one sentence of a multi-sentence mechanism can be true in isolation and misleading in use.

**Question 2 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "A supplier who notifies the Authority of affected milestones seven working days after a dependency's original due date retains the day-for-day extension for those milestones."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The clause requires notification "within five working days of the dependency's original due date", and failure to notify within that period "shall constitute waiver of the extension". Seven working days exceeds five; the extension is waived; the statement asserting retention is contradicted — false. Notice the clause's design: it converts the Authority's delay into the Supplier's procedural burden, and a supplier who misses the notification window loses protection for a delay that was not their fault. This tests tracking a notification deadline as carefully as a delivery deadline — in disputes, the former is frequently decisive.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "Liquidated damages apply automatically whenever the Supplier misses an Annex 4 milestone date."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence scopes liquidated damages to misses of "the milestone date, as extended under this clause" — the original Annex 4 date is not the test where an extension has arisen. A supplier can miss the Annex 4 date yet be inside the extended date, attracting no damages; "automatically whenever" is therefore contradicted. The phrase "as extended under this clause" is four words doing heavy commercial work. This tests catching a modifying phrase that changes what a claim actually tests, and recognising that a candidate who reads past it would produce exactly the wrong escalation.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "Annex 5 lists dependencies owed by the Supplier to the Authority."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage describes Annex 5 dependencies as ones "the Authority has fulfilled" — they are owed by the Authority, not to it by the Supplier. The statement reverses the direction of obligation, contradicting the passage — false, not cannot say. Direction-of-obligation errors are among the most consequential misreadings in multi-party transition work: dependency registers, RACI charts, and contract annexes all encode who owes whom, and a reversed reading assigns risk to the wrong balance sheet. This tests verifying the direction explicitly when a statement names two parties and a relationship.

**Question 5 (hard) — Integrating the clause**

The Authority delivers a dependency six working days late. The Supplier notifies affected milestones four working days after the dependency's original due date, and subsequently delivers eight days after the original milestone date. Applying the clause, which is correct?

- A) The Supplier is liable for liquidated damages for eight days of delay.
- B) The Supplier is liable for liquidated damages for two days of delay — the milestone was validly extended six days, and delivery exceeded the extended date by two.
- C) The Supplier has no liability — the Authority caused the original delay.
- D) The extension was waived because notification followed the dependency's due date.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Assemble the mechanism step by step. The dependency was six days late, entitling day-for-day extension: six days. Notification came four working days after the original due date — within the five-day window, so the extension is preserved (option D misreads the window as expiring at the due date itself; the clause allows five working days after it). The milestone therefore moves six days; delivery at eight days late exceeds the extended date by two; damages apply to those two days — option B. Option A ignores the valid extension; option C over-extends the Authority's culpability. This tests operating a clause's full machinery in sequence — the pre-escalation analysis this role owes before any damages conversation with a supplier executive.

For Questions 6 to 9, the candidate reads this extract from a board paper prepared by a programme director.

> "The transition remains on track for the December milestone. Supplier performance has stabilised following the interventions reported previously, with October deliverables received on schedule. The residual risk profile is consistent with this stage of comparable programmes. The independent assurance review commissioned in September has reported; its recommendations are being incorporated into the delivery plan and will be reflected in the next iteration. The board is asked to note progress and approve the release of the next funding tranche."

**Question 6 (easy) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "October deliverables were received on schedule."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated verbatim: "October deliverables received on schedule". This tests locating a direct statement — and, at a senior level, the second glance that follows it: "received" is a delivery event, not an acceptance event. The paper does not say the October deliverables passed review, met quality standards, or were accepted; deliverables can be received on schedule and rejected a week later. The statement as written claims only receipt, so it is true, but a strong candidate notes what the paper did not say as much as what it did.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "The independent assurance review found no significant issues."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The paper says the review "has reported" and that "its recommendations are being incorporated" — nothing about what the review found, how severe its findings were, or how many recommendations it made. A review with minor suggestions and a review with red-rated findings would both fit the sentence as drafted. The finding's absence is not evidence of absence: cannot say. This tests the governance instinct this role requires — "recommendations are being incorporated" is a common formula for acknowledging a review without disclosing it, and a strong candidate's answer notes the correct response is neither suspicion nor acceptance but the question the drafting has made necessary.

**Question 8 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "The board has already released the funding tranche that the paper discusses."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The paper "asks" the board to "approve the release of the next funding tranche" — a request for a future decision. If the tranche were already released, there would be nothing to approve; the request's existence contradicts the statement — false. Worth noticing en route: the paper couples "note progress" and "approve funding" into a single motion, a framing that invites approval as though it followed automatically from noting. This tests reading a governance paper's requested decision as drafted, and recognising that coupling two items is itself a choice someone made.

**Question 9 (hard) — What the paper establishes**

Judged strictly on the passage, which statement most accurately characterises the support the paper provides for releasing the funding tranche?

- A) Strong — on-track status, stabilised supplier, normal risk, and a completed assurance review all point to release.
- B) The paper asserts on-track status and offers one verifiable fact (October receipts) plus three unverifiable characterisations (stabilised performance, "consistent" risk profile, recommendations "being incorporated") — and it withholds the assurance findings that bear most directly on the funding decision; the support as documented is thin, whatever the underlying reality.
- C) None — the paper is misleading and the programme is in trouble.
- D) The support is irrelevant since funding decisions are financial, not delivery, matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Audit the paper's evidential texture claim by claim. "On track" — asserted, criteria undisclosed. "Stabilised following interventions" — a characterisation whose baseline and measure are not given. "Consistent with comparable programmes" — comparison set unnamed, so unverifiable as offered. "Recommendations being incorporated" — activity claimed, content withheld. Against these, one concrete verifiable: October deliverables received on schedule. Option B counts precisely this — one fact, three characterisations, one material omission — and draws the calibrated conclusion that the documented support is thin, which is a statement about the paper, not the programme. Option C converts thin documentation into an accusation the passage cannot support. Option D is a category error: tranche approval is precisely a delivery-confidence decision. This tests evaluating what a document establishes, as distinct from what it asserts and from what is actually true — three layers senior governance reading must hold apart.

For Questions 10 to 12, the candidate reads this extract from a government service standard guidance page.

> "Service teams must understand the difference between user needs and user wants. A need is something a user must be able to do to achieve their goal — for example, prove their identity to access a service. A want is a preference about how that happens. Teams should prioritise meeting needs for all users, including those with access needs or low digital confidence, over optimising preferences for confident users. Research with a representative range of users, conducted throughout the service's life cycle rather than only at the start, is the primary mechanism for distinguishing needs from wants. Metrics alone cannot make this distinction, because usage data shows what users do within the options offered, not what they were trying to achieve."

**Question 10 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "According to the passage, usage metrics can establish what users were trying to achieve."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states the opposite explicitly: "usage data shows what users do within the options offered, not what they were trying to achieve." The statement is contradicted — false. This tests reproducing the passage's argument accurately, which is load-bearing for the **User focus** skill: metrics are bounded by the option space a service offers, so a service can score excellent task-completion on tasks that miss the user's actual goal. This is the analytical basis for the passage's claim that research, not metrics alone, distinguishes needs from wants.

**Question 11 (moderate) — Logical completion**

According to the passage, a team that optimises the experience of digitally confident users while some users with access needs cannot complete the service has:

- A) Correctly prioritised the majority.
- B) Inverted the passage's stated priority — preferences of confident users have been served ahead of the needs of all users, which the passage explicitly subordinates them to.
- C) Followed the guidance, provided metrics show high satisfaction.
- D) Made a decision the passage does not address.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage's priority rule is explicit: "prioritise meeting needs for all users, including those with access needs or low digital confidence, over optimising preferences for confident users." Users who cannot complete the service have unmet needs; optimising confident users' experience is preference-optimisation; serving the latter while the former persists is precisely the inversion option B names. Option A imports a majoritarian principle the passage rejects. Option C compounds the error with the metrics fallacy question 10 dismantled. Option D is false: this is the passage's central case. This tests applying a stated rule to a concrete configuration — the same reasoning form internal user advocacy takes.

**Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement at leadership level**

Presented with the task of writing to a supplier's account director after their transition team missed a second consecutive readiness milestone, with the relationship still needed for two more years, which opening paragraph is most effective?

- A) "It has come to our attention that certain milestones may not have been achieved in accordance with previously agreed timescales, which is potentially a matter of some concern at this juncture."
- B) "Your team's repeated failures demonstrate a fundamental lack of competence and commitment that we are no longer prepared to tolerate."
- C) "The readiness milestones of 14 June and 12 July were both missed, the second by nine working days. I want to resolve the underlying cause with you before the September milestone, which protects the cutover date we both need. Please come to Thursday's review able to discuss what changed on your side since May, when delivery was consistently on time, and what you need from us to restore that position."
- D) "Further to previous correspondence and pursuant to the terms of the agreement between our organisations, we hereby formally register dissatisfaction with recent delivery performance and reserve all contractual rights and remedies available to us."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C does five things the situation demands: it states the facts with dates and magnitudes; it declares intent (resolve, not punish) anchored to a shared stake; it references the positive baseline (on time until May), converting the message from character judgement to change investigation; it assigns a concrete next step with a date; and it opens the door to bilateral causes. Option A is fog — writing designed to be deniable, which provides no facts to act on. Option B converts a delivery problem into a relationship injury two years before it can be afforded. Option D performs firmness legalistically without stating a single fact or asking for anything. This tests the **Community collaboration** skill of adapting feedback so it is effective and lasting, exercised in writing at commercial altitude: firm on facts, purposeful about the future, and impossible to dismiss as either vague or vindictive.

- **Score for whether a candidate operates a clause's full machinery** rather than stopping at the first relevant sentence.
- **Watch for whether a candidate audits a board paper's evidential texture** — sorting each claim as verifiable fact, characterisation, or omission.
- **Note whether a candidate tracks a verb for its exact event type** — received is not accepted; reported is not disclosed.
- **Note whether a candidate verifies direction of obligation** when two parties share a sentence.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

- **Rewarding an answer that credits a mechanism from a single sentence** rather than reading to the end of a multi-sentence clause.
- **Missing when a candidate hears more than a verb actually said** — "received on schedule" is not "accepted".
- **Crediting an answer that treats absence of findings as absence of issues.**
- **Missing when a candidate confuses thin documentation with bad reality**, or accepts framing as evidence.
- **Accepting an answer that reverses an obligation in multi-party language.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to identify the most effective — and often least effective — courses of action. At senior levels it is the most direct assessment of the four, because it samples the substance of leadership: how a candidate uses authority, handles conflict between legitimate interests, escalates, develops and protects their people, and whether the interests of users and the integrity of processes survive sustained organisational pressure.

Formats include most-effective selection, most-and-least pairs, full ranking, and independent rating of each response. Timing is generous — typically 20 to 30 minutes for 15 to 20 scenarios — and scoring keys are developed with experienced senior practitioners and occupational psychologists against behaviour frameworks. For GDAD leadership roles, expect the key to reward evidence-based decision-making, transparent escalation, genuine empowerment of teams, proportionate intervention, commercial judgement with suppliers, and visible championing of user outcomes and inclusion.

For a lead service transition manager, the scenarios have a distinctive shape. The role holds sole responsibility for co-ordination across projects, suppliers, and service teams — so the dilemmas are rarely "what should be done?" and usually "what should be done by whom, at what level, in what order, and at what cost to which relationship?" The role manages managers, so people scenarios concern development, delegation, and repair rather than task supervision. The role faces suppliers as a commercial counterpart, so firmness and relationship preservation must coexist. And the role operates in governance structures where its recommendations acquire institutional force — so honesty under pressure is tested not as private virtue but as public act.

A calibration note: senior SJT keys penalise two patterns with special consistency — the heroic override (solving problems personally that systems and people should solve, which scores as failure to lead) and the procedural hide (following process into outcomes known to be wrong, which scores as failure to own). The consistently rewarded pattern sits between: design the system, empower the people, intervene at the right level, and own the outcome regardless.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Portfolio and escalation scenarios** map to sole responsibility for cross-project, cross-supplier co-ordination and the **Ownership and topic** skill: accountability for issues, proactive problem-searching, and excellent user outcomes as the test of everything.
- **People and team scenarios** map to the **Community collaboration** skill: active networking, feedback adapted to be effective and lasting, diagnosing and rectifying team-dynamic problems, and using Agile health checks to surface issues and stimulate the right responses.
- **Supplier scenarios** map to the **Change management** skill (managing high-impact, complex change) exercised commercially: holding release policy against contractual pressure and incentive games.
- **Capacity and risk scenarios** map to the **Availability and capacity management** duties: initiating remedial action on the role's own authority, at the right moment, with defensible arithmetic.
- **Life-cycle and benefits scenarios** map to the **Service focus** skill: keeping the entire life cycle in view, communicating service benefits credibly, and championing service focus within the team and beyond.
- **User advocacy scenarios** map to the **User focus** skill: collaborating with user researchers, representing users internally, distinguishing needs from desires, and championing research when it is inconvenient.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — A manager's transition is in trouble**

One of the service transition managers reporting to the candidate is running a mid-sized transition that is visibly slipping: two readiness milestones missed, and the operations team is complaining directly to the candidate. The manager is capable but newly promoted, and has not raised any concerns. What is the most effective response?

- A) Take over the transition personally — it is slipping, and the candidate is accountable.
- B) Wait for the manager to ask for help — intervening uninvited undermines their development.
- C) Meet the manager promptly: share what is being seen and heard, ask for their honest assessment, agree what support or intervention the situation needs, and establish why the slippage was not surfacing upward — fixing that channel matters as much as the milestones.
- D) Ask the operations team to raise their complaints with the manager instead.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario contains two problems, and the durable one is not the milestones — it is that the reporting channel failed: the slippage was learned about from complaints, not from the manager. Option C addresses both at the right level: the conversation is prompt, evidence-led, and developmental rather than punitive — a newly promoted manager who is not escalating is usually protecting an image of competence, and the fix is making escalation feel like competence, which is the **Community collaboration** environment-setting duty. Option A is the heroic override: it recovers one transition at the cost of teaching the manager that trouble means removal. Option B confuses empowerment with absence. Option D fixes an inbox, not the situation. This tests intervening at the level of the system, supporting the person, and staying accountable for the outcome.

**Question 2 (easy) — The health check tells a story**

A quarterly Agile health check across transition teams shows one team's scores collapsing on "safe to raise problems" and "sustainable pace" while its delivery metrics remain green. The team's manager attributes it to "a couple of negative personalities" and recommends no action. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the manager's reading — they know their team best.
- B) Treat the divergence as the signal it is: green delivery with collapsing safety and pace scores is the signature of a team burning reserves to hide problems; look deeper — skip-level conversations, retrospective observation — and coach the manager on why "negative personalities" is usually the diagnosis that prevents diagnosis.
- C) Remove the "negative personalities" from the team.
- D) Wait one more quarter to see if the scores recover.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The role summary names this exact instrument — identifying issues through Agile health checks and helping to stimulate the right responses — and the scenario tests reading one. Green delivery with red safety-and-pace is not a contradiction; it is a sequence: teams deliver on borrowed effort and suppressed concerns before the delivery line follows the safety line down. Option B responds proportionately: verify with skip-level conversation, and develop the manager's diagnostic frame — "negative personalities" localises a systemic signal in individuals. Option A delegates the reading to the person whose reading is the anomaly. Option C acts on an undiagnosed theory with maximum harm. Option D spends a quarter confirming what the instrument already showed. This tests taking instruments seriously and verifying proportionately rather than overriding or deferring.

**Question 3 (easy) — A benefits claim that cannot be supported**

A programme board paper, drafted by a programme manager and circulated for input before submission, claims that a transition will deliver "£2 million of annual savings from month one". The savings depend on decommissioning a legacy system that cannot happen until at least month nine, and the realistic year-one figure is nearer £700,000. The programme manager says the £2 million "keeps the board supportive" and the detail "can be managed later". What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it pass — benefits cases are always optimistic, and it is the programme manager's paper.
- B) Correct the figure in the input: state the month-nine dependency and the £700,000 year-one estimate, tell the programme manager the paper cannot be supported as drafted, and offer to help present the honest trajectory compellingly — a credible £700,000 rising to £2 million beats a £2 million that unravels in the month-four review.
- C) Report the programme manager to the board chair for attempted misrepresentation.
- D) Decline to provide input, so no name is associated with the paper.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The **Service focus** skill includes communicating service benefits — and communicating them credibly is the entire value of the skill, because benefits claims are a currency that inflates. Option B refuses the false figure and does the harder, more useful thing: it makes the honest number persuasive, with the trajectory and dependency visible. The offer to help matters; correction without help is just criticism. Option A launders the misstatement through silence. Option C escalates before attempting resolution. Option D protects a reputation and abandons the board to the number known to be wrong. This tests making honesty useful, holding escalation in reserve, and treating credibility as portfolio infrastructure.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Least effective: the supplier games the release policy**

The candidate discovers that a major supplier has been splitting large, high-impact changes into several "low-impact" change requests to qualify for the pre-approved route and avoid CAB scrutiny — technically within the letter of the policy's impact definitions, clearly against its intent. Nothing has failed yet. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Close the loophole: amend the policy so related changes within a defined window are assessed at their aggregate impact, and communicate the change to all suppliers with its rationale.
- B) Raise it directly with the supplier's delivery director: name the pattern, state that aggregate impact is the true measure, and require their proposals to reflect it ahead of the formal policy change.
- C) Since nothing has failed and the letter of the policy was followed, log it and take no further action.
- D) Review recent split changes retrospectively at their aggregate impact to establish whether unassessed risk has already entered production.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A, B, and D are the three components of the complete response — fix the rule, fix the behaviour ahead of the rule, and fix the exposure already created; in practice all three would be sequenced together, with D arguably first. Option C is least effective, and its reasoning is the trap: "nothing has failed yet" is survivorship logic, and "the letter was followed" concedes that the control was defeated while declining to repair it. A supplier who learns that gaming the policy draws only a log entry has learned the policy's real content. This tests defending the intent of controls, not merely their letter, and never letting quiet luck stand in for assurance.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Two managers in conflict**

Two service transition managers reporting to the candidate are in escalating conflict over a shared release calendar: each has now emailed separately blaming the other, their teams have begun mirroring the hostility, and a joint planning session collapsed last week. Both are strong performers. What is the most effective response?

- A) Decide the calendar dispute personally and instruct both to comply.
- B) Address the two layers separately and in order: first meet the two managers together to resolve the substantive calendar conflict against portfolio priorities — modelling the joint problem-solving that failed last week; then, with the substance settled, deal with the working relationship explicitly, naming the team-mirroring as the cost that makes repair non-optional.
- C) Tell them both to sort it out between themselves — they are managers.
- D) Separate their portfolios so the shared calendar dependency disappears.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Conflicts between managers arrive in two layers — a substantive dispute and a damaged working relationship — and the order matters: resolving substance first gives relationship repair a foundation. Option B also works the right mechanism on each layer: the calendar is resolved against portfolio priorities with both present, and the relationship is addressed as a professional obligation with a named business cost. This tests the **Community collaboration** skill of identifying and rectifying team-dynamic problems, tested here at one remove. Option A settles the calendar and leaves the conflict armed. Option C abdicates: the collapsed session is the evidence they cannot. Option D redesigns the portfolio around a relationship failure. Rewarded behaviours: substance before sentiment, authority exercised transparently, relationship repair required rather than requested.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A user researcher brings unwelcome findings late**

Three weeks before a flagship go-live, an embedded user researcher reports that assisted-digital users cannot complete the new application journey without help, and that the promised telephone-assistance route was quietly descoped four months ago to save money — a decision that never reached the candidate. The programme director's reaction is that the research is "too late to act on". What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — three weeks is too late; note it for the post-implementation review.
- B) Back the researcher publicly and act on the substance: establish what the assistance route would cost to restore, put the descoping decision and its user impact in front of the go/no-go with options — restore before launch, launch with an interim measure and a dated commitment, or accept the exclusion explicitly — and separately fix the governance gap that let a user-impacting descope bypass co-ordination.
- C) Refuse the go-live outright until the assistance route is fully restored.
- D) Instruct the researcher to soften the finding, since the descoping cannot be undone in three weeks.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Too late to act on" is a framing, not a fact — three weeks is late for comfort but early enough for options, and option B generates them. The essential move is placing the decision where it belongs: at the go/no-go, with the user impact quantified. Backing the researcher publicly matters — the **User focus** skill requires championing user research and representing users internally, and researchers punished for late unwelcome findings become researchers who stop finding. The governance repair matters equally, since the channel failure will recur otherwise. Option A accepts exclusion of the users least able to object. Option C pre-empts the service owner's decision. Option D is the answer scoring keys punish hardest: instructing a researcher to soften findings corrupts the evidence channel the entire user-focus duty depends on.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Capacity remediation meets budget refusal**

A capacity analysis shows a shared platform breaching its performance threshold in roughly four months under committed go-live load, with remediation needing a ten-week procurement. Remedial action is initiated, but the infrastructure budget holder refuses: "Next financial year. Nothing has actually failed." Two of the go-lives driving the load belong to other directorates. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the refusal — it is their budget, and the new financial year is only five months away.
- B) Escalate immediately to the budget holder's director with an accusation of negligence.
- C) Convert the analysis into a decision the organisation cannot mislay: quantify the breach's service impact and date, price the remediation against the cost of a capacity incident, put the paper jointly to the budget holder and the affected directorates, and if refusal stands, require it as a recorded risk acceptance at the right level, while re-examining whether go-live sequencing can trade time.
- D) Quietly deprioritise the two other directorates' go-lives to keep the platform under threshold.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Nothing has actually failed" is survivorship logic, and the five-month wait exceeds the four-month breach horizon. Option C is the **Availability and capacity management** duty executed at organisational altitude: the analysis becomes a costed, dated decision paper; the affected directorates are enlisted because their go-lives are both the cause of the load and the casualties of the breach; and the recorded risk acceptance mechanism means that if the organisation chooses the risk, it does so knowingly, at the level that owns the consequence, in writing. Option A accepts a timeline the arithmetic has already defeated. Option B escalates with accusation instead of analysis. Option D uses co-ordination authority covertly to solve a funding dispute, harming two directorates without their knowledge. Rewarded behaviours: arithmetic into decisions, stakeholders enlisted by interest, risk acceptance formalised, authority used openly.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Least effective: a predecessor's process, a team's habit**

Six weeks into the role, the candidate finds an inherited team running a transition checklist containing several steps nobody can explain ("we've always done it"), while two incidents in the last quarter trace to gaps the checklist does not cover. The team is proud of its discipline and wary of the candidate as a newcomer. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Run a structured review with the team: trace each checklist step to the risk it controls, retire the ones that control nothing, and add coverage for the two incident-shaped gaps — letting the team lead the tracing.
- B) Announce that the checklist is legacy bureaucracy and replace it with a new one drafted personally.
- C) Use the two incidents as the entry point: work back from each to the missing control, and let that evidence open the broader review.
- D) Leave the checklist untouched for another quarter while building trust.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Options A and C are complementary strengths — A is systematic, C is evidence-led and meets a wary team where their own pain is. Option D is weak but survivable, though a third incident during the wait would sit on the candidate. Option B is least effective on every axis: it discards whatever undocumented wisdom the checklist encodes, it replaces a team-owned artefact with an imposed one, and it does so through a newcomer's announcement to a proud, wary team — converting process improvement into an identity attack and guaranteeing quiet non-compliance. This tests the change-leadership principle of reforming inherited practice through the people who run it, anchored to evidence.

**Question 9 (hard) — A peer's go-live, the candidate's evidence**

A fellow lead — a peer who has supported the candidate in the past, under visible pressure from their director — asks for a two-week compression of a co-ordinated testing window so their programme's go-live can hit a politically committed date. Evidence from the last three compressed windows in the portfolio: two produced major early-life incidents. The compression is technically possible, and refusing will strain a relationship the candidate relies on. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept — the relationship and the political commitment together outweigh a risk that is only historical.
- B) Refuse flatly, citing the two incidents, and copy both directors.
- C) Treat it as a risk decision, not a favour: share the compression evidence with the peer openly, identify what could make this compression different from the failed two (scope reduction, added support cover, staged go-live), and if a residual gap remains, take the choice jointly to the service owner as a documented risk decision with both options priced — supporting whatever is decided with full effort.
- D) Accept, but privately document an objection in case the go-live fails.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The request arrives dressed as a relationship transaction; option C's first move is the reframe — window compression is a risk decision about a service, made on evidence by its owner, not traded between colleagues. The evidence is shared openly, the genuine engineering question is asked (what would make this time different?), and any residual gap goes to the service owner as a documented, priced choice, followed by full-effort support of whatever is decided. Option A prices users' risk at the value of a working relationship. Option B may reach the right answer through the wrong mechanism, converting a solvable joint problem into a positional dispute. Option D is the pattern senior keys punish most reliably: consent in public, dissent in a drawer. Rewarded behaviours: favours reframed as decisions, evidence shared not brandished, mitigation genuinely explored, decisions documented at their owner.

**Question 10 (hard) — What a portfolio pattern says about the candidate**

Preparing an annual review of the transition function, the candidate finds an unexpected pattern: transitions personally chaired at readiness reviews show faster gate decisions but materially worse early-life incident rates than those chaired by other managers. Digging further: in the candidate's reviews, open issues were more often "noted and accepted" to keep pace; in the managers' reviews, more often converted into conditions with owners and dates. Nobody else has noticed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Note it privately and chair fewer reviews, letting the pattern quietly improve.
- B) Treat this as the finding: adopt the managers' conditions-with-owners discipline explicitly, tell the managers what the data showed and what is changing — including that their practice outperformed the candidate's — and add decision-quality measures (conditions set, conditions honoured, early-life outcomes) to the function's standard review metrics so chair-dependent variation becomes visible for everyone, permanently.
- C) The sample is small and confounded — the candidate's reviews may have covered harder transitions; commission more analysis before changing anything.
- D) Standardise review chairing on the candidate to eliminate variation, applying more care than before.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The data has handed a rare and cheap kind of finding — a leadership behaviour with measurable consequences — and option B extracts its full value. The behavioural fix is specific and immediately actionable. Telling the managers is the leadership multiplier: publicly crediting their practice converts an awkward finding into cultural capital, licensing every one of them to examine their own patterns honestly. Instrumenting decision quality makes the improvement structural rather than personal. Option C's methodological caution functions as sophisticated procrastination: the remedy is beneficial under every hypothesis, so waiting for causal certainty costs incidents and buys nothing. Option A improves the numbers while hiding the lesson. Option D doubles down on the underperforming variable. Rewarded behaviours: self-directed evidence handling, credit given downward, personal findings converted into institutional instruments.

**Question 11 (hard) — The freeze exception with a minister's name on it**

During a pre-Christmas change freeze — instituted after a catastrophic December release three years ago — a director asks the candidate to authorise an exception: a "small" release enabling a feature the minister has publicly promised for the new year. The exception process requires a documented risk assessment, service owner approval, and rollback verification; the director wants authorisation today, saying the process "will take longer than the work itself". The release is genuinely small; the political commitment is genuinely real. What is the most effective response?

- A) Authorise it today — the release is small, the commitment is real, and leads exist to exercise judgement over process.
- B) Refuse all discussion of exceptions during freeze — that is what a freeze means.
- C) Run the exception process at emergency pace rather than waiving it: convene the risk assessment and service owner today, verify the rollback, and give the director a decision within days — while being honest that the answer follows the assessment, and putting the process's speed, not its existence, on the table.
- D) Authorise today on condition the process is completed retrospectively in January.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The director has framed the choice as process versus speed; option C refuses the frame — the exception process is the speed mechanism, existing precisely so freezes can bend for genuine cases without breaking for convenient ones. The distinction between accelerating a process and waiving it is the whole content of this scenario: acceleration preserves every control while compressing elapsed time; waiver deletes the controls and keeps the signature. Option A trades the control regime for a relationship moment and re-creates, with a minister's name attached, the exact unassessed December release the freeze memorialises. Option B is the procedural hide: freezes with no exception path force urgent work underground. Option D inverts the sequence that gives assurance meaning. Rewarded behaviours: process as enabler, honesty with power, urgency absorbed by pace rather than by controls.

**Question 12 (hard) — Succession, and what is owed to the function**

The candidate has led the transition function for four years. A restructure will move them to a broader role in six months. A striking amount of the function routes through the candidate personally — suppliers escalate by name, the portfolio's real dependencies live in a private inbox, and managers, though strong, each see only their slice. Nobody has asked for anything to be done about this. What is the most effective use of the remaining six months?

- A) Maintain performance to the end — succession is someone else's problem to solve.
- B) Document everything: spend the six months producing a comprehensive handbook of the function's dependencies, relationships, and judgement calls.
- C) Systematically convert personal capital into institutional capability: move supplier escalation points from a personal name to role-based routes, make the portfolio dependency picture a maintained shared artefact rather than a private model, rotate managers through cross-portfolio exposure so someone other than the candidate has seen the whole, and give the director an honest succession risk assessment — while still running the function well.
- D) Identify the strongest manager and privately groom them as successor, sharing networks and knowledge with them alone.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Nobody asked — that is the point: the **Ownership and topic** skill defines itself by proactive search for potential problems, and the function's dependence on one person is the largest unlogged risk on its register, with a six-month fuse only the candidate can see clearly. Option C names the real work: personal capital converts to institutional capability only through structural moves — escalation routes attached to roles rather than names, the dependency picture converted from private model to maintained artefact, managers given cross-portfolio sight, and the residual risk stated honestly upward. Option B mistakes documentation for transfer — judgement, relationships, and the habit of seeing across slices transfer through practice and exposure. Option A is technically true and an abdication in substance. Option D concentrates the risk it claims to solve and pre-empts a selection that is not the candidate's to make. Rewarded behaviours: unprompted risk ownership, structure over documents, capability spread rather than anointed, and honest upward accounting of what remains.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate's response designs, empowers, or accelerates** rather than personally overriding or procedurally hiding.
- **Watch for whether a candidate reframes a favour, a lateness, or a refusal into a documented risk decision** routed to its rightful owner.
- **Note whether a candidate names the decision's rightful owner** and what they need to decide well, rather than making it for them or letting it default.
- **Note whether a candidate protects the instruments that tell the truth** — researchers, health checks, uncomfortable data — rather than softening or overriding them.
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding the heroic override** — taking over a slipping transition, drafting a replacement process personally, standardising decisions on the candidate. It reads as strength and scores as failure to build capability.
- **Crediting the procedural hide** — "the letter was followed", "nothing has failed yet", "the freeze means no discussion". Controls exist for outcomes; following them into known harm is abdication with paperwork.
- **Missing when a candidate keeps consent in public and dissent in a drawer** — private objections documented for later vindication are the reliably worst-scored behaviour in senior keys.
- **Accepting an answer that spends a relationship instead of evidence, or evidence instead of a relationship** — flat refusals with directors copied, and accusations where analysis would serve, both waste what the situation needs.
- **Missing when a candidate confuses documentation with transfer, or presence with capability** — handbooks do not transfer judgement, and performance that routes through one person is risk, not indispensability.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a lead service transition manager: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested the systems reasoning this co-ordination role runs on: reading failure data for structure before rate, applying policy by precedence rather than convenience, chaining supplier commitments at their worst case, catching the cross-document impossibility that no single document owner can see, and bounding uncertain risks with tested recovery positions. The numeric section tested the arithmetic of presiding over other people's numbers — recomputing aggregates, decomposing stable headlines into moving shares, pricing both branches of wait-versus-act decisions, and catching the denominator that changed mid-series. The verbal section tested reading and writing at the altitude where words are commitments: operating contract clauses in full, auditing board papers for what they establish rather than assert, and drafting the difficult supplier letter that is firm on facts and generous about the future. The situational judgement section mapped the leadership terrain itself: intervening at the level of the system, protecting the instruments that tell the truth, converting favours into risk decisions and refusals into recorded risk acceptances, accelerating controls rather than waiving them, and building a function that will not need its current lead.
