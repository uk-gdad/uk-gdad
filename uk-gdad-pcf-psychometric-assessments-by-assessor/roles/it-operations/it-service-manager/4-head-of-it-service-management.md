# Head of IT Service Management - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of IT service management, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At head-of-function level, the character of the material shifts decisively toward the strategic. The material draws on the artefacts a head of IT service management genuinely handles — organisational service strategies, function-wide budgets and workforce plans, board papers, escalated stakeholder disputes, process ownership models, and multi-year investment cases — and the decisions that define the role's accountability: setting the vision and strategy for service management, ensuring processes are owned and maturing across the organisation, resourcing a function adequately against competing demands, representing service management at senior level, and acting as the escalation point when relationships or services break down.

At head-of-function scope, judgement is the job. The role summary is explicit: setting vision and strategy; leading a team while ensuring adequate resources and capacity; arbitrating when blockers are escalated; facilitating discussions across high-risk, complex areas under constrained timelines; directing the strategic approach for stakeholder relationships across the organisation. Every one of those accountabilities is exercised through the four capabilities this document assesses — cognitive reasoning, numerical analysis, precision reading, and situational judgement — and at this level the cost of a misjudgement is organisational, not local.

This document is organised into four assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, a mapping of the assessment's dimensions to the specific skills in the role summary, a substantial set of items with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, administration tips, and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Let the candidate work through each section without interruption, and note anything unusual about the conditions so the record is honest. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief — at this level, the situational judgement explanations in particular function as compressed leadership case studies on governance, integrity, organisational design, and influence at board level, worth discussing directly. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

The hard questions in this document are deliberately executive-grade — multi-constraint, politically textured, evidence-conflicted — because that is the terrain the role occupies, and the distractors are positions real executives take.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental operations this role demands, through realistic materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-function level, the materials are organisational: function-wide performance dashboards, process ownership matrices, policy suites with interacting provisions, escalation records, board papers, and strategic plans. The questions ask the candidate to recognise patterns that span an organisation, deduce what interacting policies compel, catch structural errors in aggregated evidence, prioritise among claims that share no common currency, and produce solutions where the evidence genuinely conflicts.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions spanning pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving, scored objectively against an executive-level norm group.

The reasoning failures available at head-of-function level are the expensive ones. A head who misses the organisational pattern funds three departmental symptoms instead of one systemic cause. A head who deduces loosely from policy interactions signs the exemption that unravels the control framework. A head who accepts a flattering aggregate presents it to a board whose decisions then rest on it. The role summary demands the opposite: leading investigative work, absorbing large amounts of conflicting information and using it to produce solutions, directing evaluation of strategies, and ensuring processes are owned and maturing.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in the role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to the **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill and the duty to ensure **processes are owned and maturing**: at organisational scope, the patterns that matter are cross-functional recurrences that no single team can see from where it sits.
- **Logical deduction** maps to the **Service management framework knowledge** skill — reasoning correctly about how framework provisions, local adaptations, and organisational policies interact — and the **Asset and configuration management** accountability for organisation-wide configuration management and policy adherence.
- **Error checking** maps to the **IT service reporting** skill — using data analytics to make decisions that enhance business performance presupposes catching structural errors that make analytics quietly wrong.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the duty to **lead a team, ensuring adequate resources and capacity**, and the **Ownership and topic** skill of proactively searching for potential problems.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to the **Strategic thinking** skill — leading the design and implementation of strategy, and directing the evaluation of strategies and policies — and the role as **escalation point**: what arrives at the head's desk is by definition what defeated the levels below.
- Reasoning about organisational influence maps to the **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Organisational pattern recognition**

A function's quarterly review shows incident-process maturity scores across six departments: Revenue 4.1, Casework 4.0, Licensing 3.9, Grants 2.4, Registrations 4.2, Appeals 2.3. Investigating further reveals Grants and Appeals are the only two departments whose service management staff report into departmental operations directors rather than into the function. What is the most reasonable initial hypothesis?

- A) Grants and Appeals have weaker staff who should be replaced
- B) The reporting-line difference plausibly explains the maturity gap: departments outside the functional line miss the standards, development, and peer pressure the function provides
- C) The maturity assessment is biased against small departments
- D) Grants and Appeals handle harder workloads

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four departments cluster at 3.9–4.2; two sit at 2.3–2.4; and the two low scorers share exactly one identified structural property — they sit outside the functional reporting line. Option B connects the pattern to a mechanism: functional lines carry standards, communities of practice, and career development, and their absence is a well-understood cause of capability divergence. Option A leaps from structure to personnel. Options C and D invent properties the stem neither states nor supports. This tests whether the candidate frames the hypothesis as plausible and testable, and a strong candidate's answer notes that ensuring processes are owned and maturing sometimes means fixing organisational design, not people.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from interacting policies**

An organisation's policies state: (i) every business-critical service must have a named process owner for incident, change, and continuity; (ii) a person may own a process for at most three business-critical services; (iii) any service without complete process ownership must be flagged in the quarterly risk report. The Grants payment service — business-critical — has named owners for incident and change, but its continuity owner retired last month and has not been replaced. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The Grants payment service must be flagged in the quarterly risk report
- B) The retired owner's other services must also be flagged
- C) A new continuity owner must be appointed within the quarter
- D) The service should be temporarily reclassified as non-critical

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Policy (i) requires complete ownership across all three processes; Grants payment now lacks a continuity owner, so ownership is incomplete. Policy (iii) states that any service without complete process ownership must be flagged. The conclusion follows in two steps. Option B is unlicensed by the given facts. Option C states a sensible action but no quoted policy compels a timescale. Option D proposes evading the control the policy exists to enforce. This tests exact application of what the policies compel, no more, and a strong candidate's answer notes that flagging promptly rather than reclassifying is what makes a head's risk reports trustworthy.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a board figure**

A draft board paper states: "Function-wide, major incidents fell 25% this year (from 48 to 36). Every department improved." The appendix shows major incidents by department: Revenue 20→12, Casework 10→8, Licensing 8→6, Grants 4→6, Registrations 6→4. Which statement is correct?

- A) Both the headline and the supporting claim are accurate
- B) The 25% fall is accurate, but "every department improved" is false — Grants rose from 4 to 6
- C) The 25% fall is miscalculated
- D) The appendix does not support any conclusion

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Headline totals: 48 and 36; fall = 12/48 = 25% — accurate. But Grants moved from 4 to 6, a 50% increase, so "every department improved" is false. This tests checking both the aggregate and every row against the prose, and a strong candidate's answer notes that a false universal claim buries the paper's most decision-relevant fact: why is Grants moving against the trend.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritising the function's attention**

Four items compete for a function's limited senior capacity this month: (1) the Cabinet Office has requested input to a cross-government service standard consultation, deadline in three weeks; (2) the Appeals department — the lowest maturity score — has asked for help redesigning its incident process; (3) a supplier's account director has requested a strategic relationship review; (4) internal audit has given 10 working days to respond to a draft report containing two findings believed to rest on factual errors. Which item must be actioned first?

- A) Item 1 — cross-government visibility matters most
- B) Item 2 — the weakest department is the biggest risk
- C) Item 4 — the audit response has the shortest hard deadline, and unchallenged factual errors become the official record
- D) Item 3 — supplier relationships underpin everything else

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Item 4 has a 10-day clock with an asymmetric consequence: unchallenged draft audit findings typically finalise as written and propagate into risk registers. Item 1 has a three-week deadline, schedulable behind item 4. Items 2 and 3 are undated. This tests separating hard deadlines with irreversible consequences from important-but-schedulable items — a strong candidate's answer notes item 2 deserves committed scheduling this month without needing to start immediately.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Conflicting evidence at escalation**

An escalation arrives: the Casework director asserts the service desk "routinely fails" her department, citing three recent incidents her staff describe as badly handled. The service desk manager presents data: Casework's SLA achievement is 96%, above the organisational average, with satisfaction at 4.4/5. Investigating the three cited incidents finds all three involved a single specialised legacy application, all were escalated to the same third-party supplier, and all breached, while the desk's own handling met its targets at every step. What best characterises the situation?

- A) The director's claim is refuted by the data — the desk performs well and she should be told so
- B) The desk's data is falsified
- C) Both accounts are accurate at their own level: overall service is strong, but a specific failure mode — the legacy application's third-party escalation path — produces genuinely bad experiences that the aggregate conceals; the escalation is a signal about that path, not about the desk
- D) Three incidents are statistically insignificant and require no action

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The director's evidence is real; the desk's evidence is real. Option C reconciles them by locating the failure precisely: a specific application's escalation path — a seam between organisations, exactly where aggregate metrics are blind. Option A wins the argument and misses a genuine defect. Option B invents fraud where measurement scope explains everything. Option D dismisses a pattern that is small in count but perfectly consistent. This tests the escalation-point discipline of arbitrating by finding the level at which everyone's evidence is true, rather than picking a winner.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about framework implementation**

Implementing a common service management framework across three environments: an in-house digital directorate, an outsourced infrastructure operation, and a small arm's-length body (ALB). Constraints: (i) the framework requires a single organisational change authority; (ii) the outsourcing contract, with four years to run, gives the supplier contractual authority over changes to infrastructure it operates; (iii) the ALB's statutory independence means policies apply to it only by agreement; (iv) the framework permits delegated change authorities provided they operate under the organisational authority's documented standards. Which implementation model is consistent with all four constraints?

- A) A single central change authority approving all changes directly, including infrastructure and ALB changes
- B) Three fully independent change authorities with no common standards
- C) A central change authority owning organisational standards, with the supplier's contractual authority and an agreed ALB authority operating as documented delegated authorities under those standards
- D) Excluding infrastructure and the ALB from the framework entirely

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option A strains against constraint (ii)'s contractual grant. Option B violates (i)'s single-authority requirement. Option D abandons the framework's scope entirely. Option C uses the framework's own flexibility: constraint (iv) permits delegated authorities under documented standards, mapping onto the supplier's contractual authority (as a delegated authority operating under the standards) and onto the ALB (authority established by agreement). This tests reading a framework's delegation provisions as carefully as its requirements, and a strong candidate's answer notes that mature implementation treats contracts and statutory independence as parameters for a federated design, not obstacles to uniformity.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits aggregation**

An improvement portfolio office reports: "The five completed initiatives delivered £2.1m in annual savings: automation £800k, channel shift £600k, supplier consolidation £400k, licence rationalisation £200k, and process redesign £100k. This exceeds the £2m target." Reviewing the underlying files: the automation figure counts gross analyst time freed, of which finance has agreed only 60% is cashable; the channel-shift figure predates its agreed −£90k digital-failure-rate adjustment; and the supplier consolidation saving is annualised from its first quarter, which preceded a contractual price step-up (post-step-up run rate: £280k/year). What is the defensible annual savings figure?

- A) £2.1m as reported
- B) About £1.67m
- C) About £1.47m
- D) About £1.85m

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Automation: £800k × 60% = £480k. Channel shift: £600k − £90k = £510k. Supplier consolidation: post-step-up £280k. Sum with the other two unchanged components lands around £1.47–1.57m — below the £2m target, not above it. This tests three specific audits of a benefits claim — cashability, agreed adjustments, and run-rate honesty across contractual steps — and a strong candidate's answer notes these audits should be applied before reporting, not after a challenge.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritising process investment across the organisation**

A maturity assessment across five organisational processes yields: incident 4.0, request 3.8, change 3.5, problem 2.2, continuity 2.0. The improvement budget can fund substantial uplift of two processes this year. Incident and request owners argue their processes serve the most daily users; the change owner cites audit interest; problem and continuity have no vocal advocates. Which pair best deserves the investment?

- A) Incident and request — maximise benefit to daily users
- B) Problem and continuity — the lowest-maturity processes, where marginal investment yields the largest risk reduction and where the absence of advocates is itself a warning sign
- C) Incident and change — reward strength and satisfy audit
- D) Spread the budget equally across all five

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Processes at 3.8–4.0 are performing; uplift there buys refinement. Processes at 2.0–2.2 are structurally deficient, with rare, catastrophic cost distributions exactly matched by maturity investment. This tests recognising that advocacy volume correlates with visibility, not risk — a strong candidate's answer names the pattern: allocating by lobby institutionalises the bias against processes whose failures are deferred, and equal spread across all five achieves substantial uplift nowhere.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Deduction from an escalation chain**

An escalation policy states: (i) departmental service issues escalate first to the departmental service manager; (ii) issues unresolved after five working days escalate to the head of IT service management; (iii) issues involving more than one department may escalate directly to the head; (iv) the head may delegate any escalation except those involving data-protection risk. A cross-department issue involving a suspected personal-data exposure is escalated directly to the head on day one. Which statement must be true?

- A) The escalation is premature — five days have not elapsed
- B) The escalation is valid under rule (iii), and rule (iv) prevents delegation
- C) The escalation should have gone to both departmental service managers first
- D) The head may delegate it to a deputy provided oversight is retained

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The issue involves more than one department, so rule (iii) authorises direct escalation — the five-day clock in (ii) governs the sequential route, not the direct one. The issue involves personal-data exposure, so rule (iv)'s exception applies, and no delegation is permitted, with or without retained oversight. This tests exact deduction under multiple interacting rules, and a strong candidate's answer notes that the exceptions in an organisation's own policies bind the head most of all — honouring a non-delegation clause under a busy diary is the organisation's most visible act of policy adherence.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint organisational design**

Restructuring on-call arrangements. Constraints: (i) every business-critical service requires 24/7 incident cover; (ii) the function has four teams — Platforms, Applications, Desk, and Specialist — and only Platforms and Applications currently have staff contracted for on-call; (iii) budget permits adding on-call contracts to exactly one more team; (iv) analysis shows 70% of out-of-hours incidents require application knowledge, 20% platform knowledge, and 10% specialist knowledge; (v) the Desk team handles no out-of-hours incidents directly but performs all triage, and untriaged incidents take 40% longer to resolve regardless of resolver; (vi) Specialist incidents, though rare, have the highest average business impact per incident. Which allocation of the additional on-call budget is best supported?

- A) Specialist — highest impact per incident must be covered
- B) Desk — triage affects 100% of out-of-hours incidents, and a 40% resolution-time penalty across the whole volume outweighs direct coverage of the rare 10%
- C) Split the budget between Desk and Specialist
- D) Add a second Applications rota — 70% of incidents dominate

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Desk on-call affects every out-of-hours incident: without triage, all incidents resolve 40% slower, including high-impact specialist ones (which still occur; they are merely triaged faster). Option A covers 10% of incidents directly while leaving the 40% penalty on all of them. Option D adds depth where cover already exists. Option C violates the "exactly one more team" constraint's intent by funding neither adequately. This tests quantifying a multiplier against the whole system versus additions to parts of it — a strong candidate's answer notes on-call design is workforce planning where the arithmetic of coverage and system effects should beat the intuition of "cover the scariest thing directly".

**Question 11 (hard) — Root cause across an organisational trend**

Over 18 months, function-wide data shows: SLA achievement stable at 94–95%; user satisfaction fallen from 4.3 to 3.6; complaint volume up 60%; the phrase "had to chase" appearing in 45% of negative comments (up from 15%); average incident resolution time improved 20%; and the proportion of incidents resolved via the self-service portal risen from 10% to 45% following a channel-shift programme. Which explanation do the data best support?

- A) Users have become harder to please and the satisfaction measure should be rebased
- B) Resolution performance has actually deteriorated and the SLA data is wrong
- C) The channel-shift succeeded operationally but degraded experience: portal-resolved incidents close faster yet leave users without the human contact and progress visibility they previously had — so speed improved while "being informed" collapsed, and the chasing complaints locate the defect in communication, not resolution
- D) Complaints are up because volumes are up

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Every operational measure improved while every experiential measure declined, and the tripling of "had to chase" comments is the fingerprint of missing progress communication in the portal journey. This tests recognising that operational dashboards can read green throughout an experience collapse if no metric watches the gap between resolution speed and communication — a strong candidate's answer proposes both the fix (status communication built into the journey) and the reporting change (an experiential measure with the same standing as SLA).

**Question 12 (hard) — Producing strategy from conflicting evidence**

Setting a function's three-year strategy. The conflicting evidence: the board wants 20% cost reduction from corporate services, including this function; incident volumes are flat but demand complexity is rising (multi-system incidents up 40% over two years); the two most experienced service managers retire within 18 months; the organisation's digital strategy will add six new citizen-facing services over the period; an external benchmark shows cost-per-supported-service 15% below comparator organisations already; and staff survey data shows the function's development and progression scores in the bottom quartile. Which strategic core best synthesises the evidence?

- A) Commit to the 20% cost reduction through headcount release, absorbing the new services within remaining capacity
- B) Refuse the cost target, citing the benchmark showing the function is already lean
- C) A strategy built on capability-shift rather than cost-cut: use the benchmark to renegotiate the target toward cost-per-service-supported (holding unit cost flat while absorbing the six new services — a real-terms efficiency exceeding 20%), fund an automation-and-knowledge programme addressing rising complexity and the retirement cliff together, and attack the development-score problem by building progression into the new capability roles — presenting the board a costed choice between unit-cost efficiency and absolute-cut service risk
- D) Accept the target but defer all new-service onboarding until year three

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C converts every piece of evidence into a load-bearing component: the benchmark becomes negotiating material for redefining the metric rather than a refusal; complexity and the retirement cliff are answered by one programme; and the development-score problem becomes structural through the new capability roles. Option A books consequences silently as a service-degradation plan wearing a savings label. Option B reads as special pleading. Option D trades the digital strategy for the budget line. This tests strategy synthesis where every piece of evidence does work rather than being averaged away.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate connects an organisational pattern to a structural mechanism** rather than jumping to a personnel explanation.
- **Watch for whether a candidate audits an aggregate against its underlying rows** before accepting a headline claim.
- **Note whether a candidate reconciles conflicting evidence by finding the level at which every account is true**, rather than declaring a winner.
- **Note whether a candidate's strategic answer uses every piece of conflicting evidence** rather than favouring the evidence that supports a single instinct.
- **Use the multi-constraint allocation item (Question 10) to observe whether a candidate quantifies a system-wide multiplier** against a partial fix.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that blames people for a pattern that tracks organisational structure.**
- **Crediting a signed-off aggregate that has not been decomposed by row or component.**
- **Missing when a candidate allocates resource by advocacy volume** rather than risk shape.
- **Accepting an improvised exception to a stated policy**, even a plausible-sounding one.
- **Rewarding a strategic recommendation that discards evidence** rather than incorporating it.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and insightfully a candidate works with quantitative information at the scale this role handles. At head-of-function level, the mathematics remains deliberately standard — percentages, weighted averages, rates, ratios, compound growth, expected values — but the numbers are organisational: function budgets and workforce plans, portfolio benefits, cost-per-service benchmarks, multi-year investment cases, downtime economics at estate scale, and board-facing figures. The tested skill is decision arithmetic: choosing the right calculation, the right base, and the right level of decomposition to support an executive decision.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions built around tables, charts, and data-rich scenarios. Calculators are normally permitted, and scoring profiles accuracy and pace against an executive norm group.

Numeracy matters at head level because the role summary's phrase — "use data analytics skills to make decisions that enhance business performance" — describes the whole texture of the job. Heads of function are rarely the people who compute; they are the people who catch: flattering baselines, uncashable benefits, unweighted averages, annualised quarters, and linear projections of compound realities.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Budget and unit-cost arithmetic** maps to the duty to **lead a team, ensuring adequate resources and capacity**, and to representing the function at senior level.
- **Weighted aggregation and decomposition** maps to the **IT service reporting** skill at decision level.
- **Investment and expected-value analysis** maps to the **Strategic thinking** skill.
- **Capacity and workforce modelling** maps to the resourcing duty and the **Ownership and topic** skill.
- **Benchmark and baseline discipline** maps to the **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill and the **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill.
- **Risk quantification** maps to the **Asset and configuration management** accountability for organisational compliance and risk.

**Question 1 (easy) — Unit cost for budget defence**

A function's annual cost is £4.2m and it supports 60 live services. The corporate benchmark for comparable organisations is £78,000 per supported service. What is the cost per service, and how does it compare?

- A) £70,000 — about 10% below benchmark
- B) £72,000 — about 8% below benchmark
- C) £78,000 — exactly at benchmark
- D) £84,000 — about 8% above benchmark

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Unit cost = 4,200,000 ÷ 60 = £70,000. Comparison = (78,000 − 70,000) ÷ 78,000 ≈ 10% below benchmark. Option B miscomputes the percentage against the wrong base. This tests basic unit-cost calculation, and a strong candidate's answer notes the figure's use is as important as its computation: "10% below benchmark" is only defensible if the benchmark's scope matches — services counted the same way, comparable complexity mix, same cost inclusions.

**Question 2 (easy) — Demand-adjusted budget trend**

Three years ago a function's budget was £3.6m supporting 45 services; this year it is £4.2m supporting 60 services. A board paper claims corporate services costs are "up 17% in three years." What has happened to the function's unit cost?

- A) Risen 17%, as the paper implies
- B) Fallen 12.5% — from £80,000 to £70,000 per service
- C) Stayed flat
- D) Fallen 30%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Then: £3,600,000 ÷ 45 = £80,000. Now: £4,200,000 ÷ 60 = £70,000. Change: −12.5%. The paper's 17% is correct as absolute spend and misleading as performance — the function absorbed 33% more services for 17% more money. This tests recognising that whenever both spend and demand move, unit cost is the honest measure, and a strong candidate's answer notes this reframe must be produced proactively, before the absolute-cost story sets the frame.

**Question 3 (easy) — Weighted satisfaction across departments**

User satisfaction with a function, by department: Revenue 4.2 (12,000 users), Casework 3.9 (6,000 users), Grants 3.0 (2,000 users). What is the user-weighted average satisfaction?

- A) 3.7
- B) 4.0
- C) 3.9
- D) 4.1

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weighted sum = (4.2×12,000)+(3.9×6,000)+(3.0×2,000) = 79,800; ÷20,000 = 3.99 ≈ 4.0. The unweighted average of 3.7 (option A) understates because the weakest score belongs to the smallest department. This tests weighted aggregation, and a strong candidate's answer notes the decomposed view — Grants at 3.0 against an estate at 3.9–4.2 — is the actionable finding a "4.0 overall" headline can bury.

**Question 4 (easy) — Downtime economics at estate scale**

Across an estate, the aggregate cost of unplanned downtime is estimated at £3,000 per minute. Last year the estate suffered 620 minutes of unplanned downtime. A resilience programme costing £1.1m per year is projected to reduce unplanned downtime by 45%. What is the projected annual net benefit?

- A) £837,000
- B) £1,860,000
- C) £760,000
- D) −£263,000

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Annual downtime cost = 620×3,000 = £1,860,000. Avoided at 45% = 279 minutes × £3,000 = £837,000. Net = 837,000 − 1,100,000 = −£263,000. The programme costs more than it saves on this arithmetic. This tests distinguishing gross avoided cost from net benefit — a strong candidate's answer notes a negative expected-value case can still be right if tail-scenario risk is argued explicitly, but that argument must be made, not smuggled inside a gross figure.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Workforce planning against a retirement cliff**

A function has 48 staff. Age and tenure data show 9 will reach retirement eligibility within three years, concentrated in senior roles; historical experience is that 70% of eligible staff retire within a year of eligibility. External recruitment to senior service roles takes an average of 7 months, and new senior hires take a further 12 months to reach full effectiveness. Internal promotion readiness currently covers 2 of the senior roles. Approximately how many senior-role capability gaps should be planned for at the three-year point if relying on external recruitment started only as each retirement occurs?

- A) None — recruitment will backfill as people leave
- B) Around 6 retirements, each followed by roughly 19 months of gap-to-full-effectiveness; with 2 covered by ready internal promotions, plan for around 4 roles in gap at any given time in the cliff period
- C) All 9 roles will be vacant simultaneously
- D) 2 roles at most

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Expected retirements ≈ 9×70% ≈ 6. Per-retirement gap under reactive recruitment ≈ 7+12 = 19 months. Internal readiness covers 2, leaving roughly 4 roles carrying 19-month gaps concurrently through the middle of the period. Option A assumes instantaneous hiring and effectiveness. This tests modelling capability gaps as a calculation, and a strong candidate's answer identifies the levers that shrink each term: earlier hiring, expanded internal readiness, and knowledge capture from the eligible cohort.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Benchmark adjustment in a consolidation case**

A consultancy proposes consolidating three service desks into one, projecting savings from a benchmark: "consolidated desks operate at £11 per contact versus your blended £14." Verification shows the £14 includes a specialist casework desk at £24 per contact (15% of contacts), whose regulatory complexity the benchmark population does not include; the two general desks blend to £12.24. The consolidation programme costs £1.8m, and the desks handle 500,000 contacts annually. If the benchmark applies only to general-desk contacts, what is the approximate realistic annual saving?

- A) £1.5m — (14 − 11) × 500,000
- B) About £527,000 — (12.24 − 11) × 425,000 general contacts
- C) About £650,000
- D) Nothing — the benchmark is inapplicable

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** General contacts = 85%×500,000 = 425,000. Credible per-contact saving = 12.24 − 11.00 = £1.24. Realistic saving = 425,000×1.24 ≈ £527,000. The consultancy's £1.5m assumes the specialist desk's £24 contacts compress to £11, unsupported by the benchmark population. This tests restricting a comparison to the comparable subset, and a strong candidate's answer notes the payback consequence: £1.8m ÷ £527k ≈ 3.4 years, a different investment decision from the headline case's 1.2 years.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Compound demand against linear budget**

Service demand (contacts) is growing 12% per year; the budget settlement allows 3% annual growth. Currently demand is 500,000 contacts and budget is £4.2m (£8.40 per contact). If both trends continue for three years, what does cost per contact need to fall to, and by what total percentage, for service levels to be maintained within budget?

- A) To about £6.53 per contact — a fall of about 22%
- B) To about £7.60 — a fall of about 10%
- C) To £8.40 — no change needed
- D) To about £5.90 — a fall of about 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Demand: 500,000×1.12³ ≈ 702,500. Budget: £4.2m×1.03³ ≈ £4.589m. Required unit cost = 4,589,000÷702,500 ≈ £6.53. Fall required ≈ 22%. The intuitive error nets the growth rates rather than compounding both series separately. This tests correct compounding, and a strong candidate's answer names what the 22% means: the productivity gain the strategy must deliver just to stand still — the quantified case for automation and channel-shift investment.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Expected value across an investment portfolio**

Four improvement investments compete for a £600k budget; each costs £300k, so two can be funded. Projected annual benefits and delivery confidence: W: £400k at 90% confidence; X: £700k at 50%; Y: £350k at 80%; Z: £900k at 30%. Ranking by expected annual benefit, which two should be funded?

- A) X and Z — highest headline benefits
- B) W and X — expected values £360k and £350k, the top two
- C) W and Y — the two highest-confidence options
- D) W and Z — balance a banker with a bet

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Expected values: W=360, X=350, Y=280, Z=270. Top two: W and X. Option A funds the two most exciting headlines and the two lowest expected values. Option C buys certainty at a £70k expected-value cost. This tests expected-value calculation cutting through headline-chasing, and a strong candidate's answer names two caveats the calculation cannot answer alone: whose confidence estimate, and whether the options' delivery risks correlate.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Decomposing an availability commitment for the board**

A board report shows estate availability of 99.7% against a 99.5% commitment — comfortably green. Decomposition by service tier shows: Tier 1 (8 citizen-critical services) 99.2%; Tier 2 (22 important services) 99.8%; Tier 3 (30 internal services) 99.9%. The 99.7% is availability-weighted equally across all 60 services. Which statement should lead the board narrative?

- A) "Estate availability of 99.7% exceeds commitment — performance is strong"
- B) "Headline availability is green, but the figure conceals the finding that matters: our eight citizen-critical services averaged 99.2% — below the 99.5% commitment — while the surplus was earned on internal services; Tier 1 recovery is the priority"
- C) "All tiers are performing within normal variation"
- D) "The weighting methodology should be revised next quarter"

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Equal weighting lets 30 internal services outvote 8 citizen-critical ones. Tier 1 sits below its commitment while the headline says the opposite. This tests verifying an aggregation's logic before its arithmetic, and a strong candidate's answer states the reporting principle: whenever a composite is green and its most critical component is red, the component leads and the composite follows with its explanation.

**Question 10 (hard) — The savings target and the demand curve**

A board sets a function a £840k savings target (20% of a £4.2m budget) over two years. Demand analysis: contact volumes growing 8% annually; each contact currently costs £8.40 (500,000 contacts). An automation programme can deflect 30% of contacts to self-service at £1.20 per contact by end of year two, requiring £500k investment, delivering progressively. Assuming demand growth and full programme delivery, what is the approximate year-two annual cost, and does it meet the target?

- A) About £3.36m — meets the target exactly
- B) About £4.13m against a £3.36m target — the programme absorbs demand growth and cuts real cost, but misses the absolute target by roughly £770k
- C) About £2.9m — beats the target
- D) The target is met if the £500k investment is excluded

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Year-two demand ≈ 583,200 contacts. Deflected 30% at £1.20 plus remaining at £8.40 plus amortised investment gives a year-two operating cost around £3.9–4.1m, against the target's implied £3.36m — a gap of roughly £0.6–0.8m. The counterfactual (no programme) would cost roughly £4.9m, so the programme is genuinely transformative even while missing the absolute target. This tests building demand-adjusted cost against a static target, and a strong candidate's answer proposes the counterfactual presentation: cost under no action, cost under the programme, and the target, side by side.

**Question 11 (hard) — Arbitrating duelling numbers**

Two directors escalate a dispute. The Operations director claims the new booking service is "unreliable — down constantly", citing 14 user-reported outages last quarter. The Digital director claims 99.8% availability, citing platform monitoring. Investigation finds: platform monitoring measures the service's core API from inside the data centre; 11 of the 14 reported "outages" occurred during the daily 07:00–07:20 batch window, when the API stays up but bookings queue silently and appear "stuck" to users; the remaining 3 were genuine brief incidents within the 99.8% figure. The batch window is a documented design feature agreed at build. Which resolution is soundest?

- A) The Digital director is right — 99.8% is accurate and the batch behaviour is as designed
- B) The Operations director is right — 14 user-experienced failures constitute unreliability regardless of measurement
- C) Both measurements are accurate for what they measure; the dispute is a design-consequence surfacing as a metrics argument: the agreed batch window creates a daily 20-minute experience failure that monitoring is not scoped to see. Resolve by fixing or masking the batch behaviour, adding user-journey monitoring, and reporting experienced availability alongside platform availability
- D) Commission an independent measurement to determine which director is correct

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 99.8% is accurate for the API measured from inside the data centre; "down constantly" is accurate for a user watching a booking hang during the batch window — eleven of fourteen complaints trace to a designed behaviour platform monitoring cannot see. This tests resisting the instinct to pick a winner between two accurate-but-partial accounts, and a strong candidate's answer names all three actions the diagnosis unlocks: fix the experience, close the blind spot, and report both measures on a shared basis.

**Question 12 (hard) — Total cost of ownership across a strategic choice**

Recommending a five-year direction for service management tooling. Option 1 — retain and extend the current platform: licences £180k/year rising 5% annually; £150k one-off integration in year one; internal support 2 FTE at £60k each throughout. Option 2 — migrate to a new platform: licences £120k/year flat (five-year price lock); migration £700k across years one and two; internal support 1 FTE from year two (2 FTE in year one during parallel running); training £80k in year one. Using five-year total cost, which option is cheaper, and by roughly how much?

- A) Option 1, by about £150k
- B) The five-year totals are within £5k of each other; the decision turns on the steady-state run rate, which favours Option 2 by about £160k a year from year six onward
- C) Option 1, by about £400k
- D) Option 2, by about £600k

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option 1 totals roughly £1,744.6k over five years; Option 2 totals roughly £1,740k — equivalent within £5k. But by year five, Option 1's annual run cost is rising 5% compounding (≈£338.8k), while Option 2's is flat at £180k — a roughly £159k annual advantage that compounds from year six onward, after migration costs are sunk. This tests looking past the appraisal window when the window happens to end at crossover, and a strong candidate's answer states both facts together: totals are equivalent over five years, but the steady-state run rate favours migration.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate computes a unit-cost or demand-adjusted figure** rather than reasoning from an absolute trend alone.
- **Watch for whether a candidate decomposes a composite figure by tier or component** before certifying it.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes gross avoided cost from net benefit**, and cashable from non-cashable savings.
- **Note whether a candidate looks past the stated appraisal window** when a comparison happens to end at a crossover point.
- **Use the duelling-numbers item (Question 11) to observe whether a candidate reconciles two accurate-but-partial figures** rather than declaring one side correct.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that presents absolute cost or count trends where unit or demand-adjusted trends tell the truth.**
- **Crediting an answer that nets growth rates instead of compounding both series.**
- **Missing when a candidate confuses gross avoided cost with net benefit** in a resilience or investment case.
- **Accepting a composite figure signed off without decomposition**, where the critical component contradicts the headline.
- **Rewarding an analysis that stops at the appraisal window's edge** when the window ends at a cost crossover.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures precision reading and disciplined inference over documents of the kind this role turns on. At head-of-function level, the passages are the instruments of organisational governance: policy suites and their delegation provisions, board minutes and their carefully negotiated ambiguities, audit and assurance reports with calibrated hedges, inter-departmental agreements, and strategy texts whose commitments will be quoted back for years. The dominant format is statement classification — **True**, **False**, **Cannot say** — supplemented by inference, best-summary, and clear-writing judgement questions.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 statements across several passages. The governing discipline — answer from the passage alone — earns its keep twice over at this level: executive experience generates the strongest expectations about what documents "must" say, and the documents were often drafted precisely to manage what they do and do not commit.

The head's working medium is authoritative text: policies whose words others will apply in situations never foreseen; board papers where a mischaracterised audit hedge becomes the organisation's official position; disputes where each side quotes the same agreement.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precision reading of policy and delegation text** maps to the **Service management framework knowledge** skill and the **Asset and configuration management** accountability for organisation-wide adherence.
- **True/False/Cannot say discipline** maps to the **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill and assurance literacy.
- **Inference under qualification** maps to the **IT service reporting** skill at decision level.
- **Reading negotiated and political text** maps to the **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill.
- **Strategy comprehension and drafting judgement** maps to the **Strategic thinking** skill.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to the duty to **represent the IT service management function at senior level** and the **User focus** leadership expectation.

For Questions 1 to 5, the candidate reads this passage.

> **Extract from the Departmental Technology Governance Policy**
> The Technology Design Authority (TDA) must approve all new technology services before procurement commences. The Head of IT Service Management must be consulted on the support model for any new service before TDA approval is granted; where the Head advises that the proposed support model is inadequate, the TDA may nonetheless approve the service, but must record the Head's advice and its reasons for proceeding in the approval record. Nothing in this policy limits the Accounting Officer's authority to halt any procurement.

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The TDA cannot approve a new service if the Head of IT Service Management advises the support model is inadequate."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The policy grants a consultation right, not a veto: the TDA "may nonetheless approve" the service, subject only to recording the advice and its reasons. This tests reading a power at its exact strength, and a strong candidate's answer notes the recorded-advice-plus-reasons provision is a substantial instrument even without a veto: it makes overriding the head deliberate, documented, and auditable.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A service procured under paragraph 9's emergency provisions never requires TDA involvement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The exemption is precise: emergency procurements are exempt from prior approval, but must be presented for retrospective review within sixty days. Involvement is rescheduled and its character changed, not removed. This tests reading an exemption clause for exactly what it removes and what obligation replaces it — a distinction where governance quietly leaks if misread.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The policy imposes the recording duty but is silent on the consequence of breaching it. Invalidity is one possible consequence among several the text neither confirms nor rules out. This tests distinguishing a duty from the consequence of its breach — a strong candidate's answer notes the defensible claim is "the recording requirement was not met", not "the approval is void", which the text will not carry.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The Accounting Officer may halt a procurement even after the TDA has approved the service."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The final sentence preserves the Accounting Officer's authority to halt "any procurement" with no temporal qualification and no carve-out for TDA-approved services, against limitation by this policy. This tests reading a saving clause, and a strong candidate's answer notes such clauses establish the hierarchy of authorities — telling the reader which decision survives a conflict.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following can properly be inferred from the passage?

- A) The TDA has previously approved services against the Head's advice
- B) A service procured normally (not under paragraph 9) without TDA approval has been procured in breach of the policy
- C) The Head of IT Service Management sits on the TDA
- D) Emergency procurements are rare

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A converts a provision's existence into a history. Option C confuses being consulted by a body with membership of it. Option D infers frequency from nothing. - B) "Under the framework's dispute provisions, we formally refer the following matter to the Board and request it be minuted as a dispute: the centre has missed the incident-resolution metric for our services in Q2 (87% against 95%) and Q3 (84%), while our user complaints have risen 40%. - C) "Frankly, the centre is failing our users and we are losing patience. If performance does not improve rapidly we will have no option but to consider alternative arrangements and to publish our own figures so our users know the truth."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B invokes the dispute provision explicitly and asks for the referral to be minuted, since the escalation clock runs from referral; grounds standing in the accountability clause; evidences the failure in the framework's own metrics; and its requests are specific, dated, and proportionate. Option A has no metric, no clause, no clock. Option C threatens actions the framework prohibits. Option D is warmth without content. This tests writing anchored to the governing text — every claim to a clause, every request specific, every prohibited move conspicuously not taken.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to evaluate responses — most effective, least effective, rankings, or independent ratings. At head-of-function level the scenarios are the role's hardest hours: a board pressing for a commitment the evidence will not support; a directorate quietly building its own service management capability; a crisis in which governance and speed pull apart; a budget round in which honesty about the function's needs competes with collective corporate discipline; an inherited senior team member who blocks the strategy; a ministerial deadline colliding with a control the head owns.

The typical format is an online assessment of 15 to 30 scenarios, generously timed, scored against the pooled judgement of experienced senior practitioners and the organisation's professional behaviours: honesty, ownership, user focus, collaboration, inclusion — and at this level, the distinctly executive behaviours: candour to power, protection of institutional controls, development of successors, and stewardship of the function beyond one's own tenure.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Candour-to-power choices** map to the duty to **represent the IT service management function at senior level**.
- **Arbitration and escalation choices** map to the role as **escalation point for business stakeholders** and the **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill.
- **Stewardship choices** map to the duty to ensure **processes are owned and maturing** and the **Asset and configuration management** accountability.
- **Capability and succession choices** map to the duty to **lead a team, ensuring adequate resources and capacity**.
- **Strategic-integrity choices** map to the **Strategic thinking** skill.
- **User-championing choices** map to the **User focus** skill at its most senior.
- **Solution-production choices** map to the **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — The board wants a date**

At the executive board, the Chief Operating Officer asks for a commitment to a go-live date for the consolidated service desk. The honest assessment: the critical dependency — HR consultation on new shift patterns — has a statutory minimum period that makes the COO's preferred date achievable only if consultation raises no substantive issues, assessed as a 40% likelihood. The COO presses: "Just give the board a date." What is the most effective response?

- A) Commit to the preferred date — momentum matters, and problems can be managed if they arise
- B) Refuse to give any date until consultation completes
- C) Give the board the honest structure: the preferred date is achievable at roughly 40% confidence with the consultation dependency as the sole determinant; offer a 90%-confidence date eight weeks later; recommend planning against the later date with an early-delivery option, and name the week in which the consultation outcome converts the estimate into a commitment
- D) Commit to the preferred date publicly while privately planning to the later one

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A 40%-confidence date presented as a commitment coordinates the organisation around a probable failure. Option C gives the board two dates with stated confidence, the determining dependency, a recommended planning basis, and the date on which uncertainty resolves — allowing the board to govern the risk rather than merely absorb it. Option A trades this meeting for next quarter's failure. Option D is a falsehood with a paper trail. This tests candour to power, calibrated commitment, and decision-support framing.

**Question 2 (easy) — The shadow service management function**

A large delivery directorate has quietly hired three service management professionals into its programme teams, is running its own incident process for its new products, and has bought tooling that duplicates the corporate platform — none of it coordinated with the function. The directorate is the organisation's most politically powerful, and its services are performing well. What is the most effective response?

- A) Escalate to the executive board as a governance breach requiring correction
- B) Treat it first as information, then as an opportunity: meet the directorate's leadership to understand what need the function was not meeting, negotiate a federated arrangement — their embedded people operating within the function's professional standards, processes, and community, with tooling rationalised over time — and take to the board a proposal, not a complaint
- C) Instruct the three professionals that their processes must migrate to the corporate platform within 90 days
- D) Ignore it — their services perform well, and the fight is not worth having

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A shadow function is a governance gap and a demand signal at once. Option B diagnoses the unmet need first, then designs a federated arrangement, then brings the board a solution with the directorate's fingerprints on it. Option A spends board capital on a complaint against the board's favourite directorate. Option C converts potential allies into resistance. Option D concedes fragmentation permanently. This tests diagnosis before enforcement and converting a governance problem into a capability coalition.

**Question 3 (easy) — A candidate's own control in the crisis**

A critical citizen-facing service is degrading during a ministerial announcement week. The fastest fix requires an emergency change; the change policy — authored by the head and approved by the board — requires even emergency changes to have a second authoriser from a defined list, and the two listed authorisers are both unreachable for at least an hour. The incident manager says: "You wrote the policy — you can make the exception." What is the most effective response?

- A) Waive the requirement — the policy's purpose is served by seniority, and the situation is urgent
- B) Hold the control but attack the problem: work the unreachable-authoriser problem in parallel (both contacts, their delegates, the escalation tree) while preparing the change for instant authorisation, use the hour on reversible mitigations, and afterwards fix the policy's real defect — an authoriser list with no depth — through proper amendment
- C) Wait passively for an authoriser to become reachable
- D) Authorise it personally and add the name to the authoriser list retrospectively

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The argument that "you wrote it, you can waive it" is exactly why a waiver would be the most expensive one available — the authority that makes controls work organisation-wide is the visible fact that they bind their owners. Option B refuses the false binary between control and speed and fixes the real defect through governance afterwards. Options A and D differ only in paperwork. Option C abandons the outcome. This tests visible self-compliance and parallel-path problem solving under pressure.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The budget round squeeze**

A corporate budget round requires all functions to model a 10% reduction. The honest analysis: a 10% cut forces withdrawal of proactive problem management and out-of-hours service coverage for second-tier services, with quantifiable increases in incident duration and continuity risk. A peer head of function advises privately: "Everyone pads their submission. Model the cut against your least painful lines, take the reduction, and quietly rebuild through in-year bids." What is the most effective approach?

- A) Follow the advice — the game is real, and unilateral honesty is unilateral disarmament
- B) Submit the honest model: the 10% scenario with its named service consequences and quantified risks, alongside an alternative constructed to achieve a 6% reduction through automation and supplier renegotiation without service withdrawal — letting the centre choose with accurate information about both
- C) Refuse to model the cut, on the grounds that the function is already benchmarked as lean
- D) Model the cut against training, development, and the improvement programme, preserving all current services

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B gives the centre what it structurally lacks — knowledge of what the money buys — while demonstrating the function is squeezing itself before asking others to choose. Option A's realism is self-consuming: padding is discovered, and a function caught gaming once is disbelieved forever after. Option C answers a required scenario with a refusal. Option D mortgages the capability that sustains future services. This tests institutional honesty under collective-action pressure and constructive alternative generation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The inherited blocker**

Six months into tenure, the picture is clear: one of four direct reports — 22 years in the organisation, deep institutional knowledge, personally close to two executive directors — is systematically blocking the service management strategy: deadlines slip in his areas, his teams receive contradicted versions of decisions, and two talented people below him have quietly asked to move. He is courteous in every direct interaction. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Work around him — route strategy delivery through the other three, and let his areas lag
- B) Address it directly and formally: a private, specific, evidenced conversation about the pattern, clear expectations with a defined period and support to meet them, documented — and a stated, honest consequence path if the pattern continues, executed if it does
- C) Raise his behaviour with the two executive directors he is close to, so they hear one side first
- D) Restructure the function so his role becomes redundant

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Every alternative converts a person problem into an organisational one. Option A institutionalises a two-speed function and teaches the two talented people that the problem is seen and will not be acted on. Option C fights the connection instead of the conduct. Option D uses organisational design as a personnel instrument. Option B is specific, evidenced, gives genuine opportunity with support, and states a consequence path honestly. This tests courage in the direct path and protection of emerging talent.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: the assurance report before the board**

Internal audit's draft report on continuity management contains one finding known to be factually wrong (it cites a test as "not performed" that was performed, with evidence held), two findings that are accurate and uncomfortable, and an overall "limited assurance" rating that the corrected factual finding might or might not change. The report reaches the audit committee in three weeks. Which response is the LEAST effective?

- A) Submit the test evidence through the audit's factual-accuracy process immediately, and accept the two accurate findings with owned remediation plans and dates
- B) Prepare a committee response that leads with the two accurate findings and their remediation, treating the factual correction as a separate, evidenced procedural matter
- C) Contact the audit committee chair informally before the process concludes, to explain that the report is flawed and its rating overstated
- D) Compile the full evidence file for the performed test, including who provided what to the auditors and when, to establish how the error occurred

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** An informal pre-emptive approach to the committee chair, outside the factual-accuracy process, reads as lobbying against an adverse rating and typically hardens the committee's reception of the report. Options A, B and D form the complete correct sequence: correct through the designed channel, own the uncomfortable findings, and build the evidence trail. This tests identifying the relationship shortcut around a formal process as the fatal option.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The users nobody invited**

An executive board is finalising a major cost-reduction package. One measure — reducing assisted-digital support to online-only channels — saves £600k and has cleared every governance gate; it reaches the head only for "implementation comment" with sign-off next week. Channel data shows the service's users are disproportionately elderly, disabled, and digitally excluded citizens for whom "online-only" means, in practice, no service; the user research is two years old but unambiguous, and no current voice in the process represents these users. What is the most effective action?

- A) Provide implementation comments as requested — the measure has cleared governance, and reopening it exceeds the remit
- B) Use the senior representation role for what it exists for: submit alongside implementation comments a short, evidenced exception paper — who the users are, what the channel data and research show "online-only" means for them, the failure-demand and safeguarding costs that will offset the £600k, and two mitigation options — requesting the board see it before sign-off
- C) Leak the concerns to a departmental user-voice charity so external pressure reopens the decision
- D) Sign off the implementation and build an informal workaround through the service desk afterwards

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is short, evidenced, cost-honest, and constructive, delivered inside the process before sign-off. Option A is proceduralism as moral cover — commenting on implementation while holding evidence the process never contained is the silence the board would least forgive. Option C might win the decision but makes the head an untrusted participant in every future one. Option D concedes the decision was wrong while denying the board the chance to fix it. This tests user championship where users have no other advocate, working inside process at maximum strength before going around it.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The metric the centre wants moved**

A central transformation unit, reporting monthly to a Permanent Secretary, asks for the function's headline reported metric to change from "end-to-end user resolution time" to "technical restoration time" — the latter shows the transformation programme's impact more favourably, and, accurately, is the metric most peer departments report. What is the most effective response?

- A) Adopt the change — cross-government comparability is a legitimate reason, and the request is not improper
- B) Refuse and continue reporting end-to-end resolution time alone, as the honest measure
- C) Report both, explicitly defined — technical restoration for cross-government comparability, end-to-end user resolution as the department's primary internal measure — and record in the reporting standard why the user-experienced measure remains primary, so the definition cannot drift quietly later
- D) Ask the transformation unit to obtain the Permanent Secretary's written instruction for the change, so the record shows it was not a personal decision

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The request contains a legitimate kernel wrapped around an illegitimate motive, and option C separates them: give the comparable metric, protect the user-experienced primary measure, and record the rationale against future drift. Option A adopts the favourable measure as headline. Option B loses the legitimate comparability point. Option D outsources a professional-standards question to a loyalty test. This tests integrity with constructive form and institutionalising definitions against drift.

**Question 9 (hard) — The crisis with a governance fork**

At 16:40 Friday, a supplier notifies of a likely data breach: a misconfigured storage service may have exposed citizen records from two services; their forensics "should know by Monday". Statutory assessment and notification duties may apply within 72 hours of awareness. The incident policy designates the head as senior responsible owner for exactly this scenario. The Chief Digital Officer, about to board a flight, texts: "Hold off any formal reporting until we know more — no point alarming people over a maybe." What is the most effective course?

- A) Follow the CDO's steer — they are senior, the facts are genuinely uncertain, and Monday is within reach
- B) Activate the incident process now as the policy's designated owner: convene the response team, notify the data protection officer immediately so the statutory 72-hour assessment starts on accurate footing, instruct the supplier on evidence preservation and a shorter forensic timeline, prepare — without yet issuing — external notifications, and inform the CDO of the actions taken and the statutory basis, in writing, before their flight lands
- C) Split the difference: begin technical investigation but delay involving the data protection officer until the supplier's Monday report
- D) Escalate the CDO's instruction to a Permanent Secretary's office for a ruling before acting

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A statutory clock that may already be running, a policy naming the head decision-owner, and an informal instruction to wait for certainty — option B is the designated owner doing the designated job, with a written brief to the CDO before landing. Option A converts an informal text into cover for a potential statutory failure. Option C delays exactly the actor the 72-hour duty presupposes. Option D outsources a decision the policy already made. This tests ownership of designated accountability under contrary pressure and managing upward with written precision at speed.

**Question 10 (hard) — The succession reported to the board**

A board's people committee asks annually: "Is the service management function resilient to the loss of key individuals?" This year, the only ready successor and named continuity owner for three critical processes has confidentially disclosed she is likely to leave within six months for family reasons, asking that this remain private until she decides. The succession pipeline behind her is thin: two possible candidates, each 18 months from readiness. What is the most effective way to handle the committee?

- A) Report the function as resilient — her departure is not certain
- B) Disclose her likely departure to the committee in closed session — its duty to know overrides her request
- C) Report the structural truth without the personal fact: succession depth behind key roles is one-deep with an 18-month readiness gap — a red rating on the committee's own scale — with an accelerated development plan for both candidates, cross-training on the three critical processes, and a market-scan contingency; separately, honour her confidence entirely while quietly sequencing the knowledge-transfer work her possible departure makes urgent
- D) Persuade her to authorise disclosure before the committee meets

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A pipeline that is one-deep with 18-month readiness gaps is a red rating whether or not she leaves. Option C reports that truth at full strength, pairs it with the mitigation programme, and executes the knowledge-transfer work under cover of ordinary resilience practice. Option A misrepresents the pipeline. Option B pays for institutional candour with personal betrayal and buys no additional governance decision. Option D pressures someone navigating a family decision. This tests candour at the right level of abstraction and absolute faith with confidences.

**Question 11 (hard) — Ranking: the head's Tuesday**

Four items compete for personal attention on Tuesday morning: (1) a cross-government incident-reporting return, due Friday, drafted by the team but containing a categorisation choice suspected of overstating the function's performance; (2) a request from a counterpart in another department for an urgent reference call about a supplier exited last year for concealed subcontracting failures — they sign their own contract with that supplier Thursday; (3) a monthly skip-level listening session with junior analysts, scheduled for 11:00, cancelled and rearranged twice already this quarter; (4) a director's complaint, received overnight, that the service desk "failed her team again", with a demand for a meeting today. Rank the order in which these should be addressed:

- A) 4, 1, 2, 3
- B) 2, 4, 3, 1
- C) 2, 3, 1, 4
- D) 1, 2, 4, 3

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Item 2 leads: another department commits public money Thursday to a supplier exited for concealed failures, and delay here is least recoverable. Item 3 second: a skip-level session cancelled twice already is a credibility instrument, and a third cancellation tells junior analysts that listening is what happens when nothing else is on. Item 1 third: due Friday, but its content is non-negotiable before submission. Item 4 last: deserves same-day acknowledgement and a fact-gathering instruction, with the meeting tomorrow with facts. This tests cross-boundary candour, protecting upward-listening channels, and complaint-handling that develops managers instead of bypassing them.

**Question 12 (hard) — The strategy meets the machinery change**

Eighteen months into a three-year service management strategy — consolidation to a single operating model, one toolset, federated but standardised processes, all progressing well — a machinery of government change merges the department with a smaller department whose service management arrangements are entirely different (different framework edition, different toolset mid-contract, a well-regarded 40-person function led by a respected head who now reports into this role). The merged department's executive asks for a "revised strategy" in six weeks. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Extend the existing strategy to the incoming organisation: it was board-approved, it is working, and a single model was always its destination — the incoming function migrates to it on an accelerated timeline
- B) Run the six weeks as a genuine assessment with the incoming head as co-author: evaluate both operating models against the merged organisation's needs on evidence, expect the answer to be a composite — including the possibility that elements of the smaller function's model are superior and survive — and present a revised strategy with an honest transition cost line, a protected-delivery core, and named leadership roles for both functions' senior people
- C) Pause all strategy delivery pending the merger's full organisational design, submitting an interim holding position
- D) Propose the two functions continue operating separately for the strategy's remaining 18 months, merging only when the current strategy completes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Co-authorship with the incoming head is the decisive move: their function's knowledge and belief in the merged model depend on being enlisted rather than annexed. A genuine evaluation that might adopt the smaller function's superior elements purchases something no mandate can. Option A converts a board-approved strategy for a department that no longer exists into an instrument of conquest. Option C surrenders the initiative. Option D protects the strategy's original schedule at organisational cost. This tests evidence over ownership and integration leadership that enlists rather than annexes.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate's answer refuses a binary framing** by constructing an option that serves multiple legitimate interests with the honest element protected.
- **Watch for whether a candidate holds their own controls under pressure**, distinguishing a genuine crisis response from a waiver.
- **Note whether a candidate's answer keeps a confidence completely while still meeting a public duty** by reporting the structural truth.
- **Note whether a candidate identifies a relationship shortcut around a formal process** as the fatal option in least-effective items.
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, executive-level judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that gives power the number it wants at a confidence level the evidence does not support.**
- **Crediting an answer that enforces where diagnosis and enlistment were called for** — a shadow function, an incoming department, a powerful directorate.
- **Missing when a candidate waives their own control "just this once"** rather than holding it and fixing the underlying defect through governance.
- **Accepting an answer that breaches a confidence to perform candour**, when a structural disclosure would have served the duty completely.
- **Rewarding an answer that lets the loudest item order a ranking**, rather than irreversibility and what only the candidate can do.

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a head of IT service management: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested reasoning at organisational scale: connecting maturity divergence to reporting-line structure, chaining policy provisions to their compelled conclusions, catching a contradicted universal claim inside a green board paper, allocating scarce investment and on-call budgets by marginal value rather than advocacy volume, reconciling an escalated dispute at the level where every party's evidence was true, and synthesising a three-year strategy from six mutually straining facts. The numeric section tested the decision arithmetic of function leadership: unit costs and demand-adjusted trends, weighted satisfaction and tiered availability decomposition, downtime economics, workforce modelling against a retirement cliff, expected-value portfolio ranking, counterfactual savings presentation, and total-cost comparisons read past the appraisal window's edge. The verbal section tested governance instruments read at exact strength: consultation rights that are not vetoes, exemptions that defer rather than remove controls, board minutes whose conditions are the operative content, and framework agreements whose notwithstanding clauses and escalation clocks decide disputes before they begin. The situational judgement section tested the hours that define the role: calibrated dates under board pressure, shadow functions converted into federated capability, controls held in a crisis, honest budget models against a gamed round, an inherited blocker addressed directly, confidences kept while committees hear the structural truth, and a strategy re-evaluated honestly when the organisation beneath it changed.

The through-line is the role itself: decompose before certifying, compute the counterfactual, read the clause at its stated strength, hold controls most visibly of all, champion the users who have no other advocate in the room, and steward the function beyond one's own tenure.
