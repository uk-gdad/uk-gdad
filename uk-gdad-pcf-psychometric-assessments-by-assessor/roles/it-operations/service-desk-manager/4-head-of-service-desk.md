## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of service desk, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Credible assessment at head-of-function level abandons generic aptitude material entirely: the material draws on the artefacts and decisions of service leadership — multi-year performance and demand trends, prediction models, portfolio views across services and channels, workforce and supplier strategies, governance papers, and board-level trade-offs — because that is the territory in which effectiveness is actually determined.

This role's distinctive demands shape all four assessments. Accountability spans first- and second-line support across existing and emerging ICT services, so cognitive items reason across service portfolios, not single tickets. The reporting skill is explicitly predictive — "use prediction methodologies to produce and maintain a holistic view of important trends across multiple processes" — so the numeric section works forecasts, capacity models, scenario comparisons, and the honest limits of extrapolation. SLAs and performance are managed at organisational scale, project teams are advised, and the team's capability to advise on future work is a named duty, so the verbal section uses strategy papers, governance submissions, supplier schedules, and research syntheses, where a misread clause or an overclaimed finding misdirects an organisation rather than a shift. Leadership operates through other leaders, so situational judgement scenarios involve managers, directors, suppliers, and ministerially visible programmes, where a candidate's choices set culture across an entire function.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role performs daily, using leadership-grade material rather than abstract puzzles: portfolio dashboards, governance rules, transformation plans, prediction outputs, and cross-service evidence sets. The mental operations — pattern recognition, deduction, error checking, prioritisation, applied problem solving — are the same at every level; what changes at head-of-function level is the structure of the material: more interacting variables, longer causal chains, subtler anomalies, and consequences that arrive through other people's subsequent decisions.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions, adaptively scored against a senior norm group.

Cognitive assessment earns its place at this level because the defining contributions are structural. Seeing the bigger picture across groups of services is portfolio pattern recognition. Producing a holistic view of trends across multiple processes means detecting signals that only exist across datasets. Ensuring the right actions are taken to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems means auditing other people's causal reasoning, not just performing one's own.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in the role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to the **Service focus** skill and the **IT service reporting** skill: cross-process trend detection across datasets never designed to be read together.
- **Logical deduction** maps to the **Service management framework knowledge** and SLA accountability.
- **Error checking** maps to the **Ownership** and **Asset and configuration management** skills: strategy papers, prediction models, and configuration baselines cross this desk for assurance.
- **Prioritisation** maps to accountability for the whole function.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to the **Problem management** skill — coordinating investigation across teams, judging when the right actions are being taken.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Cross-service pattern detection**

A portfolio dashboard shows month-on-month change in contact volume for five services: HR system +2%; finance system +1%; case management +18%; email 0%; intranet −1%. The case management service released a major upgrade three weeks ago. Which reading is most defensible?

- A) Demand across the estate is rising and the desk needs more staff.
- B) The portfolio is stable except case management, whose +18% coincides with its upgrade — investigate the upgrade's support impact specifically before any estate-wide response.
- C) The +18% is within normal variation and needs no attention.
- D) The intranet's −1% shows users are abandoning it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four of five services sit within ±2%; one shows +18% with a proximate candidate cause. The pattern is localised, not systemic. Option A converts one service's spike into an estate-wide staffing case; option C waves away an eighteen-fold departure from the noise band; option D over-reads noise into a narrative. This tests separating a distribution's signal from its noise before responding — estate-wide responses to single-service causes are among the most expensive category errors at this level.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance framework**

A service governance framework states: "A service enters enhanced monitoring if it misses any SLA for two consecutive months, or misses three SLAs in a single month. A service in enhanced monitoring returns to standard monitoring only after three consecutive fully-compliant months." The licensing service missed one SLA in April, was fully compliant in May, and missed two SLAs in June. What is its status at the start of July?

- A) Enhanced monitoring — it has missed SLAs in two months out of three.
- B) Standard monitoring — neither trigger has fired: no SLA missed in two consecutive months, and no month with three misses.
- C) Enhanced monitoring — two misses in June exceeds the threshold.
- D) Cannot be determined without knowing which SLAs were missed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** May's clean month breaks any consecutive-miss sequence, and June's maximum count (two) does not reach the three-in-a-month trigger. Option A substitutes an invented rule for the written one. This tests exact rule application, and a strong candidate's answer notices the design gap it exposes: a service can miss an SLA every other month indefinitely without ever entering enhanced monitoring — precise reading is also how design gaps in one's own governance framework are found.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a strategy paper's logic**

A draft desk strategy paper argues: "Channel shift is succeeding: portal contacts have risen 40% year on year while phone contacts have fallen 25%. Since portal contacts cost £5 against £14 for phone, the desk's total contact costs have therefore fallen." What is the flaw?

- A) The cost figures are implausible.
- B) The conclusion does not follow: percentage changes on unstated baselines cannot establish total cost movement — if phone volumes were small and portal volumes large, total cost could have risen.
- C) Channel shift is not a legitimate strategy.
- D) Year-on-year comparisons are invalid.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The argument moves from percentage changes to an absolute conclusion without the baselines that alone could support it — a constructed counterexample with realistic volumes shows total cost could rise while both cited percentages hold. This tests demanding the baselines before accepting a rate-to-total conclusion — percentages persuade; only volumes conclude.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a head-of-function's week**

Monday, 08:30. Four items:

1. A prediction model flags that at current trend, the desk breaches its contractual SLA floor in about seven weeks — the first time the model has flagged this.
2. A director asks for participation in a machinery-of-government working group scoping the absorption of another department's 400-person desk function; first meeting Thursday.
3. One of the service desk managers has submitted their resignation this morning; their notice period is four weeks.
4. This month's board pack is due Wednesday; an analyst has drafted it and asks for review.

Which order of attention is most defensible?

- A) 4, 1, 2, 3 — nearest deadline first
- B) 3, 1, 4, 2 — the resignation first (retention conversation is most time-critical), then the SLA flag's diagnosis, then the board pack, then Thursday's preparation
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — the SLA breach risk dominates everything
- D) 2, 3, 1, 4 — the machinery-of-government work is the most strategic item

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Retention probability decays by the hour, and the first conversation shapes handover quality regardless of outcome. The seven-week SLA flag is urgent precisely because early diagnosis is what converts a prediction into prevention. The board pack has a competent draft; Thursday's meeting is genuinely schedulable. This tests sequencing by what decays fastest and what involvement uniquely changes — strategy items are usually schedulable; people items usually are not.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Anomaly in a prediction model's output**

A demand forecast model, retrained monthly, has predicted next quarter's contact volume at 52,000 — a 30% jump from this quarter's 40,000, where previous forecasts moved 2-4% per quarter. No major service launches, migrations, or organisational changes are planned. What is the most defensible first response?

- A) Accept the forecast and begin recruiting the additional analysts it implies.
- B) Reject the forecast and reuse last quarter's, since 30% is obviously wrong.
- C) Audit the model's inputs and recent training data before acting — a step-change output without a step-change cause most often signals a data or configuration issue, though a real hidden driver must also be checked for.
- D) Average the new forecast with the old one and plan for 46,000.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A step-change output absent a known driver presents two checkable hypotheses: the world changed invisibly, or the instrument broke — both worth auditing before acting. Option A spends recruitment money on an unvalidated number; option B discards what might be the model's first genuinely valuable warning; option D produces a figure with no defensible meaning. This tests owning a model's failure modes: neither credulity nor dismissal toward a surprising output, but audit.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction across a supplier boundary**

A second-line contract states: (i) incidents escalated with complete diagnostic packages carry a 4-hour supplier response target; (ii) incidents escalated without complete packages carry an 8-hour target; (iii) the completeness standard is defined in Schedule 3, and disputes over completeness are resolved monthly by joint audit. This month the supplier reclassified 60% of escalations as "incomplete" — historically the figure is 15% — and average response times have lengthened accordingly. The escalation process has not changed. Which conclusion is soundest?

- A) The supplier is in breach of contract.
- B) The desk's diagnostic quality has collapsed.
- C) Neither breach nor desk failure is yet established: the reclassification spike, against an unchanged process, makes the completeness judgements themselves the question — which the contract's own joint audit mechanism exists to settle; trigger it with the data.
- D) The response targets should be renegotiated.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A quadrupled rejection rate against a stable input is exactly the anomaly the contract's own audit mechanism exists to adjudicate. Option A asserts breach before the contractually-defined arbiter has examined a case; option B accepts the supplier's classification as fact — the precise question at issue; option D concedes the mechanism by proposing to renegotiate targets over a classification dispute. This tests interrogating the measurement boundary before accusing either party — most interface disputes are classification disputes, and contracts that anticipate this contain their own resolution machinery.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits chain**

A transformation business case argues: "Consolidating our three regional service desks into one virtual desk will standardise processes; standardised processes will raise first-contact resolution; higher FCR will reduce repeat contacts; fewer repeat contacts will cut total demand 15%, funding the programme from savings." Which link deserves the most scrutiny?

- A) Consolidation → standardised processes
- B) Standardisation → higher FCR
- C) Higher FCR → fewer repeat contacts
- D) The quantified leap: fewer repeat contacts → 15% total demand reduction, which requires repeat contacts to be a large, known share of demand and the FCR gain to be a specific, evidenced size — neither is stated.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Every earlier link holds up qualitatively; the chain's weight-bearing failure is where plausibility suddenly becomes a funding-grade quantity with no stated arithmetic. This tests auditing a benefits chain for the point where a final number was chosen to fund the programme rather than derived from the mechanism — the head-of-function's question is always "show me the arithmetic from the mechanism to the number".

**Question 8 (moderate) — Pattern across processes**

Three monthly reports: Incident management — volumes flat, but median resolution time up 12% over two quarters. Problem management — open problem count up from 14 to 23 over the same period, average age rising. Change management — change volume up 40% following a new digital programme, emergency changes doubled. Read together, what is the most coherent holistic interpretation?

- A) Three unrelated trends, each for its process owner to manage.
- B) The digital programme's accelerated change load is plausibly the common driver: more (and more rushed) changes generate harder incidents — slower to resolve — and spawn underlying problems faster than investigation capacity absorbs them; the coherent response addresses change quality and problem capacity together, at programme level.
- C) The incident team has a performance problem and needs management attention.
- D) Problem management should close records faster to reduce the count.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Each report is individually ambiguous, but together they cohere around one mechanism with the right timing signature, while flat incident volume rules out simple demand growth. This tests cross-dataset causal assembly — a holistic view of trends across multiple processes — and locating the response at the level where the shared cause can actually be addressed, rather than three process owners each locally optimising.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint transformation sequencing**

Four initiatives over four quarters, one per quarter: (T) telephony platform replacement; (K) knowledge base consolidation; (A) password-reset automation; (M) absorbing a merged department's desk (mandated to complete by end of Q3). Constraints: (i) A requires the consolidated knowledge base, so K must precede A; (ii) merged staff must bed in on stable telephony, so at least two full quarters must separate M from T; (iii) the merged department joins on the existing platform, so M must precede T. Which sequence is valid?

- A) K, M, A, T
- B) M, K, A, T
- C) K, A, M, T
- D) M, T, K, A

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Encoding the constraints and testing each candidate: option A separates M (Q2) and T (Q4) by only one full quarter; options C and D place M and T adjacent. Option B — M(Q1), K(Q2), A(Q3), T(Q4) — satisfies every constraint with two full quarters between M and T. This tests anchoring on the tightest constraint first (the separation rule), and a strong candidate's answer notes the incidental virtue that the externally-mandated item lands earliest, holding maximum buffer.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Auditing a problem investigation**

A recurring outage has taken a service down three times in six weeks. A problem manager reports: "Root cause found: each outage followed a memory spike on the application server. We've scheduled weekly server restarts, and there have been no outages in the two weeks since. Closing the problem record." As the accountable head, what is the soundest assessment?

- A) Endorse closure — the fix demonstrably works.
- B) The investigation has found a precursor and applied a suppressant, not found a root cause: what causes the memory spikes remains unknown, weekly restarts mask rather than remove it, and two quiet weeks against a three-in-six-weeks base rate is weak evidence; keep the record open with the restart as an interim workaround, and direct investigation at the spike's cause.
- C) Reject the work and reassign the investigation to the supplier.
- D) Close the record but add a monitoring alert for memory spikes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Outages follow memory spikes" is a mechanism link one step back, not the spikes' own cause; two quiet weeks against a three-in-six-weeks base rate is nearly uninformative. Option A rewards a suppressed symptom presented as a solved problem. Option D institutionalises the confusion by monitoring a symptom while closing the search for its cause. This tests the question every head of function should ask of a claimed root cause: "if we stopped the workaround, would the problem return?"

**Question 11 (challenging) — Strategic inference from long-run channel data**

Five years of contact data: phone share fallen from 70% to 45%; portal share risen from 10% to 35%; chat from 0% to 12%; email stable around 8%. Phone contacts' average complexity rating has doubled; satisfaction by channel shows phone 4.4, portal 3.7, chat 4.1. A board member proposes: "The trend is clear — accelerate phone's retirement and target a portal-first desk within two years." What is the strongest assessment of this proposal?

- A) Agree — five years of consistent migration is decisive evidence of user preference.
- B) The trend is real but the inference is flawed: the migration has been selective, moving simple contacts out of phone and leaving it the channel of complex need — phone's doubled complexity and highest satisfaction suggest it now serves precisely the contacts the portal (satisfaction 3.7) handles worst; retiring phone would force the hardest contacts onto the weakest channel. The strategy should be channel-by-need: grow portal for the simple, protect assisted channels for the complex, and fix portal experience before any further shift.
- C) Disagree — channel strategy should not change while any users prefer phone.
- D) Agree, but extend the timeline to three years.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Migration out of phone has not been random — self-serviceable contacts left, complex ones stayed, evidenced by phone's doubling complexity rating and highest satisfaction. Projecting the share trend to zero commits the classic extrapolation error of treating a selection process as a preference process. This tests recognising channel mix as an allocation of contact types to channel strengths, not a race with a single winner, and reframing the strategy as right-channel-first rather than portal-first.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate separates portfolio-level signal from noise** before responding at the right scope.
- **Watch for whether a candidate reads a governance rule's exact letter** rather than its gist.
- **Note whether a candidate demands baselines behind a rate-based conclusion**, and audits a benefits chain for its unquantified leap.
- **Note whether a candidate treats a surprising model output with audit rather than credulity or dismissal.**
- **Use the long-run channel data item (Question 11) to observe whether a candidate distinguishes a selection process from a preference process.**
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that responds at the wrong scope** — estate-wide answers to single-service anomalies, or single-process answers to cross-process patterns.
- **Crediting an answer that substitutes a rule's gist for its exact letter.**
- **Missing when a candidate accepts a rate-based argument without its baselines.**
- **Accepting an answer that confuses suppression of a symptom with resolution of its cause.**
- **Missing when a candidate extrapolates a selection process (the easy cases leaving) as if it were a preference trend.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment at head-of-function level measures fluency with the quantitative material on which service leadership decisions rest: multi-year budgets, workforce models, prediction outputs and their confidence limits, portfolio SLA aggregates, benefits cases, and cross-channel cost economics. The mathematics remains percentages, ratios, weighted averages, rates, and projections, deployed on leadership-shaped problems: several steps, several sources, assumptions that must be surfaced.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions built on tables, charts, and short data narratives.

Numeracy is explicit in the role's skill set: prediction methodologies producing a holistic view of trends are named competencies, and this role is the last numerate checkpoint before figures reach boards, directors, and ministers' offices.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Forecasting and trend arithmetic** map directly to the **IT service reporting** skill.
- **Budget and workforce modelling** map to accountability for the function's resourcing.
- **Portfolio aggregation** maps to the **Service focus** skill.
- **Benefits and cost-model assurance** map to the **Customer service management** duty to investigate automation and the **Ownership** skill.
- **SLA and performance arithmetic** map to the duty to manage and monitor SLAs and performance.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio SLA aggregation**

Across three supported channels last month: phone — 5,000 contacts, 4,650 within SLA; portal — 3,000 contacts, 2,850 within SLA; chat — 2,000 contacts, 1,820 within SLA. What was the portfolio within-SLA rate?

- A) 92.4%
- B) 93.0%
- C) 93.2%
- D) 94.0%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 9,320÷10,000=93.2%. The unweighted average of the three channel rates (93.0%) is a temptingly close trap. This tests aggregating from counts, since every downstream decision (penalty exposure, channel investment) inherits the accuracy of the board figure.

**Question 2 (easy) — Budget share and variance**

An annual budget is £3.6 million: staff £2.7 million, technology £600,000, and the remainder for training and other costs. Mid-year, technology spend is £340,000 against a straight-line expectation. What share of budget is training-and-other, and is technology over or under its mid-year expectation?

- A) 8.3% share; technology £40,000 over
- B) 8.3% share; technology £40,000 under
- C) 10% share; technology £40,000 over
- D) 12.5% share; technology on track

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Training-and-other = £300,000 = 8.33% share. Straight-line mid-year expectation = £300,000; actual £340,000 is £40,000 over. This tests both parts together, and a strong candidate's answer notes a 13% mid-year overspend on a £600,000 line is a signal worth a question about timing versus trend.

**Question 3 (easy) — Attrition and recruitment pipeline**

A function has 60 analysts. Historical attrition is 15% per year, and the training pipeline takes a quarter to bring a recruit to productive standard. Approximately how many analysts will leave over the next 12 months, and how many should be in the pipeline at any time to hold steady-state headcount?

- A) 9 leavers; about 2-3 in the pipeline at any time
- B) 9 leavers; 9 in the pipeline at any time
- C) 6 leavers; about 2-3 in the pipeline at any time
- D) 15 leavers; 4 in the pipeline at any time

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 60×0.15=9 leavers/year ≈ 2.25/quarter; with a quarter-long pipeline, steady state needs roughly one quarter's worth of leavers in flight at any time — 2-3, not the full year's 9 (option B, which would overstaff within months). This tests distinguishing an annual flow from an in-flight stock, a core workforce-planning distinction.

**Question 4 (easy) — Cost per contact across channels**

Channel economics this year: phone — 60,000 contacts at £14.00 each; portal — 50,000 contacts at £4.00 each; chat — 25,000 contacts at £7.00 each. What is the blended cost per contact across the desk?

- A) £8.33
- B) £8.68
- C) £9.00
- D) £9.26

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total cost £1,215,000 ÷ 135,000 contacts = £9.00. The unweighted average of the three per-contact figures (£8.33, option A) understates the true cost base by underweighting phone's large volume and high unit cost. This tests producing a blended figure from totals, since it is the desk's benchmark currency, quoted across departments.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Forecast from a measured growth rate**

Portal contacts have grown steadily: 30,000 two years ago, 36,000 last year, 43,200 this year. If the growth rate continues, what is the two-year-ahead forecast?

- A) 51,840
- B) 57,600
- C) 62,208
- D) 86,400

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 20% consistent annual growth compounds to 43,200×1.20×1.20=62,208. Option B applies linear growth, understating compounding. This tests verifying rate stability before compounding, and a strong candidate's answer carries the assumption visibly with the number — "62,208, assuming the observed 20% growth persists".

**Question 6 (moderate) — Confidence in a prediction range**

A demand model forecasts next quarter at 48,000 contacts, with an 80% prediction interval of 44,000 to 52,000. Capacity planning is being finalised. Which use of this output is soundest?

- A) Staff for exactly 48,000 — the central estimate is the model's best guess.
- B) Staff for 52,000 — always plan for the worst case.
- C) Staff core capacity around the central estimate with flexible surge provision (overtime agreements, cross-trained staff, supplier overflow) sized toward the upper bound, explicitly accepting a roughly 10% chance that demand exceeds even that — and document the residual risk.
- D) Ask the model team to narrow the interval before planning.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** An 80% interval implies roughly 10% probability above 52,000 and 10% below 44,000. Option A converts every above-median month into a queue; option B permanently overstaffs for the roughly 90% of quarters demand falls lower; option D asks for what only better data, not a request, can produce. This tests reading an interval as information for a mixed core-plus-surge plan, with the residual tail documented rather than ignored.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Consolidation benefits arithmetic**

A consolidation case projects merging three regional desks (costing £1.4m, £1.1m, and £0.9m annually) into one virtual desk costing £2.8m annually, after one-off transition costs of £750,000. What is the annual saving, the simple payback period, and the three-year net benefit?

- A) £600,000 saving; 15-month payback; £1,050,000 three-year net benefit
- B) £600,000 saving; 12.5-month payback; £1,150,000 three-year net benefit
- C) £400,000 saving; 22.5-month payback; £450,000 three-year net benefit
- D) £600,000 saving; 15-month payback; £850,000 three-year net benefit

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Saving = £3.4m−£2.8m=£600,000. Payback = 750,000÷600,000=1.25 years=15 months. Three-year net = (3×600,000)−750,000=£1,050,000. This tests reconciling all three figures together, and a strong candidate's answer stress-tests the flattering assumptions behind them — day-one full savings, on-schedule transition — rather than stopping at the arithmetic.

**Question 8 (moderate) — SLA penalty exposure**

A service credit regime: in any month where the within-SLA rate falls below the 92% floor, the desk credits back 2% of the monthly charge (£250,000) — £5,000 — per full percentage point below the floor. Last six months: 94.1%, 93.2%, 92.5%, 91.0%, 90.4%, 89.8%. What credits were incurred over the six months, and if the recent decline continues at its current rate, roughly what exposure does the next quarter carry?

- A) £20,000 incurred; roughly £45,000 next quarter
- B) £20,000 incurred; about £20,000 next quarter
- C) £10,000 incurred; roughly £45,000 next quarter
- D) £30,000 incurred; roughly £60,000 next quarter

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Applying the "full percentage point" convention month by month gives £0+£0+£0+£5,000+£5,000+£10,000=£20,000 incurred. Continuing the recent 0.6-point monthly decline into next quarter gives roughly £10,000+£15,000+£20,000=£45,000 — more than double the past half-year in a single quarter, because the regime's cost accelerates as performance sinks further below the floor. This tests both computing exposure with the exact rounding convention and projecting forward rather than repeating the past.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Automation at portfolio scale**

Three automation candidates with one year's funding for one: (a) password resets — 21,000 contacts/year, £9 each, automation diverts 75%, platform cost £60,000/year; (b) software requests — 9,000 contacts/year, £16 each, diverts 60%, cost £45,000/year; (c) starter/leaver processing — 4,500 contacts/year, £30 each, diverts 80%, cost £70,000/year. Automated handling costs £1.50 per diverted contact in all cases. Which ranks first on annual net saving?

- A) Password resets — highest volume always wins
- B) Software requests
- C) Starter/leaver processing
- D) Password resets and starter/leaver tie

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Net = diverted × (unit cost − £1.50) − platform: (a) £58,125; (b) £33,300; (c) £32,600. Password resets wins, but not because "volume always wins" — (c) nearly matches (b) on a fifth of the volume. This tests computing identically structured nets for each option, and a strong candidate's answer notes (b) and (c) are close enough that the ranking between them should be sensitivity-tested, not trusted at face value.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Mix effect in a headline improvement**

A board pack shows average cost per contact falling from £9.20 to £8.40 over a year, presented as an efficiency gain. Underlying data: phone cost rose from £13.00 to £14.00 per contact, and portal cost rose from £4.00 to £4.20; but portal's share of volume grew from 40% to 55%. Which statement should accompany the headline?

- A) No caveat needed — the blended figure fell, which is what matters financially.
- B) The blended fall is entirely channel mix: both channels became more expensive per contact, and the headline improvement reflects users migrating to the cheaper channel — real and valuable, but a different claim from operational efficiency, and phone's 7.7% unit-cost rise needs its own explanation.
- C) The figures are contradictory and the data team should re-extract them.
- D) The phone cost rise should be excluded as an outlier.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both channel unit costs rose while the blend fell — arithmetically possible only because the mix shifted toward the cheaper channel. This tests decomposing a headline "efficiency" figure that is actually a migration effect, before the effect saturates and the trend quietly reverses.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Capacity for an absorbed function**

A machinery-of-government change transfers a 6,000-user department in Q3. Their previous desk handled 1.4 contacts per user per month with 20 analysts. The current desk runs at 0.9 contacts per user per month, and analysts each handle 700 contacts per month. Transition experience elsewhere suggests absorbed populations run 25% above their historical contact rate for the first two quarters. How many additional analysts are needed for the first two quarters, and at steady state if the population converges to the 0.9 rate?

- A) 15 for transition; 8 at steady state
- B) 15 for transition; 12 at steady state
- C) 12 for transition; 8 at steady state
- D) 20 for transition; 20 at steady state — match their previous staffing

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Transition: 1.4×1.25=1.75 rate; 6,000×1.75=10,500 contacts; 10,500÷700=15 analysts. Steady state: 6,000×0.9=5,400; 5,400÷700=7.7→8. Option D imports the transferring desk's staffing without importing its (lower) productivity. This tests correctly applying the uplift for transition and rounding capacity up, and a strong candidate's answer flags the convergence assumption (1.4→0.9) as worth validating early, since a wrong assumption changes the budget case by half a million pounds.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate assembles every combined figure from raw counts**, never averaging averages.
- **Watch for whether a candidate reads a prediction interval as a staffing decision** (core plus surge) rather than a single central number.
- **Note whether a candidate verifies a benefits case's own arithmetic** and then stress-tests its flattering assumptions.
- **Note whether a candidate fixes a boundary or rounding convention explicitly** before computing contractual exposure.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an average of averages at portfolio scale.**
- **Crediting a plan built on a forecast's central estimate alone**, ignoring the interval it came with.
- **Missing when a candidate reads a mix effect as genuine operational efficiency.**
- **Accepting an absorbed function's staffing ratio imported wholesale** without pricing demand at the receiving desk's own productivity.
- **Missing when a candidate sizes a recruitment pipeline to an annual flow** rather than the in-flight stock.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures precision in reading, inference, and expression across the written material this role commands: strategy and policy papers, governance submissions, contract schedules, audit findings, research syntheses, and correspondence with directors, suppliers, and programme leadership. The core format presents a passage followed by statements to judge **True**, **False**, or **Cannot say**; complementary formats test best-summary judgement, inference discipline, and audience-exact wording.

The typical administration is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 statements. The governing rule — answer from the passage alone — is at its most demanding for the most experienced candidates.

At head-of-function level the stakes of verbal precision are institutional: reading errors scale into remediation programmes and investment redirection, and writing errors are quoted in board minutes and supplier disputes. A distinctive senior burden: much of what is read is written to persuade — business cases, supplier accounts, programme assurances — so separating what a text establishes from what it merely suggests is a daily defensive skill.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Exact comprehension of governance and contract text** maps to the **Service management framework knowledge** and SLA accountability.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to the **Ownership** and **IT service reporting** skills.
- **Inference restraint with research and trend narratives** maps to the **User focus** skill.
- **Faithful synthesis for boards** maps to the holistic **IT service reporting** duty.
- **Audience-exact expression** maps to leadership of managers and the advisory duty to project teams.

### Practice questions

For Questions 1 to 3, the candidate reads this audit extract.

> "The service desk's asset register was sampled against physical devices at three sites. Of 150 sampled records, 132 matched fully; 11 showed incorrect locations; 7 could not be matched to a physical device. However, the procedure's reconciliation step is specified as quarterly, and evidence showed the last three reconciliations were completed between five and seven weeks late. We recommend the desk implement automated reconciliation alerts and complete a full register validation within three months."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Control design adequate" and the procedure described as "comprehensive and current" directly contradict the statement — the deficiency is in operation, not design. This tests the design/operation distinction, audit's most load-bearing vocabulary, preserved through onward reporting rather than collapsed into "the procedure is deficient".

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** (132+11)÷150=95.3%, exceeding 90%. This tests verifying a quantitative claim against source arithmetic rather than tone — an audit's critical framing can prime an underrating of how strong the underlying match rate actually is.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes concentration at the merger site, a correlation with an obvious candidate mechanism, but asserts only location, not causation. This tests resisting a causal reading that experienced practitioners are especially prone to supply themselves — plausibility is not the passage's assertion.

For Questions 4 to 6, the candidate reads this supplier annual service review extract.

> "Our partnership has delivered consistently strong outcomes this year. Response targets were achieved in 11 of 12 months, and customer satisfaction with our second-line service averaged 4.1. - B) False

**Question 5 (moderate) — Reading the proposal's structure**

Which statement best characterises the supplier's two forward-looking proposals?

- A) Routine contract housekeeping with no commercial significance.
- B) Two linked repositionings favourable to the supplier: replacing response metrics (which they occasionally miss) with "resolution-focused" ones whose definition is not yet stated, and preparing a price increase justified by a complexity claim for which the paper offers no evidence.
- C) Customer-focused improvements that the desk should accept as drafted.
- D) A threat to terminate the contract at renewal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both proposals replace a measured obligation with an undefined or unevidenced one, and both quietly frame the desk's demand as the driver of the supplier's costs. **Question 6 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The supplier paid a service credit for February."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "We nonetheless credit the month in line with the contract" directly asserts payment. For Questions 7 to 9, the candidate reads this draft strategy paper extract.

> "User needs have evolved significantly over five years. Our research shows staff increasingly expect consumer-grade self-service, and our portal now resolves 34% of all contacts without analyst involvement, up from 9%. However, assisted channels remain critical: satisfaction among users with accessibility needs is 0.8 points lower on the portal than on the phone, and 62% of contacts from frontline operational staff — who work away from desks — arrive by phone or chat. Our strategy is therefore 'digital first, but never digital only': we will invest in portal capability while maintaining assisted channels at full quality, and we will measure success by resolution and satisfaction across every channel, not by channel shift alone. **Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The paper proposes reducing investment in assisted channels as users migrate to self-service."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The paper commits to the opposite: "never digital only", and over 40% of the requested funding goes to assisted-channel modernisation. This tests catching a common compression error — "digital first" headlines are routinely misread as "digital only, eventually", and a strong candidate's answer checks the allocation table against the stated principle.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Inference from the evidence structure**

Which conclusion is best supported by the passage's evidence as presented?

- A) The accessibility satisfaction gap proves the portal fails accessibility standards.
- B) The paper's evidence supports differentiated channel investment: self-service resolution has grown strongly (9% to 34%), while two identified populations — users with accessibility needs and deskless frontline staff — currently depend disproportionately on assisted channels, which the funding split addresses.
- C) Frontline staff prefer phone and chat and will never adopt the portal.
- D) The portal will resolve a majority of contacts within two years if the trend continues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B restates exactly what the cited evidence carries, no more. Option A converts a satisfaction gap into an unstated compliance verdict; option C converts a current channel share into a permanent preference; option D extrapolates a trend without a stated trajectory. This tests declining three distinct evidence inflations that boards are persuaded by daily.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Best summary for the board**

Compressing this paper into two sentences for the agenda pack, which version is best?

- A) "The desk requests £1.2m to continue its shift to digital self-service, which now resolves 34% of contacts. Assisted channels will be maintained during the transition."
- B) "Self-service resolution has grown from 9% to 34% in five years, but accessibility-need users and deskless frontline staff still depend on assisted channels, where satisfaction and volume data justify continued quality. - C) "The desk proposes a digital-first strategy costing £1.2m. Users increasingly expect consumer-grade experiences and the investment reflects this."
- D) "Following user research, the desk will invest £1.2m in its award-winning portal while protecting traditional channels for users who struggle with technology."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B preserves the complete decision structure: evidence, allocation, and success measure. Option A quietly recasts assisted channels as temporary scaffolding, reversing the paper's strategic centre while sounding faithful — the most dangerous species of compression. This tests synthesis that transmits a source's structure, not a plausible neighbour of it.

For Questions 10 to 11, the candidate reads this email from a chief operating officer.

I should say the committee has asked whether shared services options ought to be examined; no decision has been taken, and I'd rather hear your assessment than have the committee form one without it."

**Question 10 (challenging) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The chief operating officer has decided to move the desk into a shared services arrangement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email explicitly states "no decision has been taken", and the shared-services question is attributed to the committee, not the COO. This tests reading modality exactly under the pressure of an unwelcome-sounding message — the accurate reading is more demanding, not more comfortable, since it means first authorship of the analysis is being offered.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Choosing the reply's opening position**

Which opening approach for a written response to the COO is most effective?

- A) A defence of the desk: the headcount growth was approved, the flat costs reflect inflation absorption, and other departments' automation figures are not comparable.
- B) An acknowledgement that the questions are the right ones, followed by full-candour analysis: where the operating model is strong (with evidence), where it is genuinely behind (automation adoption, if true), what would change with a free hand — including options that reduce one's own empire — and a framework for how shared-services alternatives should be evaluated on user outcomes and total cost.
- C) A request to delay the committee discussion until a consultancy review can be commissioned.
- D) A concise factual pack — the figures, their definitions, and their context — with no assessment, since the committee should form its own view.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email explicitly asks for assessment ahead of the committee forming its own; first credible analysis anchors the discussion. Option A converts an invited assessment into a rebuttal, confirming the very concern a self-examining leadership is meant to allay. Option D withholds the requested judgement. This tests responding to scrutiny with fuller candour than expected, structured to shape the criteria by which alternatives get evaluated.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate preserves source vocabulary** (design versus operation, response versus resolution) in a summary or onward account.
- **Watch for whether a candidate separates what a persuasive document asserts from what it merely implies.**
- **Note whether a candidate verifies every quantitative claim against the passage's own arithmetic**, in both directions.
- **Note whether a candidate reads modality precisely** in pressure or scrutiny correspondence.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer drawn from professional experience** rather than the passage as written.
- **Missing when a candidate collapses audit or contract vocabulary** into a looser paraphrase.
- **Crediting an answer that accepts an implied allocation of responsibility** as if it were asserted.
- **Missing an evidence inflation** — a satisfaction gap read as a compliance verdict, a channel share read as a preference, a trend read as a destiny.
- **Accepting a summary that preserves a source's tone while reversing its structure.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to evaluate responses — selecting the most effective, identifying most and least effective, ranking, or rating each option. It measures professional judgement: weighing users, staff, suppliers, governance, truth, and organisational interest when they conflict, which at head-of-function level is the substance of the job.

The typical administration is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, generously timed. Three properties distinguish scenarios at this level: leadership acts through others, so strong responses develop and correct through the management line rather than around it; choices become precedent, since every visible decision teaches managers, suppliers, and directors what to expect next time; and dilemmas are governance-shaped — risk is surfaced, priced, and owned at the right level, and the interests of users absent from every meeting are the leader's to represent.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Accountability at organisational scale** maps to the **Ownership** skill.
- **Leading through managers** maps to responsibility for the management team's development.
- **Governance of risk and transition** maps to the duty to support emerging ICT services and advise project teams.
- **Evidence over advocacy** maps to the **IT service reporting** and **Problem management** skills.
- **Strategic user focus** maps to the **User focus** and **Service focus** skills.
- **Continual improvement culture** maps to the **Customer service management** skill.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — The major incident post-mortem**

A six-hour outage of a critical service last week embarrassed the department; the desk's initial misdiagnosis added two hours. In a review meeting chaired by the candidate, including managers and the supplier, a service desk manager presents a timeline that soft-pedals the misdiagnosis, attributing the delay mainly to the supplier's slow escalation acceptance. The supplier's account manager visibly bristles. What is the most effective intervention?

- A) Let the presentation stand — the manager's credibility should be protected in front of the supplier.
- B) Correct the record in the meeting, factually and without heat: restate the timeline including the desk's two-hour misdiagnosis alongside the supplier's slow acceptance, and redirect the meeting from allocation of blame to the mechanisms that allowed both failures; afterwards, privately coach the manager on why the desk's account must be the most accurate in the room.
- C) Adjourn the meeting and reconvene once the timeline is agreed offline.
- D) Correct the manager firmly in the meeting so the supplier sees the desk holds itself to account.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A soft-pedalled timeline left standing becomes the desk's official story, and the supplier, holding their own logs, will disprove it later at maximum cost to credibility. Option D performs accountability at the manager's expense; option A protects a falsehood the supplier's reaction shows is already contested. This tests correcting a record immediately and factually in the room, then developing the person privately — the head-level pattern for accountability moments involving a subordinate's error in front of external parties.

**Question 2 (easy) — The green dashboard and the corridor conversation**

A monthly dashboard shows all services green. A frontline analyst mentions, hesitantly, that "mornings have been chaos for weeks" on a service the dashboard rates healthy — long queues before 10:00 that vanish by the afternoon measurement snapshots. What is the most effective response?

- A) Explain the dashboard methodology so the analyst understands why the service is genuinely green.
- B) Thank them seriously, have the intraday data pulled — queue lengths by hour, not daily averages — and if the morning pattern is real, treat it as two findings: a service problem to fix, and a measurement design that averages away the worst user experience; then close the loop with the analyst either way.
- C) Ask the analyst to raise it through their manager, since the management line exists for exactly this.
- D) Note it for the next quarterly measurement review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Daily averages are constitutionally blind to intraday concentration — a service can run four-hour morning queues and average green. Option A converts a report into a rebuttal. Option D files a live user-experience failure for quarterly consideration. This tests treating inconvenient anecdote from the frontline as a hypothesis worth immediate data, and closing the loop as infrastructure that keeps ground truth flowing upward.

**Question 3 (easy) — The capability gap in a candidate's management team**

Preparing next year's structure, the desk needs serious data and automation capability at management level. One of three service desk managers — solid operationally, twenty years' service, deeply loyal — has repeatedly declined data-skills development, saying they "manage people, not spreadsheets". The other two are developing well. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Design the structure around the gap: route data-dependent responsibilities to the other two managers permanently.
- B) Have the direct conversation being avoided: the role is changing — prediction, automation, and analytics are now management-level requirements, not optional extras; offer serious, supported development with time and coaching, be honest that the requirement is not waivable, and give a genuine period to step up before any structural conclusion.
- C) Include the requirement in next year's objectives and let the appraisal cycle surface the issue formally.
- D) Move them to a project-liaison role where the gap matters less, framing it as recognition of their operational strengths.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Twenty years of service has earned a direct conversation, not a quiet re-architecture; "not waivable" said plainly, with genuine support attached, gives an actual choice with actual information. Option A embeds the gap structurally and quietly writes someone off. Option D is a misdirection both parties will eventually decode. This tests shaping capability through direct expectation plus real support, rather than an instrument that substitutes for the conversation.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The programme that wants a number**

A major digital programme, politically visible and behind schedule, asks for a signed assurance that the desk "will be ready" to support its national launch in ten weeks. The honest position: readiness is achievable but not yet achieved — training is 60% complete, the knowledge base is drafted but unvalidated, and the programme has slipped twice before, stopping earlier preparation both times. The programme director needs the signed assurance for a ministerial submission this week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Sign — the gaps are closable in ten weeks, and refusing would make the desk the story.
- B) Refuse to sign until readiness is complete, whatever the submission timetable.
- C) Provide a conditional assurance in writing: state what is complete, what remains, the dates each remaining item closes, and the dependencies on programme stability — signing to the plan and its conditions rather than to an unconditional "ready", and offering to brief the submission's drafters on accurate wording.
- D) Sign, but keep a private file note recording that readiness was incomplete at signature.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The request conflates "we will be ready" (a forecast with conditions) and "we are ready" (a state); a conditional assurance is the accurate instrument, not a hedge. Option A signs a state that does not exist; option D documents bad faith rather than avoiding it. This tests signing what is true, conditioning what is contingent, and making the accurate version usable by the people under pressure.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The automation programme and the workforce**

A three-year strategy includes automation reducing first-line demand by an estimated 35%. Finance wants the benefit banked as headcount reduction in next year's budget. Workforce analysis shows natural attrition over three years covers most of the reduction, and remaining roles can shift toward complex second-line and advisory work — but only if the reduction is phased with attrition rather than taken up front. What is the most effective position?

- A) Accept finance's timetable — automation benefits not banked promptly tend never to be banked.
- B) Contest the reduction entirely: the desk should retain all posts and redeploy everyone.
- C) Present the workforce arithmetic as the business case: commit to the full 35% benefit, but phased against attrition, showing forced early reduction pays redundancy costs to remove people whose capacity the transition itself needs — and offer finance a tracked benefits schedule with dates, so the commitment is auditable rather than deferred.
- D) Agree the reduction in principle and quietly delay its implementation through vacancy management.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The phasing is argued in finance's own currency: early forced reduction costs more, not less. Option A trades the transition's operational success for the current budget round. Option D is agreement performed while implementation is withheld, discoverable and corrosive of trust. This tests resolving a genuine tension with better arithmetic rather than capitulation or resistance, owning both the saving and its safe extraction path.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The user constituency nobody champions**

Satisfaction data is healthy except among users with accessibility needs, where portal satisfaction runs 0.8 points below average — a gap unchanged for two years. Fixing it costs £150k. The affected population is roughly 4% of users; the same £150k on general portal features would improve satisfaction modestly for the other 96%. What is the most effective position for the improvement budget round?

- A) Fund the general improvements — the greatest good for the greatest number is the defensible public-sector calculus.
- B) Fund the accessibility remediation, and frame it correctly upwards: public services carry obligations to accessible provision that are not subject to utilitarian netting; a two-year-old known gap for users who often have no alternative channel is a service failure and plausibly a legal exposure, not one bid among peers — while directing the general improvements into next year's case.
- C) Split the funding between the two, delivering partial progress on each.
- D) Defer both and commission research into whether the accessibility gap reflects genuine need.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The utilitarian framing prices a duty as if it were a preference. Two years' persistence is the profile of organisational neglect at exactly the altitude where such gaps get owned or perpetually outranked. Option D commissions research into a question two years of data has already answered. This tests distinguishing obligations from optimisations before comparing them.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the failing absorbed desk**

Three months after absorbing another department's desk, its former analysts' performance is well below standard: longer calls, lower FCR, higher error rates. Managers are frustrated; some absorbed staff are demoralised; one original team leader refers to them as "the legacy lot" in a meeting. Four candidate responses:

1. Diagnose before judging: commission a proper look at whether the gap is skills, tooling familiarity, process differences, or morale — while publicly framing the absorbed team as colleagues whose integration is the function's shared responsibility, and correcting the "legacy lot" language on the spot.
2. Set the absorbed staff a three-month performance improvement window with individual targets.
3. Pair each absorbed analyst with an experienced mentor and fund conversion training, without further diagnosis.
4. Accept a transition dip as normal and let time integrate the teams.

Which is the MOST effective and which is the LEAST effective response?

- A) Most: 1; Least: 4
- B) Most: 3; Least: 2
- C) Most: 1; Least: 2
- D) Most: 3; Least: 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option 1 sequences correctly (diagnose the mechanism, which could be skills, tooling, process, or morale) and corrects damaging language in the moment. Option 3 is premature — the right remedy for a skills-and-familiarity diagnosis not yet made. Option 2 is worse than passive: applying individual performance management to a cohort three months after an organisational upheaval attributes a structural situation to personal failing. This tests diagnosing structure before managing individuals, and treating integration language as a leadership-owned artefact.

**Question 8 (challenging) — The unwelcome prediction**

A demand model championed and board-funded now forecasts that a flagship departmental app's next release will drive desk contact volumes 20% over budgeted capacity for a quarter. The release owner, a well-connected director, disputes the model informally: "Your model overreacts; the release is mostly cosmetic." The data team stands by the forecast with moderate confidence. The board meets next week. What is the most effective course?

- A) Present the forecast with its confidence attached, the director's dissent fairly stated, the checkable assumptions listed, and a contingent mitigation plan (surge capacity triggers) that the board can fund cheaply against the risk — inviting the board to decide with the disagreement visible.
- B) Soften the forecast to a "possible moderate increase" to preserve relationships and revisit after the release.
- C) Withhold the forecast this cycle; if early post-release data confirms it, present with evidence next month.
- D) Present the forecast at full strength and characterise the director's objection as unevidenced.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The value of a funded forecasting capability is realised exactly when its output is unwelcome. Option B blurs a specific, actionable prediction into an unfalsifiable murmur. Option C converts foresight into hindsight after the surge-capacity lead time has passed. Option D wins the argument and burns the coalition the forecasting function depends on. This tests full strength on the finding, full fairness to the dissent, and a cheap contingent action that makes the board's decision easy under either truth.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The supplier failure with a personal dimension**

An out-of-hours support supplier has missed response targets for four consecutive months. The account director — known to be managing a serious family illness, and personally candid about it — keeps promising recovery that does not materialise. Escalating formally within the supplier will likely end their account leadership; not escalating leaves overnight users of critical services exposed, and governance now requires action. What is the most effective course?

- A) Give the account director one further quarter, given the circumstances and the relationship's long-term value.
- B) Escalate formally to the supplier's leadership about the service, as governance requires — framing the failure institutionally (four months of missed targets is an organisational resourcing failure, not one person's) — while separately and privately telling the account director what is being done and why before the letter lands.
- C) Escalate formally and include knowledge of the account director's personal situation so the supplier's leadership has full context.
- D) Bypass the account director informally: ask the supplier's delivery director to quietly fix the resourcing without a formal escalation on file.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four months of exposure for overnight users of critical services makes escalation non-optional. Option B keeps the escalation institutional in framing (true, and the account director's best protection), gives private notice (honouring the relationship without subordinating the service), and keeps the confidence kept — option C breaches it under the flag of "context". Option A extends users' exposure as a private kindness funded from public risk. This tests exercising formal accountability with kindness shown through its framing and notice, never through whether it happens.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The shared-services study**

The department commissions a formal study of moving the desk into a cross-government shared-services arrangement. The evidence suggests the move would degrade service for specialised user groups — but this judgement is structurally conflicted, since the move would shrink the head's own role, and everyone in the room knows it. The study team asks for the desk's nominated representative and its data. What is the most effective posture?

- A) Nominate the strongest advocate, provide the data with accompanying analysis highlighting the risks of the move, and work departmental relationships to ensure the study hears the counter-case early and often.
- B) Provide complete, unspun data; nominate a credible expert rather than an advocate; put the service-degradation concerns into the study as specific, testable claims (which user groups, which requirements, what evidence) while explicitly acknowledging the structural conflict; and privately prepare the function to succeed under either outcome.
- C) Decline close involvement so the study is visibly independent, and let its conclusions arrive on their own merits.
- D) Cooperate formally while quietly briefing sympathetic stakeholders on the study's likely flaws.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Complete, unspun data and an expert (not advocate) nomination are non-negotiable when structurally conflicted — curated evidence converts a legitimate judgement into the self-interest everyone already suspects. Converting concerns into testable claims lets the study verify them independently of the conflict. This tests maximising the testability of claims and the completeness of evidence when personally conflicted, rather than either advocacy or abdication.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The culture signal**

Reviewing exit interviews, a pattern emerges: departing analysts describe the middle management layer as "results-focused but fear-based" — mistakes hidden, sickness worked through, overtime unclaimed and expected. Dashboards show none of this: performance is strong, attrition is at benchmark, and the service desk managers described are ones directly developed and known to be reliable. What is the most effective response?

- A) Treat the pattern as serious signal despite its conflict with direct experience: triangulate quietly (staff survey cuts, overtime and sickness data, skip-level conversations run as listening rather than investigation), examine one's own contribution first — whether a results-focus and delivery pressure created the incentives the managers are transmitting — then address findings with the management team as a shared culture problem to fix, not an accusation to defend against.
- B) Dismiss it proportionately: exit interviews over-sample the disaffected, dashboards are healthy, and managers who deliver reliably have earned the benefit of the doubt.
- C) Confront the management team with the exit-interview quotes and require their response.
- D) Commission an external culture review to establish the facts independently before forming any view.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Fear-based cultures characteristically present exactly this signature — clean dashboards because fear suppresses the reporting that would dirty them, benchmark attrition because fear delays leaving as well as speaking, and warm direct experience because managers transmit pressure downward while managing upward. Option B's three individually reasonable discounts are jointly the exact reasoning by which fear-based cultures stay invisible. This tests triangulating with hard-to-fake data, examining one's own contribution as the probable source before confronting anyone, and engaging the management team as co-owners rather than defendants.

- **Score for whether a candidate acts through the management line** rather than personally bypassing, rescuing, or performing.
- **Watch for whether a candidate prices the precedent an option sets** — what it teaches managers, suppliers, and the frontline to expect next time.
- **Note whether a candidate separates an obligation from an optimisation** before applying utilitarian arithmetic to a decision.
- **Note whether a candidate examines their own contribution to a culture or performance signal** before attributing it to others.
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, executive-level judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that protects people with a falsehood** — a softened record, a signed but false assurance, an extended grace period funded by user exposure.
- **Crediting an answer that performs accountability downward** (public firmness with subordinates) while showing private flexibility with power.
- **Missing when a candidate explains away convergent weak signals** individually, each explanation reasonable, the convergence dismissed.
- **Accepting an answer that extracts a benefit before a transition has earned it** — banked headcount, a workaround closed as a root cause, a softened forecast.
- **Missing when a candidate lets utilitarian arithmetic settle a genuine obligation question.**
