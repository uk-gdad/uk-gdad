This role occupies a distinctive position: the desk's knowledge expert and its accountable owner, responsible for resourcing and developing the team, for preparing and coordinating service transition activities, and for the quality of outcomes users actually experience. ## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes this role demands, using realistic managerial material rather than abstract puzzles. For a service desk manager, that means questions built from performance dashboards, transition checklists, problem records, escalation frameworks, and resourcing constraints.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions spanning pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group, frequently adaptive, and usually reported as a speed-accuracy profile.

Cognitive assessment carries particular weight at this level for two reasons. First, the defining duty — identifying emerging issues — is applied pattern recognition under noise: distinguishing a developing problem from ordinary variation, early enough for intervention to be cheap. Second, this role is the desk's knowledge expert and final checker: transition plans, automation proposals, and process changes cross this desk for approval, and a logical gap missed becomes the incident the team handles at 3am.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in the role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to the defining duty to **identify emerging issues** and to the **Problem management** skill.
- **Logical deduction** maps to the **Service management framework knowledge**: applying framework rules to real transitions and incidents is deduction with operational stakes.
- **Error checking** maps to **Asset and configuration management** and the manager's role as quality gate in service transition.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the responsibility for **resourcing and development**.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to the **Ownership** skill and the **Customer service management** duty to investigate which processes can be automated.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a service dashboard**

A morning dashboard shows four metrics against their normal ranges: call volume 460 (normal 420-480); average wait 3.1 minutes (normal 2.5-3.5); FCR 61% (normal 66-72%); priority 1 incidents 0 (normal 0-1). Which metric most warrants investigation?

- A) Call volume
- B) Average wait
- C) First-contact resolution
- D) Priority 1 incidents

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Call volume, average wait, and P1 count all sit inside their ranges. FCR at 61% falls below its normal floor of 66% — the only out-of-range value, and substantial for a rate metric. This tests anomaly detection relative to each metric's own baseline, not the general mood of the morning — a calm dashboard elsewhere makes an FCR drift easier to miss and more informative, since it is not explained by pressure.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from transition criteria**

A service transition checklist states: "A new service may enter pilot support only if (i) the knowledge articles are published, and (ii) at least four analysts have completed training. It may enter full support only after a pilot of at least two weeks with FCR above 60%." The HR-system replacement has published articles, six trained analysts, and completed a one-week pilot with 71% FCR. What is its correct status?

- A) Eligible for full support — FCR exceeds the threshold.
- B) Eligible for pilot support, but not full support — the pilot has not yet run two weeks.
- C) Not eligible for pilot support — training is insufficient.
- D) Not eligible for either — the checklist is failed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Pilot entry conditions are both satisfied. Full support requires two conditions joined by "after": duration AND FCR. FCR passes; duration (one week of two) fails. This tests refusing to let one impressive number satisfy a compound requirement it only half-meets — duration thresholds exist because early pilot performance is unrepresentative.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a cutover plan**

A transition plan reads: "Step 1: Freeze changes to the old platform from Friday 18:00. Step 2: Migrate call-routing configuration Saturday morning. Step 3: Test inbound routing Saturday afternoon. Step 4: Update the old platform's routing tables Sunday morning to add the new bank holiday schedule. Step 5: Go live Monday 08:00." Which step contradicts the plan's own logic?

- A) Step 2
- B) Step 3
- C) Step 4
- D) Step 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1 freezes changes to the old platform from Friday evening, yet Step 4 schedules a change to that same platform on Sunday — inside the freeze window. This tests reading every later step against every earlier control, since internal contradiction is the most common defect in transition documents.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a manager's Monday**

Four items compete for attention:

1. The dashboard shows FCR five points below normal for a third consecutive day, cause unknown.
2. A director's office asks for desk performance figures for a board pack, needed Thursday.
3. Interviews for two analyst vacancies are scheduled Wednesday; interview questions are not yet finalised.
4. A team leader reports two analysts had a heated argument in front of colleagues an hour ago; both are now working in silence.

Which order of attention is most defensible?

- A) 2, 1, 3, 4 — external stakeholders outrank internal matters
- B) 4, 1, 3, 2 — the live people issue first, then the deteriorating unexplained metric, then Wednesday's interviews, then Thursday's figures
- C) 1, 4, 2, 3 — metrics first, people second
- D) 4, 3, 2, 1 — people matters first and last

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The hour-old conflict decays fastest if unaddressed. The three-day unexplained FCR decline is deteriorating now, and each day worsens both diagnosis and impact. Wednesday's interviews precede Thursday's deadline on both timing and stakes (two multi-year hires). This tests sequencing by rate of deterioration, not category or seniority of requester.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern separation in satisfaction data**

Monthly CSAT scores: Jan 4.2, Feb 4.2, Mar 4.1, Apr 4.2, May 3.6, Jun 4.1. In May, a major email outage generated 30% of that month's survey responses. Excluding outage-related responses, May's CSAT was 4.15. Which reading is most accurate?

- A) Service quality declined sharply in May and recovered in June.
- B) Underlying satisfaction was stable throughout; May's dip reflects one high-volume negative event, which merits its own review rather than a general service-quality response.
- C) The May figure should be deleted from reporting as an outlier.
- D) June's recovery proves the desk fixed its May quality problems.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The adjusted May figure (4.15) sits inside the normal band — the everyday service never moved; one bad event, heavily represented in the sample, dragged the average. This tests decomposing before diagnosing, and a strong candidate's answer proposes reviewing the specific outage rather than launching a general quality initiative aimed at a decline that did not happen.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction in problem escalation**

A problem escalation framework: (i) problems open more than 60 days require a documented action plan; (ii) problems touching more than one department's services require a named specialist consultant; (iii) any problem meeting both conditions must be reported to the monthly service governance board. Problem PR-118 has been open 45 days and affects services in three departments. Problem PR-121 has been open 70 days and affects one department, with no documented action plan. Which statement is correct?

- A) PR-118 must go to the governance board.
- B) PR-121 requires a specialist consultant.
- C) PR-118 requires a specialist consultant; PR-121 breaches the action-plan rule; neither currently requires board reporting.
- D) Both must go to the governance board.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** PR-118 triggers rule (ii) only (three departments). PR-121 triggers rule (i) only (70 days, no plan — a live breach). Board reporting requires both conditions together (an AND), and neither record meets both. This tests applying a conjunction correctly across two records rather than treating "meeting both" as "meeting either".

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking an automation proposal**

A vendor proposal for automated password reset states: "The desk receives 1,200 password tickets monthly, each costing £8 to handle, so automation saves £9,600 per month. The tool costs £4,000 monthly, yielding a net £5,600 saving. Industry experience shows 70% of password tickets are automatable." Which is the most significant flaw in the calculation?

- A) The £8 per-ticket cost is too high.
- B) The saving assumes 100% of tickets are automated, but the proposal's own figure says only 70% are automatable — the gross saving should be about £6,720, and the net about £2,720.
- C) The tool cost should be annual, not monthly.
- D) Password tickets are not really worth £8 each.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The proposal computes savings on all 1,200 tickets while stating only 70% are automatable — a self-contradiction. Correcting: 840 automated × £8 = £6,720 gross; net £2,720, about half the claimed figure. This tests checking a business case's internal consistency before its assumptions, and a strong candidate's answer notes the margin for error shrinks sharply at lower real-world uptake.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Emerging issue or noise?**

In the past ten days: (i) reopened-ticket rate has risen from 4% to 7%; (ii) three analysts independently mention that a recent knowledge base reorganisation makes articles harder to find; (iii) average handling time is up 40 seconds; (iv) CSAT is unchanged; (v) call volume is normal. What is the most coherent reading?

- A) Nothing is wrong — CSAT is stable, so users are unaffected.
- B) The signals cohere around one mechanism: analysts struggling to locate knowledge — producing longer calls and more incomplete first fixes (reopens) — which merits prompt investigation of the KB reorganisation before user-visible impact grows.
- C) Analysts are resisting change and complaining; performance-manage the three who raised it.
- D) The reopen rise and handling-time rise are unrelated coincidences.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A knowledge base harder to navigate produces exactly this signature: slower calls, more incomplete fixes surfacing as reopens, corroborated by frontline testimony. CSAT stability is a lagging indicator that does not yet reflect the problem. This tests crediting a pattern of converging weak signals before the lagging metrics confirm it.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint resourcing**

A four-week service transition must be staffed with exactly two analysts seconded half-time, while keeping the desk at its minimum of eight full-time-equivalent (FTE) analysts on phones. Ten analysts: six are transition-trained; of those six, two are the only Welsh-language call handlers (at least one must always be on phones); one trained analyst is on parental leave weeks 2-4; one trained analyst is the sole payroll-system specialist during month-end (week 4). Which pairing survives all four weeks?

- A) The two Welsh speakers
- B) The parental-leave analyst and the payroll specialist
- C) One Welsh speaker and the payroll specialist
- D) The two trained analysts who are neither Welsh speakers, on leave, nor the payroll specialist

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Option A risks Welsh-language coverage gaps daily. Option B fails outright given the leave absence. Option C collides in week 4 with month-end. Option D is the only pairing carrying no competing constraint, and the arithmetic still clears the eight-FTE floor. This tests listing each candidate's unavailability windows and choosing the pair whose windows are empty.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Root cause reasoning across a change boundary**

Following Saturday's network firmware update, Monday brings three complaint types: (i) intermittent VPN drops (38 reports, all remote workers); (ii) slow file opening on the shared drive (12 reports, all one office); (iii) a broken macro in the finance spreadsheet (2 reports). The firmware update's documented scope covers VPN concentrators and office network switches, not application software. Which triage is best reasoned?

- A) Attribute all three to the firmware update and roll it back immediately.
- B) Treat (i) and (ii) as plausibly firmware-related — matching the update's scope and timing — and investigate them against it; treat (iii) as probably coincidental, since spreadsheet macros are outside the change's mechanism, and route it normally.
- C) Treat all three as unrelated to the update, since correlation is not causation.
- D) Wait a week to see which symptoms persist.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The VPN drops and shared-drive slowness both fall within the update's documented mechanism and matching population. The macro fault has timing but no mechanism connecting it to network firmware. This tests causal triage that requires mechanism, not just timing, and a strong candidate's answer distinguishes directed investigation from both blanket attribution and blanket scepticism.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Logical audit of an improvement claim**

A team leader's quarterly review states: "Our coaching programme works: the ten analysts who received coaching improved their FCR by an average of 6 points, while the ten who declined coaching improved by only 1 point." What is the strongest logical objection?

- A) Six points is too small an improvement to matter.
- B) The comparison is undermined by self-selection: analysts chose whether to take coaching, so the coached group likely differs systematically (in motivation, in room to improve) from the decliners — the 5-point gap cannot be attributed to coaching alone.
- C) FCR is the wrong metric for measuring coaching.
- D) The sample of twenty analysts is too small to conclude anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Analysts who accepted coaching plausibly differ from decliners in ways that independently predict improvement, confounding the programme's effect with the choosers' characteristics. This tests recognising self-selection as the design flaw that even a larger sample (option D) would not rescue — the most common analytic failure in management reporting is design, not arithmetic.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate detects an anomaly against each metric's own baseline** rather than a general impression of the dashboard.
- **Watch for whether a candidate applies a compound condition (AND) correctly** across multiple records or criteria.
- **Note whether a candidate checks a document or business case for internal self-contradiction** before debating its assumptions.
- **Note whether a candidate credits converging weak signals** before lagging indicators confirm them.
- **Use the self-selection item (Question 11) to observe whether a candidate identifies a comparison's design flaw**, not just its sample size.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that lets one strong number satisfy a compound requirement.**
- **Crediting an answer that reads a composition effect as a genuine trend.**
- **Missing when a candidate dismisses a leading indicator because a lagging one is stable.**
- **Accepting a business case at headline level** without reconciling its own stated assumptions.
- **Missing when a candidate accepts a self-selected comparison as evidence of an intervention's effect.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures fluency with the quantitative material a service desk manager's decisions rest on: SLA aggregates across sites and priorities, satisfaction and quality metrics, cost-per-contact and automation economics, capacity and resourcing arithmetic, and trend analysis across months and quarters. The mathematics stays at percentages, ratios, weighted averages, rates, and projections, but the questions increasingly test judgement: which base to divide by, which comparison is fair, which projection method suits the data.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions built around tables and charts. An on-screen calculator is generally provided.

Numeracy matters at this level because the role summary makes it an explicit management competence: "use data analytics skills to make decisions that enhance business performance". This role converts desk data into resourcing cases, automation decisions, transition readiness judgements, and honest performance narratives — and is also the quality gate for other people's numbers.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages, aggregates, and weighted combinations** map to the **IT service reporting** skill.
- **Cost and benefit arithmetic** maps to the **Customer service management** duty to investigate automation.
- **Capacity and utilisation calculations** map to the responsibility for **resourcing** the team.
- **Trend measurement and projection** map to the **Problem management** and **Ownership** skills.
- **Fair comparison across units** maps to oversight of **customer service functions**.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Aggregate SLA across priorities**

Last month a desk resolved within SLA: 46 of 50 priority 1 incidents, 285 of 300 priority 2, and 583 of 650 priority 3. What was the overall within-SLA percentage across all incidents?

- A) 89.7%
- B) 91.4%
- C) 92.0%
- D) 93.1%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total met = 914; total incidents = 1,000; overall = 91.4%. Averaging the three percentages (92.2%) overweights the small priority 1 category. This tests weighted aggregation from raw counts as the only honest desk-level figure.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cost per contact**

A desk's monthly operating cost is £96,000, covering all channels. Last month it handled 8,000 contacts: 5,600 calls, 1,900 portal tickets, and 500 emails. What was the overall cost per contact?

- A) £10
- B) £12
- C) £14
- D) £17

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** £96,000÷8,000=£12. This tests selecting the relevant figures from a report carrying more numbers than the question needs, and a strong candidate's answer notes cost per contact is the baseline for later channel-shift and automation cases.

**Question 3 (easy) — Satisfaction survey response arithmetic**

A satisfaction survey was sent for 2,400 resolved tickets; 360 responses arrived, of which 306 rated the service 4 or 5 out of 5. What was the response rate, and what percentage of respondents rated 4 or 5?

- A) 12% response rate; 88% rated 4-5
- B) 15% response rate; 85% rated 4-5
- C) 15% response rate; 88% rated 4-5
- D) 18% response rate; 82% rated 4-5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Response rate = 360÷2,400=15%; satisfied share = 306÷360=85%, divided by respondents, not surveys sent. This tests keeping two different bases straight, and a strong candidate's answer notes "85% satisfied" describes respondents, not the full user population.

**Question 4 (easy) — FTE arithmetic for a secondment**

A desk has 11 analysts: nine full-time and two at 0.6 FTE each. One full-time analyst is seconded to a transition project at half-time for a quarter. What is the desk's phone-available FTE during the secondment?

- A) 9.4
- B) 9.7
- C) 10.2
- D) 10.7

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Baseline = 9+1.2=10.2; secondment removes 0.5, giving 9.7. Option C forgets the secondment; option A subtracts a full FTE for a half-time one. This tests careful part-time and partial-secondment arithmetic underpinning rota and recruitment decisions.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Automation benefit with uptake assumptions**

Password reset automation is projected to divert tickets currently costing £8 each to a self-service route costing £1 each. Monthly password volume is 1,400 tickets. Uptake is projected at 40% in quarter one, rising to 70% from quarter two. The tool costs £3,500 per month. What is the projected net monthly saving in quarter one, and from quarter two?

- A) Q1: £420 net saving; Q2 onward: £3,360 net saving
- B) Q1: £560 net loss; Q2 onward: £2,860 net saving
- C) Q1: £420 net loss; Q2 onward: £3,360 net saving
- D) Q1: £3,920 net saving; Q2 onward: £6,860 net saving

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Saving per diverted ticket = £7. Q1: 560 diverted × £7 = £3,920 gross − £3,500 = £420 net. Q2: 980 × £7 = £6,860 − £3,500 = £3,360 net. This tests including the residual self-service cost and the uptake ramp, and a strong candidate's answer notes the case is marginal at launch — the decision should fund uptake deliberately, not just the tool.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage-point drift and run-rate**

A desk's within-SLA rate has declined steadily: April 94.0%, May 92.5%, June 91.0%. The contract requires "at least 88%" each month. If the decline continues at the same rate, in which month does the desk first fail the contractual requirement?

- A) July
- B) August
- C) September
- D) October

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The drift is 1.5 points/month. July=89.5%, August=88.0% (meets "at least 88%" exactly), September=86.5% (first failure). This tests measuring the drift correctly and reading a threshold's boundary convention ("at least" includes the boundary) before briefing anyone on when it bites.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Comparing sites fairly**

Two sites' monthly figures: Site A — 3,000 contacts, 2,760 within SLA, 8 complaints; Site B — 1,200 contacts, 1,092 within SLA, 5 complaints. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Site A performs better on SLA and complaints.
- B) Both sites have identical SLA performance (91%); Site A has the lower complaint rate (2.7 versus 4.2 per 1,000 contacts).
- C) Both sites have identical SLA performance (92%); Site B has the lower complaint rate.
- D) Site B performs better on both measures.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Site A: 92% SLA, 2.67 complaints/1,000. Site B: 91% SLA, 4.17 complaints/1,000. Site A leads on both, despite the raw complaint count (8 vs 5) pointing the opposite way. This tests normalising every comparison to a rate before judging, since raw counts across different denominators reverse the honest reading.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Capacity model for a demand increase**

A department of 1,800 staff generates desk demand of 0.9 contacts per person per month. A machinery-of-government change adds 600 staff whose previous desk experienced 1.2 contacts per person per month. Each analyst handles 640 contacts per month. How many analysts does the combined demand require (round up)?

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Existing demand = 1,620; incoming demand (using the incoming population's own rate) = 720; combined = 2,340; analysts = 2,340÷640=3.66→4. This tests using the correct population-specific rate rather than assuming instant convergence, and rounding capacity requirements up, never down.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Backlog burn-down with net flow**

A desk carries a backlog of 900 aged tickets. Weekly inflow is 1,150 new tickets; ordinary weekly resolution capacity is 1,100. A recovery initiative adds temporary capacity of 120 resolutions per week. Under the initiative, how many weeks until the backlog clears, and what happens when the temporary capacity ends?

- A) 9 weeks; the backlog stays cleared afterwards.
- B) 13 weeks; after the initiative ends, the backlog regrows at 50 tickets per week.
- C) 13 weeks; the backlog stays cleared afterwards.
- D) 9 weeks; after the initiative ends, the backlog regrows at 50 tickets per week.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With the initiative, net flow = 1,220−1,150 = +70/week; 900÷70≈13 weeks to clear. Without it, net flow reverts to 1,100−1,150 = −50/week — the backlog regrows. This tests recognising that surge capacity treats accumulation, not cause, and a recovery plan is incomplete without a permanent change or the regrowth forecast attached.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Weighted CSAT across channels with a mix shift**

Channel CSAT last quarter: phone 4.3 (3,000 responses), portal 3.8 (1,000 responses). This quarter, identical channel scores — phone 4.3, portal 3.8 — but the mix shifted: phone 2,000 responses, portal 2,000. What happened to overall CSAT, and what should the report say?

- A) Overall fell from 4.18 to 4.05; service quality deteriorated.
- B) Overall fell from 4.18 to 4.05; per-channel quality is unchanged and the fall is entirely the channel mix shifting toward the lower-scoring portal — the report should show both channel scores and flag portal experience as the improvement target.
- C) Overall is unchanged at 4.3; no action needed.
- D) Overall rose because portal responses doubled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weighted overall fell from 4.175 to 4.05 while both channel scores stood still — a pure composition effect: more experience now happens on the weaker channel. This tests refusing to let a blended number tell an unblended story, and a strong candidate's answer connects this to automation's likely future effect on channel mix.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Reading a trend table for an emerging issue**

Quarterly figures for a service the desk supports:

| Metric | Q1 | Q2 | Q3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incidents | 400 | 380 | 365 |
| Of which: recurring category X | 40 | 57 | 84 |
| Average resolution time (hours) | 6.0 | 6.1 | 6.9 |

Total incidents are falling. What is the most important finding for a manager?

- A) The service is improving — incidents fell 9% over two quarters.
- B) Category X is growing about 45-47% per quarter and now represents 23% of all incidents (up from 10%); its growth is masked by the falling total, and rising resolution times suggest X may also be harder to fix — X needs problem investigation now.
- C) Resolution time is the only concern.
- D) The data is contradictory and cannot support conclusions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Category X's share rose from 10% to 23% while non-X incidents fell enough to make the total decline — a compounding problem hiding inside a falling aggregate, corroborated by rising resolution time. This tests decomposing a headline before trusting it — emerging issues live in the components, and headline metrics are exactly where they hide.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate builds every aggregate from raw counts** and decomposes every aggregate received.
- **Watch for whether a candidate states the base explicitly** (per contact, per respondent, per thousand, per FTE) before dividing.
- **Note whether a candidate resolves a threshold's boundary convention** ("at least" versus "above") before projecting a breach date.
- **Note whether a candidate models flows rather than snapshots** for backlog or queue projections.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an average of percentages across differently sized categories.**
- **Crediting an answer that switches bases mid-analysis** (e.g. response rate versus satisfaction rate).
- **Missing when a candidate forgets the residual cost in a savings case.**
- **Accepting a projection built from a headline figure** while a component is diverging sharply.
- **Missing when a candidate treats surge capacity as a permanent cure** without forecasting the post-initiative relapse.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely a candidate reads, interprets, and communicates the written material a service desk manager works with daily: service management framework definitions, contract and SLA clauses, transition documentation, satisfaction survey reports, user research summaries, supplier correspondence, and departmental policy. The dominant format presents a passage followed by statements to judge as **True**, **False**, or **Cannot say**. Complementary formats test best-summary selection, inference discipline, and clear-writing judgement.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 statements across several passages. The governing discipline is answering from the passage alone.

Verbal precision is consequential in a specific way at this level: this role operates at the boundary where operational reality is converted into words for decision-makers, and words from decision-makers are converted back into operational instruction.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise comprehension of framework and contract text** maps to the **Service management framework knowledge**.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to the **IT service reporting** skill.
- **Reading research without overclaiming** maps to the **User focus** skill.
- **Faithful summary for senior audiences** maps to the reporting and **Customer service management** duties.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to ownership of process documentation and transition criteria.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

For Questions 1 to 3, the candidate reads this contract schedule extract.

> "The supplier will provide second-line support for the case management service during core hours (08:00-18:00, working days). Priority 1 incidents will be accepted outside core hours via the emergency line, subject to a response time of one hour. For all other priorities, incidents reported outside core hours are deemed received at the start of the next core-hours period. Response time targets are: priority 1 — 30 minutes within core hours; priority 2 — 4 core hours; priority 3 — 8 core hours. 'Response' means a qualified engineer has begun diagnosis, not resolution of the incident. Service credits accrue only where the monthly achievement of any response target falls below 90%."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A priority 2 incident reported at 20:00 on Tuesday is deemed received at 08:00 on Wednesday, assuming Wednesday is a working day."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The "deemed received" clause applies to all priorities other than priority 1 when reported outside core hours, exactly matching the statement. This tests recognising a deeming provision, which restarts the response clock — a clause that matters for accurately assessing supplier breaches.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "If the supplier's engineer begins diagnosing a priority 1 incident 25 minutes after it is reported during core hours, the response target is met even if resolution takes three days."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 25 minutes is within the 30-minute target, and the defined term "Response" means diagnosis begun, not resolution. This tests checking what a quoted term means in this document, rather than in ordinary usage — response and resolution are different obligations here.

**Question 3 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The supplier consistently meets its priority 3 response target."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage defines the target and the credit mechanism but reports no performance data. This tests distinguishing what a document requires from what data shows — a common slip when process documentation is cited as evidence of practice.

For Questions 4 to 6, the candidate reads this user research summary.

> "We conducted contextual interviews with 15 caseworkers and 9 field officers, and analysed 4,000 portal sessions. Caseworkers frequently articulated a desire for a live chat channel. However, session analysis shows that 62% of failed portal journeys broke down at the service categorisation step, where users must choose among 14 categories whose labels reflect internal team structures rather than user tasks. Field officers, who work primarily on mobile devices, abandoned portal journeys at twice the rate of desktop users. We recommend restructuring categorisation around user tasks before investing in new channels. Chat may have value, but the evidence suggests users' underlying need is to complete existing journeys successfully, not to access an additional channel."

**Question 4 (easy) — Needs versus desires**

Which option best expresses the passage's central distinction?

- A) Users desire live chat, but the evidenced need is fixable journey failure — chiefly a categorisation structure that mirrors the organisation instead of user tasks.
- B) Users need live chat and desire better categorisation.
- C) Field officers need desktop computers instead of mobile devices.
- D) The research shows chat would have no value.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The desire is articulated (chat); the need is evidenced (62% of failures at categorisation). Option D overstates — the passage says chat "may have value". This tests the needs-versus-desires distinction named in the role's User focus skill.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Most failed portal journeys involved field officers."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage gives a rate (twice the abandonment rate) and a location (62% fail at categorisation), but no population sizes or absolute shares. This tests converting a quantitative claim to its exact form (rate, share, or count) before letting it support a conclusion.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Best summary for an investment board**

Compressing this passage into one sentence for a portfolio board deciding between funding chat or funding the redesign, which is best?

- A) "Research found users want chat, so we should fund it."
- B) "Research triangulating 24 interviews with 4,000 sessions found the dominant failure is task-misaligned categorisation (62% of failed journeys) with acute mobile impact; it recommends fixing categorisation before new channels, noting chat's value is unproven rather than absent."
- C) "The portal is broken for everyone and needs a complete rebuild before any other work."
- D) "Users don't really want chat; the research recommends categorisation work instead."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B preserves the method, the central finding with its figure, the differential impact, and the recommendation's exact epistemic status. Option A discards evidence; option D misreports the interviews; option C inflates a fixable defect. This tests carrying a research finding's precision into a decision-making summary, not just its conclusion.

For Questions 7 to 9, the candidate reads this draft transition readiness standard.

> "A service is ready for desk support when all mandatory criteria are met. Mandatory: knowledge articles published and reviewed by a senior analyst; at least 30% of desk analysts trained, including at least one per shift pattern; support model documented, naming second- and third-line resolver groups with agreed response times; and a rollback procedure documented and tested. Desirable: a pilot period of two weeks; automated monitoring feeding the desk dashboard. Where a desirable criterion is not met, the service desk manager may accept the service with a documented risk acceptance. Where any mandatory criterion is not met, go-live must be deferred; the service desk manager may not waive mandatory criteria."

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A service with published and reviewed knowledge articles, 35% of analysts trained across all shift patterns, a documented support model with agreed response times, and a documented but untested rollback procedure may go live if the service desk manager documents a risk acceptance."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rollback criterion requires "documented and tested" — untested fails a mandatory criterion, and the risk-acceptance mechanism belongs exclusively to desirable criteria. This tests reading a conjunction inside a criterion ("documented and tested") as strictly as the criterion itself, catching a near-miss construction.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement on a standard's clause**

The draft also contains this clause: "Training completion should ideally be considered as being achieved when analysts have, where practicable, engaged with the relevant materials and can be regarded as broadly familiar with the new service." As the reviewing manager, which replacement is best?

- A) "An analyst counts as trained when they have completed the e-learning module and passed the service's knowledge check."
- B) "Training is achieved through meaningful engagement with the learning pathway."
- C) "Analysts should familiarise themselves with the new service to an appropriate standard."
- D) Keep the draft — flexibility helps operational teams apply judgement.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A mandatory criterion needs a binary, observable definition to be auditable at a readiness review. Options B and C swap hedges for abstractions, equally unauditable. This tests recognising that a criterion two reasonable people could disagree about is not yet written.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Inference from the standard's structure**

Considering this passage as a whole, which inference about the standard's design is best supported?

- A) The standard treats verification more strictly than documentation: several criteria require evidence of review, testing, or coverage, not just the existence of artefacts.
- B) The standard's authors believe pilots are unnecessary.
- C) The standard gives the service desk manager authority to accelerate go-lives when programmes are under time pressure.
- D) The standard requires all analysts to be trained before go-live.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Mandatory criteria repeatedly pair artefacts with verification: "published and reviewed", "documented and tested", coverage by shift pattern. Option C inverts the text — discretion runs only toward accepting desirable gaps, never waiving mandatory ones. This tests reading what a document's architecture implies about its intent, beyond any single clause.

For Questions 10 to 11, the candidate reads this email from a programme director.

> "Thanks for your readiness assessment on the licensing service. I understand your concerns about the untested rollback and the training coverage on nights. However, the Minister has announced the service publicly for the 1st, and moving that date is not an option I can take to the board. I'd ask you to look again at whether your criteria genuinely apply to a service of this size — it's small, low-volume, and the supplier is confident. Happy to discuss risk-sharing arrangements. Can you confirm by Friday that the desk will support go-live on the 1st?"

**Question 10 (challenging) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The programme director has stated that the go-live date cannot be changed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Moving that date is not an option I can take to the board" is a statement about what the director is willing to propose, not about whether the date could change. This tests reading modality precisely — a positional claim dressed as a fact, common in pressure correspondence.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Choosing the reply**

Which reply to the programme director is most effective?

- A) "Confirmed — the desk will support go-live on the 1st. We'll manage the risks as they arise."
- B) "Our criteria are not negotiable and the desk will not support this go-live. The date must move."
- C) "Thank you — I understand the date's significance. Our two open items are specific and closable: the rollback needs one test (the supplier can run it next week) and night-shift coverage needs two analysts trained (bookable within days). If both close before the 1st, the desk supports go-live; if either remains open, I cannot waive a mandatory criterion, and I'd want us to jointly present the board a short options paper — including your risk-sharing suggestion — rather than have the desk absorb an undocumented risk. Can we take 30 minutes tomorrow?"
- D) "I'll defer to your judgement on whether the criteria apply, given the service's size — you know the programme best."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C separates the immovable from the solvable, states the limit of authority factually rather than personally, and relocates the risk-sharing overture to a documented, board-visible decision. Option A trades user and analyst safety for Friday's comfort. Option B offers no path forward. Option D abandons the standard under the exact pressure it exists to withstand. This tests writing correspondence that neither capitulates nor stonewalls, but converts pressure into a documented, shared, decidable problem.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate distinguishes what a document requires from what data shows** (normative versus empirical).
- **Watch for whether a candidate converts a quantitative claim into its exact form** — rate, share, or count.
- **Note whether a candidate catches a conjunction failure inside a criterion**, a near-miss construction.
- **Note whether a candidate reads modality precisely** in pressure correspondence — who is the subject of "can't", and what qualifier is attached.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer drawn from operational familiarity** rather than the passage's exact wording.
- **Missing when a candidate reads an obligation's existence as evidence of its achievement.**
- **Crediting an answer that blurs a rate, a share, and a count.**
- **Missing a conjunction inside a criterion** ("documented and tested" failing on either half).
- **Accepting a written reply that matches the pressure's binary frame** rather than restructuring toward specific, closable actions.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to evaluate possible responses — selecting the most effective, identifying most and least effective, ranking options, or rating each on a scale. It measures professional judgement: how a candidate weighs users, staff, suppliers, governance, and truth when they pull against each other.

The typical format is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, usually generously timed. At service desk manager level, scenarios carry three characteristic properties: accountability is non-transferable, decisions set systems (not just outcomes), and dilemmas are structurally two-audience — upward-facing (directors, programmes, suppliers) and downward-facing (analysts, team leaders, users).

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Accountability under scrutiny** maps to the **Ownership** skill.
- **Continual improvement instincts** map to the **Customer service management** skill: translating lessons learnt into continual service improvement.
- **Evidence-led decisions under pressure** map to the **IT service reporting** and **Problem management** skills.
- **People development and inclusion** map to the responsibility for **resourcing and development** of the team.
- **Championing users** maps to the **User focus** skill.
- **Transition stewardship** maps to the duty to prepare and coordinate **service transition activities**.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — The outage and the director's question**

A four-hour outage of the case management service yesterday was prolonged because the desk escalated it to the wrong resolver group, losing 90 minutes. A director asks directly: "What went wrong?" What is the most effective response?

- A) Explain that the supplier's documentation was confusing, which caused the misrouting.
- B) Give the facts including the desk's misrouting and the 90 minutes it cost, alongside the other factors; state what is already changing (routing matrix corrected, resolver groups validated) and when the full review will report.
- C) Keep the answer high-level — "a combination of factors, under review" — until the formal review completes.
- D) Name the analyst who misrouted the escalation and describe their corrective training.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Accurate facts including the own team's error, delivered unprompted, with correction already in motion, is the only version that builds rather than spends credibility. Option A leads with deflection. Option C withholds known facts. Option D converts a systems answer into a person answer. This tests accountability in its load-bearing form — the account given when the room is senior and the failure is partly one's own.

**Question 2 (easy) — The satisfaction dip and the board pack**

Quarterly CSAT has dipped from 4.2 to 3.9, driven by a painful email migration. The dip is explainable, and recovery actions are working. A colleague preparing the board pack suggests: "Show the six-month rolling average — it smooths the dip to 4.1 and avoids questions." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — the rolling average is a legitimate statistical choice and the dip is temporary anyway.
- B) Show the quarterly figure with the dip, alongside its cause, the recovery actions, and early evidence they are working; include the rolling average only as agreed standard presentation, not as a substitute introduced this quarter.
- C) Show both figures and let the board choose which to believe.
- D) Escalate the colleague's suggestion to their manager as attempted misrepresentation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A rolling average adopted in the quarter it flatters is concealment, whatever its statistical respectability. Option B reports the truth in decision-grade form: figure, cause, action, trajectory. Option C outsources judgement the manager is paid for. Option D criminalises a first suggestion offered openly. This tests reporting unflattering numbers with their explanations, which is what makes flattering numbers believed.

**Question 3 (easy) — The analyst error with user impact**

A well-regarded analyst bulk-closed 40 open tickets in error while tidying a queue; users received closure emails for unresolved faults. The analyst reported it themselves immediately, mortified. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank them for reporting fast; make the priority reopening tickets and sending users a correction with apology; then look at why bulk-closure without confirmation was possible at all, and fix that.
- B) Formally document the error as a performance concern to ensure accountability.
- C) Reassure the analyst it happens to everyone and quietly reopen the tickets without user communication, to avoid alarming users.
- D) Require all future bulk operations to be approved personally by the manager.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Repair the user harm first, disclose honestly, then fix the mechanism that allowed an irreversible 40-ticket action without confirmation. Option B punishes the exact behaviour (fast self-reporting) most needed. Option C hides harm from the users entitled to know. Option D substitutes a bottleneck for a fix. This tests sequencing by harm and treating an error as system evidence.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The automation announcement**

Password-reset automation approval is imminent, removing roughly 30% of first-line call volume within a year. Two analysts have privately asked whether it means redundancies; team mood is wary. The honest assessment: no redundancies planned — natural turnover and redeployment to more complex work should absorb the change, though nothing is guaranteed. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Delay any team communication until the automation is live and its effects are measurable.
- B) Tell the team the truth as held: the automation case, the honest jobs assessment including its uncertainty, the development path it opens, and a commitment to share workforce planning as it firms up — inviting questions openly.
- C) Announce that jobs are completely safe, to settle the mood and protect morale.
- D) Announce the automation as a leadership decision that is not open for discussion.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is honest including its uncertainty, and pairs it with a genuine development story. Option C mortgages future credibility to events not controlled. Option A vacates the information space during maximum anxiety. This tests carrying a team through change honestly rather than reassuring with a promise that cannot be guaranteed.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The supplier's slipping quality**

A second-line supplier's response performance has drifted down for three months: 94%, 91%, 88.5% against a 90% contractual target. The relationship is otherwise good, and team leaders want to "give them another month". What is the most effective response?

- A) Give them another month — the relationship has value and the miss is small.
- B) Invoke the service credit clause immediately and formally, to establish seriousness.
- C) Raise it formally now — a documented performance review meeting with the data, causes, and a recovery plan with milestones — while separately verifying the desk's own escalation quality isn't contributing; treat credits as an outcome of the process, not the opening move.
- D) Begin sourcing a replacement supplier before raising the issue, to negotiate from strength.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three months of monotonic decline through the contractual floor is a trend with momentum. Option C matches the escalation ladder to the evidence, protects the relationship by keeping it professional, and audits the desk's own contribution. Option A lets the drift continue; option B leads with the weapon; option D disproportionately prices a full re-procurement as a negotiating posture. This tests climbing an escalation ladder rung by rung, with documentation at every step.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The user research a candidate commissioned**

Research into why field officers underuse the support portal finds the top barrier is a categorisation structure a team leader designed last year, and recommends unwinding it. That team leader calls the research "unrepresentative — they only spoke to 24 people". What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the team leader's critique and shelve the report — 24 interviews is a small sample.
- B) Publish the findings as delivered, act on them, and note the design was the team's own — modelling that research outranks authorship; engage the team leader on the fix, where their design knowledge is genuinely valuable, and address the sample-size question on its merits (triangulated with session data, appropriate for qualitative work).
- C) Soften the report's framing before wider circulation so the team is not demoralised.
- D) Commission a second, larger study before deciding anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Acting on uncomfortable findings, with authorship acknowledged, signals evidence governs decisions. Option A launders discomfort as methodology; sample-size objections appearing only when findings displease are tells, not critiques. Option C corrupts the information channel decisions run on. This tests championing user research when it indicts the manager's own team.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The transition go-live vote**

At a go/no-go board for a new service, everything is green except one flagged item: the out-of-hours support model is unstaffed — the supplier's overnight rota has two unfilled positions until next month. The programme director proposes going live anyway: "Overnight volume is tiny; day-one users are all office-hours staff." Other board members look to you. What is the most effective position?

- A) Agree unconditionally — the director's volume argument is sound.
- B) Refuse go-live until the rota is filled, since the support model criterion is unmet.
- C) Agree conditionally, converting the argument into managed risk: time-boxed acceptance (four weeks), an interim mitigation (an on-call escalation route with defined triggers), explicit board-minuted ownership of the residual risk, and a named checkpoint to verify the rota is filled — with support contingent on those conditions holding.
- D) Abstain, noting the decision belongs to the programme, not the desk.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The director's argument has genuine content, and the mature response is neither swallowing it (an unstaffed tier is a real gap) nor blocking it outright (deferral has costs too). Option C quantifies, mitigates, owns, and time-boxes the risk explicitly. Option D withholds the very judgement the board convened to weigh. This tests being neither gatekeeper nor doormat when a readiness gap meets genuine delivery pressure.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the repeated near-miss**

Twice this month, analysts have caught themselves just before applying a decommissioned fix from the knowledge base — an article superseded six weeks ago but still surfacing first in search. No user harm has occurred yet. Four possible responses:

1. Treat the near-misses as free warnings: fix the superseded article's status today, audit the knowledge base for similar stale content, and add article review to the change checklist so decommissioned fixes are retired at source.
2. Remind analysts at the next team meeting to check article dates before applying fixes.
3. Log it as a risk in the risk register for quarterly review.
4. Wait for an actual incident, since near-misses with no harm don't justify diverting effort.

Which is the MOST effective and which is the LEAST effective response?

- A) Most: 1; Least: 4
- B) Most: 2; Least: 4
- C) Most: 1; Least: 3
- D) Most: 2; Least: 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option 1 spends cheap evidence at full value: instance correction, class sweep, and mechanism closure. Option 4 explicitly prices user harm as the admission fee for action, inverting proactive management entirely, and teaches reporters that reporting achieves nothing. This tests acting upstream of harm at the mechanism level, not just the instance.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The inherited underperformer**

A team leader inherited from a predecessor, fifteen years on the desk, widely liked, has had the desk's weakest team numbers for two years, despite "good" appraisals throughout from the predecessor. First-month observation confirms the gap is real: weak coaching, avoided conflict, stale processes. The team leader mentions how much they value the desk's "family feel". What is the most effective approach?

- A) Continue the predecessor's appraisal pattern for a year while building relationship capital, then address performance.
- B) Begin an honest, structured process now: share the data and observations directly and privately, hear their account, agree specific expectations with genuine support (coaching, training, mentoring) and review points — decoupling the person's worth from the performance question, and keeping the paper trail accurate from the first conversation.
- C) Restructure the teams so this leader's weaknesses matter less, avoiding the confrontation.
- D) Move quickly to formal capability procedures, since two years of evidence already exists.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two years of inaccurate "good" appraisals mean the person has never actually been told there is a problem — option D reads unspoken data as if it were honest feedback and would be experienced as an ambush. Option A extends the falsification. Option C taxes every other team to avoid a conversation. Option B is fair to the person, their team, and the record. This tests recognising that honesty and kindness are not opposed — falsely kind appraisals spend a person's correctable years.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The whistle from below**

A junior analyst, visibly nervous, reports privately that a team leader has been instructing analysts to "pause" aged tickets into a suspended status before month-end, removing them from the ageing report, then reactivate them after reporting — for at least six months. The team leader is the strongest operational performer and a personal ally from the manager's own analyst days. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank the analyst, promise confidentiality about their identity, verify the pattern independently in the ticket data (suspension timestamps against reporting dates), and if confirmed, address it as a data-integrity issue with the team leader directly and formally — correcting historical reporting upwards, whatever the personal cost.
- B) Tell the analyst you will watch the situation, and quietly change the report logic to include suspended tickets, closing the loophole without confrontation.
- C) Bring the analyst and team leader together to discuss the allegation openly, since fairness requires the accused to face their accuser.
- D) Ask the team leader informally whether there is anything they want to tell you about month-end practices.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option A serves the reporter (protection), the truth (verification), the organisation (accurate history), and the accused (evidence before accusation), in the right order. Option B fixes the metric while burying the conduct. Option C torches the reporter's confidentiality given the power asymmetry. Option D lets a systematic pattern become a story explained away informally. This tests protecting the powerless, verifying before acting, and paying the personal cost of a correction that implicates an ally.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The improvement budget**

£40,000 of improvement funding, usable once. Three worthy claims: (a) replace the desk's creaking remote-support tool, which analysts curse daily and which slows most calls slightly; (b) fund the portal categorisation redesign recommended by user research, removing the top failure point for field officers — a minority of users with the worst current experience; (c) fund advanced diagnostic training for all analysts, the team's own top request in the staff survey. All three cases are sound; only one can be funded. What is the most effective basis for deciding?

- A) Fund the tool — it touches every call, so total benefit is largest.
- B) Fund the training — honouring the team's top request repays their engagement and builds lasting capability.
- C) Decide from evidenced user outcomes: quantify each option's impact on users (including the equity weight of option b's concentrated harm to the worst-served group), test cheaper partial routes for the others, and publish the reasoning to team and stakeholders — whichever option wins.
- D) Split the funding three ways so each claim receives something.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The competence being tested is how the decision is made, not which option wins. Option C names user outcomes as the sovereign criterion, prices the equity dimension of concentrated harm, disciplines the comparison with quantification rather than salience, and publishes the reasoning so losing constituencies can trust the process. Option D underfunds all three thresholds while disappointing everyone slowly. This tests an allocation discipline: evidence in, reasoning visible, user outcomes sovereign.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate's answer serves all relevant audiences** — users, team, upward trust — rather than one at the silent expense of another.
- **Watch for whether a candidate distinguishes absorbing risk personally from owning it explicitly** (priced, mitigated, signed for by the right party).
- **Note whether a candidate's response to an error protects the reporting channel** rather than punishing the messenger.
- **Note whether a candidate names an evidence-based allocation criterion**, rather than deciding by salience, constituency, or seniority.
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that deflects blame upward or names a person downward** in an accountability moment.
- **Crediting an answer that purchases present comfort with future credibility** — absolute reassurances, smoothed metrics, softened findings.
- **Missing when a candidate's response makes self-reporting or speaking up less safe** for the future, whatever else it fixes.
- **Accepting informality as a substitute for a documented, formal process** where real stakes are involved.
- **Missing when a candidate splits scarce resources to avoid disappointment** rather than choosing on evidence.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a service desk manager: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested the manager's core mental moves: reading anomalies against baselines, holding compound transition criteria to every clause, catching plans that break their own freeze windows, sequencing a Monday by rate of deterioration, and refusing self-selected comparisons and mechanism-free causation. The numeric section tested the arithmetic decisions stand on: weighted aggregation, cost-per-contact, automation cases with uptake assumptions attached, boundary-precise SLA projections, backlog flows that forecast their own relapse, and the decomposition that finds a compounding problem inside a falling total. The verbal section tested the reading and writing this role converts into commitments: deeming clauses and defined terms, needs versus desires, rates versus shares, auditable criteria versus hedged ones, and replies that hold a readiness line while giving a programme director a path. The situational judgement section tested the dilemmas that define the level: accountability with the failure partly the manager's own, honest numbers under board pressure, errors treated as system evidence, research that indicts the manager's own design, risk signed for rather than swallowed, and the whistle from below answered with verification, protection, and personal cost.

The thread running through all four sections is decomposing an aggregate before trusting it, verifying before acting, and pricing risk explicitly rather than absorbing it silently.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score, and at this level connects naturally to how the candidate would actually hold a readiness line, report an uncomfortable number, and handle a report from below that implicates an ally.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can resolve a situation, protect the honest record, and strengthen the system that will face the situation's successor — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
