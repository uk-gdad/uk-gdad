# Service Desk Analyst - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for service desk analyst, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. The material is job-specific: it uses materials a service desk analyst genuinely handles — incident tickets, asset registers, service level agreements (SLAs), knowledge base articles, and calls from users who need help — and the decisions genuinely made, such as deciding which ticket to pick up first, spotting a mistake in an asset record, or knowing when to escalate an issue.

A service desk analyst is often the first person a colleague speaks to when their technology stops working. That work depends on clear thinking under time pressure, careful reading, comfort with simple numbers such as SLA percentages and call volumes, and sound judgement about people. The four assessments here — cognitive, numeric, verbal, and situational — map directly onto those everyday demands.

This document is organised into four assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, a mapping of the assessment's dimensions to the specific skills in the role summary, a substantial set of items with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, administration tips, and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. At analyst level, consider offering the platform's ungraded practice questions first. Let the candidate work through each section without interruption, and note anything unusual about the conditions so the record is honest. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the everyday thinking skills this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of rotating shapes or completing number squares, it presents the kinds of material seen every shift on the service desk — ticket queues, escalation rules, asset records, knowledge base steps — and asks the candidate to reason quickly and accurately about them.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting between 15 and 30 minutes, with somewhere between 15 and 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group; many platforms adapt difficulty, and employers usually see a balance of speed and accuracy rather than a single mark.

Cognitive ability matters for this role because the day is a stream of small, fast decisions. Which call to take first? Does this fault match a known pattern? Is an asset record consistent with what the user is saying? A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates exactly those moments in miniature.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the named skills in the role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to the **Service focus** skill: noticing when several tickets describe the same underlying fault, or when a spike of calls follows a change to a service.
- **Logical deduction** maps to the duty to know when to escalate issues: escalation rules are conditional statements, and applying them correctly is pure deduction.
- **Error checking** maps to the **Asset and configuration management** skill: spotting mismatches between records and reality.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the **Customer service management** skill: ranking tickets by urgency and impact, not just arrival order.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to the **Ownership** skill: owning an issue until it is resolved, mitigated, or handed to a new owner.
- **Attention to user detail** maps to the **User focus** skill: reading what the user actually said, carefully and completely.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in ticket references**

A department's ticket system uses references in the format INC-YYYY-NNNNN for incidents (for example INC-2026-00417) and REQ-YYYY-NNNNN for service requests (for example REQ-2026-00098). While tidying a queue, five references appear:

1. INC-2026-00521
2. REQ-2026-00104
3. INC-00522-2026
4. INC-2026-00523
5. REQ-2026-00105

Which reference breaks the standard pattern?

- A) Reference 1
- B) Reference 2
- C) Reference 3
- D) Reference 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The pattern is prefix, four-digit year, five-digit number. Reference 3 swaps the year and the number. This tests checking each element in turn — prefix, year, number — rather than judging the whole reference at a glance, and a strong candidate's answer notes that reference conventions let colleagues find and link tickets quickly, so a malformed one is worth catching early.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an escalation rule**

A service desk procedure says: "Any priority 1 incident that has not been resolved within 30 minutes must be escalated to the duty manager." A priority 1 incident was resolved after 25 minutes. A colleague asks whether it should have been escalated. Which one of the following is definitely true?

- A) The procedure was broken by not escalating.
- B) The procedure was followed, because the incident was resolved within 30 minutes.
- C) It should have been escalated anyway, to be safe.
- D) The incident should have been priority 2.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule requires escalation only when both conditions hold: priority 1, and unresolved after 30 minutes. Resolution at 25 minutes never triggers it. Option D invents information the scenario does not give. This tests concluding only what the rule guarantees — the same discipline the role's "know when to escalate" duty depends on.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking an asset record**

A user calls to report a broken laptop. The asset record reads: Asset tag LT-30412; Model: Vantage 14; Assigned to: J. Osei; Location: Leeds office; Status: In repair. The caller says: "Hi, this is Jide Osei from the Leeds office. My Vantage 14 laptop, tag LT-30412, has a cracked screen — this is the first time I've reported it." Which field in the record looks wrong?

- A) Asset tag
- B) Assigned to
- C) Location
- D) Status

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The tag, assignee, and location all match the caller. But the status says "In repair" while the caller says this is the first report — the laptop cannot already be in repair. This tests methodical field-by-field comparison, the asset and configuration management skill of tracking, logging, and correcting information.

**Question 4 (easy) — Applying a knowledge base article**

A knowledge base article for printer faults says: "Step 1: Check the printer is powered on. Step 2: If powered on, check the network cable is connected. Step 3: If connected, restart the printer. Step 4: If the fault continues after restart, log a ticket for second-line support." A user reports their multi-function device is powered on, the network cable is connected, and it has already been restarted twice, but it still will not print. What should be done next according to the article?

- A) Ask them to restart it a third time.
- B) Ask them to check the power again.
- C) Log a ticket for second-line support.
- D) Tell them the article does not cover their situation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Walking the situation through the steps in order: powered on ✓, cable connected ✓, restarted ✓ — step 4's condition is met. This tests following a documented sequence faithfully without skipping ahead or looping back unnecessarily, and knowing when the article itself hands the issue on.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Prioritising a ticket queue**

It is 09:05 on a Tuesday. Four items are waiting:

1. A priority 2 incident: a whole floor in the Cardiff office has lost access to the case management application (logged 08:55).
2. A priority 4 service request: a user wants a second monitor (logged yesterday, SLA target is five working days).
3. A priority 3 incident: one user cannot print to the multi-function device but can print to another printer nearby (logged 09:00).
4. A voicemail from a user who says their password reset "didn't work" and they cannot log in at all (not yet logged).

Which order of attention is most defensible?

- A) 2, 3, 4, 1 — first in, first out
- B) 1, 4, 3, 2 — highest impact first, then the unlogged caller, then the workaround case, then the routine request
- C) 4, 1, 3, 2 — always deal with phone messages before tickets
- D) 1, 3, 4, 2 — handle everything already in the system before logging new work

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 has the highest impact (a whole floor blocked). Item 4 comes next — the user is completely blocked and the issue is not even logged yet. Item 3 has a workaround, lowering its urgency. Item 2 is routine. This tests ranking by impact-weighted urgency, never letting unlogged work stay unlogged for long.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across incoming calls**

During Monday morning, calls between 09:00 and 09:40 include: five users cannot open email; two users cannot open email and also cannot open the intranet; one user reports a slow laptop. All seven email callers are based in the Newcastle office; the slow-laptop caller is in Bristol. What is the most reasonable working hypothesis?

- A) Seven users coincidentally forgot their email passwords.
- B) There is a site-level issue affecting Newcastle, possibly network-related, since some users have lost more than one service.
- C) The slow laptop in Bristol is causing the email problems in Newcastle.
- D) The email service is down nationally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** All seven email issues cluster at one site; two callers losing more than one service suggests the problem sits below the applications. Option D would predict calls from many sites; option C connects two unrelated facts. This tests forming a sensible working hypothesis that changes the next action — flagging a possible site issue rather than troubleshooting seven mailboxes individually.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction from categorisation rules**

Routing rules: (i) hardware faults on multi-function devices go to the print vendor; (ii) all account lockouts go to the identity team unless caused by a known application fault; (iii) application faults in the HR system go to the HR applications team. A user is locked out of the HR system. The known-issues list shows a current HR system fault that is locking out some users. Where should the ticket go?

- A) The print vendor
- B) The identity team
- C) The HR applications team
- D) Nowhere — the user should wait until the known issue is fixed

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rule (ii)'s exception ("unless caused by a known application fault") applies here, stepping the lockout rule aside; rule (iii) then routes it to the HR applications team. Option D fails the ownership duty — the ticket is still logged, linked, and routed even when a known issue exists. This tests noticing and correctly applying an exception clause.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking a handover note**

At shift handover: "INC-2026-00610: user Priya Shah, Swansea, cannot access shared drive. Checked permissions — correct. Escalated to second line at 16:40. User called back at 16:20 asking for an update; told her escalation had already been done." What is logically wrong with the note?

- A) Permissions cannot be correct if the user has no access.
- B) The user is said to have been told about the escalation at 16:20, but the escalation happened at 16:40.
- C) Shared drive issues should never be escalated.
- D) The ticket reference has the wrong format.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The note claims the escalation happened at 16:40, yet claims the user was told at 16:20 — twenty minutes earlier — that it "had already been done". This tests extracting timestamps and reading them in order, spotting a timeline contradiction that would mislead the next shift.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Applied problem solving on a fault**

A user reports their laptop cannot reach any websites. Facts: (i) their email client is sending and receiving normally; (ii) a colleague on the same office network browses the web without difficulty; (iii) the user's browser shows a proxy error message; (iv) the user installed "a free PDF tool" yesterday. What is the most likely area of the fault?

- A) The office network is down.
- B) The website the user wants is down.
- C) Something on the user's own laptop — most plausibly a browser or proxy setting changed around the time the new software was installed.
- D) The user's network cable is unplugged.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Fact (ii) rules out A; failing on all websites rules out B; fact (i) rules out D. The proxy error and the timing of the new software both point to the laptop itself. This tests elimination as a diagnostic method — each fact removing a whole class of causes.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

Three desk-side visits this week — Aled (specialised equipment), Bea (new starter setup), and Chris (recurring laptop crash) — one visit per day at most, Monday to Friday. Constraints: (i) Aled's equipment requires a vendor engineer only on site Thursday; (ii) Bea starts Wednesday, and setup must complete before her first day; (iii) Chris's crash logs finish Wednesday morning, and his visit must happen after; (iv) all of Monday is committed to phones. What is the only workable plan?

- A) Aled Tuesday, Bea Wednesday, Chris Thursday
- B) Bea Tuesday, Aled Thursday, Chris Friday
- C) Aled Thursday, Bea Monday, Chris Friday
- D) Chris Tuesday, Bea Wednesday, Aled Thursday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Constraint (iv) eliminates any Monday visit (C). Constraint (iii) eliminates Chris on Tuesday (D). Constraint (i) pins Aled to Thursday, eliminating A. Only B satisfies every constraint. This tests finding the most restrictive constraint first and treating scheduling as pass/fail against every rule, not an average.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Spotting a pattern across time**

"Cannot log in" incidents logged over two weeks. Week 1: Mon 41, Tue 12, Wed 10, Thu 11, Fri 9. Week 2: Mon 44, Tue 13, Wed 11, Thu 40, Fri 10. A change to the identity service was deployed on the evening of the Wednesday of week 2. Which statement best fits the data?

- A) The Thursday spike in week 2 is normal — Thursdays are always busy.
- B) Mondays show a regular spike (probably expired passwords after the weekend), and the unusual Thursday spike in week 2 immediately follows the identity service change, so the change is a credible suspect worth reporting.
- C) The identity change caused the Monday spikes.
- D) There is no pattern in the data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Mondays are consistently high in both weeks, a baseline that makes the week 2 Thursday spike (roughly fourfold) stand out, immediately after a related change. Option A is contradicted by week 1's Thursday; option C fails since Monday spikes predate the change. This tests separating a recurring rhythm from an anomaly and treating a plausible correlation as a signal to report, not a proven cause.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate checks a record or reference field by field** rather than judging it at a glance.
- **Watch for whether a candidate notices an exception clause** ("unless") in a rule before applying it.
- **Note whether a candidate ranks a queue by impact-weighted urgency** rather than arrival order or channel.
- **Note whether a candidate uses elimination systematically** in a diagnostic item, ruling out whole classes of cause.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and consider offering ungraded practice items first.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that treats correlation as proof** rather than a signal worth reporting.
- **Missing when a candidate misses an exception clause** in a rule.
- **Crediting first-in-first-out prioritisation** over impact-weighted urgency.
- **Missing a table or record-reading error where a candidate answers from the wrong field or row.**
- **Accepting a scheduling answer that fails even one stated constraint.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately a candidate works with the kinds of numbers this role genuinely produces: ticket volumes, SLA percentages, call handling times, first-contact resolution rates, asset counts, and simple trends over days and weeks. The core operations are percentages, averages, ratios, differences, and reading tables and charts correctly.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions, each usually presenting a small table or chart followed by one or more multiple-choice questions. An on-screen calculator is often allowed.

Numeric confidence matters for this role because the role summary asks for reports, an interpreted data set, and requests resolved to a minimum of the agreed SLA — measured in percentages and clock-times.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and SLA arithmetic** map to the **Customer service management** skill: resolving requests "to a minimum of the agreed service level agreement".
- **Reading tables and interpreting data sets** maps directly to the **IT service reporting** skill.
- **Averages and rates** map to daily call-handling work: average handling time, calls per hour, first-contact resolution rate.
- **Counts, differences, and reconciliation** map to the **Asset and configuration management** skill: making counts agree — register versus stock check.
- **Trend awareness** maps to the **Service focus** and **User focus** skills.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of tickets resolved**

On Monday a desk received 80 incident tickets and resolved 60 of them the same day. What percentage of Monday's tickets were resolved the same day?

- A) 60%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 60÷80=0.75=75%. This tests basic part-of-whole calculation, and a strong candidate's answer sense-checks via fractions (6 of 8 = 3 of 4 = 75%) rather than repeating the raw count as a percentage.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average handling time**

Five calls this morning lasted 4, 6, 5, 9, and 6 minutes. What was the average (mean) handling time?

- A) 5 minutes
- B) 6 minutes
- C) 7 minutes
- D) 9 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum=30; mean=30÷5=6 minutes. Option D is the longest single call, not the average — a common mix-up. This tests basic mean calculation, and a strong candidate's answer notes one long call can pull a team's average up.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a simple table**

Weekly report of tickets logged by category:

| Category | Tickets logged |
|---|---|
| Password and account | 45 |
| Hardware | 20 |
| Printing and MFDs | 15 |
| Applications | 30 |
| Other | 10 |

How many tickets were logged in total, and which category was largest?

- A) 110 total; Applications largest
- B) 120 total; Password and account largest
- C) 120 total; Applications largest
- D) 110 total; Password and account largest

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total=120; largest single value is 45 (Password and account). This tests careful addition across a full column, and a strong candidate's answer notes password issues make up 37.5% of tickets — a self-service reset argument.

**Question 4 (easy) — SLA target check**

The SLA for priority 3 incidents says 90% must be resolved within 8 working hours. Last week a desk resolved 63 of its 70 priority 3 incidents within 8 working hours. Did the desk meet the SLA?

- A) Yes — exactly 90% were resolved in time.
- B) Yes — 93% were resolved in time.
- C) No — only 63% were resolved in time.
- D) No — 88% were resolved in time.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 63÷70=0.9=90%, meeting a "minimum of 90%" exactly. Option C reads the raw count as a percentage. This tests SLA arithmetic in both directions — what percentage was achieved, and how many can be missed while still hitting the target.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Percentage change in call volume**

A desk took 500 calls in March and 575 calls in April. By what percentage did call volume increase?

- A) 7.5%
- B) 13%
- C) 15%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** (575−500)÷500=15%. Option D reads the raw increase as a percentage; option B divides by the new figure instead of the original. This tests measuring percentage change against the starting value.

**Question 6 (moderate) — First-contact resolution rate**

"First-contact resolution" (FCR) means the ticket was resolved during the user's first call, with no follow-up needed. Yesterday a desk handled 140 calls; 98 were resolved at first contact. The desk's FCR target is 65%. Which statement is correct?

- A) FCR was 70%, which beats the target by 5 percentage points.
- B) FCR was 65%, exactly on target.
- C) FCR was 70%, which beats the target by 5%.
- D) FCR was 42%, below target.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 98÷140=70%; 70−65=5 percentage points above target. This tests the distinction between percentage points and percent — option C's "5%" would mean a much smaller gap (3.25 points) than the actual 5-point difference.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Ratio of analysts to calls**

A desk expects 360 calls today and each analyst can handle about 45 calls per day. How many analysts are needed today, and if only 6 are available, roughly how many calls per analyst does that imply?

- A) 8 needed; 60 calls each if only 6 available
- B) 8 needed; 45 calls each if only 6 available
- C) 7 needed; 51 calls each if only 6 available
- D) 9 needed; 40 calls each if only 6 available

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 360÷45=8 needed; 360÷6=60 calls each if short-staffed. This tests converting a staffing shortfall into a concrete, checkable per-person figure.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a two-way table**

Monthly report by site:

| Site | SLA met | SLA missed |
|---|---|---|
| Leeds | 180 | 20 |
| Cardiff | 135 | 15 |
| Newcastle | 88 | 22 |

Which site had the worst SLA performance in percentage terms?

- A) Leeds
- B) Cardiff
- C) Newcastle
- D) They all performed equally.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Leeds 90%, Cardiff 90%, Newcastle 80%. Comparing raw missed counts (20 vs 22) misleads, since Leeds handled almost twice Newcastle's volume. This tests converting to percentages before comparing groups of different sizes.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Working backwards from an SLA**

Next week a desk expects 240 priority 3 incidents. The SLA requires 90% resolved within 8 working hours. What is the maximum number of those incidents the desk can miss while still meeting the SLA?

- A) 12
- B) 24
- C) 36
- D) 90

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 10% of 240 = 24. This tests working backwards from a target percentage to an allowable count — a "budget" of misses that lets a desk track progress mid-week.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Combining averages**

Average call handling time on Tuesday: morning — 60 calls at 5 minutes; afternoon — 40 calls at 8 minutes. What was the overall average handling time for the day?

- A) 6.5 minutes
- B) 6.2 minutes
- C) 6.8 minutes
- D) 13 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total minutes = 300+320 = 620; total calls = 100; average = 6.2 minutes. Option A is the unweighted average of 5 and 8. This tests weighted averaging when groups differ in size — sites, shifts, or weeks.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Trend and projection from a chart description**

Open tickets at the end of each week: week 1: 120; week 2: 132; week 3: 145; week 4: 160. If this keeps growing at roughly the same rate, about how many open tickets should be expected at the end of week 6?

- A) About 175
- B) About 190
- C) About 210
- D) About 320

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Week-on-week increases are 12, 13, 15 — creeping upward, so extending at roughly 15–16 per week gives week 5≈175, week 6≈191. This tests measuring the pattern in the differences before projecting, rather than eyeballing the last number.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Asset stock reconciliation**

The asset register says the Leeds store room holds 84 laptops. A physical stock check counts 78. Separately, 5 laptops issued to new starters this week were taken from the Leeds store but never recorded as removed. After correcting the register for those 5, how many laptops remain unaccounted for?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Raw discrepancy 84−78=6; correcting the register for the 5 unrecorded removals (84−5=79 should be on the shelf) leaves 79−78=1 unaccounted for. This tests reconciliation as careful subtraction — explaining each known difference before treating the remainder as a genuine exception.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate identifies the correct denominator or base** before calculating a percentage.
- **Watch for whether a candidate distinguishes percentage points from percent.**
- **Note whether a candidate combines figures from unequal groups by totals** rather than averaging averages.
- **Note whether a candidate reconciles a discrepancy by explaining each known difference** before treating the remainder as a genuine exception.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that reads a raw count as a percentage.**
- **Crediting a percentage change divided by the wrong base.**
- **Missing when a candidate averages averages** from differently sized groups.
- **Accepting a projection that is not grounded in the measured differences.**
- **Missing when a candidate stops a reconciliation one step early**, without applying a known correction.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads, understands, and draws conclusions from written material of the kind this role actually uses: IT policies, service notices, knowledge base articles, emails from users, and short reports. The most common format presents a passage of 100 to 250 words followed by statements to judge as **True**, **False**, or **Cannot say**. Other question types ask for the best summary, what a user's email is actually asking for, or the clearest rewrite of a clumsy sentence.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 statements spread across several passages. The golden rule is to answer only from the passage.

Verbal reasoning is a daily working skill in light disguise for this role. Every call begins with listening and reading, and the role also requires communicating outwards: ticket updates the next shift can act on, an outage notice explained plainly, reports others can trust.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading comprehension of procedures and notices** maps to the **Customer service management** skill.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to the **Asset and configuration management** skill: recording only what is known to be true.
- **Identifying what a user is really asking** maps to the **User focus** skill.
- **Faithful restatement and summary** maps to the **IT service reporting** skill.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to the **Ownership** skill: a written handover is the baton passed to a new owner.

### Practice questions

For Questions 1 to 3, the candidate reads this passage.

> "From Monday 2 March, the department's remote access service will require multi-factor authentication (MFA) for all users. Users who have not enrolled a second factor by Friday 27 February will be unable to connect remotely until they enrol. Enrolment takes about ten minutes and can be completed from the office network or by calling the service desk. Desk analysts may enrol users over the phone only after completing identity verification. The change does not affect access from departmental offices."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "After 2 March, a user who has not enrolled a second factor will be unable to log in from a departmental office."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "The change does not affect access from departmental offices" directly contradicts the statement. This tests reading to the end of a passage before judging — the contradicting sentence appears after the alarming one.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A service desk analyst may enrol a user over the phone without verifying their identity, provided the user is calling from an office number."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Analysts "may enrol users over the phone only after completing identity verification" — "only" is an absolute condition with no stated exceptions. This tests checking the strength of qualifying words like "only".

**Question 3 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Most users had already enrolled a second factor by 27 February."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage sets the deadline and its consequences, but never states how many users met it. This tests treating a plausible statement as unsupported when the passage genuinely does not address it.

For Questions 4 to 6, the candidate reads this passage.

> "Service desk analysts must log every user contact as a ticket, including contacts resolved during the call. Where a contact reports a fault already covered by an open major incident, the analyst should link the new ticket to the major incident record rather than troubleshooting individually. Users should be given the major incident reference and the current estimated restoration time, where one has been published. Analysts should not give personal estimates of restoration times. Tickets linked to a major incident are closed automatically when the major incident is resolved."

**Question 4 (easy) — Identifying the main obligation**

Which of the following best summarises the passage's core instruction to analysts?

- A) Analysts should avoid logging tickets during major incidents to save time.
- B) Every contact is logged; contacts about a known major incident are linked to it, and users are told the reference and any published restoration estimate.
- C) Analysts should troubleshoot each major-incident-related call individually to be thorough.
- D) Analysts should estimate restoration times from their own experience so users get an answer.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B faithfully compresses all four instructions and adds nothing. Options A, C and D each contradict one explicit sentence. This tests summarising without dropping or adding an obligation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "If no restoration time has been published, the analyst should tell the user their own best guess."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Analysts should not give personal estimates of restoration times" directly contradicts a "best guess". This tests recognising that a personal guess is exactly what a stated prohibition rules out.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A ticket linked to a major incident may need to be reopened by the analyst if the user's fault continues after the major incident is resolved."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states linked tickets close automatically on resolution, but says nothing about what happens if an individual fault persists afterwards. This tests restraint — the statement is operationally sensible but unsupported by this text.

For Questions 7 to 9, the candidate reads this email.

> "Hi, sorry to bother you. My laptop has been slow all week, but that's not why I'm writing. This morning I got an email saying my mailbox is nearly full and I'll stop receiving messages tomorrow unless I clear space. I'm in hearings all day and can't sort this out myself. I've also noticed the email came from an address I don't recognise — is it even genuine? Please can someone help before tomorrow? Thanks, Sam (Casework, Cardiff)."

**Question 7 (moderate) — What is the user actually asking for?**

Which of the following best captures Sam's need?

- A) A fix for a slow laptop this week.
- B) Urgent help with the mailbox-full warning before tomorrow, including a check of whether the warning email is genuine.
- C) Training on how to archive email.
- D) A new laptop, since the current one is slow.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sam explicitly sets the slow laptop aside; the core, deadlined request is the mailbox warning, plus a genuine authenticity question. This tests reading for the stated need, its deadline, and its conditions — including a flagged doubt, not just a surface request.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Choosing the best written reply**

Which reply to Sam is clearest and most appropriate?

- A) "Hi Sam — your mailbox quota issue has been remediated via retention policy adjustment pending infrastructure-side archival execution."
- B) "Hi Sam — thanks for flagging this. First, please don't click anything in that email: we'll check whether it's genuine and confirm. Meanwhile we've raised ticket INC-2026-00712 and will make sure your mailbox keeps receiving messages — no action needed from you today. We've also logged your slow laptop as a separate ticket and will follow up after your hearings."
- C) "Hi Sam — this is probably fine, these warnings usually sort themselves out. Ignore it."
- D) "Hi Sam — please free up 500MB by deleting old attachments before 9am tomorrow, per policy EM-14."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B addresses the possible phishing risk first, takes ownership with a reference, removes the burden from an unavailable user, and captures the secondary issue. Option A is system jargon; option C dismisses a possible phishing risk; option D ignores Sam's unavailability. This tests writing accurately, plainly, and shaped around the reader's actual situation.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Inference from a service report extract**

A monthly report states: "Complaints about the service desk fell from 30 in June to 18 in July. The desk answered 12% fewer calls in July, as the new online self-service portal absorbed simple password resets. Average call duration rose from 5.1 to 6.4 minutes." Which inference is best supported?

- A) The desk gave worse service in July, because calls took longer.
- B) The remaining calls in July were likely more complex on average, since simple password resets — typically quick calls — moved to the portal.
- C) The portal caused the complaints to fall.
- D) Desk staff worked more slowly in July.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Removing short calls from the mix raises the average duration of what remains, even with unchanged staff performance. Options A and D misread a composition effect as worse or slower service; option C links two facts the passage never connects. This tests asking "did the work change?" before "did the people change?" when a metric moves.

For Questions 10 to 11, the candidate reads this passage.

> "The department will replace multi-function devices (MFDs) at all sites during the autumn. Each site will lose printing for one working day during its swap. Site swap dates will be announced at least ten working days in advance. Analysts should direct users' questions about swap dates to the published schedule rather than answering from memory. During a site's swap day, urgent printing can be done at the nearest alternative site, and the service desk can redirect print queues on request. Specialised IT equipment, including large-format scanners, is out of scope for this programme."

**Question 10 (challenging) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A user at a site whose swap is underway has no way to print urgently that day."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage offers two explicit routes for urgent printing on a swap day, directly contradicting "no way". This tests hunting for a counter-example to an absolute statement before considering Cannot Say.

**Question 11 (challenging) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Large-format scanners at each site will be replaced after the MFD programme finishes."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Out of scope for this programme" says nothing about after the programme — a future replacement is neither promised nor denied. This tests distinguishing familiarity (the passage mentions scanners) from actual support for a specific future claim.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads to the end of a passage** before judging a statement, since qualifying sentences often follow.
- **Watch for whether a candidate treats a plausible statement as unsupported** when the passage genuinely does not address it.
- **Note whether a candidate hunts for a counter-example to an absolute claim** ("no way", "all", "never") before marking Cannot Say.
- **Note whether a candidate's written reply is accurate, plain, and shaped around the reader's actual situation.**
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer drawn from outside experience** rather than the passage.
- **Missing when a candidate stops reading at the first relevant sentence**, before a later qualifying one.
- **Crediting a summary that adds detail** the passage does not state, however sensible.
- **Missing when a candidate confuses familiarity with support** for a specific claim.
- **Accepting a written reply chosen for formality over plainness and accuracy.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to judge possible responses. Common formats include choosing the most effective response, choosing the most and least effective from four options, ranking all the options, or rating each response's effectiveness on a scale.

The typical format is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, usually untimed or generously timed, because it measures judgement rather than speed. Scoring compares choices against a key agreed by experienced professionals and calibrated to the organisation's values — user focus, ownership, working openly with colleagues, escalating honestly, and treating everyone with respect.

The SJT is arguably the most job-relevant assessment for this role, because it lives in exactly these moments: a caller is upset, a queue is building, the answer is uncertain, a colleague is struggling, or a rule points one way and kindness seems to point another.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Handling difficult calls with care** maps to the **Customer service management** skill: "empathise with the end user" and "use customer-focused metrics to achieve the desired outcome".
- **Knowing when to escalate** maps to the role-level duty to know when to escalate issues.
- **Staying with an issue** maps to the **Ownership** skill.
- **Accurate records and honesty** maps to the **Asset and configuration management** and **IT service reporting** skills.
- **Seeing the user behind the ticket** maps to the **User focus** and **Service focus** skills.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — The angry caller**

A caller is angry: their laptop failed this morning, they have a deadline at noon, and this is their second call because the first ticket "went nowhere". They raise their voice and say the desk is useless. What is the most effective first response?

- A) State calmly that help cannot be given while they are shouting, and ask them to call back later.
- B) Listen without interrupting, acknowledge the frustration and the deadline, apologise for the poor experience so far, then confirm the ticket details and set out what will happen right now.
- C) Explain that the first analyst followed procedure and the delay is not the desk's fault.
- D) Transfer the call straight to a manager, since the caller is upset.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B lets the user be heard, acknowledges the impact honestly, and moves immediately to concrete action — empathy followed by a plan. Option A escalates the conflict; option C defends the desk instead of helping; option D passes emotion upwards without attempting a recovery squarely within the role. This tests the pattern: acknowledge, take ownership, act.

**Question 2 (easy) — Unsure of the fix**

A caller has a fault on an application rarely supported. The knowledge base article is unclear, and there is little confidence the suggested steps are safe. The caller says "just try something". What is the most effective response?

- A) Try a few likely fixes — the right one will probably turn up.
- B) Tell the caller their application is not supported and close the ticket.
- C) Be honest about wanting to check first, place them briefly on hold or arrange a call-back, and confirm the correct approach with a senior analyst or the article's owning team.
- D) Read the unclear article steps to the caller and let them decide whether to try them.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C is honest about the limits of current knowledge, protects the user from harm, and uses the escalation route explicitly given by the role. Option A risks making things worse to avoid a moment of awkwardness. Option D transfers a decision the caller cannot judge. This tests calibrated confidence: act within competence, verify at its edge, escalate beyond it.

**Question 3 (easy) — The queue and the colleague**

Mid-morning, calls are queuing. A new analyst nearby has been on one call for 25 minutes, looks flustered, and is repeating the same steps. There is a moment between calls. What is the most effective action?

- A) Stay focused on the next queued call — the queue is the priority.
- B) Send a quick message offering help, and if accepted, either take over the call warmly or suggest the next diagnostic step — then take the next queued call.
- C) Interrupt their call and take over, announcing that it will be handled from here.
- D) Mention it to a manager at the end of the week so training can be arranged.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B helps two people at once — the stuck caller, and a colleague who learns a next step instead of a humiliation — for a small cost against the queue. Option C solves the call but damages the colleague's confidence. Option D helps nobody today. This tests team-level service, not individual statistics.

**Question 4 (easy) — Logging the awkward ticket**

Just before shift ends, a quick call is resolved — a user's monitor cable was loose. Logging it properly takes three minutes, and a lift home is waiting. A colleague says: "It was nothing — don't bother logging it." What is the most effective response?

- A) Skip logging this once; it genuinely was trivial.
- B) Log it tomorrow morning from memory.
- C) Log it now, briefly but accurately, before leaving.
- D) Ask the colleague to log it tomorrow instead.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Desk statistics drive staffing and SLA reporting, and "trivial" contacts are data that only become visible as patterns if logged. Option B fades into vaguer, less accurate records; option D transfers the duty to someone with less knowledge of the call. This tests small, consistent integrity in record-keeping.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The security-shaped request**

A caller says they are a private secretary calling on behalf of a director who is locked out and about to join a ministerial call. They ask for the director's password to be reset and read out over the phone "to save time", but cannot answer identity verification questions for the director's account. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reset the password and share it — the situation is clearly urgent and senior.
- B) Refuse and end the call.
- C) Explain warmly that identity verification cannot be bypassed for any account, and offer the fastest compliant route — for example, contacting the director directly through a verified channel, or engaging a manager immediately given the urgency.
- D) Ask more questions until comfortable, then reset it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Urgency plus seniority plus a request to bypass procedure is a classic social-engineering pattern. Option C pairs a firm "no" to the bypass with a fast legitimate path. Option A could hand an account to an impostor; option B fails a genuine user completely. This tests holding a security line without abandoning genuine service.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The pattern in the queue**

During one hour, four calls from different users all describe slow logins in the Swansea office. Each fault is mild, and each user is content with a workaround. The shift is busy. What is the most effective action beyond resolving each call?

- A) Nothing further — each user was helped and each ticket is closed.
- B) Link the tickets, note the common site and symptom, and flag the pattern to a senior analyst or manager as a possible underlying problem.
- C) Email all Swansea users warning of major problems ahead.
- D) Start investigating the Swansea network personally between calls.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four similar reports from one site in one hour suggests a shared cause individual workarounds will never fix. Option A lets an emerging problem grow unrecorded. Option C overreaches knowledge and remit; option D overreaches the role. This tests seeing the service behind the symptoms and escalating a well-described pattern.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The tempting shortcut on records**

Updating an asset record after a call reveals the previous analyst recorded a laptop as returned to store, but the user says they still have it. Correcting the record means reopening a closed task and possibly flagging a colleague's error. What is the most effective response?

- A) Leave the record — it is the previous analyst's responsibility.
- B) Correct the record with a factual note of what the user said, and mention it to the colleague or a senior analyst so any similar errors can be caught.
- C) Correct the record but keep it quiet to protect the colleague.
- D) Tell a manager the colleague is making errors, without touching the record.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B fixes the data and addresses the cause, factually and without blame. Option A walks past a known-wrong record; option C hides a signal that would let the error repeat; option D raises the person without fixing the fact. This tests correcting the record, stating the facts, and sharing the learning through the lightest appropriate channel.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Promising what cannot be promised**

A caller with an ongoing fault asks: "Can you promise this will be fixed by tomorrow? I'll tell my team it will be." Second-line support owns the ticket, and the timescale genuinely is not known. The caller is pleasant and clearly under pressure. What is the most effective response?

- A) Promise tomorrow — it will probably be fine, and the caller needs reassurance.
- B) Say it is out of the candidate's hands and they should contact second line themselves.
- C) Be honest that a time cannot be promised because another team is working on it, commit to what can be controlled — chasing an update today and calling back with whatever is learned — and log their deadline pressure on the ticket.
- D) Say nothing is ever certain in IT and they should plan for the worst.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C separates what is controllable from what is not, and promises only within the first set. Option A buys thirty seconds of relief at the cost of a broken promise; option B breaks the ownership chain by washing hands of the relationship. This tests honesty about limits paired with genuine commitment to what can be delivered.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The colleague under pressure and the metric**

A desk publishes each analyst's daily resolved-call count. A colleague quietly confides they have started closing tickets as "resolved" after giving users a workaround, then advising them to "call back and open a new ticket if it recurs" — it keeps their numbers high, and they suggest doing the same, adding "everyone does it". What is the most effective response?

- A) Adopt the practice — if everyone does it, refusing just makes the candidate's numbers look worse.
- B) Decline personally but say nothing further; their tickets are their business.
- C) Decline, explain briefly why it misleads the desk's data and harms users, and if the practice continues or really is widespread, raise the pattern with a senior analyst or manager — focusing on the metric's design as well as the behaviour.
- D) Announce at the team meeting that the colleague is gaming the statistics.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C gets three layers right: personal decline, honest peer feedback, and system-level escalation of a pattern rather than a person if it persists. Option A joins a practice that breaks reporting and user trust; option B protects one's own hands while the data and users keep suffering; option D chooses the maximum-humiliation channel. This tests decline, explain, escalate the pattern — not the person.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective**

At 16:45, fifteen minutes before the desk closes, a major application goes down. Calls flood in. A manager is in a meeting and unreachable for a few minutes. Desk procedure says a suspected major incident should be reported to the duty manager immediately. Consider these options:

1. Follow the major incident procedure: alert the duty manager through the standing route, keep logging and linking the incoming calls, and tell callers honestly that the issue is being escalated.
2. Wait for the regular manager to leave the meeting, since they know the application best.
3. Keep answering calls individually and troubleshooting each one until closing time.
4. Post in the team channel that the application "seems broken" and carry on as normal.

Which is the MOST effective and which is the LEAST effective response?

- A) Most: 1; Least: 3
- B) Most: 1; Least: 4
- C) Most: 2; Least: 3
- D) Most: 3; Least: 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option 1 uses the standing route designed to work when a regular manager is unreachable, and gives honest status while protecting data. Option 3 is worst: it absorbs remaining capacity troubleshooting symptoms individually, raises no alarm through any channel, and runs the clock into the unstaffed evening unreported — while feeling busy and diligent. This tests recognising that heroic individual effort that delays the alarm is not diligence but risk.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inclusion on the phones**

A caller with a stammer is struggling to describe their fault. Calls are queuing, and average handling time is monitored. A colleague messages: "Wrap it up, queue's building." What is the most effective response?

- A) Politely hurry the caller along, offering to guess the rest of their fault description.
- B) Give the caller the time needed, use patient techniques — letting them finish, confirming understanding, offering to switch to email or chat if they would prefer — and briefly reply to the colleague that this call needs proper time.
- C) Ask the caller to email the desk instead, and take the next queued call.
- D) Transfer the caller to a quieter colleague.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Letting the caller finish, confirming understanding, and offering an alternative channel as a genuine choice are standard good practice for callers with speech differences. Option C converts difficulty into exclusion when imposed rather than offered; option D exports the discomfort and makes the caller start again. This tests adapting the service to the user, never the user to the service.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate's response acknowledges impact before or alongside taking action**, rather than one without the other.
- **Watch for whether a candidate holds a security line without abandoning genuine service** in the social-engineering scenario.
- **Note whether a candidate escalates a pattern rather than a person** when raising a colleague's concerning behaviour.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes what is controllable from what is not** when a caller wants a promise.
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that promises a time or outcome that is not actually controllable.**
- **Crediting an answer that trades security verification away under pressure of urgency or seniority.**
- **Missing when a candidate escalates to avoid ownership, or owns to avoid escalation** — both extremes are weak.
- **Accepting an answer that fixes a record but hides the pattern**, or raises a person without fixing the record.
- **Missing when a candidate lets a monitored metric override genuine service to an individual user.**

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a service desk analyst: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested the fast, careful thinking the desk runs on: spotting a malformed ticket reference, applying escalation rules with their exception clauses, reconciling asset records against what users say, and prioritising a queue by impact rather than arrival order. The numeric section tested the desk's own numbers: SLA percentages, handling times, first-contact resolution, weighted averages, and stock reconciliation. The verbal section tested the discipline of answering only from the text, judging True, False, and the deceptively difficult Cannot Say, and choosing replies written in the user's language. The situational judgement section tested the moments that define first-line work: the angry caller, the security-shaped shortcut, the colleague under pressure, and the pattern hiding in four similar calls.

The thread running through all four sections is field-by-field checking, impact-weighted prioritisation, dividing by the right denominator, reading to the end before judging, and promising only what is controllable.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can resolve a situation, protect the honest record, and treat users well under pressure — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
