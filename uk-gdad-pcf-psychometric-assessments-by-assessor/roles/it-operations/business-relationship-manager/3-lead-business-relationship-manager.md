# Lead Business Relationship Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for lead business relationship manager, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At leadership level, the versions worth attention are job-specific: they draw on the artefacts and situations the role genuinely owns — portfolio-level engagement strategies, spending review and services strategy commitments, escalation cases that arrive precisely because they defeated the team below, external partner relationships, and the culture a candidate's choices create for the business relationship managers they lead. And they test the judgements that distinguish the lead role: directing analysis rather than performing it, arbitrating between senior stakeholders who each hold legitimate positions, facilitating high-risk discussions under constrained timelines, and seeing groups of services as a system rather than as a list.

A lead business relationship manager leads engagement with internal and external stakeholders to ensure complex needs and challenges are understood, prioritised and communicated to relevant audiences, and may lead a team of business relationship managers. The role ensures strategic demands such as spending reviews and services strategy commitments are met and appropriately communicated, promotes ways of working that support meaningful connections and outcomes, sets conditions that enable a culture of positive impact, and enables others to implement activities that improve relationship maturity.

This document is organised into four assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why employers use it at this level, a mapping of the assessment dimensions to the specific skills in the role summary, a substantial set of items with full worked explanations for you to use as an answer key, administration notes, and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring. The challenging items are genuinely leadership-calibrated: ambiguity, competing goods, organisational politics, and the development of a candidate's people are part of the design.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate you assess at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Let the candidate work through each section without interruption, and note anything unusual about the conditions so your record is honest. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. ## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes this role demands, using realistic materials rather than abstract puzzles. At leadership level the emphasis falls on reasoning about systems and about other people's reasoning: evaluating analysis the candidate did not perform, choosing the simplest adequate approach among several defensible ones, deducing consequences through chains of organisational rules, and prioritising a portfolio where every item arrives pre-labelled "urgent" by someone senior.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving, scored objectively against a norm group — for lead roles, usually a senior-managerial one. Adaptive difficulty and separate speed/accuracy reporting are standard.

For a lead business relationship manager, cognitive assessment matters because errors at this level propagate through other people. Directing business analysis means the framing of the question determines what the team finds; the bigger-picture view of groups of services becomes the organisation's consolidation and investment decisions; and escalations arrive stripped of easy structure, since the structured versions were resolved below this level.

### What it measures for the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Service focus** at this level: seeing the bigger picture across groups of services.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic thinking**: reasoning from commitments to consequences before events do it for you.
- **Error checking** maps to directing business analysis: reviewing more analysis than is produced, finding the flaw in work not built by the candidate.
- **Choosing the simplest adequate approach** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**'s sufficiency judgement.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Ownership and topic** and escalation duties.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to the duty to facilitate discussions across high-risk, complex areas under constrained timelines.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a service portfolio**

A candidate reviews the annual figures for five internal services their function partners across:

| Service | Users | Annual cost (£k) | Satisfaction | Support tickets/year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expenses | 8,000 | 240 | 3.9/5 | 1,600 |
| Room booking | 7,500 | 90 | 4.1/5 | 300 |
| HR self-service | 8,200 | 310 | 3.8/5 | 1,700 |
| Travel booking | 3,000 | 220 | 2.6/5 | 1,900 |
| Print management | 6,000 | 85 | 4.0/5 | 250 |

Which service most clearly warrants attention first, and on what pattern?

- A) HR self-service — it has the highest cost.
- B) Travel booking — it combines the fewest users with near-highest cost and tickets and the lowest satisfaction, giving it the worst per-user profile on every dimension at once.
- C) Expenses — it has the most users, so problems there affect the most people.
- D) Print management — its low ticket count suggests under-reporting.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Normalising by users: travel booking costs roughly £73 per user against £30 for expenses and £12–£38 elsewhere, and generates roughly 0.63 tickets per user, triple or more the other services, while sitting a full point below the next-worst satisfaction score. One service is an outlier in the same direction on every normalised dimension at once, and that convergence is the pattern — invisible to anyone reading the columns raw. Option A reads a single absolute column. Option C uses reach as a proxy for priority when expenses' per-user profile is mid-pack. Option D invents a hypothesis against the simpler reading that a healthy service generates few tickets. This item tests normalising then looking for the outlier on every axis at once.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction through delegation rules**

An engagement framework states: (i) business relationship managers may commit the function to engagement activities costing up to £5,000 without approval; (ii) commitments between £5,000 and £25,000 require lead BRM approval; (iii) commitments above £25,000 require director approval, and the lead BRM must be consulted before the request reaches the director. A BRM on a candidate's team has agreed with a unit to run a discovery exercise costing £18,000, and work has started. Which statement must be true?

- A) The framework has been breached unless the candidate approved the commitment.
- B) The BRM should have sought director approval.
- C) The commitment was within the BRM's own delegated authority.
- D) The discovery exercise must now be stopped.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £18,000 falls in band (ii), requiring lead BRM approval. Work has started, so either the lead approved it or the framework was breached — the disjunction collapses to A's conditional. Option B applies band (iii) to a band (ii) amount. Option C applies band (i). Option D states a remedy no rule requires. This item tests precise banding, and the leadership texture matters: delegation frameworks only scale the leader if bands are applied precisely, by the team and by the leader — treating every band-(ii) event as a crisis teaches a team to stop telling their lead things.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a commissioned analysis**

A BRM on a candidate's team submits a shared-needs analysis concluding: "All four units want case tracking functionality, so a single shared case tracking platform is justified. Unit interviews confirmed the need: Unit W (12 interviews), Unit X (10), Unit Y (2), Unit Z (11). Combined demand is 35 confirmed user needs." As the director of this analysis, which is the most important weakness to send back?

- A) The interview counts should be equal across units.
- B) Unit Y's conclusion rests on 2 interviews — far too thin to "confirm" a unit-level need — so the all-four-units claim, and with it the shared-platform justification, currently overstates the evidence.
- C) 12 + 10 + 2 + 11 = 35 is correct, so the analysis is sound.
- D) Interviews are the wrong method entirely; only quantitative data can confirm needs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Directing analysis means finding the inference that outruns its evidence. Option C confirms an irrelevant sum — 35 interviews is not "35 confirmed user needs". Options A and D impose false absolutes. The load-bearing weakness is Unit Y: the headline claim of all four units depends on a unit whose evidence is two conversations, and if Unit Y drops out the case becomes "three of four", which may still justify sharing but on different economics. This item tests finding a conclusion's weakest load-bearing point and testing whether it holds the weight the recommendation places on it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation at the point of escalation**

Monday, 08:30. Four escalations sit in a candidate's queue, all marked urgent by their senders:

1. A director complains that her unit's favoured investment was ranked below the cut line by the prioritisation forum on Friday and demands the ranking be "corrected" before it is published on Wednesday.
2. One of the candidate's BRMs reports that a unit head shouted at her in a meeting and she does not feel able to return to that unit alone; the unit's quarterly planning session — which she was due to facilitate — is Thursday.
3. An external delivery partner emails that, without a signed data-sharing renewal by Friday, they must legally suspend a data feed that three services consume.
4. The candidate's director asks for "thoughts by tomorrow" on a draft restructure of the BRM function that would move two of the candidate's team to another division.

Which ordering of first attention is most defensible for Monday morning?

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4 — the director outranks the others and the publication deadline is nearest.
- B) 2, 3, 1, 4 — duty of care to the BRM first and it shapes Thursday's plan; then the legal deadline with lead time for signatures; then the ranking complaint; then the restructure response.
- C) 3, 4, 1, 2 — legal risk, then the director, then the director's complaint, then the team issue.
- D) 4, 1, 2, 3 — the director's request governs the candidate's own team's future and must come first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 2 comes first: a team member has faced behaviour no process should normalise, and the conversation is time-critical since it shapes Thursday's facilitation plan. Item 3 is a hard legal deadline with a dependency chain requiring days to mobilise. Item 1 is not a "correction" — a forum outcome a director dislikes is to be defended or appealed, not edited on demand; the measured reply protects the forum's integrity. Item 4 deserves reflective quality later in the day, not first-thing reactivity. Options A, C and D over-weight hierarchy or treat the shouting incident as residual, which signals to the whole team what escalations are worth. This item rewards people first, then legal clocks, then governance integrity, then reflective strategy.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The simplest adequate approach**

A director asks whether her directorate's five annual conferences should consolidate onto one events-management platform. Available approaches: (a) a six-week discovery with user research across all five conference teams; (b) a two-day desk review of what each conference currently uses, costs, and complains about, followed by a one-hour workshop with the five organisers; (c) a market scan and procurement options analysis of events platforms; (d) piloting the leading platform on the next conference and evaluating afterwards. Which approach should be directed first, and why?

- A) Approach (a) — user research should always precede consolidation decisions.
- B) Approach (b) — the cheapest sufficient test of whether a shared need exists at all; the other approaches each assume an answer this one is designed to establish.
- C) Approach (c) — knowing the market is the precondition for any decision.
- D) Approach (d) — real usage evidence beats any analysis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The decision-relevant question is not "which platform?" but "is there a shared need worth consolidating?" — and approach (b) answers precisely that for two days' effort. Approach (a) spends six weeks before establishing whether research-grade evidence is even needed. Approach (c) analyses a market before knowing whether the organisation is a coherent buyer. Approach (d) commits a real conference to a platform nobody has established a shared need for. This item tests **Business analysis**'s explicit skill of identifying the simplest of a variety of approaches — matching the weight of the method to the weight of the present decision.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction across a services strategy commitment**

The services strategy commits: (i) by next April, every citizen-facing service must offer an assisted-digital route; (ii) services with fewer than 1,000 annual transactions may share an assisted-digital route with another service, provided a shared-route agreement is signed; (iii) no service may claim compliance through a shared route whose signed agreement it cannot produce. An audit of three services in a candidate's portfolio finds: Service P (12,000 transactions) has its own route. Service Q (600 transactions) uses Service R's route and produces a signed agreement. Service R (30,000 transactions) has its own route, which is the one Q shares. Which statement is correct?

- A) All three services comply with the strategy as audited.
- B) Q is non-compliant because it lacks its own route.
- C) R is non-compliant because it shares its route with Q.
- D) Q's compliance cannot be assessed without knowing R's transaction volume.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** P has its own route, satisfying rule (i) directly. Q is below the 1,000 threshold, so rule (ii) permits sharing, and the signed agreement satisfies rule (iii). R has its own route, and nothing prohibits a compliant service from hosting a shared route. Option B ignores the small-service exception. Option C invents a restriction the strategy does not contain. Option D imports an irrelevant condition — the threshold applies to the sharing service, not the host. This item tests catching a compliance misreading before it becomes a remediation demand on a unit that did everything right.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a portfolio dashboard**

A quarterly portfolio dashboard, compiled by a candidate's team for the executive committee, includes:

- "Engagement coverage: 28 of 30 in-scope units engaged this quarter (93%)."
- "Pipeline conversion: 15 of the 60 opportunities logged this year have been funded (25%), up from 20% last year."
- "Escalations: 9 raised this quarter, 9 resolved — resolution rate 100%."
- "Satisfaction: mean 4.1/5 across units responding (12 of 30 units)."

Which line most needs correction or caveat before the dashboard is submitted?

- A) The engagement coverage line — 28 of 30 is 93.3%, so it should say 93.3%.
- B) The satisfaction line — a 4.1 mean drawn from a 40% response rate is presented as portfolio-wide satisfaction, and the non-responding 18 units may differ systematically.
- C) The escalations line — 9 raised and 9 resolved does not establish that the *same* nine were resolved, so "resolution rate 100%" may mix cohorts and should state cohort explicitly.
- D) The pipeline line — 15 of 60 is 25%, which is correctly computed, so no change is needed anywhere.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option B's critique is real but the line already discloses "12 of 30 units", so the fix is prominence. Option C identifies the more misleading defect: "9 raised, 9 resolved, 100%" reads as this quarter's escalations were all resolved, but nothing establishes the resolved nine are the raised nine — a quarter resolving nine old cases while its own nine remain open would show identical figures. Cohort-mixing of this kind is how flow metrics flatter. Options A and D are cosmetic or empty. This item tests interrogating every ratio for its cohort before a dashboard ships upward.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Multi-constraint facilitation under a deadline**

A cross-government data-sharing initiative facilitated by a candidate must reach signed agreement by Friday, or funding lapses. Five parties must sign: departments A, B, C, a regulator R, and an external delivery partner E. Constraints: (i) R will not sign until both A and B have signed, and R's signatures are executed only at its authorisation board, which meets on Thursdays; (ii) E will not sign until R has signed; (iii) C's legal team requires 48 hours between receiving the final text and C signing; (iv) A and B will each sign one working day after the final text is agreed, and the final text will be agreed at Monday's drafting session; (v) apart from R's board rule, each party signs one working day after its preconditions are met, at the earliest. Assuming the text is agreed Monday, which is the earliest day all five signatures can complete, and through which chain does the critical path run?

- A) Thursday; the critical path runs through C.
- B) Friday; the critical path runs through E, via A/B then R's Thursday board.
- C) Wednesday; all parties can sign in parallel once the text is agreed.
- D) Friday; the critical path runs through C's 48-hour legal review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A and B sign Tuesday. R's precondition completes Tuesday, but R executes only at its Thursday board, so R signs Thursday. E's precondition completes Thursday, so E signs Friday — zero margin. C receives text Monday, completes 48-hour review Wednesday, signs Thursday. All five complete Friday, with the critical path A/B → R's board → E — option B. This item rewards drawing the dependency graph and walking the calendar, and it tests recognising a calendar constraint (R's Thursday board) that no goodwill can compress — the critical path management skill facilitation under constrained timelines requires.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Evaluating a consolidation pattern across services**

Reviewing a portfolio's underlying services, a candidate notices: four business-facing services (grants, licensing, inspections, appeals) each separately contract for document storage, identity verification, and payment processing — twelve contracts in all, with three different suppliers per capability across the four services. Renewal dates are scattered across the next 30 months. A colleague proposes: "Consolidate everything now — twelve contracts into three shared capabilities." Which considered response best reflects lead-level service thinking?

- A) Agree: twelve contracts into three is obviously simpler, and simplicity is the goal.
- B) Disagree: the services own their contracts, and interference would damage relationships.
- C) Agree with the destination but sequence by the constraint: confirm the capabilities genuinely are common (identity assurance levels and payment types may differ by service), then consolidate each capability opportunistically at its renewal windows over the 30 months — earliest renewals first as pathfinders — rather than breaking contracts now for a big-bang migration.
- D) Commission a full enterprise architecture review before any position is taken.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The pattern of duplicated capabilities is real, but option A's "now" ignores that consolidating mid-contract means exit penalties and forced migration timelines. C sequences by the real constraints: verify commonality first (labels can mask genuine differences), then ride the renewal calendar as consolidation opportunities open, with earliest renewals as pathfinders. Option B converts relationship stewardship into estate paralysis. Option D commissions a heavyweight study where a targeted analysis suffices. This item tests **Service focus**'s bigger-picture duty of getting the best from underlying services: endorse the destination, verify commonality, let the constraint calendar set the route.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Reasoning about a rule a candidate is asked to waive**

An engagement framework requires that any investment proposal reaching the prioritisation forum must carry a business-unit sponsor at deputy-director level or above — a rule a candidate introduced after unsponsored proposals repeatedly collapsed post-funding for lack of business ownership. A well-evidenced proposal from a frontline team now reaches the forum's deadline sponsorless: their deputy director retired last month and the replacement starts in three weeks; the unit's director general "supports the idea in principle" but will not formally sponsor ("that's what deputies are for"). The team asks the candidate to waive the rule this once. Which reasoning best serves the situation?

- A) Waive the rule: the proposal is well-evidenced, and rules must serve outcomes, not block them.
- B) Refuse the waiver: rules applied inconsistently are not rules, and the next unsponsored proposal will cite this one.
- C) Neither waive nor refuse mechanically: the rule's *purpose* is secured business ownership post-funding, so test whether that purpose can be met by other means within the deadline — e.g. the DG formally naming an interim sponsor, or conditional forum entry with sponsorship confirmed before funds release; if no purpose-equivalent exists, hold the rule and give the team a fast track into the next cycle.
- D) Let the forum itself vote on whether to waive the rule, remaining personally neutral.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This item tests reasoning about rules as mechanisms rather than totems. C reasons from the rule's purpose — secured business ownership — and tests whether a purpose-equivalent exists (a DG-named interim sponsor, conditional entry) before deciding, and holds the rule if none exists. Option A ignores that a waiver granted on evidence quality becomes the citation for every future sponsorless proposal. Option B is correct in insight but stops short of the constructive alternative C provides. Option D abdicates the framework's guardianship. This item rewards satisfying a rule's purpose another way, or holding the rule and fixing the hardship through process, never letting quality of advocacy substitute for what the rule protects.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Directing analysis under conflicting executive narratives**

Two executive narratives circulate about an organisation's flagship internal platform. The COO's office says: "Adoption has plateaued at 60% because the remaining 40% of teams are laggards who need mandation." The service owner says: "Adoption has plateaued because the platform lacks the specialist features the remaining teams need — we need investment, not mandation." Both cite the same adoption curve. A candidate is asked to direct a two-week analysis to inform the executive decision between mandation and investment. Which analytical design most directly serves the decision?

- A) Survey all teams on whether they prefer mandation or investment.
- B) Segment the non-adopting 40% by workload type and blocker: sample teams from each segment, establish for each whether the platform *can* serve their work today (blocker = willingness) or *cannot* (blocker = capability), and size the segments — because mandation is the answer only for the first kind and investment only for the second, and the mix is the decision.
- C) Benchmark adoption rates for comparable platforms in other departments to see whether 60% is actually a plateau.
- D) Interview the COO's office and the service owner to reconcile their narratives.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both narratives treat the 40% as homogeneous, and B measures the heterogeneity — segmenting by whether non-adoption is willingness or capability, then sizing the segments, which dissolves the false binary into a sequenced mix. Option A asks the affected population to vote on the diagnosis. Option C answers a different, background question. Option D analyses the narratives instead of the world. This item tests designing an investigation backwards from the decision, making a deadlocked executive question empirically decidable.

### Administration tips

- **Watch for review-mode reading** (Question 3) — finding a conclusion's weakest load-bearing inference in a colleague's work is the lead-level cognitive skill this section samples most directly.
- **Score sequencing items (Question 8) for method** — drawing the dependency graph and identifying the calendar-bound link is the reliable technique.
- **Note whether a candidate reasons from a rule's purpose** (Question 10) rather than mechanically waiving or refusing.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.
- **Use the sufficiency item (Question 5) as debrief material** — asking a candidate to name the cheapest approach that could change their next action often reveals more than the answer alone.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding absolute-column reading** over normalised profiles.
- **Confirming arithmetic while missing inference** — a sum that checks can still carry a claim that doesn't.
- **Treating bands and thresholds impressionistically** rather than placing a value in its band precisely.
- **Over-weighting seniority in prioritisation items.**
- **Rewarding the heavyweight method by default.**
- **Missing joint criticality** in dependency items — finding one critical path is not enough.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures a candidate's ability to interpret numerical information, execute the right calculations accurately, and judge what a computed figure legitimately supports. Calculators are usually allowed; what is tested is the choice and interpretation of calculation.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes around tables, charts, and data-rich passages. Leadership-normed versions concentrate on multi-year cost and benefit structures, weighted and normalised comparisons, compound trends against capacity and budgets, threshold and tolerance regimes, and questions where every option is a correct calculation but only one answers the decision at hand.

For a lead business relationship manager, the relationship with numbers is supervisory and adversarial at once. A candidate's signature converts their team's arithmetic into the function's official claims, so they must catch the cohort-mixed ratio, the unweighted mean, and the cashability double-count before an executive committee does. Suppliers and units bidding for investment present numbers chosen to flatter their case. And the numbers a lead sets become incentives — a target chosen carelessly is gamed into meaninglessness by rational people.

### What it measures for the role

- **Multi-year cost, benefit and phasing structures** map to the duty to ensure spending reviews and services strategy commitments are met.
- **Portfolio-level normalisation and weighting** map to **Service focus**.
- **Threshold, tolerance and regime arithmetic** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** with external partners.
- **Trend and capacity reasoning** map to **Strategic thinking**.
- **Metric design and incentive effects** map to setting conditions that enable a culture of positive impact.
- **Reviewing others' models** maps to directing business analysis.

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio engagement coverage**

A function partners 45 in-scope business units through a team of five BRMs. This quarter, structured engagements were held with 36 units. What percentage coverage is that, and how many additional units would have been needed to reach 90%?

- A) 80% coverage; 5 more units needed
- B) 80% coverage; 4 more units needed
- C) 75% coverage; 6 more units needed
- D) 84% coverage; 3 more units needed

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Coverage: 36 ÷ 45 = 80%. The 90% requirement is 40.5 units, rounding up to 41 (40 gives 88.9%, just short). Additional needed: 41 − 36 = 5. Option B is the conventional-rounding trap. The leadership use of this arithmetic is distribution, not totals — 80% coverage across five BRMs could be an even spread or a skewed one hiding a workload or capability signal, worth checking whenever a team-level rate is reported.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a spending review settlement table**

A directorate's settlement for digital across three years:

| | Year 1 (£m) | Year 2 (£m) | Year 3 (£m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core delivery | 12.0 | 11.4 | 10.8 |
| Transformation | 4.0 | 5.0 | 6.0 |
| Cyber (ring-fenced) | 2.0 | 2.0 | 2.5 |

By what percentage does total annual funding change from Year 1 to Year 3?

- A) Up 7.2%
- B) Up 12.8%
- C) Down 4.5%
- D) Up 4.5%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Year 1 total £18.0m; Year 3 total £19.3m; change ≈ 7.2%. The plausible distractor is reading core delivery alone (down 10%) and concluding the settlement shrinks. The composition is the real communication challenge: total funding grows while core delivery falls and transformation rises sharply, meaning two audiences reading the same table can honestly report opposite headlines. This item tests presenting total and composition together.

**Question 3 (easy) — Escalation load across a team**

Five BRMs handled the following escalation counts this year, against portfolios of different sizes:

| BRM | Escalations | Units in portfolio |
|---|---|---|
| P | 18 | 12 |
| Q | 8 | 4 |
| R | 12 | 10 |
| S | 6 | 6 |
| T | 16 | 13 |

Which BRM's portfolio has the highest escalation rate per unit?

- A) P
- B) Q
- C) T
- D) S

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Per-unit rates: P 1.5, Q 2.0, R 1.2, S 1.0, T ≈1.23. Q's 2.0 is highest, despite P and T carrying larger raw counts. The supervisory reading goes further: a high per-unit rate is a signal, not a verdict, pointing at possibly genuinely harder units, over-escalation, or more honest recording. This item tests normalising first and interpreting against context second.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Compound growth versus a flat budget**

Cloud hosting costs across a portfolio are £1.6m this year and have grown 12% annually, compounding, for three years. The spending review settlement holds hosting budgets flat at £1.6m for the next three years. If growth continues, what cumulative shortfall accrues over the three settlement years?

- A) About £1.25m
- B) About £1.15m
- C) About £0.65m
- D) About £0.58m

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compounding: year 1 shortfall £0.192m, year 2 £0.407m, year 3 £0.648m, cumulative ≈ £1.25m. Option B is the linear projection, understating the gap and widening the understatement each year. This item tests never briefing a compounding pressure from a linear estimate, since the linear number is always the comfortable one, borrowed from years when the money will not be there.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted satisfaction with a composition change**

Last year a portfolio's satisfaction survey: Unit group 1 (heavy users, 200 respondents) scored 3.6; group 2 (light users, 600 respondents) scored 4.2. This year: group 1 (400 respondents) scored 3.8; group 2 (400 respondents) scored 4.2. A team member drafts: "Weighted satisfaction fell from 4.05 to 4.0 — our performance declined." What is the accurate reading?

- A) The draft is right: 4.05 to 4.0 is a decline in performance.
- B) Weighted scores fell, but no group's experience worsened: group 1 improved (3.6→3.8) and group 2 held (4.2) — the fall is composition: heavy users doubled their share of respondents. Performance improved or held everywhere; the mix changed.
- C) The figures must be miscalculated, since no group fell but the average did.
- D) The survey should be discarded because respondent numbers changed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both weighted means check out (4.05 last year, 4.0 this year). Every group's own score improved or held; the mean fell purely because heavy users' share of respondents doubled. Option A narrates a mix change as performance change, precisely the false sentence an executive will remember. This item tests decomposing rate change from mix change before allowing anyone to narrate a moving average.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Service credit arbitration**

An external partner's contract specifies: monthly availability below 99.5% earns a 5% service credit on that month's £40,000 charge; below 99.0%, a 10% credit; below 98.0%, a 15% credit and a formal improvement plan. The quarter's figures: Month 1: 99.6%; Month 2: 98.9%; Month 3: 97.8%. The partner has offered "a goodwill 5% credit on the quarter (£6,000) in full and final settlement". As arbiter of the escalated dispute, what does the contract actually yield, and how does the offer compare?

- A) The contract yields £10,000 plus an improvement plan; the £6,000 offer is £4,000 short and waives the plan.
- B) The contract yields £6,000, so the offer is exactly right.
- C) The contract yields £12,000; the offer is half.
- D) The contract yields £10,000, and the offer is £4,000 short but the improvement plan is negotiable.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Month 1 no credit; Month 2 (98.9%, below 99.0%) 10% = £4,000; Month 3 (97.8%, below 98.0%) 15% = £6,000 plus the plan trigger. Contractual total £10,000 plus the plan, against a £6,000 "full and final" offer that also extinguishes the plan — often more valuable than the credit itself, since it changes the future rather than compensating the past. Option D treats an entitlement as negotiable from the start. This item tests computing what the words already grant before discussing goodwill.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The metric that will be gamed**

A candidate is choosing a headline efficiency metric for their function's new performance framework. Candidates: (a) number of engagements held per quarter; (b) percentage of demand items given a decision within 10 working days; (c) stakeholder-reported value score (annual survey); (d) percentage of funded investments still meeting their benefits profile at 12 months. Which pairing best balances early behaviour signals against outcome truth, and why?

- A) (a) alone — it is the most controllable by the team.
- B) (b) and (d) — a fast leading indicator that is hard to game without genuinely deciding faster, paired with a lagging outcome measure that catches whether fast decisions were also good ones.
- C) (a) and (c) — activity plus perception covers both bases.
- D) (d) alone — outcomes are all that matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Metric (a) is trivially gameable and rewards motion over outcome. Metric (c) is slow and coarse. Metric (b) is a strong leading indicator whose honest improvement route is the desired behaviour, paired with (d) as the lagging truth-teller that catches whether speed compromised soundness. Option D leaves a team steering by a light twelve months behind them. This item tests Goodhart's question — what is the cheapest way to improve this number without improving anything real — as the check to run on any metric before adopting it.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Benefits at portfolio level: the double-count hunt**

Three funded initiatives in a portfolio each claim benefits, and a candidate's team has consolidated them for the executive committee:

| Initiative | Claimed annual benefit | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Digital forms programme | £400k | 20,000 staff-hours saved at £20/hour |
| Contact-centre channel shift | £350k | 25% call reduction × 56,000 calls × £25/call |
| Shared CRM consolidation | £300k | Retiring 3 legacy systems (£180k licences) + 6,000 staff-hours saved at £20/hour |
| **Portfolio total** | **£1,050k** | |

Investigation shows the digital forms programme's hour savings include 8,000 hours from the same processes the CRM consolidation also claims its 6,000 hours from, and the channel-shift call reduction assumes the digital forms are live. Which statement best assesses the portfolio claim?

- A) The total is sound: each initiative's arithmetic checks out individually.
- B) The total overstates: up to £120k of staff-hour savings may be claimed twice across forms and CRM (the overlapping processes cannot yield both claims in full), and the channel-shift benefit is *dependent* on forms delivery, so £350k of the total carries forms-programme risk — the honest portfolio range is roughly £930k–£1,050k with a stated dependency.
- C) The total understates, since benefits usually compound.
- D) The CRM claim should be deleted entirely because it overlaps with forms.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The double-count's maximum is the smaller overlapping figure, £120k, giving a floor of £930k. The channel-shift benefit is correlated with, not additive to, forms delivery — a dependency the committee must know before treating the total as bankable. Option A audits each row and never the joins. Option D over-corrects, deleting far more than the overlap. This item tests that portfolio benefits consolidate by deduplication, not addition, and that dependencies between benefit lines are risk information an honest pack states.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Capacity arithmetic for a team a candidate leads**

A candidate's five BRMs each have roughly 1,600 productive hours per year. Portfolio demand next year: 45 units requiring on average 100 hours/year of core partnering; the spending review adds a fixed 900 hours of work; two strategic programmes need a combined 1,650 hours of dedicated partnering; and experience shows 15% of total capacity must be held back for unplanned escalations. Can next year's demand be met, and what is the approximate surplus or shortfall?

- A) Shortfall of about 250 hours — roughly 0.16 of a full-time BRM.
- B) Surplus of about 950 hours.
- C) Shortfall of about 1,450 hours.
- D) Exact balance: demand equals capacity.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Capacity: 8,000 hours minus a 15% escalation reserve = 6,800 plannable hours. Demand: 4,500 + 900 + 1,650 = 7,050. Shortfall ≈ 250 hours. Option B forgets the reserve, the most common capacity-planning error, producing teams that fit on paper and drown in practice. This item tests never omitting the escalation reserve, and presenting the result as a range across the model's two soft assumptions (the reserve percentage and productive hours per person) rather than a false point estimate.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Trend, seasonality and an external partner's claim**

An external delivery partner, seeking contract expansion, presents: "Demand for our managed service has grown 35% in six months (March: 2,000 transactions; September: 2,700). At this rate, volumes will double within a year — expand capacity now or face failure." A candidate's own records show last year's monthlies: March 2,100 → June 1,750 → September 2,650 → December 2,050. What is the strongest assessment?

- A) The partner is right: 35% in six months is a compounding trend that must be met with expanded capacity.
- B) Last year shows the same March-to-September rise (2,100 → 2,650, +26%) followed by a December fall — the "growth" is substantially seasonal pattern presented as trend; the like-for-like signal is September-on-September (2,650 → 2,700, +1.9%), which supports roughly flat demand, not doubling.
- C) Demand is actually declining, since March this year (2,000) is below March last year (2,100).
- D) The two data sets conflict, so no assessment is possible without a third year.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The partner's arithmetic is correct; the inference is flawed, since last year's records show an identical seasonal shape. Year-on-year comparisons show essentially flat demand. Option C makes the mirror-image error from a single comparison. Option D overstates the ambiguity — two years of monthly shape is enough to identify seasonality this pronounced. This item tests finding the comparison the data structure actually permits when a counterparty presents an arithmetically true, inferentially false claim.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Choosing what the number supports: investment arbitration**

Two directors escalate a deadlock for a candidate's arbitration. £500k of discretionary investment remains. Director A's bid: automate document processing — model shows £150k/year savings from year 1, high confidence. Director B's bid: modernise the field-worker mobile platform — model shows £80k/year efficiency plus "unquantified but substantial" user experience and retention benefits for 900 field staff whose attrition (18%, versus 11% organisation-wide) exit interviews link to "unworkable tools". Both bids are competently costed at £500k. Which analysis most defensibly supports an arbitration?

- A) Fund A: £150k/year beats £80k/year, and unquantified benefits are inadmissible.
- B) Fund B: user experience always outweighs cash savings.
- C) Test whether B's unquantified benefit can be bounded before deciding: each point of excess attrition among 900 staff is 9 leavers/year, and at a conservative £15k full cost per replacement, closing even half the 7-point attrition gap is worth roughly £470k/year — an order-of-magnitude that, if the tools-attrition link survives scrutiny, makes B's true return several times A's; commission that scrutiny within a fortnight and arbitrate on the bounded number.
- D) Split the funding £250k each, since both cases have merit.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Comparing £150k with £80k directly awards the decision to whichever director quantified more. C bounds the unquantified using data already in the stem, arriving at a rough but conservative figure that dominates both quantified lines, and commissions rapid scrutiny before funding on it. Option B asserts a value instead of computing one. Option D funds neither bid fully, since both are costed at £500k. This item tests making an unquantified good estimable — bounding it conservatively — so a decision is argued in one currency, which is how user experience stops losing arbitrations it deserved to win.

### Administration tips

- **Watch for decomposition in Question 5** — splitting rate change from mix change before narrating a moving average is a strong lead-level signal.
- **Score the double-count item (Question 8) for whether a candidate reviews the joins**, not just each row.
- **Note whether a candidate presents a capacity or benefits model as a range** rather than a false point estimate (Questions 9 and 8).
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.
- **Use the explanations to debrief on real arbitration and consolidation practice.**

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Linear projection of compounding costs.**
- **Narrating a mix change as a performance change.**
- **Accepting a counterparty's aggregation or comparison frame** without recomputing at the regime's own period.
- **Adding portfolio benefits without an overlap review.**
- **Presenting point estimates from assumption-sensitive models.**
- **Letting quantification asymmetry decide contests** — rewarding the better-quantified bid regardless of the true underlying value.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely a candidate extracts, evaluates and deploys meaning from written material. Passages of 100–250 words — policy extracts, contractual clauses, ministerial correspondence, research summaries, escalation chains — are followed by True/False/Cannot-say judgements, inference questions, argument evaluations, and clear-writing choices. The governing discipline is strict: answer from the passage alone.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with several passages and two to four questions each, against a senior norm group. Leadership-normed versions distinguish themselves through the stakes written into the passages and through wrong options one modal verb, one scope term, or one substituted concept away from right.

Why does this matter for the role? A lead's audiences include executive committees, external partners, and occasionally the centre of government, and the role sets the drafting standard a team learns from. Misread tolerances move money; over-promised commitments become grievances; ambiguous policies authored by the candidate become a year of interpretive disputes that escalate back to them.

### What it measures for the role

- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to directing business analysis: the boundary between what evidence establishes and what it merely suggests.
- **Reading commitments, conditions and delegations precisely** maps to the duty to ensure spending reviews and services strategy commitments are met and appropriately communicated.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to influencing senior stakeholders and arbitrating escalated blockers.
- **Reading external and contractual text** maps to leading engagement with internal and external stakeholders.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to communicating complex needs to relevant audiences and to culture-setting.
- **Inference calibration** maps to **Strategic thinking** and long-term relationship stewardship.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot say on a services strategy clause**

Passage: "The services strategy commits each directorate to publishing a service performance dashboard by December. Dashboards must include availability, user satisfaction and cost per transaction. Directorates unable to compute cost per transaction may publish with the other two measures, provided a plan to add cost data within six months is included. The digital function will provide a dashboard template, though its use is optional."

Statement: "A directorate that publishes a December dashboard showing only availability and user satisfaction, with no cost data and no plan to add it, has met the strategy's commitment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The concession to publish with two measures is conditional on including a plan to add cost data; the statement's directorate has no plan, failing the concession's proviso — contradicted, False. The classic mistake is reading the concession as unconditional and stopping before the proviso. Note also the trap-in-waiting: the template is optional, the dashboard is not — attaching the right qualifier to the right noun is the whole game.

**Question 2 (easy) — False versus Cannot say on an escalation email**

Passage (from a unit head, escalating): "I am escalating the CRM data quality issue to you directly. We first raised this with your team in March. Since then we have had two workshops and a promised fix date of August, which has now passed. I do not doubt your team's effort or goodwill, but the issue remains unresolved, and my director is asking me weekly for a date I cannot give."

Statement: "The unit head believes the lead BRM's team has not made an effort on the issue."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states the opposite explicitly — "I do not doubt your team's effort or goodwill" — contradicting the statement. Escalations are routinely mis-summarised in this direction, because the fact of escalation feels like an accusation; this email is close to a model escalation, and reading it accurately reveals what resolution actually requires: a date the unit head can give their own director.

**Question 3 (easy) — Main point of a partner communication**

Passage (from an external delivery partner's quarterly letter): "We remain committed to the partnership and to the joint roadmap agreed in January. We should flag that the exchange-rate movements of recent months have materially increased our licence input costs, which under clause 14 we absorb until the annual review. We are not seeking any in-year change. We would, however, welcome early discussion of the cost model ahead of the annual review, and we attach an options paper for that conversation."

Which sentence best states the letter's main point?

- A) The partner is invoking clause 14 to raise prices immediately.
- B) The partner is signalling that it will seek cost model changes at the annual review, and is asking to begin that negotiation early — while explicitly honouring current terms in the meantime.
- C) The partner is threatening to exit the partnership over exchange rates.
- D) The letter is a routine reaffirmation of commitment with no operative content.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The letter's structure is reaffirm, flag a pressure while showing it is currently the partner's problem, disclaim any in-year demand, then request early discussion with an attached options paper — the opening of a negotiation, courteously conducted. Option A reverses clause 14's effect. Option D mistakes courtesy for emptiness. This item tests reading correspondence for its operative structure, not its tone.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say on a delegation letter**

Passage (from the director general's delegation letter): "The lead business relationship manager may approve engagement commitments up to £25,000, and may sub-delegate approval up to £5,000 to named business relationship managers. Sub-delegations must be recorded in the delegations register. Commitments involving external partners require director approval regardless of value. This delegation is personal to the postholder and lapses on a change of postholder until renewed."

Statement: "A named BRM with a recorded sub-delegation may approve a £4,000 engagement commitment involving an external partner."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The sub-delegation route permits up to £5,000, but the external-partner rule overrides "regardless of value" across every band, including the lead's own — and authority not held cannot be sub-delegated. "Regardless of value" and similar override language is where delegation instruments concentrate their meaning, and misreading it permissively produces a junior colleague acting in good faith on misdescribed authority. The final sentence rewards equal care: the delegation is personal and lapses on a change of postholder, a transition detail an outgoing lead must flag.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from a ministerial commitment**

Passage (extract from a minister's written answer to Parliament): "The department is committed to ensuring that no citizen is left behind by the transition to digital services. Assisted digital support will be available for every major service by next April. We expect the great majority of transactions to be digital by 2028, and the department keeps the balance of provision under continuous review."

Which inference is best supported?

- A) The department has committed to keeping non-digital channels open until 2028.
- B) By next April, assisted digital support is committed for every major service; beyond that, the passage commits to review, not to any particular channel provision.
- C) After 2028, non-digital channels will close.
- D) The commitment to assisted digital support extends to every service the department runs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The hard commitment is dated, scoped and checkable — assisted digital for every major service by April. Everything else is aspiration or forecast. Option D drops the scope word "major". Options A and C convert a forecast's shadow into content it never had. This item tests transmitting exactly a commitment's scope — the aspiration as aspiration, the forecast as forecast, the commitment as the only thing called one.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Argument evaluation in an escalated dispute**

Passage (from a service owner, disputing a unit's escalation): "The unit claims our release process is 'blocking their business change'. In fact, our process approved 94% of their release requests this year. The three rejections all failed security checks — the same checks every unit's releases face. The unit's real problem is that it submits requests late and understaffed for remediation: its average remediation time after a failed check is 6 weeks, against a departmental average of 2. Our process is not the blocker; their resourcing is."

Which is the strongest evaluation of the service owner's argument?

- A) The argument is conclusive: 94% approval proves the process is not a blocker.
- B) The argument has real force — high approval rate, uniform checks, slow unit remediation — but its conclusion overreaches: approval *rate* says nothing about approval *speed*, and if the unit's complaint is about elapsed time in the process rather than rejection frequency, the cited evidence does not touch it. The arbitration needs cycle-time data before either account is accepted.
- C) The argument fails because it is self-interested; the service owner would say this.
- D) The argument fails because 94% means 6% of releases were blocked, which confirms the unit's claim.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence genuinely damages one version of the unit's claim but says nothing about elapsed time, the measure a "blocking" complaint about business change most plausibly turns on. B names both the force and the gap and specifies the missing evidence. Option A accepts a rate as proof about a duration. Option C is the genetic fallacy. This item tests defining a dispute's actual question precisely enough that the deciding evidence can be identified and demanded.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say with survey evidence**

Passage (from a candidate's team's annual report draft): "The stakeholder survey achieved 210 responses from a distribution of 300 (70%). Of respondents, 78% agreed that 'the BRM function understands our business priorities', up from 71% last year. Respondents from units with a dedicated BRM agreed at 85%; respondents from units sharing a BRM agreed at 62%. Verbatim comments in shared-BRM units most frequently referenced 'not knowing who to contact'."

Statement: "Giving every unit a dedicated BRM would raise agreement in the currently shared units to about 85%."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes an association and a suggestive mechanism, but the statement makes a causal prediction with a magnitude that three unstated inferential steps separate from the data — allocation is not random, the mechanism is unquantified, and a true causal effect need not equal the observed gap. Cannot say. This item also tests a drafting hazard: placing the split beside the verbatims invites readers to draw the statement's conclusion without it ever being asserted, and a lead signs off neither knowingly nor negligently.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement: the escalation response**

A director has escalated to a candidate in writing, copying the candidate's director, claiming their function "sat on" her unit's investment proposal for two months. Records show: her team submitted on 3 June missing the mandatory benefits section; the candidate's BRM requested it on 5 June and twice since; the completed submission arrived 28 July; the next available forum is 14 August. Which reply is best?

- A) "Thank you for your email. There are always two sides to these situations, and I think it would be unhelpful to get into the details of who did what when. Let's put this behind us and focus on the future."
- B) "Your team submitted an incomplete proposal on 3 June and ignored three requests for the benefits section until 28 July. The delay is entirely of your unit's making, as the attached email trail proves. I would appreciate a correction to your characterisation, given who was copied."
- C) "Thank you for raising this — the timeline matters, so here it is: initial submission 3 June without the mandatory benefits section; our requests of 5 June, 19 June and 10 July; completed submission 28 July; booked for the next forum, 14 August. Two things from us: we should have escalated to you sooner when the section didn't arrive — we'll do that in future. And to keep momentum, my team can pre-review the completed pack this week so 14 August is a decision, not a discussion. Happy to talk today if useful."
- D) "I have asked my team for a full report on the handling of your proposal and will respond substantively once I have it, which I expect within ten working days."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C corrects a false record with dated facts alone (no adversarial adjective needed), owns one thing the function could have done better, offers forward value, and offers voice contact. Option A abandons the record while copied to a director. Option B wins the argument and loses the relationship. Option D converts a two-minute records check into ten days, leaving the false claim standing. This item tests the four-part template — facts dated, one thing owned, value forward, voice offered.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Reading a joint commitment with a buried asymmetry**

Passage (from a draft memorandum of understanding with another department): "Both departments commit to sharing service performance data quarterly. Department X will provide its data in the agreed common format. Department Y will provide its data in the agreed common format where its systems permit, and otherwise in its native format with a mapping document. Either department may pause sharing for security reasons, giving notice where practicable. This memorandum does not create legally binding obligations."

A candidate leads engagement for Department X. Which reading should most inform their advice on signing?

- A) The MoU is balanced: both departments commit to quarterly sharing in the common format.
- B) The MoU is asymmetric in two places — Y's format obligation is qualified by "where its systems permit" while X's is absolute, and the security pause requires notice only "where practicable" — and the whole instrument is non-binding; advice should weigh whether X is content to be firmly committed in practice while Y retains discretion at both the format and continuity level.
- C) The MoU is unacceptable and should be rejected because it is not legally binding.
- D) The asymmetries are irrelevant because MoUs are never enforced anyway.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** X's format obligation is absolute; Y's is self-qualified with a trigger Y itself controls. B assembles the asymmetries into advice — X is reputationally committed while Y retains graceful exits at two levels, which may still be worth signing, but with eyes open. Option A is the single-pass reading the drafting invites. Option C mistakes non-binding status, standard for such instruments, for a defect. This item tests building a two-column table of who-must-do-what and reading every qualifier for who controls its trigger.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Synthesis across three sources in an escalated conflict**

Source 1 (a candidate's BRM's escalation note): "Unit F's head refuses to attend the shared-platform onboarding sessions and told me the platform 'will never work for casework'. I've tried three times. Recommend we escalate to his director."
Source 2 (email from Unit F's head to the candidate, same week): "Your colleague is enthusiastic, but each session she has offered clashes with our statutory reporting deadlines — the 1st and 15th of each month are impossible for my team, as I've said each time. On the platform: our casework requires field-level audit trails, and nobody has yet shown me the platform has them. Show me that, and we'll engage."
Source 3 (platform team's feature list): "Field-level audit logging: available, enabled on request per unit; not included in standard onboarding demo."

Which synthesis best serves the next action?

- A) Source 1 is right: three refusals constitute obstruction, and director-level escalation is warranted.
- B) Source 2 reframes everything: the "refusals" have a stated scheduling cause and the "never work" position has a stated technical condition, which Source 3 shows the platform meets but does not demonstrate by default. The blocker is two information gaps, not a resistant stakeholder: schedule around the 1st/15th, and have the platform team demo audit logging to Unit F. Coach the BRM on surfacing conditions behind refusals before recommending escalation.
- C) The sources conflict irreconcilably; convene all parties to establish whose account is true.
- D) Source 3 settles it: the feature exists, so Unit F's objection was baseless and the escalation should note this.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The three accounts interlock rather than conflict — the BRM's note omits the causes the unit head supplies, and the feature list resolves the technical condition while explaining why three onboardings failed to surface it. B converts the synthesis into two cheap actions. Option A escalates a scheduling clash and an unanswered question. Option D weaponises evidence when the actual objection was "nobody has shown me". This item also rewards the coaching move: recording outcomes without conditions is what turns refusals into requirements when surfaced.

**Question 11 (challenging) — What a candidate's own drafting commits them to**

A candidate is finalising their function's commitment in the directorate's published engagement charter. Legal and practical review will hold them to whatever ships. Which drafting is best?

- A) "The BRM function will resolve all stakeholder issues within 10 working days."
- B) "The BRM function will always be available to support any business need, whatever it takes."
- C) "The BRM function will acknowledge stakeholder issues within 2 working days, provide a named owner and a first substantive response within 10, and where resolution requires other teams, will coordinate actively and keep the stakeholder informed at least fortnightly until resolution."
- D) "The BRM function will endeavour, where reasonably practicable and subject to competing priorities, to respond to stakeholder issues in a timely manner appropriate to the circumstances."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C commits only what the function controls — acknowledgement, named ownership, a first response, and communication cadence when others hold the fix — each checkable and honest about dependency. Option A promises resolution that depends on other teams. Option B is unbounded liability. Option D commits to nothing measurable and damages trust at publication. This item tests drafting that is ambitious about what a function owns and explicit about what it does not.

### Administration tips

- **Watch for whether a candidate separates what a passage states, signals, and omits** (Question 11-adjacent reasoning throughout) — conflating the three is a common error at this level.
- **Note the scope-word discipline in Questions 1, 5 and 4** — tracking "regardless of value", "where practicable", "major" precisely is the reading habit this role depends on.
- **Score the synthesis item (Question 10) for whether a candidate hunts for interlocking causes** before escalating.
- **Use the clear-writing item (Question 8) as a live discussion point.**
- **Time by passage, not by statement.**

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Partial-clause reading** — concessions with provisos, delegations with overrides, commitments with scope words.
- **Ratifying a category error** — an approval rate offered as proof about speed, or similar mismatches.
- **Importing hostility or reassurance the text doesn't carry.**
- **Converting signals into commitments in either direction.**
- **Rewarding the option that "wins the record" over one that corrects it while preserving the relationship.**
- **Under-weighting whether a candidate would want their own drafting quoted back at them.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to judge the effectiveness of possible responses — most effective, most and least effective, rankings, or independent ratings. There is rarely a flawless answer; differentiation comes from consistently preferring responses that are active, honest, developmental and systemic over those that are passive, political, self-protective or merely fast.

The typical format is 15 to 25 scenarios in 30 to 45 minutes, often untimed. Leadership-normed SJTs are distinguished by second-order dilemmas — not "what do I do?" but "what do I do, given that my team, peers, seniors and external partners will all learn from what I do?" — legitimate goods in genuine tension, and the strongest options usually operating at two levels at once, resolving the instance while adjusting the system, the person, or the precedent.

For a lead business relationship manager, the SJT is effectively a simulation of the role's hardest hours. The role's duties — point of escalation, arbitration, facilitation under constrained timelines, culture-setting, enabling others — cannot be performed by procedure; they are performed in moments of choice under ambiguity, exactly what an SJT samples.

### What it measures for the role

- **Escalation and arbitration scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** at this level.
- **Team development scenarios** map to the duty to enable others to improve relationship maturity and to leading a team of BRMs.
- **Culture-setting scenarios** map to setting conditions that enable a culture of positive impact.
- **Strategic commitment scenarios** map to ensuring spending reviews and services strategy commitments are met and appropriately communicated.
- **External partner scenarios** map to leading engagement with external stakeholders.
- **Ownership scenarios** map to **Ownership and topic**, at a scale where owning usually means building the ownership structure rather than doing the work.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded. For each scenario, ask the candidate to choose the option indicated.

**Question 1 (easy) — The escalation a BRM should have kept**

A BRM on a candidate's team forwards them an email chain with the note: "Unit K's head is refusing to prioritise the data cleansing work — can you take this one?" Reading the chain, the candidate finds a routine disagreement about sequencing, politely expressed, that the BRM has not yet discussed with the unit head by phone or in person — only email. What is the most effective response?

- A) Take the escalation; unit heads respond faster to leads, and the work matters.
- B) Return it with coaching: talk through with the BRM what an actual conversation (not email) might surface about the unit head's constraints, help them plan it, and make clear the candidate is available if a real impasse remains after that conversation.
- C) Decline the escalation and tell the BRM that escalations require a completed escalation form documenting attempts at resolution.
- D) Email the unit head personally, copying the BRM, asking him to engage with the BRM's sequencing proposal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Taking a mild, email-only disagreement (option A) teaches a team that difficulty is transferable and hollows out BRMs' standing in their own portfolios. B is developmental: the coaching question upgrades the next hundred disagreements, planning help makes success likely, and an explicit backstop keeps stretch feeling safe. Option C reaches the outcome through the wrong instrument — process as a wall rather than conversation as craft. Option D is escalation-by-stealth. This item tests asking, of any escalation, whose growth resolving it belongs to.

**Question 2 (easy) — The culture moment in the team meeting**

In a candidate's team meeting, a BRM describes "winning" a dispute with a difficult unit: she quietly gathered examples of the unit's process failures over a month, then presented the dossier at a governance board, where the unit head was "finally shown up". Two junior team members look admiring. The dispute's substantive outcome was correct — the unit's process genuinely needed the change. What is the most effective response?

- A) Congratulate her on the outcome; the method is secondary to being right.
- B) Say nothing in the meeting but raise the method privately with her later.
- C) Acknowledge the outcome genuinely, then open up the method in the meeting: ask what the unit head's working relationship with us looks like now, contrast the dossier-ambush with early sharing of concerns, and set the norm explicitly — we surface problems with partners before we surface them at boards; then reinforce it privately with her.
- D) Publicly rebuke the approach so the two admiring juniors are left in no doubt.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Success is currently laundering an ambush technique in front of two impressionable juniors. C acknowledges the correct diagnosis, lets the team reason to the relationship cost, converts the moment into standing culture, and follows up privately. Option A endorses the method by silence in front of admirers. Option B protects the individual but abandons the audience, who leave with the lesson intact. Option D buys the norm at the price of the person, teaching the team that meetings are dangerous. This item tests unpacking a bad method with the audience that witnessed the good outcome while the example is warm.

**Question 3 (easy) — The commitment slipping quietly**

A directorate committed in the services strategy to onboarding all 20 major services to the new support model by December. It is September; 11 are onboarded; the onboarding team privately tells a candidate the realistic year-end number is 15–16, but their programme board reporting still shows "Green — on track for 20", because "the gap might still close". The candidate's role includes ensuring services strategy commitments are met and appropriately communicated. What is the most effective response?

- A) Leave the reporting to the onboarding team; the commitment is theirs to manage and December has not arrived.
- B) Press the team to work faster, since the commitment must be met as stated.
- C) Get the honest trajectory onto the table now: work with the onboarding lead to re-baseline the report (15–16 by December, 20 by a stated date, with causes), brief the affected service owners and the strategy's executive sponsor before the next board, and turn the remaining gap into a resourced recovery plan rather than a December surprise.
- D) Report the discrepancy to the programme board personally, so the record is corrected by someone outside the team.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A false Green is how December surprises are manufactured. C re-baselines the report with the team that owns delivery, briefs stakeholders early with causes and a plan, and converts the gap into a resourcing decision the executive can take. Option A outsources named accountability. Option B mistakes pressure for management. Option D corrects around the team rather than with it, a relationship-breaking move to hold in reserve, not use first. This item tests choosing honest early communication over a good-looking report followed by a crisis.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the poached commitment**

At a cross-directorate board, a peer director announces that "the BRM function has agreed" his directorate's new system will be prioritised in next quarter's delivery slate. No such agreement exists: one of a candidate's BRMs had an exploratory conversation with his team, which he has now converted into a public commitment. Contradicting him flatly at the board will embarrass him; staying silent creates a phantom commitment the function cannot honour and displaces genuinely prioritised work. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Stay silent at the board, then have the record corrected by emailing the secretariat a clarification afterwards.
2. In the meeting, without heat: "Let me add context — our teams have had a useful early conversation, and I want to be accurate about where it stands: exploration, not yet agreement. The prioritisation route is [the forum]; I'll make sure this proposal gets a fast, fair passage there, and [director] and I can align this week on what it needs."
3. Contradict directly: "That is not correct — no agreement exists, and this is not how prioritisation works."
4. Let the claim stand and work afterwards to make it true, reshuffling next quarter's slate to accommodate the system.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A phantom commitment must die in the room where it was born. Response 2 kills the claim with precision framed as accuracy rather than accusation, names the real process, and courts rather than corners the director. Response 4 is least effective — it overturns evidence-based prioritisation on whoever claims most boldly and never resolves what the BRM's "exploratory conversation" became. Response 1 corrects the record too late to prevent the impression hardening; response 3 achieves accuracy at maximum relational cost. Order: 2, 3, 1, 4. This item tests correcting public inaccuracies publicly, gently, and with a path forward attached.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The two seniors and the constrained timeline**

A candidate is facilitating a high-risk discussion: the finance director and the operations director must jointly agree, by Thursday's submission deadline, which of two incompatible options goes into the spending review — Option 1 (finance's preference: consolidate regional processing into two centres, larger savings, significant staff impact) or Option 2 (operations' preference: modernise in place, smaller savings, minimal disruption). It is Tuesday. Both directors have entrenched publicly; each has separately told the candidate the other "doesn't understand the real constraints". A third session together seems likely to re-run the deadlock. What is the most effective facilitation move?

- A) Run the third joint session anyway, but open it by presenting a personal recommendation between the two options, to give the discussion a concrete anchor.
- B) Meet each director separately today with one task each: not "argue your option" but "state the conditions under which you could live with the other's" — then build Wednesday's joint session around the two conditions lists, which usually reveal a composite or sequenced option (e.g. modernise-in-place now with a costed consolidation trigger at defined volume thresholds) that neither pure option allowed.
- C) Escalate on Tuesday to the director general who owns both directors, presenting both options and asking for a decision by Wednesday.
- D) Draft the submission with both options included and a note that agreement could not be reached, protecting the deadline.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A third positional re-run repeats what has already failed twice, and A's own recommendation spends the facilitator's neutrality. B changes the question — from arguing positions to specifying conditions — which unlocks entrenched negotiation by revealing what each side's real requirement actually is, and conditions lists compose in ways positions cannot. Option C is premature surrender of the function's core value on Tuesday. Option D transfers the decision to whoever reads the spreadsheet. This item tests stopping the collection of arguments and starting the collection of conditions when positions deadlock.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the partner's overreach on a candidate's data**

An external delivery partner's account director sends a candidate their draft "Partnership Success Report", which they intend to publish on their website and circulate to other government departments. It uses the organisation's satisfaction data (shared for joint service improvement), describes results with claims like "transformed the department's operations", and includes a supportive quote attributed to the candidate's director — who, on checking, was never asked. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Reply formally that the data was shared for improvement purposes only, that publication is not authorised, that the attributed quote must be removed, and that the partnership's data-sharing terms are now under review.
2. Call the account director: state plainly that the draft cannot proceed — unauthorised data use and an unapproved quote are serious — and that an explanation of how it happened is wanted; then offer the legitimate path: a jointly drafted case study, approved through both organisations' processes, with claims the evidence supports; and log the incident with the commercial team so the pattern is visible if it repeats.
3. Approve the report with light edits; partner marketing that praises the work reflects well on the directorate too, and the relationship benefits from generosity.
4. Say nothing to the partner, but warn the director about the fabricated quote so they are not blindsided.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 4
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 holds the boundary at full strength while converting a violation into a governed opportunity — a phone call permits an honest explanation, a diagnosis question reveals whether this is process or strategy, and the legitimate path redirects the partner's real interest. Response 3 is least effective, normalising unauthorised data use and a fabricated quote. Response 1 gets the substance right but leads with maximum formality before any conversation; response 4 is self-protection wearing diligence's clothes while the harm continues. Order: 2, 1, 4, 3. This item tests holding boundaries at full strength, in person first, with a legitimate alternative ready to offer.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The team member ready before the team believes it**

A candidate plans to give the lead role on next quarter's highest-profile engagement — the spending review coordination with the finance directorate — to a BRM who joined from another department eight months ago. She is, in the candidate's judgement, the strongest strategic thinker on the team. Two longer-serving BRMs have separately signalled they expected the role; one has muttered about "favouritism towards new people", and the candidate is aware the team is watching the decision as a signal about how advancement works. What is the most effective way to proceed?

- A) Give the role to the most senior long-serving BRM to protect team cohesion, and give the newer BRM a comparable role next cycle when the optics are easier.
- B) Split the role between the newer BRM and the most aggrieved long-serving one, so both are satisfied.
- C) Make the decision on capability and stand by it: give her the role, tell the two long-serving BRMs directly and separately why — including what specifically would position each of them for the next comparable role — and tell the team the criteria used, so the decision teaches how advancement here actually works.
- D) Ask the team to discuss and agree among themselves who should take the role, so the outcome carries collective legitimacy.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A watched decision is a published rule about how roles are earned. C publishes the right rule and pays the full communication cost that makes it land as merit: direct reasons, specific development paths for the aggrieved, and transparent criteria for the team, which is what actually protects cohesion. Option A publishes tenure-over-capability and teaches that grievance signalling works. Option B decorates avoidance as compromise and confuses the finance directorate's single point of contact. Option D abdicates a leader's least delegable duty. This item tests making people decisions on capability, then spending generously on the losers' development.

**Question 8 (challenging) — The inherited commitment that cannot be met**

Two weeks into leading a newly merged BRM function, a candidate discovers a written commitment made by their predecessor to a major delivery unit: dedicated BRM support "at least three days per week" for their transformation programme, for eighteen months. Resourcing has since changed; honouring it would consume a quarter of the merged team's capacity and force the candidate to strip partnering from six smaller units — several already fragile relationships. The delivery unit's director has planned around the commitment, mentions it warmly, and is politically influential. The commitment was real, written, and improvident. What is the most effective response?

- A) Honour the commitment in full: the function's word is its currency, and the reputational cost of retreat exceeds the capacity cost of delivery.
- B) Quietly under-deliver: assign the three days nominally but let the actual support settle toward what capacity allows, relying on delivery-phase busyness to blur the difference.
- C) Go to the director early with the full picture: the commitment was made and responsibility for it is taken as the function's commitment; the resourcing that made it possible has changed, and honouring it as written would break partnering for six other units; then propose a renegotiated package built on what the programme actually needs now — perhaps two dedicated days plus senior escalation access and named cover for peaks — and offer transition arrangements while the new package beds in.
- D) Write to the director explaining that commitments made by the predecessor under previous resourcing assumptions cannot bind the merged function, and set out the support level the new structure can offer.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option A optimises the visible obligation at the expense of six invisible ones. Option B makes the function's word actually worthless while adding deception. Option D is legalism that the influential director will remember as repudiation. C takes responsibility as the function's own, shows the constraint in full with real numbers, and proposes a package built on the programme's current needs rather than the old promise's shape — offered early, which is partnership rather than damage control. This item tests renegotiating inherited improvident commitments openly, at the level of the need behind the promise, never honouring into portfolio failure or eroding into distrust.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the survey a candidate is asked to soften**

A candidate's function's annual stakeholder survey — which is published to all participating units as a matter of established practice — this year contains a bruising result: units rate "the digital directorate acts on feedback" at 2.4/5, with verbatims naming two live programmes. The candidate's director, whose directorate this is, asks them to "hold the detailed results back this year — just publish the headline scores — while we get the programme issues sorted; publishing the detail now would damage confidence right when the programmes need support." Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Publish everything as usual without further discussion; established practice is the shield against any accusation of manipulation.
2. Agree to publish headlines only, and plan to restore full publication next year once scores recover.
3. Take the concern seriously and propose the version that serves it honestly: publish the full results on schedule *with* a response — each bruising finding paired with the action being taken, dated — and offer the director pre-briefings with the units named in the verbatims; make the case that suppressing detail units know they submitted is discovered quickly and converts a performance problem into a trust problem, whereas publishing-with-response converts it into credibility.
4. Tell the director that publication will proceed in full regardless of their view, since survey integrity is not negotiable.

- A) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 2
- B) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 2
- C) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The director's concern is legitimate; the proposed instrument — suppression — is the error, since units who submitted the feedback will detect its absence and the discovered suppression confirms the score's own accusation. Response 3 serves the concern honestly: full publication with dated actions, the only message that genuinely raises confidence, plus prepared briefings. Response 2 is least effective, since a plan to restore publication "once scores recover" corrupts the instrument permanently into advertising. Response 4 is right on principle but spends the relationship before attempting persuasion. Response 1 wins on integrity but ignores the director's concern entirely. Order: 3, 4, 1, 2. This item tests serving a legitimate concern with an honest instrument and making suppression's discovery-cost visible to whoever requests it.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The blocker who is right too late**

A cross-organisational data platform a candidate's function has championed reaches its final approval gate. Every unit has signed off except one: the head of the analysis unit — a rigorous, unpopular figure whose objections have twice delayed the programme — now raises, at the gate itself, a concern about the platform's approach to record-level provenance that would cost eight weeks to address. The programme board's mood is "overrule and proceed"; two directors describe the objection as "obstruction as usual". Reading the technical detail overnight, the candidate concludes the concern is substantially valid — provenance as designed would genuinely limit several promised analytical uses. The programme has momentum, a communicated go-live date, and eighteen months of the candidate's function's sponsorship behind it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Support proceeding: the objection arrived at the last responsible moment's far side, the process gave the analysis head earlier opportunities he did not use, and eight weeks of delay damages the platform's credibility more than a provenance limitation the roadmap can fix later.
- B) Support the objection on its merits and say so before the board: the concern is valid, the eight weeks buys capability the platform promised, proceeding knowingly limited converts a delay into a broken promise; pair this with naming the process failure — a concern this fundamental surfacing at the gate means earlier reviews failed to draw it out — and commission that fix alongside; then work the room before the vote, because the board must not learn the merits from the meeting's most unpopular voice alone.
- C) Propose proceeding to go-live as scheduled while a parallel workstream addresses provenance, satisfying both the date and the concern.
- D) Abstain from the gate decision, noting the function's role is engagement rather than technical adjudication, and facilitate the board toward its own conclusion.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The load-bearing fact is that the candidate has concluded the objection is valid, and everything else is pressure to weigh a true finding by its social packaging. Option A spends credibility on concealment. Option C is the trap the question is built around — provenance is foundational and cannot be retrofitted in a workstream running alongside live service. Option D is abdication dressed as neutrality when the function is not neutral, having sponsored the platform for eighteen months. B calls the merits publicly, prices the delay honestly, names and fixes the process failure, and works the room before the vote, since a correct argument first heard from an unpopular messenger tends to lose. This item tests judging arguments separately from their messengers and treating "right but late" as two problems to solve separately.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The quiet signal about a candidate's own function**

At a leadership away-day, a director general trusted by a candidate mentions, almost in passing: "Between us — the efficiency review is going to look hard at whether the BRM function justifies its cost. Nothing's decided. I'd get your evidence in order." The candidate leads that function. Their team does not know; morale is good; the annual stakeholder survey is about to launch; and two of the candidate's BRMs are finalists for roles in other departments. What is the most effective response over the following fortnight?

- A) Tell the team at the next team meeting exactly what was said, so they hear it directly rather than through rumour, and can contribute to the evidence-gathering.
- B) Say nothing to anyone and begin quietly assembling the function's value evidence personally, ready for whenever the review formally engages.
- C) Treat the signal as the planning input it is: commission the value-evidence work now through legitimate framing the team will recognise as good practice anyway — tightening the survey's outcome questions, refreshing the benefits attribution for partnered investments, documenting the escalations resolved and duplications prevented — brief the director that this is being done and why; say nothing speculative to the team, but act on the two flight-risk finalists *now* on their own merits: honest career conversations about their value and their path here, which serve retention whatever the review concludes.
- D) Go back to the DG, ask for specifics about the review's scope and timing, and request that the BRM function be given formal notice and a fair opportunity to make its case before any conclusions form.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The signal is an informal early warning, not a formal process, and C matches that register: evidence work proceeds at full pace through framings that are good practice regardless, the director is briefed without speculation spreading, and the two flight-risk finalists get career conversations on their merits now. Option A breaches the DG's trust and ends future early warnings. Option B leaves evidence-gathering slower and the finalists unaddressed. Option D converts a confidence into a procedural demand and embarrasses its giver. This item tests matching the register of soft intelligence and acting through legitimate framings, since the steady-state disciplines this section rehearses are what a mature function should hold regardless of any review.

### Administration tips

- **Read the role summary alongside these items** — the scoring key for a lead relationship role is effectively a paraphrase of the skill definitions.
- **Apply the copy test when scoring**: what happens if everyone who hears about a response copies it — leadership scenarios are scored on precedent as much as outcome.
- **Watch for register-matching** (Questions 9 and 11) — confidences answered in confidence, public claims corrected in public, first violations met by phone before letterhead.
- **Find the least effective option by its absent victim** — the six small units, the team watching the decision, next year's survey respondents.
- **Use the collision cases as debrief material** — inherited commitments, phantom agreements, requests to soften findings.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding absorption of a team's difficulty** rather than coaching-with-backstop.
- **Letting outcomes launder methods** — a correct result achieved by ambush or suppression still teaches the watching team the method.
- **Rewarding correction in the wrong forum.**
- **Rewarding the deferral dressed as synthesis** — "proceed now, fix in parallel" and similar options that promise both goods usually deliver neither.
- **Weighing messengers instead of merits.**
- **Under-weighting protection of invisible commitments** in favour of visible ones.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a demanding set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a lead business relationship manager: cognitive ability, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tests normalising a service portfolio to find the outlier every raw column hides, reasoning precisely through delegation bands and strategy carve-outs, and sending back a commissioned analysis whose conclusion outran its evidence. The numeric section tests compounding a hosting pressure a flat settlement cannot hold, decomposing a falling average into the mix change it really was, computing a service-credit entitlement before a goodwill offer can anchor below it, de-duplicating a portfolio benefits pack at its joins, and bounding an "unquantifiable" attrition benefit so user experience can win an arbitration in the only currency the room accepts. The verbal section tests reading charters, delegation letters, ministerial answers, MoUs and escalation chains at the level where a proviso, an override, or a self-judged qualifier changes what an organisation is bound to. The situational judgement section tests the judgement calls that define the role: escalations returned as coaching, phantom commitments corrected in the room, inherited promises renegotiated early, bruising surveys published with responses attached, and an unpopular objector supported because he was right.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as your answer key and your talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more valuable to the candidate — and more informative to you — than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which you administered this assessment alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate you assess for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can arbitrate with evidence, communicate commitments exactly, and grow judgement in others — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
