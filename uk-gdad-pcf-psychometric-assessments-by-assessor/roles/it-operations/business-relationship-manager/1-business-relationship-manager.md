# Business Relationship Manager - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for business relationship manager (sometimes known as a business partner), within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. It gives you a structured instrument to present, score and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. For a business relationship manager, that means far more than abstract puzzles. The assessments described here are job-specific: they use the kinds of artefacts the role genuinely handles — opportunity pipelines, engagement logs, business cases, stakeholder maps, satisfaction survey results, service review packs, and user needs evidence — and the kinds of decisions the role genuinely makes, such as deciding which new idea to document and champion first, judging when to escalate a strained relationship, and turning conversations across an organisation into a coherent pipeline of opportunities that adds value.

Why does this matter for the role? A business relationship manager is responsible for ensuring consistency in engagement across the business, prepares documentation to progress new ideas and investments, maintains a live view of activity, and supports continuous improvement. That work draws constantly on the four capabilities these assessments measure — cognitive ability, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

This document is organised into four assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why employers use it for this role, a mapping of the assessment dimensions to the specific skills in the role summary, a substantial set of items with full worked explanations for you to use as an answer key, administration notes, and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate you assess at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Let the candidate work through each section without interruption, and note anything unusual about the conditions so your record is honest. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Unlike a generic intelligence test, it presents the everyday materials of the job — opportunity pipeline entries, engagement records, service catalogue extracts, stakeholder maps, meeting notes, and improvement logs — and asks the candidate to reason quickly and accurately about them.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing responses against a norm group, and modern platforms often adapt question difficulty based on previous answers. Employers usually receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score.

For a business relationship manager, cognitive ability assessment is particularly relevant because professional value lies in bringing structure to a stream of unstructured conversations. Teams across an organisation share perspectives, challenges and ambitions, and the job is to turn that into a well-maintained view of activity, a documented pipeline of opportunities, and clear cases for support. That requires holding many moving parts in mind at once: which team raised which need, which opportunities depend on which decisions, where two records contradict each other, and what genuinely deserves attention first.

### What it measures for the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**: investigating problems and opportunities in existing processes means noticing recurring themes across large and messy sets of engagement notes.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic thinking** and **Service management framework knowledge**: working within a strategic context, and applying a Level 3 service management framework, both require reasoning validly from stated rules to their consequences.
- **Error checking** maps to the duty to use relevant systems to maintain a view of activity and business-related knowledge: a pipeline record that contradicts a meeting note quietly destroys the trust others place in that data.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Ownership and topic** and **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: owning an issue while managing many relationships at once depends on rapidly ranking what matters most by impact and urgency.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**, **Service focus** and **User focus**: stakeholders bring tangled situations, and the role must take inputs, establish coherent frameworks, and contribute to recommendations grounded in user needs evidence.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in an opportunity pipeline**

A team records opportunities in a pipeline system using a fixed reference format: a two-letter directorate code, a hyphen, the four-digit year, a hyphen, then a three-digit sequence number (e.g. "FN-2025-014" for the fourteenth Finance opportunity logged in 2025). While tidying the pipeline, a candidate sees these entries:

1. FN-2025-014
2. HR-2025-007
3. OP-2025-21
4. FN-2025-015
5. DG-2025-102

Which entry breaks the reference format?

- A) Entry 2
- B) Entry 3
- C) Entry 4
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The format requires a three-digit sequence number, so entry 3 should read "OP-2025-021", not "OP-2025-21". Entries 1, 2, 4 and 5 all follow the pattern. This looks trivial, but it is exactly the kind of consistency check this role depends on — a malformed reference silently drops out of searches and reports, so an opportunity can disappear from a governance pack without anyone noticing. In an assessment, a strong candidate scans structured data field by field rather than reading entries as whole words, since the eye forgives what a parser will not.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an engagement rule**

A directorate's engagement standard states: "Every new digital investment idea must have a completed opportunity canvas before it can be presented at the monthly demand board, unless the sponsoring director requests an expedited slot." A colleague tells a candidate: "The records team's scanning idea was presented at this month's demand board, and it did not have a completed opportunity canvas." Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) The engagement standard was breached.
- B) The sponsoring director requested an expedited slot.
- C) Either the sponsoring director requested an expedited slot, or the standard was breached.
- D) The records team did not know about the opportunity canvas requirement.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The standard is a conditional rule with exactly one permitted exception. From the facts given, neither A nor B must be true on its own — each is merely possible. D invents a motive the facts do not support. The only conclusion guaranteed in every scenario consistent with the evidence is the either/or statement in C. This item rewards deducing only what the information guarantees — before telling a stakeholder their team skipped the process, a strong candidate checks whether an expedited slot was granted, since jumping to accusation A would damage exactly the relationships the role is employed to build.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a stakeholder map**

A candidate maintains a stakeholder map for the corporate services portfolio. Their rule set says: (i) every stakeholder entry must name one primary contact; (ii) every "High influence / High interest" stakeholder must have a named relationship owner from the candidate's team; (iii) contact details must be current within six months. During a review the candidate finds an entry for the Estates team dated fourteen months ago, marked "High influence / High interest", with a primary contact named and no relationship owner recorded. How many of the three rules does this entry break?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Testing each rule separately: rule (i) is satisfied (a primary contact is named); rule (ii) is broken (High/High with no relationship owner); rule (iii) is broken (fourteen months exceeds the six-month currency requirement). Two rules are broken. Checking each rule independently, rather than forming an overall impression, matters because partially compliant records are the norm — and the remediation actions differ per rule, since rule (ii) needs an ownership decision while rule (iii) needs a refresh conversation with Estates.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across competing engagement demands**

It is Monday morning. Four items compete for a candidate's attention:

1. The monthly demand board meets Wednesday and needs the documentation pack for two new opportunities, which the candidate has drafted but not yet circulated for sponsor sign-off.
2. A team leader in Operations has emailed overnight, unhappy that an issue the candidate owns — intermittent failures in a shared reporting tool — has recurred and is disrupting month-end work due Friday.
3. The candidate's continuous improvement action from last month's retrospective — documenting the engagement playbook — is due to be shared with the team at Friday's meeting.
4. A colleague in Digital asks if the candidate can "have a quick chat sometime this week" about an idea they want to raise.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 2, 1, 3, 4
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 4, 1, 2, 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weighing impact and urgency together: item 2 combines both, since an owned issue is actively disrupting live month-end work, and **Ownership and topic** means holding an issue until it is mitigated, resolved, or handed to a new owner — so re-engaging comes first. Item 1 has a hard Wednesday deadline and depends on other people's sign-off, so it must start today. Items 3 and 4 both point at Friday or "sometime this week"; the playbook has a fixed team commitment, while the chat can be scheduled in the gaps. Option B ignores the live disruption; C demotes a team commitment below an undated chat; D confuses responsiveness with priority. This item rewards sequencing by impact-weighted urgency and starting dependency-laden work early.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across engagement records**

Reviewing six months of engagement notes, a candidate tallies how often each theme was raised by the four business units they partner with:

| Theme | Unit A | Unit B | Unit C | Unit D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slow onboarding of new starters to systems | 4 | 5 | 4 | 0 |
| Duplicate data entry across tools | 2 | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| Reporting tool reliability | 1 | 0 | 1 | 6 |

The four units are comparable in size and workload. As a business relationship manager shaping a pipeline of opportunities, what is the most reasonable first interpretation of these figures?

- A) Slow onboarding is a cross-organisation theme worth a shared opportunity; Unit D's reporting spike warrants its own investigation.
- B) Unit D has the most problems and should be the top engagement priority.
- C) The onboarding theme can be ignored because Unit D never raised it.
- D) The tallies are too small to support any interpretation.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two distinct patterns sit in this table. "Slow onboarding" clusters at 4–5 mentions across Units A, B and C, a broad, consistent signal suggesting a shared underlying process problem worth a single cross-organisation opportunity. "Reporting tool reliability" is near-zero everywhere except Unit D, a local anomaly needing investigation before any conclusion. Option B misreads the table — Unit D's total is roughly comparable to others, and volume alone does not set priority. Option C treats one unit's silence as a veto, though Unit D's zero might simply mean fewer new starters. Option D is over-cautious. This item mirrors real work exploring the perspectives of many teams: aggregate to find shared needs, and isolate anomalies for follow-up.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction about approval sequencing**

An organisation's investment process has these rules: (i) an opportunity cannot receive funding approval until it has passed the demand board; (ii) the demand board will not consider an opportunity until a sponsor is confirmed and an opportunity canvas is complete; (iii) security review can run in parallel with anything, but funding cannot be released until security review is complete. The records digitisation opportunity has a complete canvas, no confirmed sponsor, and a completed security review. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) The opportunity can be considered at the next demand board.
- B) Funding can be released as soon as the demand board passes it.
- C) The opportunity cannot currently be considered by the demand board.
- D) The security review will need to be repeated once a sponsor is confirmed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rule (ii) requires both a confirmed sponsor and a complete canvas for board consideration. The canvas is complete but the sponsor is not confirmed, so the board cannot yet consider it — C must be true, A must be false. B assumes the board will pass it, which is a possibility, not a guarantee. D invents a repetition requirement the rules do not contain — rule (iii) explicitly allows security review to run in parallel with anything. This item reflects a real duty: knowing exactly which precondition is missing so the next action actually unblocks progress.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking between systems**

A pipeline system and this month's demand board pack should agree. The pipeline shows: opportunity OP-2025-031 "Field tablet refresh", status "Awaiting sponsor", estimated benefit £40,000 per year. The board pack shows: OP-2025-031 "Field tablet refresh", status "Sponsored — ready for review", estimated benefit £40,000 per year. The board meets tomorrow. The first action should be to:

- A) Correct the pipeline to "Sponsored — ready for review", since the board pack is the more formal document.
- B) Correct the board pack to "Awaiting sponsor", since the pipeline is the system of record.
- C) Check with the opportunity's contact which status is true, then correct whichever record is wrong before the board meets.
- D) Flag the discrepancy verbally at the board meeting and let the board decide.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The two records conflict, and nothing in the evidence establishes which is correct. Options A and B both resolve the conflict by assumption, each with a real chance of writing a falsehood into the records the day before a governance decision. Option D outsources a data quality problem to a senior meeting. Option C is the disciplined move: verify against the source, then fix the wrong record. This item tests whether a candidate resolves contradictions by evidence or by assumption — the role depends on others trusting the view of activity maintained.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with user needs evidence**

Three teams have asked a candidate to champion investment in a new shared file transfer tool. The candidate gathers evidence: Team 1 says transfers fail "constantly"; the service desk logs show 11 failed-transfer tickets from Team 1 in the last quarter, 2 from Team 2, and 1 from Team 3. A survey run by the candidate shows all three teams rate the current tool 2 out of 5 for ease of use, and free-text comments across all teams focus on the confusing upload interface rather than failures. What is the most defensible reading of the evidence?

- A) The evidence supports replacing the tool because everyone rates it poorly.
- B) The main shared problem is usability of the interface; the failure problem is concentrated in Team 1 and needs separate diagnosis.
- C) Team 1 is exaggerating, since only 11 tickets were logged.
- D) The survey should be discounted because satisfaction scores are subjective.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separating the two signals: ticket data shows failures cluster heavily in Team 1, a localised pattern needing its own diagnosis. Survey data shows a genuinely shared problem, but it is usability, not reliability. Option A overreaches — poor ratings support an interface problem, not necessarily replacement. Option C confuses fewer tickets than "constantly" implies with exaggeration, when users often stop logging tickets for recurring faults. Option D discards valid data. This item tests whether a candidate lets each evidence stream answer the question it is capable of answering, combining quantitative and qualitative evidence as **User focus** requires.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

A candidate must arrange three meetings this week — a sponsor briefing (S), an opportunity workshop (W), and a follow-up with the service owner (F) — across Monday to Friday, at most one of these meetings per day. Constraints: (i) S must happen before W, because the sponsor's steer shapes the workshop; (ii) the sponsor is available only Tuesday and Thursday; (iii) preparing the workshop materials takes one full working day after S, so W cannot occur on the day immediately after S; (iv) F must take place the day immediately after W. On which day must the workshop W take place?

- A) Tuesday
- B) Wednesday
- C) Thursday
- D) Friday

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** S needs the sponsor, so S is Tuesday or Thursday. If S is Thursday, W would need Saturday at the earliest, and F beyond the week — impossible. So S must be Tuesday. With S on Tuesday, W cannot be Wednesday (barred by (iii)), so W is Thursday or Friday. If W is Friday, F would need Saturday — impossible. So W must be Thursday, with F on Friday. The method — eliminating impossible placements rather than hunting directly for the right one — is the reliable way to handle multi-constraint items under time pressure, the same reasoning needed when sequencing engagement activities around busy stakeholders' diaries.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Deduction from strategy commitments**

A digital strategy states: (i) all new investment proposals must demonstrate alignment with at least one of the three strategic priorities: improving user experience, reducing running costs, or strengthening security; (ii) proposals aligned only with reducing running costs must include a statement confirming no negative impact on user experience; (iii) any proposal touching citizen data must strengthen security or hold a formal risk acceptance. A proposal reaches a candidate: it touches citizen data, claims alignment only with reducing running costs, includes the no-negative-impact statement, and holds no risk acceptance. Which is the correct assessment?

- A) The proposal is compliant: it aligns with a strategic priority and includes the required statement.
- B) The proposal is non-compliant: because it touches citizen data without strengthening security or holding a risk acceptance.
- C) The proposal is non-compliant: cost-only proposals are not allowed to touch citizen data.
- D) Compliance cannot be determined without knowing the size of the investment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rule (i) is satisfied; rule (ii) is satisfied; rule (iii) is breached, since the proposal touches citizen data without strengthening security or holding a risk acceptance. B is correct. Option A stops checking after two rules, the classic error of partial verification. Option C invents a prohibition the strategy does not contain — the fix is obtaining a risk acceptance, not abandoning the proposal. Option D imports an irrelevant consideration. This item reflects preparing documentation to obtain support: running a completeness check before a proposal reaches a board is the difference between a smooth approval and a rejection that costs a stakeholder credibility.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Problem decomposition across a process chain**

A monthly management report is assembled through four steps: Team A extracts data from the finance system (takes 1 day), Team B cleanses and merges it with operational data (2 days), Team C drafts commentary (1 day), and Team D formats and publishes (1 day). Steps must run in that order, except that Team C can start drafting commentary using the previous month's data while Team B works, needing only half a day at the end to update figures. The report is due 4 working days after the finance system closes. Recently the report has been late, and each team blames the next. Which single change most directly addresses the structural cause of lateness?

- A) Ask every team to work faster.
- B) Have Team C start commentary in parallel with Team B, as the process already allows, cutting the critical path from 5 days to 4.5 — then examine the remaining half-day gap.
- C) Move the deadline to 5 days after close.
- D) Merge Teams C and D to reduce handovers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Run sequentially, the chain takes 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 5 days against a 4-day deadline — late by design, not by anyone's slowness. Exploiting the overlap already permitted gives 1 + 2 + 0.5 + 1 = 4.5 days, a real improvement using no extra resource, still 0.5 days over. Option A demands effort without changing structure. Option C redefines the problem away without trying improvement first. Option D restructures on no evidence that handover friction, rather than sequence arithmetic, causes the delay. This item tests the **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill: investigate the process, find the structural cause, contribute a recommendation that works.

### Administration tips

- **Let the candidate use real or realistic artefacts where you can** — an anonymised pipeline extract or stakeholder map tests the same skill the items simulate.
- **Score conditional-rule items (Questions 2, 6, 10) for whether the candidate deduces only what the evidence guarantees**, not what feels likely.
- **Watch for whether a candidate checks every rule in multi-rule items** (Questions 3, 10) — the breach usually hides in the last one checked.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.
- **Use the elimination-based items (Question 9) as debrief material** — asking a candidate to talk through their method often reveals more than the answer alone.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Reading structured data as prose** rather than scanning field by field.
- **Concluding more than the evidence allows** — the most common wrong answers in deduction items are over-strong conclusions.
- **Prioritising by loudness or seniority alone**, rather than impact and urgency together.
- **Resolving contradictions by assumption** rather than verifying against the source.
- **Losing the second constraint** in multi-rule items.
- **Abandoning method under time pressure** — pattern-guessing tends to increase as a session runs long, worth noting if a candidate's accuracy drops toward the end.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures a candidate's ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from numerical information of the kind this role genuinely handles. It is not a mental arithmetic contest: calculators are usually permitted, and the skill being tested is choosing the right calculation, executing it accurately, and interpreting the result sensibly in context.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes, presenting tables, charts and short data-rich passages, each followed by two to four multiple-choice questions.

For a business relationship manager, numeric reasoning matters because numbers are the currency of the cases prepared and the improvement work supported. When documentation is prepared to progress a new idea or investment, the persuasive core is usually numeric. When a view of activity is maintained across the business, that view is full of counts, percentages and trends. Stakeholders will quote numbers, sometimes selectively; credibility rests on being able to check them quickly and restate the figure that actually matters.

### What it measures for the role

- **Percentages and percentage change** map to **Business analysis (IT operations)**: quantifying how big a problem is, and how much better a solution would make it, almost always means percentage comparisons.
- **Ratios and proportions** map to **Service focus** and **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: comparing like with like keeps a view across many stakeholders fair.
- **Averages and distributions** map to **User focus**: knowing when a mean is distorted by outliers is part of using quantitative data about users honestly.
- **Rates and trends** map to **Strategic thinking**: communicating how activities meet strategic goals often means projecting a trend responsibly.
- **Reading tables and charts accurately** maps to the duty to maintain a view of activity via relevant systems.
- **Benefit and cost calculations** map to the duty to prepare documentation and obtain support for new investments.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of engagements delivered**

A team's engagement plan committed to 40 stakeholder engagement sessions this quarter. 34 were delivered. What percentage of the planned sessions was delivered?

- A) 80%
- B) 85%
- C) 88%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 34 ÷ 40 = 0.85 = 85%. A quick sense-check: 90% of 40 is 36 and 80% is 32, so 34 sits nearer 36, consistent with 85%. This item tests denominator discipline: the plan (40) goes on the bottom because the question asks for a percentage of the plan. Reporting delivery against plan is bread-and-butter reporting, and getting the denominator right separates "85% of plan" from a meaningless "34% delivered".

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a satisfaction survey table**

An annual partner satisfaction survey returns these results for the question "The digital team understands our business needs":

| Response | Count |
|---|---|
| Strongly agree | 18 |
| Agree | 42 |
| Neither | 25 |
| Disagree | 10 |
| Strongly disagree | 5 |

What percentage of respondents agreed or strongly agreed?

- A) 55%
- B) 58%
- C) 60%
- D) 65%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total respondents = 18 + 42 + 25 + 10 + 5 = 100. Favourable = 18 + 42 = 60. 60 ÷ 100 = 60%. Never assume a total conveniently equals 100 — always add the column. Note also what the number does not say: 60% favourable leaves 25% on the fence and 15% unfavourable, and a candidate who reads the fence-sitters as an engagement opportunity rather than passive support is showing real interpretive skill.

**Question 3 (easy) — Ratio of opportunities to conversions**

Last year a directorate logged 48 opportunities in the pipeline, of which 12 progressed to funded investments. What is the ratio of logged opportunities to funded investments, in simplest form?

- A) 3:1
- B) 4:1
- C) 12:48
- D) 6:2

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 48:12 simplifies by dividing both sides by 12, giving 4:1. Option C states the ratio unreduced and reversed; option D equals 3:1 and is not simplest form. Order matters — "logged to funded" puts logged first. This conversion ratio is a genuinely useful management figure: a stable 4:1 ratio applied to 60 logged opportunities predicts about 15 funded investments, useful for setting expectations.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Percentage change in service desk demand**

A shared reporting tool generated 250 support tickets in the first quarter and 290 in the second quarter. What is the percentage increase in tickets from the first to the second quarter?

- A) 14%
- B) 16%
- C) 40%
- D) 86%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (290 − 250) ÷ 250 = 40 ÷ 250 = 0.16 = 16%. Option A results from dividing by the new value (40 ÷ 290 ≈ 13.8%), the single most common percentage-change error, which always flatters an increase. The old value is the base, because change is measured relative to where you started.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average satisfaction across units**

A candidate partners with three business units. Their satisfaction scores (out of 5) and respondent counts this quarter are: Unit A scored 4.2 from 50 respondents, Unit B scored 3.0 from 150 respondents, and Unit C scored 4.0 from 100 respondents. What is the overall average satisfaction score across all respondents, to one decimal place?

- A) 3.5
- B) 3.7
- C) 3.6
- D) 3.4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A simple average of 4.2, 3.0 and 4.0 gives 3.7 — wrong, because it gives tiny Unit A the same weight as Unit B, which has three times the respondents. Weighting: (4.2×50) + (3.0×150) + (4.0×100) = 210 + 450 + 400 = 1,060, divided by 300 respondents = 3.53, rounding to 3.5. The unweighted 3.7 is the trap, and it is not harmless — it overstates satisfaction precisely because the largest partner unit is the least satisfied, the one insight most needed. Whenever groups differ in size, weight by the count behind each score.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Cost comparison for an investment case**

A candidate is documenting an opportunity to replace a legacy meeting-room booking tool. The current tool costs £18,000 per year in licences plus £6,000 per year in support. The proposed tool costs £30,000 in one-off implementation, then £15,000 per year in licences with support included. Over three years, how do total costs compare?

- A) The proposed tool costs £3,000 less than the current tool.
- B) The proposed tool costs £3,000 more than the current tool.
- C) The costs are equal.
- D) The proposed tool costs £9,000 more than the current tool.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Current tool: (£18,000 + £6,000) × 3 = £72,000. Proposed tool: £30,000 + (£15,000 × 3) = £75,000. Difference: £3,000 more for the proposed tool. The one-off implementation cost is the item most often mishandled. A strong candidate would present both the three-year and steady-state view — from year four onwards the proposed tool saves £24,000 − £15,000 = £9,000 annually — since that is what genuinely obtains support for an investment.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Proportion and coverage of engagement**

An organisation has 32 teams in scope for business partnering. A candidate's engagement log shows they held at least one structured engagement session with 24 of them this year. The team's target is at least 85% coverage. What was the actual coverage, and how many more teams would have needed to be engaged to meet the target?

- A) 75% coverage; 4 more teams needed
- B) 80% coverage; 3 more teams needed
- C) 75% coverage; 3 more teams needed
- D) 85% coverage; target met

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Actual coverage: 24 ÷ 32 = 75%. The target requires 85% of 32 = 27.2 teams; since a fifth of a team cannot be engaged and "at least 85%" must round up rather than down (27 teams gives 84.4%, failing; 28 gives 87.5%, passing), the requirement is 28 teams, a gap of 4. Option C is the near-miss trap of rounding down. When a minimum-percentage target meets a small whole-number population, convert to a count and check the whole numbers either side of the threshold.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a trend for a strategic conversation**

Monthly active users of a self-service HR portal championed by a candidate:

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Active users | 1,200 | 1,320 | 1,450 | 1,600 | 1,760 | 1,940 |

Approximately what has the month-on-month growth rate been, and what would July's figure be if the trend continues?

- A) About 10% per month; roughly 2,130 users in July
- B) About 10% per month; roughly 2,000 users in July
- C) About 15% per month; roughly 2,230 users in July
- D) About 5% per month; roughly 2,040 users in July

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Testing several intervals (Feb ÷ Jan ≈ 1.10, Jun ÷ May ≈ 1.10) confirms roughly 10% monthly compounding growth. July's projection: 1,940 × 1.10 ≈ 2,134. Two habits matter: testing the rate at more than one point, since a series can decay, and presenting projections conditionally — "if the current 10% monthly growth continues" — since a director remembers the number long after forgetting the caveat.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Benefits arithmetic for an opportunity canvas**

An opportunity canvas being prepared claims a new digital form will save time for caseworkers. Evidence: 5,200 applications are processed per year; the current paper process takes an average of 22 minutes of caseworker time per application; the digital form is expected to cut this to 14 minutes. Caseworker time is costed at £30 per hour. What is the estimated annual saving, to the nearest £1,000?

- A) £21,000
- B) £26,000
- C) £35,000
- D) £42,000

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Time saved per application: 22 − 14 = 8 minutes. Total: 5,200 × 8 = 41,600 minutes = 693.33 hours. Cost saving: 693.33 × £30 ≈ £20,800, rounding to £21,000. Keeping units visible at every step (minutes → hours → pounds) protects against the unit slips that produce options like D. Worth noting for documentation: this is a time-released saving, only cashable if the released hours are redeployed — a distinction finance colleagues will press on.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Two years ago, 40% of the teams a candidate partners with rated engagement "good" or better. This year, 50% do. A colleague drafts a slide saying "engagement satisfaction has increased by 10%". As the owner of this data, what is the accurate statement?

- A) The colleague is right: the increase is 10%.
- B) Satisfaction increased by 10 percentage points, which is a 25% relative increase.
- C) Satisfaction increased by 25 percentage points.
- D) Satisfaction increased by 10 percentage points, which is a 20% relative increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The gap between 40% and 50% is 10 percentage points. The relative increase is (50 − 40) ÷ 40 = 25%. Option D divides by the new value (10 ÷ 50 = 20%), the recurring wrong-denominator error. This distinction moves in both directions in this role's work — a supplier whose availability fell from 99% to 97% might describe "a 2% reduction" when incidents affecting users have actually tripled.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Interpreting a two-way table under time pressure**

A quarterly report cross-tabulates the 60 opportunities currently in a candidate's pipeline:

| | Aligned to strategy | Not yet assessed | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sponsored | 18 | 6 | 24 |
| Unsponsored | 22 | 14 | 36 |
| Total | 40 | 20 | 60 |

Which statement is supported by the table?

- A) Most sponsored opportunities have not been assessed for strategic alignment.
- B) Exactly 75% of sponsored opportunities are aligned to strategy, a higher alignment rate than unsponsored opportunities show.
- C) Sponsorship causes strategic alignment.
- D) 40% of all opportunities are sponsored and aligned.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sponsored alignment rate: 18 ÷ 24 = 75%. Unsponsored: 22 ÷ 36 ≈ 61%. Both parts of B check out. Option A is false — most sponsored opportunities (18 of 24) have been assessed and found aligned. Option C leaps from association to causation. Option D confuses the cell (18 ÷ 60 = 30%) with a row or column total. Two-way tables reward identifying precisely which subgroup a percentage should be computed over.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Combining measures for a continuous improvement case**

A team wants to show that a new triage step, introduced at the start of Q3, improved the handling of partner requests. The data:

| Measure | Q2 (before) | Q3 (after) |
|---|---|---|
| Requests received | 300 | 360 |
| Requests resolved within 5 days | 210 | 288 |
| Average days to resolve | 4.8 | 4.1 |

Which summary most fairly represents the improvement?

- A) Resolutions within 5 days rose from 210 to 288, an increase of 78 requests, proving the triage step worked.
- B) The within-5-day rate rose from 70% to 80% despite a 20% rise in demand, and average resolution time fell — consistent with a genuine improvement, though other Q3 factors cannot be ruled out.
- C) Demand rose 20%, so performance cannot be compared between quarters.
- D) Average days fell by 0.7, a 15% improvement, which is the only figure worth reporting.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rates: Q2 210 ÷ 300 = 70%; Q3 288 ÷ 360 = 80%, against a 20% rise in demand — a higher proportion of a larger workload, resolved faster. Option B states this and adds the honest caveat that a before/after comparison is evidence, not proof. Option A leans on raw counts and the word "proving". Option C surrenders too easily — rates exist to make changed volumes comparable. Option D discards two of three relevant measures. This item rewards normalising for demand, reporting rates alongside volumes, and claiming exactly as much as the evidence supports.

### Administration tips

- **Watch for denominator discipline** (Questions 1, 4, 10) — naming what a percentage is "of" before calculating is the single most diagnostic habit in this section.
- **Score the weighted-average and two-way-table items (Questions 5, 11) for method, not just the final figure.**
- **Note whether a candidate presents a projection with its assumption stated** (Question 8), which is a genuine reporting-maturity signal.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.
- **Use the explanations to debrief on real reporting practice** — several items map directly onto figures a candidate would produce for a service review.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Dividing by the new value** in percentage-change calculations.
- **Confusing percentage points with percentages.**
- **Averaging averages** without weighting by group size.
- **Ignoring one-off versus recurring costs.**
- **Comparing raw counts across changed volumes** rather than converting to rates.
- **Over-claiming from projections and associations** — language like "will reach" or "proves" where the evidence only supports "if the trend continues" or "consistent with".

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate understands, evaluates and uses written information of the kind this role runs on. A short passage — a policy extract, a stakeholder email, a service report, a strategy statement — is followed by items asking what follows from it. The classic format is True / False / Cannot say: True means the passage guarantees the statement, False means it contradicts it, Cannot say means the passage provides insufficient information either way. Other formats test inference, identifying the main point, and judging which rewrite of a sentence is clearest.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes, with several passages of 100–250 words, each carrying two to four questions. The discipline the test demands — answering strictly from the passage, setting aside what the candidate happens to know or believe — is close to the centre of the job. Working days are a stream of words: emails that must be read for what they actually commit to, framework definitions where "shall" and "should" change an obligation into a suggestion, strategy documents whose commitments must be represented faithfully, and documentation the candidate writes themselves, where imprecise wording can send an opportunity to a board with a claim its sponsor never made.

### What it measures for the role

- **Reading comprehension of policy and process documents** maps to **Service management framework knowledge**: precise reading of framework definitions is the substance of a Level 3 qualification.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**: evidence says exactly what it says, and concluding more produces recommendations that collapse under challenge.
- **Inference and careful reading between the lines** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: noticing what an email does not say, without inventing meaning that is not there.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to the duty to prepare documentation and obtain support: sponsors and boards read summaries first, and sometimes only.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Strategic thinking** and **User focus**: ambiguous or jargon-heavy prose fails both goals.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot say on a service report**

Passage: "The shared printing service met its availability target in eleven of the twelve months this year. The single miss occurred in March, when a supplier firmware fault caused four days of intermittent outages. The supplier has since replaced the firmware across the printer estate, and no related faults have been recorded in the eight months following the fix."

Statement: "The shared printing service missed its availability target in March because of a supplier firmware fault."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Both components of the statement are directly supported: the miss occurred in March, and it is attributed to the firmware fault causing four days of intermittent outages. This item trains matching a statement to the passage clause by clause. Note what would flip the answer: "repeatedly missed" would be contradicted by "eleven of twelve months" (False); "the supplier's first failure" would be unaddressed (Cannot say).

**Question 2 (easy) — Cannot-say discipline on a stakeholder email**

Passage (email from a finance business partner): "Thanks for the opportunity summary. I can see the attraction of the proposal, and the projected savings are certainly material. However, I am not yet in a position to confirm finance's support. We would want to see the assumptions behind the savings figure, and ideally an option that phases the spend across two financial years."

Statement: "Finance has decided to oppose the proposal."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says finance is "not yet in a position to confirm support" — a pending position with stated conditions, which contradicts "has decided to oppose" rather than merely failing to confirm it. So the answer is False, not Cannot say. This distinction — silence versus a passage pointing the other way — is the most commonly confused boundary in verbal tests, and reading caution as opposition in the workplace can trigger needless escalation.

**Question 3 (easy) — Identifying the main point**

Passage: "Attendance at the monthly service review has fallen from nine business units to four over the past two quarters. Several invitees have said the meeting repeats information already available on the dashboard. Two units have asked whether their standing agenda slot could be replaced by a short written update. Meanwhile, the issues raised at the review that do generate discussion are almost always about upcoming changes rather than past performance."

Which sentence best states the main point?

- A) The service review meeting is too long.
- B) The service review in its current form is losing relevance, and its content and format need rethinking.
- C) Business units no longer care about service performance.
- D) The dashboard should be decommissioned.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four converging signals — falling attendance, feedback about duplication, requests for written updates, discussion gravitating to future changes — support one conclusion, which is what B states. Option A picks a cause never mentioned. Option C over-interprets. Option D inverts the evidence, since the dashboard is the preferred substitute. In main-point items, the right answer covers the breadth of the evidence without exceeding it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say on a strategy extract**

Passage: "The digital strategy commits the department to reducing the number of standalone case management systems from fourteen to no more than six by 2028. Consolidation decisions will be taken by the design authority, informed by business impact assessments. No system will be decommissioned without a supported migration path for its users, and systems supporting statutory functions will be consolidated last."

Statement: "Under the strategy, at least eight standalone case management systems will be decommissioned by 2028."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The strategy commits to reducing the number of standalone systems; the statement claims decommissioning specifically. A system can stop being standalone through merging or re-scoping without formal decommissioning, so the passage guarantees a reduction of at least eight but not the mechanism the statement names. This is a deliberately fine distinction, and one where imprecise strategy communication manufactures resistance a relationship manager then spends months unwinding.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from an email exchange**

Passage (two emails). From a service owner to a candidate, Monday: "We can support the pilot going live next month provided the two open security actions are closed by the 15th." From the security lead to the candidate, Thursday: "One of the two actions on the pilot is now closed. The second depends on a supplier patch currently scheduled for the 20th."

Which inference is best supported?

- A) The pilot will not go live next month.
- B) On current plans the service owner's condition will not be met, so the pilot's go-live is at risk unless the patch is accelerated or the condition renegotiated.
- C) The security lead has failed to manage the supplier effectively.
- D) The service owner will withdraw support for the pilot.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** On current plans the condition fails, since the patch date (20th) falls after the deadline (15th) — but schedules can move and conditions can be renegotiated, so A and D overstate by converting a risk into a certainty. C assigns blame with no evidence. B states exactly what follows, naming the two realistic paths. This is verbatim the reasoning a relationship manager performs before deciding whether to convene the parties.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Framework language: shall, should, may**

Passage (extract from an organisation's engagement standard): "Relationship owners shall record every structured engagement in the partnering system within five working days. Relationship owners should share a written summary with the stakeholder after each engagement. Relationship owners may invite subject matter experts to engagements where specialist topics are expected."

Which statement accurately reflects the standard?

- A) Sharing a written summary with the stakeholder is mandatory.
- B) Recording engagements in the partnering system is recommended but optional.
- C) Recording engagements within five working days is mandatory; sharing summaries is recommended; inviting experts is permitted at the owner's discretion.
- D) Inviting subject matter experts is required for specialist topics.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Shall" denotes mandatory, "should" recommended, "may" discretionary — mapping the three clauses gives exactly option C. Option A promotes a "should" to mandatory; B demotes a "shall" to optional; D converts a "may" into a requirement. Precision with modal verbs is one of the cheapest credibility investments in this role — misstating "should" as "have to" misrepresents the standard, and the misstatement travels.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say on user research findings**

Passage: "Research with 25 caseworkers across three sites found that 19 used the workaround spreadsheet daily. Participants consistently described the official system's search as 'too slow for live calls'. Younger participants were no more likely than older participants to use the official system's advanced features. Site managers, who were not included in the research, are believed by the research team to rely on the official system's reports."

Statement: "Most caseworkers in the organisation use the workaround spreadsheet daily."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 19 of 25 research participants is strong evidence within the sample, but the passage gives no information about the wider organisation's caseworker numbers or whether the sample was representative — Cannot say. Note the companion trap: what managers are "believed" to do is a belief, not a finding. This sample-versus-population discipline is central to representing research honestly — "19 of the 25 caseworkers we spoke to" rather than "most caseworkers".

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

A candidate is drafting the summary line of an opportunity document for a demand board. Which version is clearest and most useful to the board?

- A) "Leveraging synergies across the estate, this transformational opportunity will optimise stakeholder-facing paradigms going forward."
- B) "This proposal asks the board to approve £45,000 to pilot digital appointment booking in two clinics, aiming to cut missed appointments by a quarter within six months."
- C) "It was determined by the team that the potentiality of appointment-related digitalisation should be examined at this point in time by the board."
- D) "The board is invited to note various considerations relating to appointments, bookings, clinics, budgets, and timescales."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B answers what is being asked, how much, to do what, and by when, in twenty-six words. Option A is jargon without content. Option C smothers a simple request in passive voice and noun inflation. Option D lists topics without a request. This item tests documentation skill directly: boards fund proposals they can understand in one reading.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Evaluating an argument**

Passage (from a supplier's renewal proposal): "Our service has achieved 99.2% availability this year, up from 98.9% last year. Customer organisations that renewed for three years last cycle reported higher satisfaction than those that renewed for one year. We therefore recommend a three-year renewal, which will maximise your satisfaction while locking in current pricing against inflation."

Which is the strongest criticism of the argument?

- A) The availability figures are irrelevant to a renewal decision.
- B) The satisfaction comparison assumes renewal length caused satisfaction, when satisfied customers may simply be more likely to choose longer renewals.
- C) Inflation may not occur, so price-locking has no value.
- D) 99.2% availability is too low to justify any renewal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The proposal reverses a plausible causal arrow: satisfied organisations are naturally more willing to commit for three years, so the correlation may reflect selection, not benefit. This dismantles the recommendation's load-bearing claim. Option A goes too far. Option C attacks the weakest, most defensible part. Option D asserts a standard the passage never sets. Argument-evaluation items reward finding the claim the conclusion actually rests on.

**Question 10 (challenging) — True/False/Cannot say with a qualifier chain**

Passage (governance minute): "The board agreed in principle to the records digitisation opportunity, subject to a revised benefits estimate and confirmation of storage costs. The chair noted that, should the revised estimate fall materially below the current figure, the opportunity would need to return to the board rather than proceed under delegated authority. No date was set for the return of the paper."

Statement: "If the revised benefits estimate falls materially below the current figure, the opportunity can proceed under delegated authority once storage costs are confirmed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The minute states a specific carve-out: if the revised estimate falls materially below the current figure, the opportunity must return to the board rather than proceed under delegated authority. The statement claims the opposite in exactly that scenario, directly contradicting the carve-out — False, not Cannot say, since the passage speaks precisely to this scenario. Misremembering exactly what a conditional approval permitted is the kind of error that costs a team its credibility with governance for a year.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Synthesis across two passages**

Passage 1 (dashboard commentary): "Uptake of the new expenses app reached 62% of eligible staff by end of June, against a target of 60%. Support tickets per hundred users fell for the third consecutive month."

Passage 2 (email from a union representative): "Members in field-based roles tell us the expenses app is effectively unusable on the shared devices they carry, and many have reverted to paper claims, which are now taking longer to process than before the app launched."

Which statement do the two passages, taken together, best support?

- A) The union representative's claims are contradicted by the uptake data.
- B) The app has succeeded overall, so the field-based concerns can be addressed in a future phase.
- C) Aggregate uptake and support figures are healthy, but they may mask a poor experience for field-based staff on shared devices, which merits targeted investigation — especially as the paper fallback is degrading.
- D) The dashboard data must be wrong.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passages are not in logical conflict — 62% uptake leaves 38% unaccounted for, and users who abandon an app stop raising tickets about it, so falling ticket rates can even be a symptom of the problem. Option A misreads aggregate success as contradiction of subgroup failure. Option B accepts the subgroup's exclusion too cheaply and misses that the paper fallback is now slower than before launch. Option D abandons one source. Option C holds the quantitative and qualitative evidence together, exactly what **User focus** and **Business analysis** exist to do.

### Administration tips

- **Emphasise the "answer from the passage" rule especially strongly** — experienced candidates lose marks by answering from professional convention.
- **Watch the False/Cannot-say boundary specifically** (Questions 2 and 4) — it is the most commonly confused distinction, and a candidate's pattern here is informative.
- **Note how a candidate handles modal verbs** (Question 6) — "shall" versus "should" versus "may" precision is a strong predictor of documentation accuracy on the job.
- **Use the clear-writing item (Question 8) as a live discussion point.**
- **Time by passage, not by statement.**

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding answers built on outside knowledge** rather than the passage.
- **Treating plausible as proven** in inference items.
- **Confusing silence with contradiction.**
- **Skimming past modal verbs and qualifiers.**
- **Rewarding summaries that sound impressive over ones that are specific and reader-focused.**
- **Letting sympathy for one voice in a multi-source passage set the answer**, rather than what the evidence actually reconciles to.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks the candidate to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Unlike the other three assessments, it has no single formula: it measures practical judgement, alignment with professional behaviours, and instinct for what actually works in organisations with real people, real constraints, and real history. Common formats include choosing the most effective response, choosing the most and least effective from a set, ranking all responses, or rating each independently.

The typical format is 15 to 25 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed (30 to 45 minutes), scored against a key defined by experienced practitioners. There is rarely a perfectly right answer; responses are scored on a gradient, with the difference between candidates showing in consistently preferring active, collaborative, user-focused choices over passive, political, or unilateral ones.

For a business relationship manager, the SJT is arguably the most job-relevant assessment of the four, because judgement in ambiguous interpersonal situations is the job. A candidate at this level sits between the digital function and the business, absorbs frustration from both directions, owns issues other people must fix, champions ideas that compete for scarce funding, and hears things in corridors that governance has not yet heard.

### What it measures for the role

- **Ownership scenarios** map to **Ownership and topic**: whether a candidate holds an issue until it is genuinely resolved or a new owner is found.
- **Conflict and negotiation scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: influencing decisions and dealing with challenging situations while preserving the relationship.
- **Prioritisation and demand scenarios** map to **Strategic thinking** and **Business analysis (IT operations)**: connecting choices back to strategic goals and evidence rather than noise or seniority.
- **User advocacy scenarios** map to **User focus**: several scenarios offer a politically convenient option that quietly sacrifices users.
- **Process and standards scenarios** map to **Service focus** and **Service management framework knowledge**: whether a candidate upholds, adapts, or abandons process under pressure.
- **Team development scenarios** map to the duty to support continuous improvement that matures team capability.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded. For each scenario, ask the candidate to choose the option indicated (most effective, or most and least effective as stated).

**Question 1 (easy) — The unanswered stakeholder**

A candidate returns from a week's leave to find that a head of unit they partner with emailed twice during their absence about a delayed laptop rollout for her team, and received no reply — the candidate's out-of-office pointed to a shared mailbox that nobody monitored. She has now copied the candidate's line manager, describing the digital function as "impossible to reach". What is the most effective first action?

- A) Reply, copying the line manager, explaining that the shared mailbox failure was not the candidate's fault.
- B) Phone the head of unit, apologise for the silence, get the current facts on the rollout, and commit to a specific update by a specific time — then fix the shared mailbox gap so it cannot recur.
- C) Forward her email to the rollout team and ask them to respond directly.
- D) Wait until a full rollout status is available before making any contact.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relationship damage is about feeling unheard, so repair must be human and fast: a call, an apology without defensiveness, and a concrete commitment. B also fixes the system that caused it, stopping the same failure damaging the next colleague's leave. Option A optimises for blame allocation, which will land badly with someone who feels ignored. Option C passes the relationship to a delivery team, when the relationship is precisely what the role owns. Option D confuses complete information with worthy of contact. The pattern this item rewards: acknowledge fast, commit specifically, fix the instance, then fix the system.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit for an idea**

In a cross-team workshop facilitated by a candidate, a quiet colleague from the operations team suggested a change to the onboarding process that later becomes a funded opportunity. At the demand board, a senior manager congratulates the candidate on "your excellent idea". What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the praise; as the facilitator, the pipeline entry is genuinely the candidate's work.
- B) Thank the manager and correct the record then and there: name the operations colleague as the source of the idea, and follow up by letting the colleague and their manager know it was funded.
- C) Say nothing at the board, but privately tell the colleague their idea was funded.
- D) Email the board afterwards with a formal correction and an apology.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This role exists to set the conditions for a culture of meaningful connections, and nothing sets those conditions faster than teams seeing that ideas shared with a business partner come back credited and funded. B corrects attribution gracefully in the moment and completes the loop with the colleague and their manager. Option A takes a small reputational gain at the cost of the trust the whole function runs on. Option C leaves the board's record wrong. Option D treats a light-touch social correction as a governance event. SJT keys consistently reward generous, immediate, low-drama attribution.

**Question 3 (easy) — The tempting shortcut**

A team a candidate partners with wants to buy a £4,000 software subscription and asks them to "just put it through as an existing-service renewal so we can skip the new-demand paperwork — it's basically the same tool as the one we already use". The candidate knows the demand process requires new subscriptions to be assessed, partly to check security and duplication. What is the most effective response?

- A) Process it as a renewal this once, and tell the team the proper route must be used next time.
- B) Refuse and report the team's request to the governance board as an attempted process breach.
- C) Explain why the assessment exists, decline the workaround, and offer to help them through the new-demand route quickly — including checking first whether the existing tool already meets the need.
- D) Tell the team to submit it however they wish; the process is their responsibility, not yours.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C holds the process and the relationship together: it declines the mislabelling, explains the genuine purpose of assessment, and converts the role into help — including the sharp-eyed suggestion that "basically the same tool" might mean no purchase is needed. Option A trades a control away for comfort, and "just this once" becomes precedent. Option B escalates a question into an accusation. Option D abandons **Service focus** — coherent frameworks survive because people make the compliant route easier than the workaround.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the stalled issue**

Six weeks ago a candidate took ownership of a recurring failure in the interface between the HR and finance systems, which causes duplicate records that both teams manually correct. The fix sits with a supplier, who has twice missed promised dates. Both teams are losing patience with the candidate personally, since they are the visible owner. Identify the **most** effective and **least** effective responses.

1. Tell both teams the supplier keeps missing dates, so the delay is outside the candidate's control, and share the supplier's latest promised date.
2. Convene the supplier and both teams: get a realistic date with intermediate checkpoints from the supplier, agree an interim workaround that reduces the manual correction burden, and send all parties a single shared status updated weekly until resolution.
3. Hand the issue to the service desk queue so it is tracked formally, and move on to other work.
4. Escalate immediately to the supplier's account director demanding contractual penalties.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 1
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 attacks all three dimensions — the unreliable fix date, the ongoing manual burden, and the eroding trust — with one shared status replacing two anxious teams chasing separately. Response 3 is least effective because it is disguised abandonment: a queue is not an owner, and **Ownership and topic** rules out exactly that move. Response 1 is weak but honest; response 4 is premature as a first move but a legitimate escalation tool if checkpoints fail. Option A's pair captures the logic: the best answer fixes pain, process and trust together; the worst makes ownership disappear while looking procedurally tidy.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Competing sponsors**

Two directors a candidate partners with each want the single available delivery slot next quarter. Director A's project digitises a paper process used by 3,000 staff, with a solid benefits case. Director B's project fixes a clunky journey for 400 external users with accessibility needs, with a weaker financial case but strong user research showing real exclusion. Director B phones the candidate: "You're our business partner — make sure we get the slot." What is the most effective response?

- A) Advocate for Director B as requested; external users with accessibility needs must always come first.
- B) Advocate for Director A; the benefits case is stronger and benefits cases exist to settle exactly this question.
- C) Tell Director B that both cases will be presented objectively to the prioritisation forum — including the accessibility evidence, which the financial case understates — and help both directors strengthen their submissions rather than championing one privately.
- D) Suggest both directors escalate to their common superior to decide.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The trap is the phrase "you're our business partner" — an invitation to convert a trusted-broker role into a private advocate role. C declines that conversion while genuinely serving Director B, committing to put the accessibility evidence squarely in front of the forum. This is **Stakeholder relationship management** working as designed: supporting mutual needs while focusing on user needs, without becoming one stakeholder's lobbyist against another. Option A substitutes a personal rule for the organisation's process, making the candidate unusable as a partner to Director A. Option B discards user research as evidence. Option D teaches directors that lobbying beats process.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The corridor disclosure**

A team leader mentions to a candidate, informally over coffee, that his team has quietly started using a free consumer file-sharing app to send case documents to an external partner organisation "because the official transfer tool keeps timing out". Case documents include personal information. He adds: "Don't make this into a thing — I'm telling you because you're the one person who actually listens." What is the most effective response?

- A) Respect the confidence; report nothing, but encourage him to stop using the app.
- B) Tell him clearly that personal data in a consumer app is a risk that cannot be sat on, help him report it to the security team that day — framing it as fixing a broken tool that forced a workaround — and take ownership of getting the timeout problem investigated so the compliant route becomes usable.
- C) Report the team to the security team immediately without telling him, to avoid tipping anyone off.
- D) Advise him to delete the app history and revert to the official tool once the timeouts are fixed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two obligations collide — trust and a live data-protection risk — and B honours both to the maximum extent possible: honest about the limits of confidence, brings him into the reporting rather than going around him, frames the report as a symptom of a failing tool, and commits to fixing the timeout so the workaround does not return. Option A makes the candidate complicit. Option C treats a colleague who trusted them as a suspect. Option D compounds a risk into misconduct by encouraging concealment of evidence.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the improvement that lands on a candidate**

In a team retrospective, colleagues agree that engagement records are inconsistent — different people record different things, and reports are unreliable. Someone suggests a standard template, and all eyes turn to a candidate: "You're good at this — can you sort it?" Their own workload is already full, and they privately think the team's tooling, not just the template, is the real problem. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Accept the task and quietly add it to a backlog, where it will realistically wait months.
2. Agree to lead a first, small step — drafting a template with two volunteers within a fortnight — while putting the tooling hypothesis to the team as a follow-on question with evidence to be gathered from the next month's records.
3. Decline, explaining the workload is full, and suggest the team lead assigns it to someone else.
4. Accept, then redesign the team's entire recording toolchain to fix the problem properly before releasing any template.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 1
- B) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 is a small, dated, shared first step plus honesty about a deeper hypothesis surfaced as a question to be tested, and it distributes the work rather than concentrating it in one "good at this" person. Response 1 looks cooperative but is a silent commitment failure that blocks anyone else picking the task up — worse than an honest decline. Response 3 is suboptimal but honest; response 4 over-scopes and delays all value. The pattern: small committed steps beat both silent acceptance and grand redesigns, and hypotheses earn status through evidence, not seniority of voice.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The overheard misinformation**

At a directorate all-hands, a well-liked senior manager tells the audience that "the new case management system is being imposed on us without any user input". A candidate knows this is wrong: two rounds of user research included eight staff from this very directorate, and their feedback changed the rollout plan. The manager outranks the candidate, and the meeting is large. What is the most effective response?

- A) Publicly correct the manager from the floor, citing the research rounds and participant numbers.
- B) Say nothing; contradicting a senior manager in public would damage the relationship.
- C) Speak to the manager privately straight after the meeting with the research facts, offer to connect her with the staff who participated, and suggest a joint follow-up message or session so her audience hears the corrected picture from her.
- D) Email the whole directorate afterwards with the correct information, copying the manager.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C corrects the misinformation for the audience it reached, preserves the manager's standing, and lets the correction come from the person who made the error — while the joint follow-up ensures it actually reaches the audience. Option A wins the argument and loses the relationship. Option B abandons the truth, poisoning adoption. Option D publicly exposes the manager's error in writing at email speed. The pattern: correct privately, equip the person to fix it publicly, convert the moment into connection.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The pipeline under pressure**

Late on Friday, a candidate's head of function asks them to add a senior director's pet idea to next week's demand board pack "with a positive recommendation — it will make the board go smoothly". The candidate's investigation notes from earlier in the month show the idea duplicates roughly 70% of an existing service's capability, and the affected service owner has not been consulted. What is the most effective response?

- A) Add the idea with a positive recommendation as instructed; the head of function owns the pack.
- B) Add the idea to the pack, but with a genuine assessment — the duplication evidence and the missing consultation — and tell the head of function on Friday what the pack will say and why, offering a path that keeps the board smooth: a two-week deferral to consult the service owner and test the overlap.
- C) Refuse to include the idea and tell the head of function the process must be followed.
- D) Include the idea with a positive recommendation, but privately brief the service owner so they can object at the board.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B keeps the assessment honest — a recommendation not believed is not one to sign — and treats the head of function as a partner, giving them the news on Friday with a constructive route attached. Option A makes the candidate the author of advice known to be wrong. Option C defends the process but abandons the people, surprising and cornering the head of function. Option D manufactures an ambush at the board, using the service owner as a proxy for an objection not made openly. The pattern: dissent early, in private, with evidence, always attached to a workable alternative.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the failing relationship**

A candidate's relationship with the head of a major business unit has deteriorated. He cancels meetings, answers their emails tersely, and last week told a colleague that "the digital partnering thing adds a layer without adding value". His unit's engagement scores are the candidate's lowest, and two of his ideas were rejected by the demand board earlier this year. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Reduce contact to essential items only, and focus energy on the units that value partnering.
2. Ask his deputy — with whom the candidate gets on well — to act as the channel into the unit from now on.
3. Request a short one-to-one with him, open by asking what would make partnering worth his time, listen without defending, revisit what happened to his two rejected ideas — including what could make a future submission succeed — and agree one concrete thing to deliver for the unit in the next month.
4. Raise the deteriorating relationship with the line manager and ask for advice before doing anything.

- A) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 1
- B) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 2
- C) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 1
- D) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 3 addresses the diagnosis directly — asking his definition of value, absorbing criticism without defensiveness, reopening the rejections as learning, and staking credibility on one delivered outcome, which is how trust is actually rebuilt. Response 1 is least effective because it ratifies his critique and abandons the unit's users along with its head — the lowest-scoring unit is precisely where the role earns its keep. Response 2 institutionalises the breakdown; response 4 is reasonable preparation but delays contact as a complete response. Order: 3, 4, 2, 1.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The improvement that threatens a colleague**

While mapping engagement activity, a candidate finds that a monthly report a colleague in their own team has produced for years is read by almost nobody: the three units that receive it confirm they stopped using it when the dashboard launched, and its production takes her about two days a month. She is proud of the report and has mentioned it in her performance conversations. The team is under pressure to free capacity for higher-value partnering work. What is the most effective response?

- A) Present the readership evidence at the next team meeting and recommend the report be discontinued to free two days of monthly capacity.
- B) Say nothing; two days a month is a small price for a colleague's morale, and it is not the candidate's report to question.
- C) Share the evidence with her privately first, frame it as the dashboard having overtaken the report rather than the report failing, and explore with her what the freed two days could become — ideally letting her propose and own the change, and carry the improved offer to the three units herself.
- D) Suggest to the team lead, without naming the report, that a general audit of all team outputs be run to find low-value work.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C sequences correctly: evidence goes to her first, framed accurately and kindly, and the freed capacity is offered as her opportunity to shape and own. This is what supporting continuous improvement looks like when the improvement has a face. Option A ambushes a colleague in front of the team, teaching everyone present that improvement findings are attacks. Option B protects one person's feelings at the price of capacity the pressured team needs. Option D launders a known finding through a fake-general process, which colleagues see through. The pattern: take evidence to the person, separate the work's obsolescence from their worth, hand them authorship of the change.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The spending review squeeze**

A spending review requires a directorate to model a 15% reduction in digital spend. A candidate's head of function asks them, as the person closest to the business units, to sound out "quietly, without alarming anyone" where cuts would do least harm. Within a day, a unit head the candidate partners with asks directly: "I hear there's a cuts exercise — is my modernisation project safe?" The project is, in the candidate's honest view, one of the more likely candidates for deferral. What is the most effective response?

- A) Deny any knowledge of a cuts exercise, as the candidate was asked to keep it quiet.
- B) Tell him his project is probably going to be deferred, so he can prepare.
- C) Acknowledge that scenario planning for the spending review is happening without confirming specifics, decline honestly to speculate on individual projects before any decision exists, commit to telling him as soon as there is something real to tell, and separately press the head of function to communicate about the exercise before rumour does the communicating.
- D) Refer his question to the head of function and say nothing further.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C threads confidentiality and candour precisely: it refuses the lie (the exercise exists and he has already heard so), refuses the leak (naming his project pre-empts a decision no one has made), gives him something true and useful, and pushes the systemic fix — getting ahead of the rumour, since "quietly" has already failed. Option A spends integrity on a secret already half-out. Option B feels loyal but is a betrayal of process that also harms him if the decision goes the other way. Option D is technically safe and relationally empty. The pattern: be honest about the existence of uncertainty, refuse speculation, commit to early truth, fix the communication gap.

### Administration tips

- **Score each option against the anatomy of a strong answer**: active over passive, going to the difficult person rather than around them, fixing the system as well as the instance, honesty about limits, user needs held visible under pressure, early private dissent with an alternative attached.
- **Watch the verbs a candidate's own reasoning uses at debrief** — "convene", "agree a date", "commit", "own" signal the behaviours the key rewards; "wait", "forward", "hope" signal passivity.
- **For most/least formats, note whether a candidate can identify the floor as readily as the ceiling** — the least effective option is often the more diagnostic of the pair.
- **Use these scenarios as debrief material** — asking a candidate to talk through their reasoning often reveals more than the score alone.
- **Keep conditions generous or untimed**, consistent with the format's intent.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an option that avoids all discomfort** — silence, delay, or routing around people should generally score lower than an early, small, direct move.
- **Confusing escalation with abdication** — escalating with evidence and a recommendation is different from referring a problem upward so it stops being one's own.
- **Over-weighting hierarchy** — both automatic compliance and reflexive non-challenge upward misread what strong answers usually look like.
- **Missing when users are off-stage** — in scenarios where users are absent and stakeholders are present, the politically smooth option often quietly costs users.
- **Rewarding self-protective documentation** over genuine ownership of a problem.
- **Treating honesty and kindness as alternatives** — the strongest answers in this section are usually both at once.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material covering the four types most relevant to a business relationship manager: cognitive ability, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tests checking pipeline references and stakeholder maps for the inconsistencies that quietly erode trust in data, reasoning through governance rules to find the one missing precondition that actually blocks progress, and sequencing a crowded week by impact and urgency. The numeric section tests the honest, weighted way to combine satisfaction scores, separating percentage points from percentages, converting minutes saved into pounds for an opportunity canvas, and normalising performance figures for changing demand. The verbal section tests reading strategy extracts, minutes, and stakeholder emails with the precision that keeps "reduced" from becoming "decommissioned" and caution from becoming opposition. The situational judgement section tests difficult judgement calls involving frustrated stakeholders, tempting shortcuts, corridor disclosures, and colleagues whose pride is attached to work the organisation has outgrown. Every section maps directly to the skills the role names: business analysis, ownership, service focus, service management framework knowledge, stakeholder relationship management, strategic thinking, and user focus.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as your answer key and your talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more valuable to the candidate — and more informative to you — than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which you administered this assessment alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate you assess for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can turn conversations into pipelines, contradictions into clarity, and strained relationships into working ones — never the whole of that decision.
