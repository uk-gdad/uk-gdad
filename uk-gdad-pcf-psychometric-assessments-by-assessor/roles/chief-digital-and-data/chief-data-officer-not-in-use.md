# Chief Data Officer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel, or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for chief data officer within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. The role sits at the very top of an organisation's data leadership: the postholder ensures the organisation can use data to make better decisions, policies and services, and creates an environment where data protection and privacy are balanced with access, sharing and innovation. Because the decisions this role makes shape strategy, governance, ethics, quality, security and capability across an entire organisation, the assessment material below is demanding, strategic, and firmly grounded in the realities of public sector data leadership.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and judgement a candidate brings to the work. They are widely used across the Civil Service and the wider public sector for recruitment, promotion, development conversations and talent planning. For an executive data leader, they typically cover four areas: cognitive ability (how a candidate reasons with complex, ambiguous information), numeric reasoning (how a candidate interprets budgets, performance metrics and capacity data), verbal reasoning (how a candidate extracts precise meaning from dense policy, regulatory and strategic documents), and situational judgement (how a candidate chooses between plausible courses of action in politically sensitive, high-stakes situations).

This document is organised into four main assessment sections, one for each of those areas. Each section explains what the assessment measures, why it matters specifically for a chief data officer, and then offers a substantial set of items built directly from the role's real duties: defining and leading a data strategy aligned with the wider government data strategy, working with senior leaders to secure support, determining the data and technology the organisation needs, making data easy to use, reuse and share, improving data quality, ensuring ethical and compliant use of data, raising data literacy across the organisation, partnering with cyber security, and building the data skills the organisation needs. Every scenario, table, passage and dilemma is set in a UK government digital and data context, so the item pool is grounded in the job the candidate would actually do.

How should you use this document? Present each section's items to the candidate under the same conditions you would give any other candidate for this role level: the same time limit and the same items, with the worked answers and explanations held back as your answer key and not shown to the candidate until scoring and debrief. Use the preparation tips to plan a fair, consistent session, and the common pitfalls to sharpen your own scoring judgement. Treat the result as one input among several — alongside interview, portfolio, and reference evidence — never as a standalone verdict on a candidate.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes needed for a role, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Unlike a generic intelligence test, every item is built from the kinds of artefacts a chief data officer really handles: data strategies, architecture decision papers, governance board minutes, risk registers, quality dashboards, regulatory correspondence, and cross-government policy documents. The assessment predicts performance by asking a candidate to do, under time pressure, condensed versions of what the job demands every week.

Administer this section as an online, timed test, typically 15 to 30 minutes, with objective scoring against a norm group of comparable senior leaders. Many modern platforms adapt question difficulty based on a candidate's previous answers, and it is worth requesting a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score, so a candidate who works carefully is not penalised relative to one who works fast. If your platform offers a few ungraded practice items first, let every candidate use them so the interface holds no surprises for anyone. Keep the questions used in formal selection close to genuine work content, as they are here, so that what is measured predicts job success rather than test-taking skill.

For a chief data officer, cognitive assessment matters because the role is intellectually integrative. The postholder must hold an organisation-wide vision and strategy for data while spotting a subtle inconsistency in a single governance paper; must reason about trade-offs between data protection and data sharing where both principles are legitimate; must detect the flawed assumption inside a confident business case for a new data platform. This assessment samples exactly these capabilities: pattern recognition across programmes, logical deduction from policies and rules, error checking of board-level artefacts, prioritisation across competing strategic demands, and structured problem solving under ambiguity.

### What it measures

The role summary names duties rather than in-use skill definitions, so the mapping below ties cognitive dimensions to those duties directly.

- **Pattern recognition** maps to defining a vision and strategy for data: a candidate must see recurring themes across departmental data issues, spot where local fixes signal systemic architecture gaps, and recognise when metrics across services are telling one coherent story.
- **Logical deduction** maps to ensuring the organisation uses data ethically and complies with data standards and regulations: given a rule set (UK GDPR principles, government data standards, internal policies), a candidate must derive what is and is not permitted in a novel situation.
- **Error checking** maps to improving the quality of data used across the organisation: the postholder sets the tone for precision, personally reviewing dashboards, definitions and papers where a single wrong figure can misdirect ministers or boards.
- **Prioritisation** maps to leading implementation of the data strategy and working with senior leaders to align priorities: the role constantly sequences initiatives against constrained funding, capability and political attention.
- **Problem solving** maps to determining the data and data technology the organisation needs, working with cyber security to keep data secure yet accessible, and increasing data literacy: each is a multi-constraint problem where the best answer balances several legitimate goods.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) - Pattern recognition in quality metrics**

Four directorates report monthly data quality scores (completeness) for their case records over five months:

- Directorate A: 82, 84, 86, 88, 90
- Directorate B: 91, 90, 91, 90, 91
- Directorate C: 95, 89, 94, 88, 93
- Directorate D: 78, 85, 79, 86, 80

Which directorate's pattern most strongly suggests a recurring process problem tied to alternating reporting periods, rather than steady improvement, stability, or random variation?

A) Directorate A
B) Directorate B
C) Directorate C
D) Directorate D

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** Directorate A shows a steady upward trend (+2 each month), consistent with sustained improvement. Directorate B is stable within one point. Directorate C oscillates but stays in a high band (88-95), which could be moderate random variation. Directorate D alternates sharply and regularly between a high-70s value and a mid-80s value (78, 85, 79, 86, 80), a clean two-period cycle. A regular alternation like this typically indicates something systematic that differs between alternate periods, such as a batch load that runs every other month or two teams taking turns with different processes. This item tests whether a candidate recognises cyclical signatures in quality metrics, which tells a chief data officer where to direct root-cause investigation rather than generic quality training.

**Question 2 (easy) - Logical deduction from a data sharing rule**

A candidate's data sharing policy states: "Personal data may be shared with another public body only if a data sharing agreement is in place AND either (a) the data subjects have been informed via a privacy notice, or (b) a statutory gateway applies." A directorate wants to share personal data with a local authority. A data sharing agreement is signed. No privacy notice covers the sharing, and no statutory gateway applies. Is the sharing permitted under the policy?

A) Yes, because a data sharing agreement is in place
B) No, because neither condition (a) nor (b) is satisfied
C) Yes, provided the local authority is also a public body
D) Cannot be determined from the information given

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule has the structure: agreement AND (a OR b). The agreement is in place, so the first condition holds. But (a) fails (no privacy notice) and (b) fails (no statutory gateway), so the disjunction (a OR b) is false, making the whole conjunction false. The sharing is not permitted. Option A mistakes a necessary condition for a sufficient one. Option C addresses a fact already assumed. This item tests exactly the kind of deduction the role performs when a team asks "we have an agreement, so we can share, right?" — a chief data officer's value often lies in seeing the full logical structure of a rule, not just its most visible clause.

**Question 3 (moderate) - Error checking a board paper**

A paper to a candidate's executive committee contains the following statements:

1. "The organisation holds 412 data sets, of which 60% (247) are catalogued in the enterprise data catalogue."
2. "Of the catalogued data sets, 40% have a named data owner."
3. "Therefore 99 data sets have a named data owner, meaning 24% of all our data sets have accountable ownership."

Which single arithmetic claim is wrong?

A) 60% of 412 is 247
B) 40% of 247 is 99 (rounded)
C) 99 out of 412 is 24%
D) All three claims are arithmetically consistent

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** Check each claim in turn. Claim 1: 412 × 0.6 = 247.2, so 247 is a fair rounding. Claim 2: 247 × 0.4 = 98.8, which rounds to 99 — also fair. Claim 3: 99 ÷ 412 = 0.2403, so 24% is correct. All three claims are arithmetically consistent, so the answer is D. If a candidate selects A, B or C, that suggests they re-computed carelessly or assumed an error must exist rather than checking. The lesson is double-edged: error checking means confirming correctness as much as finding faults, and a candidate who "corrects" an accurate paper would, in the job, damage trust just as surely as one who misses a genuine error. This item tests whether a candidate computes before challenging — assessments at this level deliberately include papers with no error, because real board papers are sometimes right.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Prioritisation under constraint**

A candidate has funding to start exactly two of four initiatives this year:

- Initiative W: Enterprise data catalogue completion (prerequisite for X and Z)
- Initiative X: Automated data quality monitoring (requires W)
- Initiative Y: Data literacy programme for senior leaders (independent)
- Initiative Z: Cross-government data sharing pilot (requires W)

The candidate's strategy commits them to demonstrating cross-government sharing "within two years". Which pair should be started now?

A) X and Z
B) W and Y
C) W and X
D) Y and Z

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** X and Z both require W, so option A and option D each include at least one initiative that cannot start (X or Z without W). That leaves B (W and Y) or C (W and X). Both are feasible, so the tiebreaker is strategic: the two-year sharing commitment needs W finished this year so Z can run next year — both B and C achieve that. C delivers quality monitoring sooner, but Y is independent, meaning it can only ever be done by directly funding it, and building senior leader literacy this year creates the sponsorship needed for Z's cross-government negotiations next year. B sequences dependencies correctly and invests in the enabling condition (leadership support) that the role summary highlights: working with senior leaders to align priorities and secure their support. This item mirrors real executive prioritisation and tests whether a candidate respects hard dependencies first, then chooses by strategic enablement, not by which initiative feels most tangibly "data".

**Question 5 (moderate) - Pattern recognition in incident reports**

Over six months, a candidate's organisation logs these data incidents: (1) spreadsheet of case data emailed to wrong recipient; (2) analyst granted access to a restricted data set without approval; (3) personal data retained past its retention date in a legacy system; (4) spreadsheet extract shared with a contractor lacking a signed agreement; (5) access revocation missed for a leaver; (6) unencrypted extract saved to a personal drive. Which underlying theme best explains the largest number of these incidents?

A) Weak technical security controls in core systems
B) Uncontrolled data extracts and handling outside governed systems
C) Failures in joiner-mover-leaver access processes
D) Inadequate retention scheduling

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Classify each incident: (1) extract emailed — extract handling; (2) access approval failure; (3) retention failure; (4) extract shared without agreement — extract handling; (6) extract on personal drive — extract handling; (5) access process failure. Extract handling accounts for three of six incidents; access processes for two; retention for one. Option B explains the plurality. The cognitive skill this item tests is abstraction: individually, these look like six unrelated mistakes, but a chief data officer must see that half of them share one root cause — data leaving governed systems as ad hoc extracts — which points to a single strategic intervention (governed self-service access to reduce the need for extracts) rather than six separate remediations.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Logical deduction about strategy alignment**

The wider government data strategy requires each department to publish an annual data maturity self-assessment. A candidate's internal policy states: "Any document published externally must first pass information assurance review." Their data maturity self-assessment has not yet passed information assurance review. Which conclusion follows necessarily?

A) The candidate will breach the government data strategy this year
B) The self-assessment must not be published externally yet
C) The information assurance team is a blocker to strategic compliance
D) The self-assessment contains sensitive information

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The internal policy is a conditional: external publication requires prior assurance review. Since the review has not happened, publication is not yet permitted — that is the only necessary conclusion. Option A does not follow: the year has not ended and the review may yet pass. Option C ascribes blame without evidence about the team's speed or behaviour. Option D confuses the purpose of a review with its outcome; documents go through review whether or not they contain sensitive material. This item tests whether a candidate resists the dramatic conclusion (A or C) that executives are constantly pressed toward, in favour of disciplined deduction that distinguishes what must be true from what merely might be.

**Question 7 (hard) - Problem solving with conflicting constraints**

A candidate must recommend a hosting approach for a new analytical data platform. Constraints:

- Cyber security requires all personal data at rest to remain in UK data centres.
- The preferred vendor's machine-learning service runs only in EU regions.
- Analysts need the ML service for a ministerial priority project within six months.
- Anonymised data may leave the UK under the candidate's policy.

Which recommendation best satisfies all constraints?

A) Grant a security exemption to allow personal data in EU regions
B) Drop the ML service and build in-house within six months
C) Anonymise the data in the UK platform, then use the EU-region ML service on the anonymised data
D) Delay the ministerial project until the vendor opens a UK region

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through each option against all four constraints. Option A violates the security constraint by exception rather than solving the problem — and normalises exemptions, a governance cost. Option B likely fails the six-month deadline and duplicates a commodity capability. Option D fails the ministerial deadline outright. Option C uses the explicit permission in the organisation's own policy (anonymised data may leave the UK) to decompose the problem: keep personal data at rest in the UK, transform it there, and send only anonymised data to the EU ML service. Every constraint is satisfied. The generalisable skill this item tests is searching the constraint set for the degree of freedom deliberately built into it — here, the anonymisation clause — rather than treating constraints as a wall requiring an exemption or a delay. A strong candidate's answer will also note what should be validated before finalising: that the anonymisation is robust enough that the data is genuinely no longer personal data, a judgement that would be tested with a data protection officer.

**Question 8 (hard) - Deduction with layered rules**

Three rules govern a candidate's data catalogue:

- Rule 1: Every data set classified OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE must have a named data owner.
- Rule 2: Every data set with a named data owner must have a documented retention schedule.
- Rule 3: No data set with a documented retention schedule may be marked "retention: indefinite" unless a legal hold applies.

Data set K is classified OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE, is marked "retention: indefinite", and no legal hold applies. How many of the three rules does data set K necessarily breach?

A) Exactly one
B) Exactly two
C) At least one, but the exact number cannot be determined
D) All three

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Follow the chain. Rule 1 requires K to have a named owner — we are not told whether it does, so Rule 1 may or may not be breached. If K has an owner, Rule 2 requires a retention schedule — again not stated. Rule 3 is the interesting one: it applies only to data sets with a documented retention schedule. If K lacks a schedule, Rule 3 does not apply to it (though earlier rules are breached); if K has a schedule, Rule 3 is breached because "indefinite" is marked with no legal hold. Trace the cases: if K has an owner and a schedule, only Rule 3 is breached (one breach). If K has an owner but no schedule, Rule 2 is breached (one breach, Rule 3 inapplicable). If K has no owner, Rule 1 is breached, and Rule 2 and 3 are inapplicable or unsatisfiable in ways that depend on unstated facts. In every case at least one rule is breached, but the count varies with facts not given — so C is correct. A candidate who answers D is pattern-matching "everything is wrong here" instead of tracing rule applicability, a distinction that matters enormously when adjudicating compliance disputes — exactly what this item tests.

**Question 9 (hard) - Prioritisation with stakeholder dynamics**

Five items compete for the single remaining slot on next month's executive committee agenda, which a candidate influences:

1. Approval of the data ethics framework (blocked for two months; ethics board frustrated)
2. Funding decision for data quality tooling (deadline in six weeks; next committee is in five weeks)
3. Briefing on a new cross-government data standard (no decision needed)
4. Data breach lessons-learned report (regulator expects evidence of board oversight "in due course")
5. Celebration of the data literacy programme's first cohort (morale value)

Which item most needs THIS month's slot?

A) Item 1, because it has waited longest
B) Item 2, because its deadline precedes the following committee
C) Item 4, because regulators outrank internal matters
D) Item 1 and 4 should be combined into one slot

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test each against the question "what is lost if it waits one month?" Item 2's deadline is in six weeks and the next committee is in five weeks — it can wait and still meet its deadline, though with little margin; note the trap of misreading this timing. Item 3 needs no decision, so it can be circulated in writing. Item 4's regulator expectation is "in due course" — real, but elastic by its own wording. Item 5 can be honoured in many channels. Item 1 has been blocked for two months: an unapproved ethics framework leaves the whole organisation making ethical judgements without an agreed standard, and a frustrated ethics board is a governance relationship deteriorating in real time. Waiting costs the most for Item 1, both substantively and relationally. Option D looks pragmatic but combining an approval item with a lessons-learned report dilutes both and often results in neither being done properly. This item tests marginal analysis: prioritising by what deteriorates if deferred, not by loudness, recency, or apparent seniority of the stakeholder.

**Question 10 (moderate) - Error checking definitions**

A candidate's metadata standard defines: "A data owner is the senior accountable officer for a data set's quality, access decisions and lifecycle. A data steward is the practitioner who operationally maintains quality and metadata on the owner's behalf." A directorate's submission lists: "Data set: Grant applications. Data owner: Grants Casework Team. Data steward: Director of Grants." Which two errors should be flagged?

A) The owner should be an individual, not a team; owner and steward roles appear reversed
B) The steward should be a team, not an individual; the data set name is too vague
C) The owner should be the chief data officer; the steward should be the casework team
D) There are no errors; the submission is compliant

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** The standard defines the owner as "the senior accountable officer" — an accountable individual in a senior role, not a team; accountability assigned to a team is accountability assigned to no one. Second, the seniority is inverted: the Director of Grants is the natural senior accountable officer (owner), while the casework team contains the practitioners who would steward the data day to day. So the two errors are: owner is not an individual, and the roles are reversed — option A. Option C is wrong because the chief data officer should not own every data set; the role builds the ownership system rather than absorbing it, a point directly connected to the duty to ensure data is properly managed by coaching the whole organisation rather than doing everything centrally, which is what this item tests.

**Question 11 (hard) - Problem solving: sequencing a capability build**

A candidate's workforce analysis shows: 3 data architects (need 6), 12 data engineers (need 14), 1 data ethicist (need 2), 40 analysts with basic skills (need 40, but 25 need upskilling in new tooling). Recruitment takes 6 months per specialist hire; upskilling takes 3 months per cohort of 12 or 13 analysts and requires one architect seconded per cohort as trainer. New tooling arrives in 9 months and analysts must be trained before using it. What is the earliest all 25 analysts can be upskilled without delaying tooling adoption, if the work starts now and cohorts run sequentially with one seconded architect?

A) 6 months — both cohorts trained in parallel
B) 6 months — two sequential 3-month cohorts
C) 9 months — training must wait for new architect hires
D) 3 months — one cohort covers all 25 analysts

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** 25 analysts need upskilling; cohorts hold 12 or 13, so two cohorts are needed (12 + 13 = 25). Option D fails because one cohort cannot hold 25. Run sequentially at 3 months each: months 0-3 and 3-6, finishing at month 6 — before the tooling arrives at month 9. Option A gives the right duration for the wrong reason: parallel cohorts would need two seconded architects, and with only 3 architects against a need of 6 the scenario specified seconding just one. Option C wrongly assumes training depends on new hires; the constraint given is one seconded architect, which is already available. The deeper reading, which a strong candidate's answer should surface: with 3 architects against a need for 6, seconding even one is a genuine strategic cost, and this item mirrors a real chief data officer trade-off — sacrificing scarce architecture capacity now to build organisation-wide capability, which the role summary describes as ensuring the organisation has people with the level of data skills it needs.

**Question 12 (hard) - Integrative reasoning across strategy documents**

A candidate's data strategy commits to: (i) "all new services will consume master data from the single customer record by 2027"; (ii) "no new point-to-point data integrations from 2026". A programme launching in 2026 proposes a direct feed from the legacy customer database "as a temporary measure until the single customer record is ready in 2027". The single customer record programme is currently rated amber-red. Which reasoning best identifies the core strategic risk of approving the request?

A) The temporary feed will breach commitment (i) immediately
B) Approving a "temporary" point-to-point feed against commitment (ii), while the target platform is amber-red, creates a precedent likely to multiply and outlive its justification
C) The legacy database may contain poor-quality data
D) Amber-red programmes should be cancelled, removing the dilemma

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A is imprecise: commitment (i) bites in 2027, and a 2026 feed does not breach it immediately. Option C is a real but generic concern, not the core strategic risk. Option D is a non sequitur — amber-red means intervention, not automatic cancellation. Option B integrates all the facts: the request directly contravenes commitment (ii); its justification ("until 2027") depends on a delivery date that the amber-red rating makes doubtful; and exceptions granted while the strategic alternative is weak become templates for the next programme's identical request. Soon the organisation has a new generation of point-to-point integrations, the exact architecture the strategy exists to retire. This item tests second-order reasoning — precedent, incentive, and dependency on uncertain futures — not just first-order rule breaches. Note that recognising the risk does not dictate refusal; approval might still follow with a hard sunset clause and a decommissioning plan. The item tests whether a candidate can name what is truly at stake before negotiating.

**Question 13 (moderate) - Pattern recognition: adoption curves**

Four directorates adopted a candidate's enterprise data catalogue over 12 months. Monthly active users as a percentage of directorate staff: Directorate P rose fast to 60% by month 4 then fell steadily to 20% by month 12. Directorate Q rose slowly but steadily to 45% by month 12. Directorate R spiked to 70% in launch month, fell to 10% by month 3, and stayed there. Directorate S stayed under 5% throughout. Which directorate most likely embedded the catalogue into a routine business process?

A) Directorate P
B) Directorate Q
C) Directorate R
D) Directorate S

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** R's signature — launch spike, rapid collapse — is classic mandated-training usage with no ongoing need. P's pattern — quick rise then steady decline — suggests initial enthusiasm eroding, perhaps because the catalogue was not maintained or did not reward continued use. S never started. Q's slow, steady, sustained growth to a substantial plateau is the signature of usage driven by an embedded process: each month more people encounter the workflow that requires the tool, and they keep using it. This item tests whether a candidate can distinguish genuine embedding from launch theatre, essential to knowing whether "ensure data can be easily used, reused and shared" is actually being achieved — and it changes the intervention: P needs a value-retention fix, R needs a workflow integration, S needs engagement from scratch, Q needs protecting and celebrating.

### Preparation tips

- **Present real artefacts where possible.** A recent governance paper, dashboard or strategy, interrogated the way these items do — what rule structure is really being applied, which figure would be re-computed, what pattern a metric is showing — is the kind of stimulus this section rewards, and gives every candidate for this role level the same calibrated material.
- **Note whether a candidate paces differently across item types.** Logical deduction items punish skimming — a strong candidate will map ANDs, ORs and "only if" clauses explicitly. Pattern items usually yield to a first structured look; do not read a quick answer there as carelessness.
- **Score "no error" as a legitimate finding.** As Question 3 shows, some items contain no error. A candidate who computes and confirms correctness is demonstrating the skill, not failing to find a fault expected of them.
- **Give the sitting proper conditions.** With 15 to 30 minutes, no single question deserves a disproportionate share; the same discipline applies to how you administer as to how a candidate should answer.
- **Keep the sitting professional.** A quiet room, a good screen, and an alert candidate measurably improve the quality of the signal you get — treat the assessment with the same seriousness you would a select committee appearance.

### Common pitfalls

- **Rewarding an answer built from outside experience rather than the material given.** Senior candidates are often tempted to import context ("in my department we would..."). This assessment scores reasoning from the presented facts; experience should sharpen a candidate's reading, not replace it — mark down an answer that substitutes one for the other.
- **Missing a necessary-versus-sufficient confusion in a candidate's reasoning.** Policy-style items frequently hinge on this, as in Question 2. Check that a candidate reads "only if" as a gate, not a permission.
- **Rewarding pattern-matching to the dramatic conclusion.** Senior leaders are trained to spot risk, and items sometimes offer alarming options (Question 6's "you will breach the strategy") that do not logically follow — do not let confident alarm substitute for sound deduction when scoring.
- **Letting one multi-layered deduction item consume disproportionate scoring weight.** A single item like Question 8 can be genuinely hard; note where a candidate spends unusual time or struggles, but weigh it against overall performance rather than anchoring on it.
- **Missing the "no error" possibility when checking a candidate's error-checking answer.** Confirming correctness is part of the skill being measured — do not assume a wrong answer means the candidate missed a real error if in fact there was none to find.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates a candidate's ability to analyse, interpret and make sound decisions using numerical data drawn from the real demands of the role. The emphasis is on business mathematics — percentages, ratios, budget movements, unit costs, capacity figures and cost-benefit estimations — rather than abstract formulae. Item inputs are the sorts of tables, charts and financial summaries a chief data officer sees weekly: data programme budgets, platform capacity and consumption figures, data quality performance metrics, workforce and training numbers, and benefits-realisation projections.

Administer this section under a strict time limit, often allowing around a minute to ninety seconds per item, and decide in advance and apply consistently whether a calculator is allowed — most assessments at this level permit or provide one, because the test prioritises interpretation and decision-making over mental arithmetic. Accuracy under time pressure is the point: the section simulates a pre-board briefing where a candidate must extract the one number that matters from a busy table before the meeting starts.

For a chief data officer, numeric reasoning is not delegated arithmetic; it is the credibility layer beneath every strategic claim the role makes. When a candidate tells an executive committee that consolidating three legacy data platforms will save 30% of run costs, or that the data quality programme has cut error-driven rework by a measurable amount, or that the training budget covers the analyst upskilling a workforce plan requires, they are personally accountable for those numbers surviving scrutiny from finance directors, the National Audit Office and select committees. This section measures whether a candidate can move quickly and correctly between raw figures and executive conclusions.

### What it measures

Because the role summary provides duties rather than in-use skill definitions, each numeric dimension below maps to a duty:

- **Budget and cost analysis** maps to determining the data and data technology the organisation needs and the strategy for sourcing it: comparing vendor pricing models, total cost of ownership, and build-versus-buy economics.
- **Percentages and performance metrics** map to improving the quality of data used across the organisation: quality scores, error rates, and improvement trajectories are the everyday currency of a quality programme.
- **Ratios and capacity figures** map to ensuring data can be easily used, reused and shared: platform utilisation, storage growth, API call volumes and concurrency limits determine whether "easily used" is real.
- **Workforce arithmetic** maps to increasing data literacy and ensuring the organisation has people with the level of data skills it needs: cohort sizes, training costs per head, attrition, and recruitment pipelines.
- **Cost-benefit estimation** maps to leading the organisation in implementing the data strategy: sequencing initiatives requires comparing projected benefits against costs and risk.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) - Percentage change in a data programme budget**

A candidate's data programme budget was £8.4 million last year. This year it is £9.66 million. What is the percentage increase?

A) 12%
B) 15%
C) 18%
D) 21%

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Increase = £9.66m − £8.4m = £1.26m. Percentage increase = 1.26 ÷ 8.4 = 0.15 = 15%. A quick sanity check: 10% of 8.4 is 0.84, and 5% is 0.42; together 1.26 — confirming 15%. Executives are often given the increase pre-computed in papers; this item tests the ten-second verification habit, because a briefing that says "an 18% uplift" when the true figure is 15% will be caught by a finance director if the candidate does not catch it first.

**Question 2 (easy) - Data quality improvement**

A candidate's master customer record had 42,000 duplicate entries at the start of the year. The data quality programme removed 60% of duplicates by June, then removed 25% of the remaining duplicates by December. How many duplicates remain at year end?

A) 6,300
B) 12,600
C) 10,500
D) 15,750

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** After June: 42,000 × (1 − 0.60) = 42,000 × 0.40 = 16,800 remaining. By December, 25% of the *remaining* are removed: 16,800 × (1 − 0.25) = 16,800 × 0.75 = 12,600. The trap is compounding versus adding: 60% + 25% is not 85% of the original. Option A (6,300) results from wrongly applying 85% removal to the original. Sequential percentage reductions apply to successively smaller bases — a pattern that recurs in every multi-phase remediation programme a candidate would ever report on, and this item tests whether they hold that distinction under time pressure.

**Question 3 (moderate) - Platform cost comparison for sourcing strategy**

A candidate is comparing two sourcing options for the organisation's analytical data platform over three years:

- Option 1 (cloud vendor): £35,000 per month platform fee, plus storage at £180 per terabyte per month. Storage starts at 200 TB and grows by 50 TB at the start of each year (so year 1: 200 TB, year 2: 250 TB, year 3: 300 TB).
- Option 2 (managed service): flat £1.0 million per year, all storage included.

What is the total three-year cost difference, and which option is cheaper?

A) Option 1 is cheaper by £120,000
B) Option 2 is cheaper by £120,000
C) Option 1 is cheaper by £240,000
D) The options cost the same

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option 1 platform fees: £35,000 × 36 months = £1,260,000. Storage: year 1: 200 TB × £180 × 12 = £432,000; year 2: 250 × £180 × 12 = £540,000; year 3: 300 × £180 × 12 = £648,000. Storage total = £1,620,000. Option 1 total = £1,260,000 + £1,620,000 = £2,880,000. Option 2 total = £1,000,000 × 3 = £3,000,000. Difference = £3,000,000 − £2,880,000 = £120,000, so Option 1 is cheaper by £120,000 — option A. The realistic trap is the growing storage: a candidate who prices all three years at 200 TB understates Option 1 by £216,000 and overstates its advantage. A strong candidate's answer would also weigh factors the arithmetic omits — exit costs, price-rise clauses, and the risk that storage grows faster than 50 TB per year, which would erode Option 1's advantage at £2,160 per additional terabyte per year. This item tests disciplined multi-step cost modelling and, at the executive level, knowing which assumptions the model is most sensitive to.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Training budget per head**

A candidate's data literacy programme must train 1,200 staff. A vendor charges £15,000 per cohort of up to 25 people, or £680 per person for individual online licences. Mixing is allowed. What is the lowest total cost to train exactly 1,200 staff?

A) £720,000
B) £816,000
C) £734,400
D) £700,000

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Cohort cost per head at full capacity: £15,000 ÷ 25 = £600, cheaper than £680 per individual licence. So fill cohorts: 1,200 ÷ 25 = 48 exact cohorts. Cost = 48 × £15,000 = £720,000. Because 1,200 divides evenly by 25, no partial cohort arises, and mixing with licences cannot beat £600 per head. Option B is 1,200 × £680 (all licences). Option C mixes unnecessarily. This is procurement arithmetic a candidate would use whenever negotiating enterprise training or tooling, and this item tests whether they reduce pricing models to cost per unit at realistic fill rates and check whether volumes hit the pricing breakpoints exactly.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Storage growth and capacity ceiling**

A candidate's data platform currently stores 480 TB and storage grows at 15% per year, compounding. The platform's contracted ceiling is 800 TB. During which year of growth does storage first exceed the ceiling?

A) Year 3
B) Year 4
C) Year 5
D) Year 6

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compound at 15%: year 1: 480 × 1.15 = 552 TB. Year 2: 552 × 1.15 = 634.8 TB. Year 3: 634.8 × 1.15 = 730.02 TB. Year 4: 730.02 × 1.15 = 839.52 TB — first breach of 800 TB. So the ceiling is exceeded during year 4. The executive point this item tests: compounding growth breaches ceilings sooner than linear intuition suggests (linear 15% of 480 = 72 TB per year would suggest breach midway through year 5). Contract renegotiations take a year or more in public procurement, so a candidate who can do this projection mentally is showing that "we have plenty of headroom" actually means "we must start commercial action now" — a strong candidate's answer should draw that conclusion.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Interpreting a quality metrics table**

Error rates per 10,000 records across three core data sets, measured in two quarters:

| Data set | Q1 errors | Q2 errors | Records (Q2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payments | 84 | 63 | 2,400,000 |
| Casework | 120 | 102 | 1,500,000 |
| Workforce | 45 | 45 | 600,000 |

Which data set achieved the largest *percentage* reduction in its error rate, and how many actual errors does its Q2 rate imply?

A) Payments; 15,120 errors
B) Casework; 15,300 errors
C) Payments; 63 errors
D) Workforce; 2,700 errors

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Percentage reductions: Payments: (84 − 63) ÷ 84 = 21 ÷ 84 = 25%. Casework: (120 − 102) ÷ 120 = 18 ÷ 120 = 15%. Workforce: 0%. Payments wins with 25%. Actual Q2 errors for Payments: rate is per 10,000 records, so 2,400,000 ÷ 10,000 = 240 blocks; 240 × 63 = 15,120 errors. Option C mistakes the rate for the count — the most common misreading of normalised metrics. Option B correctly computes Casework's count (150 × 102 = 15,300) but Casework's percentage improvement is smaller. This item tests both framings a candidate would routinely need when reporting quality improvements to a board: the rate for fair comparison, the count for operational impact.

**Question 7 (hard) - Benefits realisation ratio**

A candidate's data strategy business case projected £5.2 million of annual benefits once fully implemented, for £13 million of one-off investment. Two years in, £9.1 million has been spent (70% of budget) and realised annual benefits are £2.86 million. If benefits realisation continues at the same rate *per pound spent*, what annual benefit should be projected at full spend, and how does it compare with the original projection?

A) £4.09 million; 21% below projection
B) £5.2 million; on target
C) £3.9 million; 25% below projection
D) £4.7 million; 10% below projection

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Benefit per pound spent so far: £2.86m ÷ £9.1m = £0.3143 of annual benefit per pound. At full spend of £13m: 0.3143 × 13 = £4.086m ≈ £4.09m projected annual benefit. Compare with £5.2m original: shortfall = (5.2 − 4.09) ÷ 5.2 = 1.11 ÷ 5.2 = 21.3% ≈ 21% below. A useful cross-check: spend is at 70% and benefits are at 2.86 ÷ 5.2 = 55% of target — benefits are lagging spend, so a below-projection outcome is directionally right, ruling out B immediately. This item tests arithmetic that a chief data officer is regularly challenged on at investment committees; a candidate who can produce the "benefit per pound" framing on the spot is demonstrating exactly the skill that changes such a conversation from advocacy to evidence.

**Question 8 (hard) - Workforce planning with attrition**

A candidate employs 60 data professionals. Annual attrition is 10%. Their strategy requires 75 data professionals in post at the end of two years. Recruitment happens at the end of each year (after attrition is applied to those in post during the year). If the same number of people is recruited at the end of each year, how many must be recruited per year (rounded up to whole people)?

A) 8 per year
B) 11 per year
C) 14 per year
D) 17 per year

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Let R be the annual intake. End of year 1: 60 × 0.9 + R = 54 + R. During year 2, that whole cohort experiences 10% attrition, then the year-2 intake arrives: (54 + R) × 0.9 + R = 48.6 + 0.9R + R = 48.6 + 1.9R. Set this at or above the target: 48.6 + 1.9R ≥ 75, so 1.9R ≥ 26.4, giving R ≥ 13.89 — rounded up, 14 per year. Verify: intake of 14 gives 48.6 + 26.6 = 75.2 in post, meeting the target; intake of 11 gives only 69.5. The trap in this item is forgetting that the people recruited at the end of year 1 are themselves exposed to attrition during year 2 — a candidate who models end of year 2 as 48.6 + 2R also lands on 14, but with less margin, and with different attrition figures the shortcut produces material errors. The wider lesson this item tests: workforce projections are exquisitely sensitive to modelling assumptions (when intake lands, who attrites), so a strong candidate's answer should note that when HR presents a model supporting a claim that the organisation will have the data skills it needs, the assumptions should be questioned before the answer.

**Question 9 (moderate) - API usage ratios**

A candidate's data sharing platform serves three consumer groups. Monthly API calls: internal services 4.2 million, other government departments 1.4 million, approved external researchers 0.4 million. Platform costs of £180,000 per month are allocated by call volume. How much should other government departments be charged per month, and what is that as a cost per thousand calls?

A) £42,000; £30 per thousand calls
B) £60,000; £42.86 per thousand calls
C) £42,000; £3 per thousand calls
D) £36,000; £25.71 per thousand calls

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total calls = 4.2m + 1.4m + 0.4m = 6.0 million. Departments' share = 1.4 ÷ 6.0 = 23.33%. Charge = £180,000 × (1.4/6.0) = £42,000. Cost per thousand calls = £42,000 ÷ 1,400 thousand = £30. Option C makes the classic units error, dividing by 14,000 rather than 1,400 (calls in thousands). Cross-charging for shared data services is a live issue whenever a chief data officer makes data "easily used, reused and shared" across government, and this item tests whether a candidate can build a defensible, transparent unit-cost model — often the difference between other departments adopting a platform and building their own.

**Question 10 (moderate) - Weighted data maturity score**

A candidate's data maturity assessment scores five dimensions, weighted: governance 30%, quality 25%, architecture 20%, skills 15%, ethics 10%. This year's scores (out of 5): governance 3.2, quality 2.8, architecture 3.6, skills 2.4, ethics 4.0. What is the weighted overall score?

A) 3.20
B) 3.08
C) 3.00
D) 3.14

**Answer: D**

**Explanation:** Compute each contribution: governance 3.2 × 0.30 = 0.96; quality 2.8 × 0.25 = 0.70; architecture 3.6 × 0.20 = 0.72; skills 2.4 × 0.15 = 0.36; ethics 4.0 × 0.10 = 0.40. Sum step by step: 0.96 + 0.70 = 1.66; + 0.72 = 2.38; + 0.36 = 2.74; + 0.40 = 3.14. The weighted overall score is 3.14, option D. The main distractor is option A (3.20), which is the simple unweighted average (16.0 ÷ 5) — the error of ignoring the weights entirely. Weighted scores of exactly this kind drive the annual maturity self-assessment an organisation reports against the government data strategy, and board members will ask "why did the overall score move?" This item tests whether a candidate practises the contribution-by-contribution layout, which both prevents errors and prepares the explanation the role would need to give. A strong candidate's answer also notices what the decomposition reveals strategically: skills (2.4) is the weakest dimension, and lifting it by one point would add 0.15 to the overall score — precise ammunition for a capability investment case.

**Question 11 (hard) - Cost of poor quality**

Analysis shows 6% of the 250,000 casework decisions made annually rely on records with a critical data error. Of those affected decisions, 30% require rework at an average cost of £220, and 5% are appealed at an average cost of £3,100. What is the total annual cost of this data quality problem?

A) £990,000
B) £3,315,000
C) £2,325,000
D) £1,222,500

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Affected decisions: 250,000 × 0.06 = 15,000. Rework: 15,000 × 0.30 = 4,500 cases × £220 = £990,000. Appeals: 15,000 × 0.05 = 750 cases × £3,100 = £2,325,000. Total = £990,000 + £2,325,000 = £3,315,000. Options A and C are the two components individually — a trap for a candidate who stops after one branch of the calculation. This "cost of poor quality" arithmetic is among the most powerful tools a chief data officer has: converting an abstract quality score into pounds is usually what unlocks executive support for quality investment, turning "we should improve completeness" into "this defect costs us £3.3 million a year and the fix costs £800,000" — this item tests whether a candidate completes both branches of the calculation.

**Question 12 (hard) - Build versus buy break-even**

A vendor data catalogue costs £250,000 per year in licences plus £150,000 one-off implementation. Building in-house costs £900,000 one-off plus £80,000 per year to run. After how many complete years of operation does the in-house option become cheaper (cumulative cost, counting the one-off costs at year 0)?

A) After 3 years
B) After 4 years
C) After 5 years
D) After 6 years

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Cumulative vendor cost after n years: 150,000 + 250,000n. Cumulative build cost: 900,000 + 80,000n. Set build below vendor: 900,000 + 80,000n < 150,000 + 250,000n → 750,000 < 170,000n → n > 4.41. The first complete year satisfying this is year 5. Verify: at year 4, vendor = £1,150,000, build = £1,220,000 (vendor still cheaper); at year 5, vendor = £1,400,000, build = £1,300,000 (build now cheaper). So C. In a sourcing strategy role this break-even framing needs one more executive layer: five years exceeds a typical spend-approval horizon and assumes the build estimate is right; public sector build overruns commonly erase paper break-evens. This item tests whether a candidate can produce the threshold arithmetic and, separately, judge how the risk should be priced either side of it.

**Question 13 (moderate) - Reading a budget variance table**

A candidate's data directorate's quarterly report shows:

| Programme | Budget (£k) | Actual (£k) | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data platform | 1,850 | 2,072 | +12% |
| Quality programme | 640 | 576 | −10% |
| Literacy programme | 300 | 285 | −5% |
| Governance office | 410 | 449 | ? |

What is the missing variance for the governance office, and what is the overall variance across all four programmes?

A) +9.5%; +5.7%
B) +9.5%; +4.5%
C) +8.7%; +5.7%
D) +9.5%; +6.3%

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Governance variance: (449 − 410) ÷ 410 = 39 ÷ 410 = 0.0951 ≈ +9.5%. Overall: total budget = 1,850 + 640 + 300 + 410 = 3,200. Total actual = 2,072 + 576 + 285 + 449 = 3,382. Variance = (3,382 − 3,200) ÷ 3,200 = 182 ÷ 3,200 = 0.0569 ≈ +5.7%. So A. Note that the four percentage variances cannot be averaged: (12 − 10 − 5 + 9.5) ÷ 4 = +1.6% — percentages on different bases never average simply, a mistake that materially misstates the position. This item tests whether a candidate notices where the variance concentrates — the platform's +12% (£222k) dominates the total — since knowing this instantly is what turns a table into a narrative for the overspend conversation with finance.

**Question 14 (easy) - Proportion of records shared**

Of 3.6 million records in a candidate's organisation's core data sets, 2.7 million are available through the internal data sharing service. What percentage is not yet available?

A) 33%
B) 25%
C) 75%
D) 20%

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unavailable records: 3.6m − 2.7m = 0.9m. Proportion: 0.9 ÷ 3.6 = 0.25 = 25%. The distractor 33% comes from computing 0.9 ÷ 2.7 — dividing by the wrong base. "Percentage of what?" is the single most valuable reflex this item tests: the same two numbers yield 25% or 33% depending on the base, and in a briefing the difference between "a quarter of our data is not yet shareable" and "a third" is the difference between an accurate and a misleading message about progress on making data easy to use, reuse and share.

**Question 15 (hard) - Prioritising quality investment by expected return**

A candidate has £600,000 to invest in data quality remediation and three candidate data sets. For each, analysts estimate the annual cost of poor quality (CoPQ), the remediation cost, and the fraction of CoPQ the remediation would remove:

| Data set | Annual CoPQ | Remediation cost | CoPQ removed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer master | £1,800,000 | £400,000 | 40% |
| Payments reference | £900,000 | £200,000 | 70% |
| Property records | £1,200,000 | £300,000 | 50% |

Funding whole projects only, which combination maximises total annual CoPQ removed within budget?

A) Customer master + Payments reference
B) Customer master + Property records
C) Payments reference + Property records
D) Customer master alone

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute annual CoPQ removed per project: customer master 1,800,000 × 0.40 = £720,000; payments reference 900,000 × 0.70 = £630,000; property records 1,200,000 × 0.50 = £600,000. Affordable pairs within £600,000: customer master + payments reference costs £600,000 exactly and removes £720,000 + £630,000 = £1,350,000. Customer master + property records costs £700,000 — over budget. Payments + property costs £500,000 and removes £1,230,000. So option A wins, removing £1,350,000 annually. Note the tempting shortcut of ranking by benefit-per-pound (payments: £3.15 per £1; property: £2.00; customer master: £1.80) — greedy ranking would pick payments then property for £1,230,000, missing the better combination. This item tests whether a candidate checks affordable combinations rather than just unit ratios: a discipline directly transferable to annual portfolio prioritisation, where the best portfolio is not always built from the individually most "efficient" projects.

### Preparation tips

- **Build your own numbers into the calibration.** Where you can, base items on your organisation's real finance pack or quality dashboard figures, so the exercise is grounded and the candidate's fluency with real figures is genuinely tested.
- **Confirm the four workhorse operations before scoring.** Percentage change, compound growth, weighted averages, and unit-cost comparisons cover most executive-level numeric items — knowing them lets you judge a candidate's working quickly.
- **Check that a candidate names the base before dividing.** Most wrong answers are right arithmetic on the wrong base; a candidate's working should show "percentage of what?" being asked explicitly.
- **Note whether a candidate estimates before computing.** A candidate who estimates first (roughly 15% of 8.4 is about 1.3) and only then computes precisely is showing a habit worth crediting, especially where estimate and calculation would flag a catch.
- **Credit the decomposition layout on multi-step items.** Writing each component on its own line, as in the cost-of-poor-quality item, prevents dropped branches and matches how a candidate would justify numbers to a board.
- **Watch for scale-qualifier handling.** £k, per 10,000 records, millions of calls — these are where senior candidates slip under pressure. Check that a candidate circles or notes the units before answering.

### Common pitfalls

- **Missing a scale-qualifier misread.** Confusing monthly with annual figures, or ignoring qualifiers like "in thousands" or "per 10,000 records", as in Question 6. At executive level the items assume a candidate will be given busy tables; the errors are in the labels, not the arithmetic, so check the candidate's reading of labels specifically.
- **Missing an averaged-percentages error.** As Question 13 shows, variance percentages cannot simply be averaged. This error survives into real board papers depressingly often; the items test for it deliberately, so watch for it in a candidate's working.
- **Accepting a partial total as complete.** Multi-branch calculations (rework plus appeals; platform fee plus storage) offer partial totals as distractors. Check whether a candidate included every component before accepting an answer.
- **Rewarding over-calculation.** Sometimes estimation or a directional cross-check answers the question faster than full computation, as in Question 7 where the spend-versus-benefit ratio ruled out "on target" instantly — a candidate who does this is showing strength, not shortcutting.
- **Letting one multi-stage item dominate a live sitting.** A single cost model can devour five minutes; note candidates who bank the quick percentage items first and return, versus those who lose time to one item.
- **Missing a compounding-versus-adding error.** Sequential percentage changes multiply; they never add. Questions 2 and 5 both turn on this — check specifically for it when a candidate's answer is close but wrong.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well a candidate understands, analyses and draws correct conclusions from written business information, mirroring the exact reading demands of the role. For a chief data officer, that means dense passages of the kind that cross the desk daily: data protection legislation summaries, cross-government data standards, regulator correspondence, data sharing agreements, strategy documents, audit findings, and escalation emails from directors. A candidate answers based strictly on the text provided, ignoring outside knowledge — a discipline that is harder for experts, because they know so much that is not on the page.

The classic format presents a passage followed by statements a candidate must judge **True** (the passage logically supports it), **False** (the passage contradicts it), or **Cannot Say** (the passage provides insufficient information either way). Multiple-choice comprehension items — "which of the following best summarises..." — are usually mixed in. Time the section tightly, often under a minute per question, to simulate the reality of absorbing a briefing pack in the minutes before a meeting.

For this role, this assessment matters acutely. A chief data officer is the organisation's authority on what data standards and regulations actually require — not what people assume they require. The difference between "the regulation requires X" and "the guidance recommends X" changes what can be mandated. The difference between "the department must" and "the department should" changes a compliance position. Misreading a single modal verb in a data sharing gateway can turn lawful sharing into a reportable breach. Verbal precision is also the heart of the role's influence: when briefing an executive committee, a summary of a 40-page policy must be both faithful and short, and this section measures exactly that faithful compression.

### What it measures

Mapped to the duties in the role summary:

- **Extracting rules from regulatory text** maps to ensuring the organisation uses data ethically and complies with data standards and regulations: the role must read legislation-adjacent text with legal-adjacent precision.
- **Distinguishing stated fact from inference** maps to working with senior leaders to align priorities: leaders will quote documents selectively, and the role must know what a document actually commits anyone to.
- **Comprehending strategy and standards documents** maps to defining a data vision aligned with wider government data strategy: alignment starts with accurate reading of what the wider strategy actually says.
- **Reading agreements precisely** maps to ensuring data can be easily used, reused and shared: sharing operates through agreements whose exact wording determines what is permitted.
- **Interpreting audit and security findings** maps to working with cyber security to keep data secure while accessible: security reports mix findings, risks and recommendations, and conflating them distorts decisions.

### Practice questions

**Passage 1 (for Questions 1-4): Extract from a draft internal data sharing policy**

"Personal data may be shared between directorates without a new data sharing agreement where the sharing is within the purposes recorded in the organisation's record of processing activities. Where sharing extends beyond those recorded purposes, a data protection impact assessment must be completed before sharing begins, and the Data Protection Officer must be consulted. Sharing with bodies outside the organisation always requires a data sharing agreement, except where sharing is required by law. The Chief Data Officer may approve exceptions to this policy in writing, save that no exception may be granted to the requirement to consult the Data Protection Officer where a data protection impact assessment identifies a high residual risk."

**Question 1 (easy)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "Sharing personal data between directorates always requires a data sharing agreement."

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage states directly that personal data "may be shared between directorates without a new data sharing agreement" where sharing falls within recorded purposes. Since at least one circumstance exists in which no agreement is required, the word "always" makes the statement false. Note the reasoning pattern: a universal claim ("always") is falsified by a single textual counterexample. This item tests whether a candidate checks whether the text truly closes every case, since assessment writers use universal quantifiers deliberately to probe this.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "If a directorate wants to share data for a purpose not in the record of processing activities, the Data Protection Officer must be consulted."

**Answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage says that where sharing "extends beyond those recorded purposes, a data protection impact assessment must be completed before sharing begins, and the Data Protection Officer must be consulted." The statement paraphrases this faithfully: a purpose not in the record is sharing beyond recorded purposes, triggering both obligations. The answer is True on the text alone. Precision point: the question asks only about consultation, one of the two conjoined requirements, and a conjunction guarantees each of its parts — this item tests whether a candidate holds that logical structure.

**Question 3 (hard)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "The Chief Data Officer may exempt a directorate from completing a data protection impact assessment for sharing beyond recorded purposes."

**Answer: True**

**Explanation:** The exception power reads: "The Chief Data Officer may approve exceptions to this policy in writing, save that no exception may be granted to the requirement to consult the Data Protection Officer where a data protection impact assessment identifies a high residual risk." The only carve-out from the exception power concerns consulting the DPO in the high-risk case. The requirement to complete an impact assessment is part of "this policy" and is not carved out, so on the text the Chief Data Officer may except it. A candidate may answer Cannot Say here, importing real-world doubt about whether such an exemption would be lawful under UK GDPR — a reasonable professional instinct, but the question asks what this policy text permits. This item tests the discipline of reading what is written, not what should have been written — and in the job, spotting that this draft permits something questionable is precisely the comment a chief data officer would send back to the drafters, which is worth noting favourably at debrief even where the candidate's chosen answer differs.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "Sharing required by law with an external body must be recorded in the record of processing activities."

**Answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage exempts legally required external sharing from the data sharing agreement requirement, but says nothing about whether such sharing must be recorded in the record of processing activities. Recording obligations for this case are simply not addressed. Cannot Say is correct: the text neither supports nor contradicts the statement. This item tests whether a candidate can locate the exact sentence that would need to exist for the answer to be True or False — if none exists, the answer is Cannot Say, however plausible the statement sounds.

**Passage 2 (for Questions 5-8): Extract from a cross-government data standards bulletin**

"From April, all departments publishing statistics derived from administrative data should adopt the harmonised geography standard. Departments that cannot adopt the standard by April must publish a statement explaining their timeline for adoption. Early adopters have reported that mapping legacy records to the new geography codes required, on average, four months of effort, though one large department completed the work in seven weeks by using automated matching tools. The standards authority intends to consult later this year on making adoption mandatory for all published statistics, including those derived from survey data."

**Question 5 (easy)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "From April, adopting the harmonised geography standard is mandatory for statistics derived from administrative data."

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The bulletin says departments "should" adopt the standard — a recommendation — and explicitly provides for departments that "cannot adopt the standard by April", requiring only a published statement of their timeline. Both features contradict mandatory status; a mandate is precisely what the authority "intends to consult later this year on making". The should/must distinction is the single most consequential reading skill this item tests in standards work: a chief data officer will often be the person correcting a briefing that inflates a "should" into a "must", or deflates a "must" into a "should".

**Question 6 (moderate)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "Automated matching tools reduce the effort of mapping legacy records for most departments."

**Answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports one department's experience: it "completed the work in seven weeks by using automated matching tools", against an average of four months. That is a single case, and the passage does not even establish that the tools caused the speed (the department may have had cleaner data). Generalising to "most departments" goes well beyond the text. This is the inference trap in its purest form: a vivid example invites a general conclusion the passage never states. This item tests the same critical eye a chief data officer applies when a vendor cites one department's success as proof their tool will transform another's.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "The standards authority will make the standard mandatory for survey-derived statistics this year."

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says the authority "intends to consult later this year on making adoption mandatory". An intention to consult on a change is not a decision to make the change, and certainly not a commitment to complete it this year. The statement asserts a future fact the passage does not support — and the passage's wording ("intends to consult") positively indicates the decision has not been taken, which contradicts the confident "will make". Some scoring schemes would accept Cannot Say here; the stronger reading is False because the statement misrepresents a consultation intention as a settled decision. In either marking scheme, recognising the gap between "intends to consult on X" and "will do X" is the tested skill — a gap that matters every time leaders are briefed on what forthcoming regulation "will" require.

**Question 8 (easy)**

Which of the following best summarises the bulletin's main message?

A) The harmonised geography standard has failed to achieve adoption
B) Departments are expected to adopt a new geography standard from April, with transparency required from those who cannot, and mandatory status under consideration
C) Automated matching tools are now required for statistics production
D) Survey-derived statistics are exempt from all geography standards

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B captures all three load-bearing elements: the April expectation ("should adopt"), the transparency requirement for non-adopters (published timeline statement), and the possible future mandate (consultation intention). Option A contradicts the reported early-adopter progress. Option C inflates one department's tooling choice into a requirement. Option D overreads: survey statistics are outside the current expectation but the consultation would bring them in, which is not an exemption "from all geography standards". This item tests identifying every element that carries the passage's meaning — the same skill as writing a one-paragraph board summary of a long standards document.

**Passage 3 (for Questions 9-11): Escalation email from a director**

"Subject: Urgent — analytics access blocked. My performance team's access to the case management extract was revoked on Friday by the security team, citing the new access control standard. We have monthly performance returns due to the executive committee in nine days. I understand the standard allows continued access where a service need is documented and approved by the information asset owner; my team documented the service need in October. I am asking you to direct the security team to restore access today. If access is not restored this week, the returns will be late for the first time in three years, and I will need to note the cause in my submission."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "The performance team's documented service need has been approved by the information asset owner."

**Answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** Read the director's own words precisely: the standard "allows continued access where a service need is documented AND approved by the information asset owner; my team documented the service need in October." The director attests to documentation, but says nothing about approval — the second, conjoined condition. The email neither confirms nor denies approval, so Cannot Say. This precise gap is very plausibly why access was revoked, and a candidate who spots it should recognise that it changes the whole response to the escalation: instead of directing security to restore access, a strong candidate's answer would ask one question — "was the documented need approved by the asset owner?" This item tests whether a careful reading of an angry email reveals that the sender's own account contains the answer.

**Question 10 (easy)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "The performance returns have been late before."

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The director writes that if access is not restored, "the returns will be late for the first time in three years". "For the first time in three years" means the returns have not been late at any point in the last three years — but be careful: it also implies they may have been late more than three years ago. Does the statement "have been late before" contradict the passage? Strictly, the passage only rules out lateness within three years; before that, the text is silent. On the most rigorous reading, the answer would be Cannot Say for lateness more than three years ago. However, the natural reading of "late for the first time in three years" in context — a director emphasising an unbroken record — supports False as the intended answer in most test batteries, and the phrase "for the first time" is doing the work. This item illustrates a real feature of verbal tests: occasionally two readings are defensible, and the better-supported, more natural reading wins. A candidate who articulates why one reading is stronger than the other, even where their final answer differs, is showing the skill this item is designed to surface.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Which of the following most accurately characterises what the director is asking the chief data officer to do?

A) Approve the team's service need as information asset owner
B) Overrule the access control standard permanently
C) Instruct the security team to restore access immediately
D) Extend the performance returns deadline

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** The operative sentence is: "I am asking you to direct the security team to restore access today." That is option C. Option A misidentifies the request — the director never asks the chief data officer to act as asset owner, and nothing says they hold that role for this data set. Option B overstates: restoring one team's access is not permanently overruling the standard. Option D is not requested anywhere. Executives are routinely asked to act at speed on escalations; accurately isolating the actual request — as distinct from the pressure, context and implied threats around it — is the first step of a sound response, and it is exactly what this item measures.

**Passage 4 (for Questions 12-14): Extract from an internal audit report on data management**

"The organisation has made measurable progress in data governance since the previous audit. Data ownership is now assigned for 78% of critical data sets, against 31% two years ago. However, ownership assignment has not consistently translated into active management: fewer than half of assigned owners could describe their responsibilities when interviewed, and quality metrics exist for only 40% of critical data sets. The audit found no instances of unlawful data processing. The retention schedule remains unimplemented in two legacy systems, which together hold an estimated 1.9 million records past their disposal date. We rate the overall control environment as 'requires improvement', principally because monitoring controls have not kept pace with the expansion of ownership assignment."

**Question 12 (easy)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "Data ownership assignment has more than doubled in two years."

**Answer: True**

**Explanation:** Ownership is "now assigned for 78% of critical data sets, against 31% two years ago". Since 31% × 2 = 62%, and 78% exceeds 62%, assignment has more than doubled. The verification is arithmetic embedded in text — a hybrid skill this item uses because executive reading is full of quantified claims. This item tests whether a candidate does the small sum; "more than doubled" and "nearly doubled" are cheap phrases until someone checks them, and a chief data officer is that someone.

**Question 13 (moderate)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "The audit found that some data processing was unlawful."

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The report states plainly: "The audit found no instances of unlawful data processing." The statement directly contradicts this, so it is False. The distinction worth pausing on: the audit finding "no instances" is a statement about what the audit found, not a guarantee that no unlawful processing exists — but the question asks what the audit found, and on that the text is explicit. Had the statement read "no unlawful processing occurred in the organisation", the answer would have been Cannot Say, because an audit's not finding something does not establish its absence. This item tests sensitivity to a one-word-level difference between question stems that flips the answer.

**Question 14 (hard)**

Which of the following best captures the audit's central criticism?

A) The organisation has failed to assign data ownership
B) Data is being processed unlawfully in legacy systems
C) Governance structures have expanded faster than the monitoring and active management needed to make them effective
D) The retention schedule is the organisation's most serious control weakness

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** The report's own summary sentence carries the verdict: the control environment "requires improvement, principally because monitoring controls have not kept pace with the expansion of ownership assignment" — and the body supports it (owners who cannot describe responsibilities; quality metrics for only 40% of critical data sets). That is option C: structure has outrun substance. Option A contradicts the reported 78% progress. Option B contradicts the explicit "no instances of unlawful processing" finding (records past disposal date are a retention control failure, which the report does not characterise as unlawful processing). Option D elevates one finding above the report's own stated principal concern — the word "principally" settles the ranking. For a chief data officer, this is the most personally relevant passage in this section: "ownership assigned but not active" is among the most common failure modes of real data governance programmes, and this item tests whether a candidate reads audit reports for their central criticism rather than their most quotable statistic.

**Passage 5 (for Questions 15-16): Extract from a draft data ethics framework**

"Automated decision-making that produces legal or similarly significant effects for individuals must include meaningful human review before a final decision is issued. Teams proposing new uses of data for analytics must complete an ethics screening questionnaire; where the screening identifies potential for significant public concern, the proposal must be referred to the Data Ethics Committee, whose recommendation the accountable director may depart from only with written reasons copied to the Chief Data Officer. Nothing in this framework limits obligations arising under data protection legislation, which continue to apply in full."

**Question 15 (moderate)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "The Data Ethics Committee can veto an analytics proposal that raises significant public concern."

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The framework gives the committee a recommendation, not a veto: the accountable director "may depart from" the recommendation, provided written reasons are copied to the Chief Data Officer. A body whose advice can be departed from, however formally, does not hold a veto — the decision rights rest with the director, constrained by a transparency requirement. A candidate often answers True because ethics committees feel like approval gates; the text describes an advise-and-explain model instead. This item tests exactly the distinctions governance design turns on — recommend versus approve, consult versus consent — which a chief data officer must draft and defend.

**Question 16 (moderate)**

True, False, or Cannot Say: "Complying with this ethics framework ensures compliance with data protection legislation."

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states: "Nothing in this framework limits obligations arising under data protection legislation, which continue to apply in full." The framework explicitly positions itself as additional to, not a substitute for, legal obligations — so complying with the framework cannot be said to ensure legal compliance; the two regimes run in parallel. If the passage had been silent on the relationship, Cannot Say would be right; the explicit continuation clause tips it to False because the statement asserts a sufficiency the text takes care to disclaim. This item tests the practical echo of a real problem: teams regularly assume that passing the ethics process "covers" their data protection duties, and the chief data officer is the person who must keep the two obligations distinct in the organisation's mind.

### Preparation tips

- **Draw items from regulatory and standards text the role actually uses.** A page of UK GDPR, a government data standard, or an organisation's own data policies, with each obligation paraphrased and every "must", "should", "may" and "unless" noted, gives grounded, well-calibrated material.
- **Score the Cannot Say discipline strictly.** For every statement, check whether a candidate can point to the exact sentence that makes it true or false. If they cannot, and the correct answer is Cannot Say, credit that even where the candidate initially hesitated — experts often fail these items by knowing too much, and consciously fencing off that expertise is itself a skill worth rewarding.
- **Watch for quantifiers and modals in a candidate's reasoning.** "Always", "only", "must", "intends to", "for the first time in three years" — these small words decide most answers; check whether a candidate's justification engages with them directly.
- **Ask a candidate to compress a passage as a check.** A one-sentence summary of a passage, checked against the summary-question options, tests both a test skill and the core of the briefing craft the role requires.
- **Time the section consistently across candidates.** Give roughly 45-60 seconds per question and keep the limit uniform; the skill under time pressure is targeted re-reading, so note whether a candidate scans for the relevant sentence rather than re-reading the whole passage each time.
- **Review a candidate's errors by type, not just by question.** Sort mistakes into categories: imported outside knowledge, missed a modal verb, missed half a conjunction, treated an intention as a fact. Naming the dominant error type at debrief is more useful feedback than a raw score.

### Common pitfalls

- **Rewarding outside knowledge over textual reading.** The most common failure for senior specialists is answering from what they know the law or standard really says instead of the passage. Score reading, not expertise — mark from the text alone, as in Question 3.
- **Confusing "the report found no X" with "no X exists" when marking.** Findings, absences and guarantees are different claims, as Question 13 shows. Check that a candidate's answer matches the claim type in the passage, not a stronger or weaker one.
- **Accepting a likely outcome as a stated fact.** "Intends to consult on making mandatory" is not "will make mandatory" (Question 7). Passages at this level are full of hedged futures; check that a candidate keeps the hedge.
- **Missing one half of a conjunction in a candidate's reasoning.** Requirements joined by "and" (documented AND approved, in Question 9) are the classic hiding place for the fact the passage quietly omits.
- **Allowing poor time allocation to distort scoring.** A candidate who reads every passage exhaustively before seeing the questions is spending time inefficiently; note this as a pattern rather than simply marking the final answers.
- **Letting the emotional register of a passage distort scoring.** Escalation emails and audit reports carry tone — urgency, blame, reassurance. Question 11 tests whether a candidate separates what is asked from how forcefully it is asked; check your own scoring does the same.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates a candidate's decision-making, interpersonal skill and professional conduct through realistic workplace dilemmas. Unlike logic or numeric tests, there is no single objective right answer: several responses may seem reasonable, and the assessment measures how well a candidate's behavioural choices align with the values of the organisation and the demands of the role. Typical formats present a scenario and ask the candidate either to select the **most effective** and **least effective** actions from a set, or to rank or rate every option from best to worst. Timing is usually generous or untimed, because the point is natural judgement rather than speed.

For a chief data officer, situational judgement is arguably the assessment closest to the job itself. The role's duties are saturated with judgement calls that have no formula: balancing data protection and privacy against access, sharing and innovation; deciding when to escalate an ethical concern and when to coach; choosing between confronting and enlisting a resistant director; weighing a ministerial deadline against a security control. The scenarios in a chief-level assessment involve the role's real stakeholders — permanent secretaries and boards, directors and their teams, the data protection officer, the chief information security officer, regulators, other government departments, vendors, and the public whose data the role stewards. Score against the values of the UK public sector: honesty, integrity, impartiality and objectivity, alongside the digital and data profession's commitments to user needs, transparency, collaboration, accessibility, and inclusion.

The dilemmas below are demanding on purpose. Present each scenario in full and let the candidate commit to their own answer before you reveal the discussion below — it is your answer key. Where a candidate's instinct differs from the suggested key, that is useful debrief material rather than an automatic mark against them: at executive level, a candidate who can articulate why they would act differently is showing valuable judgement in its own right.

### What it measures

Mapped to the duties in the role summary:

- **Strategic influence and senior stakeholder management** maps to working with senior leaders to align priorities and secure their support: many scenarios test whether a candidate builds coalitions or burns them.
- **Ethical leadership** maps to ensuring the organisation uses data ethically and complies with standards and regulations: scenarios probe whether a candidate upholds principles when they are expensive.
- **Balancing protection with innovation** maps to creating an environment where data protection and privacy are balanced with access, sharing and innovation — the defining tension of the role.
- **Coaching and capability building** maps to increasing data literacy and ensuring the organisation has the data skills it needs: scenarios distinguish candidates who build capability from those who create dependency.
- **Security partnership** maps to working with cyber security to keep data secure while accessible and controllable: scenarios test collaboration with the chief information security officer under pressure.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (moderate) - The minister's deadline versus the impact assessment**

A minister's office requests a new analysis combining benefits data with health referral data, for announcement in twelve days. A candidate's data protection team advises that this combination is a new purpose requiring a data protection impact assessment, which typically takes four weeks. The policy director says the announcement date is fixed and asks the candidate to "find a way". Rate the options:

- Option A: Authorise the analysis to proceed now and complete the impact assessment retrospectively after the announcement.
- Option B: Ask the data protection team to run a focused, accelerated impact assessment scoped tightly to this analysis, in parallel with preparing the data under strict controls, and give the policy director a clear go/no-go date.
- Option C: Tell the policy director the analysis cannot happen and the announcement must change.
- Option D: Suggest the analysis proceed using aggregated, anonymised data that may reduce the analytical precision but avoids the new-purpose issue, while the full assessment runs for the detailed version.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B treats both the legal duty and the democratic deadline as real constraints and manages them actively: impact assessments can legitimately be prioritised and scoped, parallel preparation loses no time, and a go/no-go date converts an open-ended conflict into a managed decision. Option D is a close second and in some scenarios would be the winner — offering an anonymised route shows exactly the protection-with-innovation balance the role exists to strike; it loses to B only because it concedes analytical value before testing whether the compliant full version can be achieved in time, and B can incorporate D as the fallback at the go/no-go point. Option C is honest but prematurely absolute: it treats a process duration as immovable and spends political capital before exploring options — the "least effective-looking safe option". Option A is clearly least effective: a retrospective impact assessment defeats the purpose of the safeguard, likely breaches UK GDPR's requirement for prior assessment of high-risk processing, and teaches the organisation that political pressure suspends data protection. This item tests whether a candidate recognises that choosing A once invites the same demand every quarter.

**Question 2 (hard) - The director who hoards data**

A candidate's data strategy commits the organisation to making data easily used, reused and shared. One director consistently refuses other teams access to her directorate's case data, citing quality concerns: "they'll misinterpret it and my team will carry the blame." Her data is among the most requested in the organisation. Rate the options:

- Option A: Escalate to the permanent secretary that the director is blocking the data strategy.
- Option B: Meet the director to understand her concerns, then propose publishing the data with documented quality caveats, agreed interpretation guidance, and a named contact route — making her team's context travel with the data.
- Option C: Use the candidate's authority under the data strategy to mandate that the data be shared within 30 days.
- Option D: Arrange for requesting teams to submit their intended uses so the director can approve them case by case.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B treats the director's stated concern as information rather than obstruction — and her concern is substantively reasonable: misinterpreted data does cause real harm, and fear of blame is a rational response to a real accountability gap. B solves the actual problem (context loss) rather than the surface problem (refusal), and converts a blocker into a co-designer of good sharing practice. This is "coaching the whole organisation" in action, which this item tests. Option D is partially effective but institutionalises the bottleneck: case-by-case approval does not scale, keeps power over reuse with one person, and quietly abandons "easily" from "easily used, reused and shared". Option C may eventually be needed and is not disgraceful — strategies require enforcement — but reaching for mandate before dialogue converts a solvable quality-assurance problem into a compliance battle, and mandated sharing by a resentful team tends to arrive late and poorly documented. Option A is least effective: escalating before engaging spends the permanent secretary's attention on a problem not yet attempted to be solved, damages the relationship with every director watching, and signals that the strategy is implemented by complaint rather than leadership.

**Question 3 (moderate) - The analytics tool the security team blocked**

A candidate's chief information security officer (CISO) has blocked deployment of a machine-learning feature in their analytics platform, citing an unresolved vulnerability in how the vendor handles authentication tokens. Analysts across three directorates are vocal that the block is costing them weeks of manual effort. The vendor disputes the CISO's assessment. Rate the options:

- Option A: Accept the block and tell analysts the feature is unavailable until further notice.
- Option B: Convene the candidate's platform architects, the CISO's team and the vendor to examine the specific vulnerability together, agree what evidence or mitigation would resolve it, and set a review date — while publicly backing the CISO's interim decision.
- Option C: Override the block for a limited pilot group under monitoring, accepting the risk on the candidate's own authority.
- Option D: Ask the vendor to escalate their dispute to the CISO's line manager.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The duty tested here is working with cyber security to ensure data is secure while staying accessible — a partnership duty, not a contest. Option B honours the partnership in both directions: it takes the CISO's technical concern seriously and backs their interim authority publicly (essential for the security culture the role also depends on), while refusing to let "blocked" become a steady state with no exit criteria. Defining what evidence would resolve the disagreement is the leadership move; most security stand-offs persist because nobody specifies the conditions for change. Option A is safe but passive: it accepts an indefinite cost without testing the disagreement, and quietly teaches analysts that security concerns are unanswerable. Option C — overriding a live security objection on a disputed vulnerability — mistakes decisiveness for judgement; if the CISO is right, authentication tokens have been exposed on the candidate's own signature. Option D is least effective: inviting a vendor to go around the security chief attacks the trust between the roles at its foundation, arms a commercial party in an internal governance matter, and would be remembered long after the vulnerability is patched.

**Question 4 (hard) - The flawed statistic already with the minister**

Reviewing a briefing pack on Sunday evening, a candidate notices that a headline statistic their team supplied for a ministerial speech on Tuesday overstates the improvement in service processing times: a data quality fix last month reclassified thousands of cases, and the like-for-like improvement is 8%, not the briefed 19%. The candidate's team lead believes "nobody will check the basis". Rate the options:

- Option A: Correct the figure immediately: alert the policy director and private office on Monday morning with the accurate number, a clear explanation, and a suggested form of words.
- Option B: Let the speech proceed with 19% but publish the methodological note quietly afterwards.
- Option C: Ask the team to re-run the analysis to see whether any defensible method produces a number close to 19%.
- Option D: Correct the figure for future use but take no action on Tuesday's speech, since the briefing has already been submitted.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** This is an integrity item, and integrity items at executive level are deliberately expensive: option A embarrasses the candidate's own team, creates Monday-morning friction, and possibly weakens a ministerial announcement — and it is still clearly right. Honesty and objectivity are Civil Service values precisely for the moments they cost something, and a chief data officer's entire authority rests on the organisation believing their numbers. A also models the behaviour: correcting fast, with an explanation and a usable alternative ("processing times improved 8% on a like-for-like basis"), makes the correction easy to accept. Option C is the seductive one — "re-examine the analysis" sounds diligent — but its stated aim is to find a method that rescues a predetermined number, which is analysis backwards; it also burns the little time available. Option D draws an arbitrary line: the speech is the most public use of the wrong figure, and "already submitted" is a workflow fact, not an ethical boundary. Option B is least effective: knowingly letting a minister state a figure the candidate's own review found misleading, then covering it with a quiet note, fails the minister (who bears the public risk), fails the public, and converts an honest error into something closer to deception. This item tests whether a candidate recognises that "nobody will check" is never the standard — they are the check.

**Question 5 (moderate) - The literacy programme nobody attends**

Six months into a candidate's flagship data literacy programme, completion among senior civil servants is 12%, against 60% for staff overall. Several directors have delegated their places to junior staff. The board asks how the candidate will fix senior engagement. Rate the options:

- Option A: Recommend the permanent secretary mandate completion for all senior civil servants within three months.
- Option B: Interview a sample of directors about why they are not attending, then redesign the senior offer around what is learned — likely shorter, decision-focused sessions using the organisation's own live data and decisions.
- Option C: Report the completion figures by directorate to the board quarterly, letting visibility create pressure.
- Option D: Accept that senior completion will lag and focus resources on the engaged 60%.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The delegation pattern is diagnostic gold: directors are not refusing development, they are signalling that the current offer feels like training for someone else. Option B treats low uptake as user research — exactly how a digital organisation should respond to a service its users avoid — and the redesign it points to (short, applied to live decisions, peer-level) matches what evidence on executive education consistently shows. B also respects the duty to increase data literacy by "coaching the whole organisation": coaching starts from where learners are. Option C is a reasonable supporting move — transparent metrics are legitimate and boards respond to them — but on its own it pressures people toward an offer that is not working, generating compliance completions rather than capability. Option A has the same flaw with more force: mandates get modules completed, not minds changed, and spending the permanent secretary's authority on attendance is poor use of scarce capital. Option D is least effective: senior leaders make the organisation's biggest data-dependent decisions, so conceding their literacy concedes the programme's core purpose — and this item tests whether a candidate recognises that the duty in the role summary makes no exemption for the people who find it inconvenient.

**Question 6 (hard) - The ethically uncomfortable but lawful proposal**

A policy team proposes using historic case data to build a model predicting which benefit claimants are likely to miss appointments, so reminders can be targeted. Legal review confirms the processing is lawful. A candidate's data ethics screening flags that the model's training data reflects past patterns that correlate strongly with disability and caring responsibilities, so "targeting" could shade into differential treatment of protected groups. The policy team notes, accurately, that the ethics committee's role is advisory. Rate the options:

- Option A: Approve the model since it is lawful, with monitoring of outcomes by protected characteristic.
- Option B: Refer the proposal to the Data Ethics Committee, require the team to define the intervention precisely (supportive reminders only, no sanctions linkage), test the model for disparate impact before deployment, and commit to publishing the approach.
- Option C: Reject the proposal on the grounds that any model correlated with protected characteristics is unacceptable.
- Option D: Ask the policy team to proceed but remove disability-related variables from the model.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The role summary makes a chief data officer accountable for ethical use of data, not merely lawful use — the gap between those two standards is where this question lives. Option B does four things a mature data ethics leader does: uses the governance process designed for exactly this case; narrows the intervention so the model's worst use (sanctions targeting) is designed out rather than merely hoped against; tests for disparate impact empirically instead of arguing about it in the abstract; and commits to transparency, which disciplines everyone and honours public sector openness. Option A is not reckless — outcome monitoring is real governance — but it accepts the risk before shaping the intervention, and "lawful plus monitored" is a floor, not a judgement. Option C mistakes caution for ethics: refusing all correlation with protected characteristics would prohibit most beneficial targeting too (including support that disabled claimants might want), and it abandons the balance of protection with innovation the role exists to strike. Option D is least effective and the most instructive: removing the explicit disability variables does not remove the correlation — the model will reconstruct it through proxies (appointment history, location, claim type) — so D delivers the same differential impact while destroying the ability to measure it. This item tests whether a candidate is the kind of chief data officer who is the person in the room who knows why the statistically naive option, dressed as the responsible one, fails.

**Question 7 (moderate) - The data breach during the board meeting**

Midway through a board meeting where a candidate is presenting the data strategy, their deputy messages: a contractor has uploaded a data set containing 40,000 citizens' personal details to an unsecured external file-sharing service; it has been exposed for at least three hours; the security incident process has been triggered. Rate the options:

- Option A: Finish the strategy presentation, then deal with the incident.
- Option B: Ask the chair for a brief pause, confirm with the deputy that containment is underway and the incident team has what it needs, inform the board of the incident in outline, and hand the strategy slot to a later meeting if necessary.
- Option C: Leave the meeting immediately to personally direct the incident response.
- Option D: Message the deputy to handle it and update the candidate hourly, and continue the meeting without informing the board.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Two duties collide: leading the strategic conversation and being accountable for a live data protection incident with statutory clock implications (a reportable breach must reach the ICO within 72 hours, and citizen risk is accruing in real time). Option B resolves the collision correctly: it verifies that the operational response is running (the deputy and the incident team execute; the candidate does not need to seize the controls), and it tells the board now — they are the accountability forum for exactly this kind of risk, they will ask why they heard about it late, and transparency with the board is the internal face of transparency with the public. Option C overcorrects: a chief data officer personally directing containment duplicates the incident commander role, signals distrust of the process built for exactly this, and removes the candidate from the governance level where they are actually needed. Option D gets the delegation right and the transparency wrong — sitting in a board meeting while withholding a major live incident from that board is a choice hard to defend afterwards. Option A is least effective: it treats presentation continuity as more important than an unfolding exposure of 40,000 citizens' data, delays both an assurance check and the board's knowledge, and fails the basic test this item poses — if the exposure worsened during the slides, what would the candidate say they were doing?

**Question 8 (moderate) - The department that wants a candidate's data but not their standards**

Another government department urgently requests a bulk extract of a candidate's organisation's address-matched service data for a cross-government initiative the Cabinet Office champions. Their request asks for the raw extract by secure transfer; the candidate's standard route is API access with field-level controls, audit logging and an agreed retention limit. Their lead says building to the API "would add six weeks we don't have". Rate the options:

- Option A: Provide the bulk extract, given the cross-government priority and their department's own security accreditation.
- Option B: Offer a middle path: a one-off, minimised extract (only the fields the initiative needs, with retention and deletion terms in the data sharing agreement) now, alongside a committed migration to API access for any ongoing need.
- Option C: Decline anything but API access, since standards exist to be applied consistently.
- Option D: Ask the Cabinet Office sponsor to adjudicate between the two departments.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The role's duties pull in both directions by design: ensure data can be easily used, reused and shared — and ensure it stays secure and controlled. Option B is the balance made concrete: it meets the genuine urgency (collaboration across government is a real value, and six weeks is a real cost to a live initiative), while conceding none of the substantive protections — minimisation replaces "raw", contractual retention and deletion replace open-ended holding, and the one-off framing prevents the exception becoming the route. It also distinguishes the two questions the requester has merged: "can we have it fast?" (yes) and "can we have it ungoverned?" (no). Option A gives away the substance: "their accreditation" covers their systems, not the appropriateness of an unminimised bulk extract, and raw bulk transfers are how cross-government data incidents happen. Option C has integrity but applies a control mechanism (the API) as if it were the objective; the objectives are minimisation, auditability and retention, all achievable in B's structure. Option D is least effective: escalating a solvable technical-commercial disagreement to the political sponsor makes two departments look incapable of collaborating, invites a decision made on momentum rather than data governance, and — whichever way it went — would establish that pressure on the sponsor, not engagement with the chief data officer, is how departments get the organisation's data.

**Question 9 (hard) - A candidate's own strategy is failing**

Eighteen months into the three-year data strategy a candidate authored, the honest picture is mixed: the data platform is delivered and adopted, but the master data programme — the strategy's centrepiece — is a year behind, its lead supplier is underperforming, and two directorates have started building local workarounds. The annual strategy refresh is due to the executive committee next month. Rate the options:

- Option A: Present the refresh emphasising the platform's success, describe master data as "rephased", and absorb the workarounds as "federated delivery".
- Option B: Present an honest assessment: name the master data programme as off-track and the supplier as underperforming, bring options (recompete, descope, or resequence around the workarounds' momentum), and recommend one with its costs.
- Option C: Delay the refresh six months to give the master data programme time to recover.
- Option D: Commission an independent review of the master data programme and present the refresh after it reports.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** The scenario tests whether a candidate applies to their own strategy the standards applied to everyone else's data: accuracy, transparency, decision-usefulness. Option B does. Executives forgive off-track programmes; they do not forgive discovering them late, and the workarounds detail is the tell — the organisation is already routing around the delay, so the committee will learn the truth from someone, and the only question is whether they learn it from the candidate with options attached. Bringing a recommendation (not just a confession) is what makes B leadership rather than bad news. Option D is respectable — independent assurance has its place — but as a substitute for next month's honest refresh it functions as deferral, and enough is already known to name the problem; commissioning a review can be part of B's recommendation rather than a reason to delay it. Option C is deferral without even the assurance benefit. Option A is least effective and corrosive: "rephased" and "federated delivery" are euphemisms that spend personal credibility to buy one comfortable meeting, guarantee a harder meeting later, and — worst for a chief data officer — model exactly the massaged reporting the role exists to discourage across the organisation. This item tests whether a candidate recognises that a leader who launders their own bad news forfeits the standing to challenge anyone else's numbers.

**Question 10 (moderate) - The talented analyst and the shadow database**

A skills audit reveals that a highly regarded analyst in a policy directorate has built a personal database combining extracts from four corporate systems, which her team now depends on for weekly reporting. It is well designed, but it sits outside all governance: no access controls beyond her team, no backups, unmanaged personal data, and definitions that quietly diverge from corporate standards. Rate the options:

- Option A: Instruct that the database be shut down within a week and reporting revert to corporate systems.
- Option B: Recognise the database as evidence of unmet need and real talent: commission the candidate's team to work with the analyst to migrate it into the governed platform, fix the personal data handling immediately as the one non-negotiable, credit her design, and use the case to understand what corporate systems failed to provide.
- Option C: Report the incident through the data protection channel and let that process determine the outcome.
- Option D: Leave it running but require the analyst to document it and add backups.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Shadow systems are the classic chief data officer dilemma because they are simultaneously a governance failure and a message: four systems' extracts were combined because no governed route met a real weekly need. Option B reads both signals. It fixes the genuinely urgent risk immediately (unmanaged personal data cannot wait for a migration plan), brings the asset inside governance rather than destroying the value, and — critically for the capability-building duty — treats the analyst as talent to develop, not a culprit to punish. How this case is handled will be retold across the organisation: B's version recruits every capable improviser to come forward; A's version teaches them to hide better. Option A is least effective for exactly that reason, and it also breaks the team's weekly reporting with nothing ready to replace it — governance that destroys the business need it should serve. Option C is not wrong about personal data (and B includes that fix), but outsourcing the whole judgement to an incident process treats a leadership and capability question as a compliance event. Option D inverts the priorities: it formalises the ungoverned system while leaving the personal data risk live — documentation and backups make a shadow database more durable, not more legitimate.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor scoring in the published values.** The Civil Service values (honesty, integrity, impartiality, objectivity) and the digital and data profession's emphasis on user needs, transparency, accessibility, inclusion, and working in the open should underpin your scoring key at this level, just as they underpin the item design.
- **Watch for the "both duties are real" move in a candidate's answer.** The best chief-level answers rarely pick one value and sacrifice the other; they restructure the situation so protection and access, deadline and diligence, can both be substantially honoured — as in the impact assessment and cross-department sharing scenarios. Credit a candidate who asks "is there an option that changes the shape of the problem?"
- **Note a candidate's escalation reflex.** Escalation and mandate are legitimate tools that score badly as first moves. Score in favour of engaging directly with the person or the problem before invoking authority.
- **Look for integrity under cost.** The hardest items make honesty expensive; a candidate who chooses the costly-but-honest option is showing exactly the judgement this section is designed to surface, and it is worth naming explicitly at debrief.
- **Debrief disagreements with the answer key constructively.** Where a candidate's answer differs from the suggested key, ask them to explain their reasoning. Sometimes it reveals a value weighting worth discussing; sometimes their reasoning is sound and context-dependent. Either way, the articulation is useful evidence.

### Common pitfalls

- **Rewarding an idealistic answer over an effective one.** "Consult everyone, delay the decision, commission a review" sounds virtuous but often scores poorly because it dodges the judgement the scenario demands. Check whether a candidate's chosen option moves the situation forward while honouring the values, not just sounds principled.
- **Rewarding passive or deflecting options.** Options that hand the problem to a sponsor, a process, or the future (Question 8's adjudication request, Question 9's delays) are classic "least effective" keys. Executive roles are paid to absorb difficulty, not reroute it — mark accordingly.
- **Over-rewarding decisiveness.** The mirror-image error: overriding the CISO, mandating the training, shutting down the shadow database. Force is sometimes right, but as a first move it usually signals that influence has failed — do not let boldness alone earn top marks.
- **Ignoring the values context when scoring.** These scenarios are scored against UK public sector culture: transparency with boards and ministers, collaboration across departments, user needs, accessibility, and inclusion. An answer that would be praised in a hard-driving commercial culture (Question 4's "nobody will check") can be the worst available here.
- **Missing second-order effects in a candidate's reasoning.** At chief level, every decision is also a precedent and a story the organisation tells itself afterwards. Look for candidates who evaluate options by what they teach — about whether pressure beats process, whether honesty is safe, whether improvisers get punished.
- **Forgetting who executes.** Options where a candidate proposes to personally do the operational work (directing incident response, taking on development themselves) usually score below options where they verify, empower and stay at the governance altitude the role occupies — check for this pattern.

## Conclusion

You have now worked through a complete set of psychometric assessment resources built specifically for the chief data officer role: cognitive practice grounded in the strategies, policies and quality metrics the role handles daily; numeric reasoning drawn from the budgets, capacity figures and benefits cases defended at boards; verbal reasoning built on the standards, agreements and audit reports whose exact wording shapes decisions; and situational judgement dilemmas featuring the ministers, directors, security chiefs and analysts who populate the real working week.

A theme worth carrying into scoring and debrief: the four assessments measure, in miniature, the same disciplines that make a chief data officer effective. Computing before challenging, naming the base before quoting the percentage, reading what the text actually says, holding both halves of the protection-and-innovation balance, choosing honesty when it is expensive — these are simultaneously test techniques and leadership habits. A candidate who shows them consistently across sections is showing more than test aptitude.

When scoring, work section by section against the worked answers above, and note not just whether an answer matches the key but how the candidate reasoned their way there — the explanation is often more informative than the verdict, particularly in the verbal and situational judgement sections where a close call can be argued either way. Keep your scoring notes and the candidate's raw answers on file, consistent with your organisation's recruitment and record-keeping policies, so the assessment can be reviewed if challenged.

At debrief, share the areas of strength and the areas of concern candidly, using the explanations above to show your reasoning. Where the candidate's answer differs from the key but their reasoning is sound and defensible, say so — at executive level, this material is as much about the quality of a candidate's judgement as about matching a predetermined answer. A candidate who understands why an answer was scored as it was leaves with useful feedback even if unsuccessful.

Administer this assessment identically for every candidate for this role level: the same items, the same time limits, and the same conditions, so that no candidate is advantaged or disadvantaged by variation in delivery. Used consistently and interpreted carefully, it gives you a structured, comparable signal about how a candidate reasons, calculates, reads, and judges under the kinds of pressure this role will bring — nothing more, and nothing less.
