# Lead User Researcher - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for lead user researcher. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A lead user researcher is an expert practitioner leading and aligning research across several teams, responsible for ensuring those teams take a user-centred, evidence-based approach, and for developing and assuring good research practice across an organisation. The role is notably organisational rather than project-shaped: establishing and aligning research programmes to meet organisational goals, synthesising findings across programmes into strategic insight that informs a roadmap, setting and assuring standards including ethics and safeguarding, directing stakeholder relationship strategy, negotiating with senior stakeholders, and leading a community of practice.

Three features shape the material below. First, the candidate is accountable for research they did not conduct — assuring quality across an organisation means forming judgements about other people's evidence, frequently at speed, frequently from a written output alone, and frequently while a finding is already influencing a decision. Second, the role synthesises across programmes: strategic insight comes from noticing what several bodies of evidence, gathered for different purposes by different teams, together imply — including where they contradict one another. Third, the role negotiates with people who have more organisational power and different incentives, which depends entirely on the candidate's evidence being unassailable.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate diagnoses a systemic cause behind evidence assembled by other people at organisational scale, and whether they interrogate a quantitative claim (a vendor figure, a business case, a board paper) before accepting or repeating it. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward action that changes a system or a process rather than solving a problem personally, and calibrates escalation to genuine severity — safeguarding and physical risk go up immediately and in parallel, while practice problems go to the person first.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic: bodies of evidence assembled by other people, standards being applied inconsistently across teams, findings that appear to conflict until what each measured is established, and prioritisation problems where the units being ranked are programmes rather than tasks.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority.

Three things distinguish this section at this level. First, most of the reasoning is about other people's work — assuring research quality across an organisation means the candidate rarely sees the raw data, so reading an output for the shape of the study behind it is a core competence. Second, synthesis across sources gathered for different purposes: strategic insight requires holding several incomplete pictures in mind without forcing premature agreement. Third, prioritisation questions are genuinely hard — when units are programmes with different time horizons and reversibility, no option dominates, and the discriminating factor is reasoning from value of information rather than importance, urgency or profile.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing evidence assembled by others** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, assuring research quality across the organisation.
- **Cross-programme synthesis** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, producing strategic insights to inform the organisation's roadmap.
- **Reasoning under competing hypotheses** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, helping the organisation continually assure and improve its practices.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Agile research practices** and **Research management**, aligning programmes to organisational goals.
- **Diagnosing systemic exclusion** maps to **Inclusive research**.
- **Reasoning about positions and incentives** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

The organisation's standard requires six elements: research questions, method, sample composition including access needs, analysis approach, limitations, and date. A report being assured states the research questions, method, analysis approach and date, describes the sample as "12 participants recruited via the panel," and contains no limitations section. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four elements are present. The sample description gives a number and route but nothing about composition or access needs, and limitations are absent — two unmet requirements, giving B. This tests whether the candidate recognises that "12 participants recruited via the panel" describes sample *source*, not sample *composition*, exactly the distinction the standard exists to force — and whether they notice access needs and limitations are the two requirements most commonly omitted across most organisations, the fields to check first when assuring at scale.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an organisational rule**

A standard states: "Research involving participants who may be considered vulnerable requires an approved safeguarding protocol, unless the research is conducted entirely through an organisation already covered by a standing partnership agreement." A programme lead reports: "Our study with people experiencing homelessness ran last month without an approved safeguarding protocol." Ask which must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) The study ran through a standing partnership organisation.
- C) Either the study ran entirely through a standing partnership organisation, or the standard was breached.
- D) Participants were placed at risk.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, licenses only the disjunction. Option D is the interesting distractor at this level — the conclusion professional instinct reaches for — but whether anyone was actually placed at risk is a separate empirical question. This tests whether the candidate resists assuming a branch and notices the word "entirely" in the exception, narrowing it substantially: a study using the partnership for most but not all recruitment would not qualify, which is exactly the partial compliance teams tend to assume is sufficient.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading a study from its output**

A report being assured states: "Participants were positive about the new service. All 16 participants said they would recommend it," noting recruitment through the department's user panel, sessions in the department's offices, and the recommendation question asked by the facilitator as the final session item. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) Sixteen participants is too small a sample for a recommendation measure.
- B) Three separate features — panel recruitment, departmental setting, and a facilitator asking directly at the end — all bias in the same direction towards socially desirable responses, so unanimity here is evidence about the conditions rather than about the service.
- C) A recommendation question is not a good measure of service quality.
- D) The report should have used a validated scale.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unanimity is itself the signal something is wrong. Each factor alone would warrant caution; together they compound rather than partially cancel. This tests whether the candidate identifies the compounding structure rather than a single weakness — option A misidentifies size as the issue when it is validity, since 160 participants under the same conditions would produce the same unanimity. When feeding this back, naming the compounding matters, because fixing one factor changes nothing.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation across programmes**

Leading research across four programmes with capacity for substantial support to one this quarter: (1) a programme choosing a service model next month, committing four years of build; (2) a programme with a live service and persistent 28% drop-off affecting 200,000 users a year; (3) a programme launching in six weeks with screens already signed off; (4) a programme preparing a business case due in nine months. Ask which is strongest.

- A) The second, because it affects the most users.
- B) The first, because the decision is imminent, commits four years, and research is the only input that could still change it — giving it by far the highest value of information.
- C) The third, because launching untested is a risk.
- D) The fourth, because business cases shape long-term investment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Value of information is the right frame. This tests whether the candidate identifies the third programme as the weakest despite sounding responsible — signed-off screens six weeks from launch means findings arrive with nowhere to go, research for reassurance rather than decisions — and resists the profile trap in option D, since attention is not the same as decision leverage.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-programme synthesis**

Three unrelated pieces of evidence land in the same month: a discovery finding people struggle to prove identity without a passport or driving licence; live-service analytics showing 45% drop-off at identity verification for a licensing service; a support desk report naming identity queries as its highest call driver across four services. Ask for the strongest synthesis.

- A) Three teams have identified usability problems with their identity steps.
- B) The organisation has a cross-cutting identity assurance problem affecting multiple services, most likely concentrated among people without common photographic documents — which is a strategic issue for the roadmap rather than four separate service fixes.
- C) Identity verification is difficult and should be simplified.
- D) The three findings are unrelated and should be reported separately.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests whether the candidate identifies a shared cause and reframes the unit of the problem as belonging on a roadmap rather than in four separate backlogs, where each team would build a partial workaround. Option A describes without synthesising; option C is true and strategically useless; option D misses the pattern entirely.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a synthesis**

An organisational insight report drawing on eleven studies contains the headline "users across all services want proactive notifications," supported by material from three studies, all about services with statutory deadlines. Ask for the most useful feedback.

- A) The claim is invalid and should be removed.
- B) Note that the supporting evidence comes exclusively from deadline-driven services, so the claim as written over-generalises; suggest either narrowing it to that service type or testing whether it holds in services without deadlines before it enters the roadmap.
- C) Suggest adding more studies.
- D) Approve it; three studies is a reasonable basis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and the scope of the claim is wrong, and this matters because the report is destined for a roadmap. This tests whether the candidate preserves the finding while correcting its scope, rather than discarding something real (A), letting an over-generalised claim justify investment it was never evidenced for (D), or reaching for the expensive fix before the cheap one (C).

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Across the organisation, six teams consistently report that recruitment produces digitally confident participants in work, despite services serving many people who are neither, and all six use the central recruitment contract. Ask for the strongest diagnosis and response.

- A) Each team should supplement its recruitment locally.
- B) The contract's supply model is structurally mismatched to the organisation's user base; the response is to document the pattern with evidence across all six teams, specify what suitable supply would look like, and take it to whoever owns the contract — while enabling teams to use partnership routes in the meantime.
- C) The teams should write better screeners.
- D) Recruitment should be brought in-house.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Six teams with the same problem and the same supplier is one supply problem, not six recruitment problems. This tests whether the candidate diagnoses at the level that fixes the mechanism while unblocking teams immediately — rather than distributing the cost indefinitely (A), misdiagnosing a screener as the fix for a supply that does not contain the participant type (C), or proposing a large change (D) without first specifying and testing what is needed.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study being assured used a prototype with a broken save-and-return function throughout. Four claims: (i) participants could not find the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned when interrupted; (iii) sessions took longer than expected; (iv) participants misunderstood the declaration wording. Ask which claims are compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; the fault was incidental.
- C) Claims (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, since an inability to save and return plausibly caused both the abandonment on interruption and the extended durations, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only claim (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests scoping a flaw precisely rather than treating it as total or trivial. A broken save-and-return function manufactures abandonment on interruption and plausibly extends duration; content comprehension is untouched. Option A discards two sound findings and, at organisational scale, teaches teams that any fault invalidates everything — encouraging concealment. Option D catches one compromise and misses the other.

**Question 9 (harder) — Contradiction between programmes**

Two teams researching adjacent services reach opposite conclusions about the same user group: one reports a preference for completing in a single sitting, the other for saving and returning. Both studies look methodologically sound. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Commission a third study to settle it.
- B) Examine what differs between the two contexts before treating it as a contradiction — the services may impose different demands, such as whether third-party documents are needed — because a preference expressed about a fifteen-minute task and one about a three-week process are answers to different questions.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apparent contradictions between sound studies are usually context differences masquerading as disagreements. This tests whether the candidate reaches the synthesis — preference depends on whether the task can be completed with information already held — that is more useful than either finding alone, rather than spending money answering a question that dissolves on inspection (A), abdicating the synthesis the role exists to produce (C), or picking a winner on an irrelevant criterion (D).

**Question 10 (harder) — Standards that are not being followed**

The organisational research standard requires ethics review for research with people who may be considered vulnerable. Reviewing the past year, eleven of fourteen qualifying studies went through review; the three that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all conducted by experienced researchers. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the three breaches to the head of profession and require retrospective review.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: three failures in one directorate under the same pressure suggests the review process is not usable under deadline conditions, so investigate what the process demands and how long it takes, then redesign it to have a fast route — while addressing the specific breaches.
- C) Remind all researchers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory checkpoint before all fieldwork.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The distribution of the failures is the most informative feature — concentrated in one directorate under identical pressure by experienced people who know the requirement, pointing at a process that cannot be complied with when time is short. This tests whether the candidate handles both layers, remediation and process investigation, rather than choosing the weakest available compliance response (C) or adding friction for the eleven studies that complied to catch the three that did not (D).

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for a roadmap decision**

The organisation is deciding whether to invest in a shared identity capability. Available evidence: qualitative findings from three programmes indicating identity difficulty; analytics showing high drop-off at identity across four services; a supplier's claim of 94% first-time success; a support desk cost model. Ask for the most rigorous contribution.

- A) Present the qualitative and analytics evidence supporting the investment.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that the supplier's 94% figure requires interrogation of its denominator and population before it can be compared with the organisation's own drop-off data — and identify the one or two things not yet known that would most change the decision.
- C) Recommend the investment on the strength of the converging evidence.
- D) Recommend further research before any decision.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests whether the candidate makes the evidence base legible rather than advocating (A, C) or delaying (D) — flagging that the supplier's figure likely counts only users with acceptable documents, excluding precisely the population driving the organisation's drop-off. Converging evidence can share the same blind spot, and naming the residual uncertainty is what tells the decision what it actually rests on.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern in the candidate's own assurance**

Reviewing a year of assured research outputs, studies from two teams are consistently strong and studies from four are consistently weak; the two strong teams both have researchers who joined from the same previous organisation. Ask for the most useful inference and action.

- A) The four weaker teams need training.
- B) Something transferable is likely operating in the two strong teams — a practice, a routine, a set of expectations — and the highest-value action is to find out what it is by examining how they actually work, then build it into the organisation's standards and community of practice rather than assuming the difference is individual talent.
- C) Recruit more researchers from that organisation.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the four weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is an opportunity to identify a transferable practice rather than a reason to grade people. This tests whether the candidate goes and looks — the step people skip — rather than assuming a knowledge deficit (A), drawing an unusable inference from two people (C), or increasing scrutiny without improving capability (D).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads an output for the shape of the study behind it, rather than taking a claim at face value.
- Note whether the candidate scopes a methodological flaw precisely, identifying which claims it compromises and which survive.
- Watch for whether the candidate synthesises unrelated evidence into a shared, actionable cause rather than reporting findings separately.
- Score for whether the candidate reads the distribution of a compliance failure (concentrated versus scattered) as diagnostic of process versus conduct.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes sample source from sample composition, and a vendor's claimed denominator from the organisation's own.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an audit that accepts sample source ("recruited via the panel") as equivalent to sample composition.
- Missing when a candidate treats converging evidence as automatically stronger without checking for a shared blind spot.
- Rewarding a candidate who reaches for training as the answer to a systemic, contract-level or process-level problem.
- Accepting friction added for a compliant majority to address a small number of non-compliant, concentrated cases.
- Missing when a candidate condemns a flawed study wholesale rather than scoping precisely which claims survive.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At lead level the emphasis is almost entirely on interrogation — the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims, and the candidate is frequently the only person in the room able to establish that.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. At this level the numbers encountered are increasingly consequential — business cases, vendor claims, benefits realisation figures, board-level performance metrics — and increasingly detached from the people who produced them. Being the person who asks what the denominator is, which population it represents, and how the metric is defined is a large part of what the role is for.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents before calculating. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, assuring research quality across the organisation.
- **Aggregating across programmes** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, synthesising findings into strategic insight.
- **Comparing rates between user groups** maps to **Inclusive research**.
- **Judging sampling adequacy** maps to **User research methods**, helping the organisation adopt and improve its methods.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Across a programme, 36 of 48 participants completed the core task unaided. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 36 ÷ 48 = 75%, giving C. This tests basic percentage calculation and whether the candidate asks whether aggregating 48 participants across a programme is meaningful, which requires comparable tasks and populations across the studies.

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruitment at programme scale**

No-show rate averages 25%; a programme needs 60 completed sessions across four studies. Ask for the number of bookings required.

- A) 72
- B) 75
- C) 80
- D) 84

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 60 ÷ 0.75 = 80, giving C. Option B comes from adding 25% to 60, an underestimate. This tests correct over-recruitment arithmetic, and whether the candidate notes that a systematic under-recruitment at programme scale does not distribute evenly — access-need and under-represented-group quotas fail first.

**Question 3 (easy) — Disaggregated rates**

Digitally confident: 30 participants, 27 completed. Low digital confidence: 18, 9 completed. Assistive technology users: 12, 3 completed. Ask for the overall completion rate and the gap between highest and lowest group rates.

- A) 60% overall; 50 point gap
- B) 65% overall; 65 point gap
- C) 65% overall; 50 point gap
- D) 60% overall; 65 point gap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Overall: 39 ÷ 60 = 65%. Group rates: 90%, 50%, 25%; gap 65 points. This tests whether the candidate not only calculates correctly but notes that the overall figure describes no group in the study and conceals that assistive technology users fail three times out of four — an organisational standard that does not require disaggregation by access need has a hole in it.

**Question 4 (easy) — Central figure and spread**

Eleven sessions produced task times of 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, and 71 minutes. Ask which summary is most defensible.

- A) Mean 15.6 minutes
- B) Median 10 minutes, range 6 to 71, with the 71-minute case described separately
- C) Median 10 minutes
- D) Mean 15.6 minutes, range 6 to 71

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean exceeds nine of eleven observations; the median (10) describes the group well but alone conceals a session over four times the typical duration — likely the most informative observation in the set. This tests whether the candidate supplies centre, spread, and explicit treatment of an outlier together.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation across studies**

Four studies: 12 participants at 50% completion; 20 at 65%; 8 at 75%; 20 at 80%. Ask for the overall completion rate across all 60.

- A) 66%
- B) 68%
- C) 70%
- D) 72%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 6+13+6+16=41 ÷ 60 ≈ 68%. This tests whether the candidate returns to counts, and notes that the unweighted mean (67.5, also rounding to 68) agrees only by coincidence — a habit that fails badly if a study's size changes. A strong candidate also asks whether aggregating four studies with potentially different tasks or populations is meaningful at all.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 120,000 start, 90,000 pass identity, 54,000 upload evidence, 48,600 submit. Ask what proportion of those who passed identity went on to submit.

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 48,600 ÷ 90,000 = 54%, giving B. This tests whether the candidate identifies the denominator the question names, and notes strategically where the losses concentrate — 30,000 at identity and 36,000 between identity and upload, against only 5,400 at submission — which is where research capacity belongs.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage points in a benefits claim**

A benefits paper states a change raised completion from 55% to 66%, describing it as "a 20% improvement." Ask whether the description is accurate.

- A) No; it is an 11% improvement.
- B) Yes; 11 ÷ 55 = 20%, so a 20% relative increase is correct, though the paper should say "20% relative increase" or "11 percentage points" to avoid ambiguity.
- C) No; it is 11 percentage points, and calling it 20% is wrong.
- D) No; it should be 16.7%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relative increase (11 ÷ 55 = 20%) is defensible arithmetic; the problem is labelling ambiguity. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes an arithmetic error from a labelling one, and recognises the governance stake: benefits papers routinely choose the flattering framing, and requiring both figures as standard practice keeps the organisation's evidence honest without accusing anyone.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A vendor claim**

A supplier states their identity solution achieves "94% first-time verification success"; the organisation's current rate, verified users divided by users reaching the identity step, is 71%. Ask for the most important question before comparing.

- A) Whether 94% is independently audited.
- B) What denominator the supplier uses — in particular whether it counts only users who present acceptable documents, or all users who reach the step, since excluding those without documents would remove precisely the population driving your 29% failure.
- C) Whether the supplier's figure is recent.
- D) Whether the supplier has government clients.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two rates are comparable only if their denominators describe the same population, and vendor metrics very commonly do not. This tests whether the candidate identifies that a supplier's 94% likely excludes exactly the population driving internal failure, so adopting the solution could leave the failure rate essentially unchanged. Options A, C and D give the appearance of scrutiny without its substance.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims a 4 percentage point completion improvement on 1.2 million annual starts is worth £5.76 million a year. Ask for the implied value per additional completed application.

- A) £4.80
- B) £48
- C) £120
- D) £96

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 0.04×1,200,000=48,000 additional completions; £5,760,000÷48,000=£120. This tests whether the candidate reconstructs an implied unit value quickly enough to challenge a business case's plausibility, a more effective challenge than disputing the total because it is specific and answerable.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A board paper reports "average time to complete: 11 minutes" for a service research showed most users taking over half an hour across multiple sittings. Ask for the most likely explanation and the right intervention.

- A) The board paper is wrong and should be corrected.
- B) The metric almost certainly measures single sessions rather than end-to-end journeys, so multi-sitting completions are either excluded or counted only for their final short visit, and abandoned sessions may be included — the intervention is to ask for the definition and the distribution, and to propose an end-to-end measure alongside it.
- C) The research sample was atypical.
- D) The two figures cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Session-based analytics and journey-based reality diverge systematically for any multi-sitting service, always flattering it. This tests whether the candidate identifies the mechanism and proposes a constructive intervention rather than disputing a probably-correctly-calculated number head-on (A), blaming the research (C), or foreclosing reconciliation (D).

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy for an organisational claim**

A directorate proposes measuring whether a design change improved completion using 40 moderated sessions before and 40 after; baseline is 58%, a 6 point improvement considered meaningful. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 80 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 6 point difference on 40 per group is about two participants and well within ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate through live analytics across the whole user base, and spending the 80 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 60 per group.
- D) Run it and report the observed difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Forty per group cannot distinguish a six-point difference from chance. This tests whether the candidate redirects rather than merely objecting, dividing analytics and moderated work by what each does well — option D is the most damaging at organisational level, since caveated findings get cited without their caveats, establishing a precedent that will be cited back for years.

**Question 12 (harder) — A claim already in a board paper**

A board paper states: "Research confirms 91% of users can complete the service independently," traced to a study of 34 participants, 31 of whom completed, recruited from the departmental panel; the board meets in three days. Ask for the most important thing to do.

- A) Ask for the figure to be removed.
- B) Contact the paper's author, explain that 34 panel-recruited participants cannot support a population rate, propose the accurate statement — that 31 of 34 panel participants completed unaided — and offer what the organisation does have, such as analytics, if the board needs a rate; then confirm the correction is made before the paper is tabled.
- C) Raise it at the board meeting.
- D) Note it for the next assurance review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A qualitative sample from a self-selected panel has become a population statistic in a document informing a decision — a category error. This tests whether the candidate goes to the author first with a specific replacement statement and confirms the correction lands, the step most often omitted, rather than removing without offering a replacement (A), correcting in the most public and least recoverable setting (C), or allowing an unsupported claim to reach the board (D).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies what a claim (a vendor statement, a benefits figure, a board metric) actually covers before treating it as comparable to other evidence.
- Note whether the candidate insists on disaggregation rather than accepting a single blended rate.
- Watch for whether the candidate reconstructs an implied unit value to sanity-check a business case's plausibility.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change and flags an ambiguous label.
- Note whether the candidate corrects a flawed figure already in a decision-facing document by going to the author with a specific replacement and confirming it lands, rather than raising it in the most public and least recoverable setting.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an average of rates that is not weighted by the underlying counts.
- Missing when a candidate compares a vendor rate to an internal rate without checking their denominators describe the same population.
- Rewarding a qualitative sample converted into a population rate without challenge.
- Accepting an underpowered comparison endorsed "with caveats" as an adequate answer.
- Missing when a candidate raises a correction to a board paper at the board itself rather than with the author beforehand.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. At this level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, organisational standards, evaluation and audit reports, ministerial correspondence, board papers, and contracts — a misread exception clause becomes a compliance failure across several teams, and a misread finding becomes advice given confidently to a director.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to mark True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, and expertise actively costs marks at this level: the more governance prose a candidate has read, the more readily they supply the clause they expect to be there. Cannot Say deserves particular attention — it is the answer experienced people avoid most, since forming a view under incomplete information is a deeply embedded professional reflex.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most", "may" is not "must", "recommends considering" is not "recommends". "Solely", "entirely", "unless" and "provided that" govern everything around them.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, setting and assuring standards including ethics and safeguarding.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, directing stakeholder relationship strategy.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Inclusive research**.
- **Reading governance prose accurately** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Directorates must submit an annual research assurance return to the head of profession, unless all research within the directorate has been conducted under a centrally managed programme. Returns must confirm that ethics review has been completed where required, and must list any studies where it was not. Where a directorate identifies a study conducted without required ethics review, it must notify the head of profession within ten working days of identifying it, separately from the annual return. Returns are published internally; notifications are not."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Every directorate must submit an annual research assurance return."

- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception for directorates where all research was conducted under a centrally managed programme. False. This tests catching an absolute-term-versus-exception pattern — note "all", not "most", material for Question 3.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Notifications of missed ethics review are published internally."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states returns are published internally and notifications are not. False. This tests reading past the point a sentence appeared to finish — the operative distinction sits after a semicolon.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A directorate where nine of ten studies ran under a centrally managed programme must submit a return."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where *all* research ran under a centrally managed programme; nine of ten does not satisfy that. True. This tests carrying "all" forward and catching partial satisfaction of an exception treated as full satisfaction.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A study conducted without required ethics review must be notified within ten working days of the study concluding."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Notification is required within ten working days of *identifying* the study, not of its concluding. False. This tests establishing what starts the clock, since misreading a trigger produces false alarm or false comfort.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Research findings must never be changed.
- B) The substance of research must remain independent of stakeholder preference, with adaptation permitted in form, escalation provided where independence is pressured, accountability located with the head of profession, and honesty required where timing fails.
- C) The head of profession is accountable for research integrity.
- D) Reasons must be recorded when findings arrive late.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary spanning the whole passage without inventing anything — A overstates a conditional prohibition into an absolute one, and C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads past the apparent end of a sentence, particularly after a semicolon, for an operative qualification.
- Note whether the candidate carries an absolute qualifier ("all", "entirely") forward to a scenario stated several lines later.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes a duty to escalate from a duty to personally refuse or comply.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes "consider whether X" from "recommend X."
- Note whether the candidate notices a document's implied stance is not the same as its assertions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False from the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate treats partial satisfaction of a compound or absolute exception as full satisfaction.
- Rewarding "consider whether X" read as "recommends X."
- Accepting fewer challenges read as equivalent to greater robustness.
- Missing when a candidate reads a rule about outcomes ("must not be altered") and a rule about conduct ("must be raised") as the same obligation.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. A lead user researcher is an expert practitioner leading and aligning research across several teams, ensuring teams take an evidence-based approach, setting and assuring standards including ethics and safeguarding, negotiating with senior stakeholders, and leading a community of practice.

Two features distinguish this level from a senior practitioner's. First, the candidate is usually acting on systems rather than instances — a candidate who fixes a flawed study has done half the job; asking why the assurance process did not catch it is the other half. Second, authority is largely informal: the candidate does not line-manage most of the people whose practice they assure, cannot compel a directorate, and influences senior stakeholders who outrank them — so options leading with instruction, mandate or escalation as a first move tend to score poorly, since escalation is capital spent rather than a tool used freely.

Four principles carry most scenarios: participants and users come first, including those in research the candidate did not commission; the integrity of the evidence is protected, and so is the person whose job it is to protect it — options that leave a researcher alone under director pressure are weak even when they preserve the finding; capability is built while the problem is solved; and escalation is calibrated to severity — safeguarding and physical risk go up immediately and in parallel, practice problems go to the person first.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Ethics and safeguarding at organisational scale** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.
- **Assurance and capability building** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance** and **User research methods**, leading a community of practice.
- **Strategic alignment** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, aligning programmes to organisational goals.
- **Influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Advocacy with sceptics** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Organisational inclusion** maps to **Inclusive research**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A researcher under pressure from a director**

A senior researcher reports a director has asked them to remove an accessibility finding from a board summary, saying it will "muddy the message." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell them to leave it in and cite your instruction.
- B) Agree with them how the finding should be presented, including a constructive framing with proposed mitigation; offer to attend the meeting; and take the request itself up with the director yourself, since a director asking a researcher to remove findings is a matter at your level, not theirs.
- C) Contact the director and instruct them not to make such requests.
- D) Escalate to the head of profession.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the finding survives, and the researcher emerges more capable and less exposed. Option B builds their judgement, gives them something constructive to bring, and takes up the director conversation personally — a power differential this role exists to absorb. Option A is least effective, leaving them defending by proxy, teaching nothing. Option C treats a conversation as an instruction. Option D escalates something within remit.

**Question 2 (easy) — Standards that exist and are not used**

An organisational research standard nobody follows; reports vary widely, and researchers describe it as unrealistic. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Reissue the standard with a communication from the head of profession.
- B) Find out why it is unrealistic by reviewing recent outputs with researchers, identify which requirements are genuinely unworkable and which are simply unfamiliar, revise the standard with them, and pair it with a lightweight routine — such as peer review — that makes compliance visible and useful rather than administrative.
- C) Introduce mandatory compliance checks.
- D) Provide training on the standard.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard everybody ignores has already failed. Option B treats the objection as data, revises with the people who will defend it, and pairs it with a routine giving compliance a moment of application. Option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act. Option C enforces something already known to be unworkable. Option D is useful and insufficient alone.

**Question 3 (easy) — Community of practice that has gone flat**

The research community of practice has declining attendance, having become a series of passively watched presentations. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Make attendance mandatory.
- B) Ask members what would make it worth their time, and reshape it around problems people currently have — for instance sessions where someone brings a live methodological dilemma, or a critique clinic on real outputs — so attending has an immediate return.
- C) Invite external speakers.
- D) Reduce frequency.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Declining attendance at a voluntary forum is direct feedback that it does not repay the time. Option B rebuilds around utility — a live dilemma or critique clinic gives attendees something usable that afternoon. Option A is least effective, mandating attendance at something people find useless and destroying the voluntary quality that distinguishes a community from a meeting.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate not managed by the candidate has published research whose central claim is unsupported, cited in a business case. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct the record with the business case author.
- B) Speak to the researcher and their lead first, set out specifically what the evidence supports, and agree how the correction will be made and communicated to those already relying on it — with a clear next step if they decline.
- C) Raise it with the head of profession.
- D) Note it for the annual assurance return.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The claim needs correcting and the relationship needs to survive years of continued assurance. Option B goes to the people responsible first and communicates to those already relying on the claim. Option D is least effective, a record rather than an intervention, while a business case is moving now.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A pattern of ethics non-compliance**

Studies in one directorate repeatedly proceeded without required ethics review, always under ministerial deadline pressure, always by experienced researchers. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the breaches and require retrospective review.
- B) Address the specific breaches, and separately investigate the review process itself — how long it takes, what it demands, whether a fast route exists — on the reasoning that experienced researchers who know the requirement and breach it under pressure are telling you the process cannot be complied with when time is short.
- C) Remind all researchers of the ethics requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-fieldwork checkpoint for all studies.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The concentration is the most informative feature. This tests handling both layers — remediation and process investigation. Option C is least effective, the weakest available response to any compliance failure. Option D imposes friction on every compliant study to catch a localised failure.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want the candidate's research capacity in the same quarter — one a failing live service, the other a ministerially visible new programme — both raised with the head of profession. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what research would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and the head of profession together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the ministerial programme.
- D) Ask the head of profession to decide.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests making a trade-off explicit and simultaneously visible to prevent parallel-negotiation dynamics, and making a recommendation rather than presenting neutral options. Option A is least effective, splitting constrained capacity producing two efforts too thin to inform either decision.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Evidence that contradicts a public commitment**

Research across two programmes shows a publicly announced channel closure will substantially disadvantage users without reliable digital access; colleagues propose framing it as "opportunities for future enhancement." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the framing; the commitment is public.
- B) Report the findings accurately and do the constructive work alongside — quantify who is affected, specify what assisted provision would mitigate it, and present finding and mitigation together so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report the findings starkly and let the organisation respond.
- D) Escalate to the head of profession before acting.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The proposed framing protects the organisation from discomfort while the disadvantage occurs anyway. This tests keeping the finding intact and making it actionable with quantification and mitigation. Option A is least effective, the precise failure professional standards exist to prevent.

**Question 8 (harder) — A serious safeguarding risk in another team's fieldwork**

A team is researching a domestic abuse support service, recruiting through the service's own contact list, running video sessions with no protocol for confirming a participant is alone. Ask what should be done.

- A) Raise it with the researcher and offer to help design a safer approach.
- B) Intervene immediately: contact the researcher and their lead, set out the specific risks — that contact via the service's list may reach people whose devices are monitored, and that a video session without an aloneness protocol may expose a participant to danger during the call — press for fieldwork to pause until safeguards are in place, and inform the head of profession in parallel.
- C) Report it to the data protection officer.
- D) Raise it at the next community of practice.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The potential harm is severe, immediate and physical. This tests whether the candidate presses for a pause and informs the head of profession in parallel rather than sequentially, since setting and assuring safeguarding standards is explicitly this role's accountability. Option D is least effective by a wide margin, deferring a live physical-safety risk to a scheduled forum.

**Question 9 (harder) — Strategic insight nobody asked for**

Synthesising across programmes, the candidate concludes the organisation's biggest user-facing problem is in transitions between three services that each work adequately alone, with no director owning it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Report it in your annual synthesis and move on.
- B) Build the case with evidence from all three services, quantify the population affected using support desk and analytics data, identify who would need to own it, and take it to a forum with cross-service authority — accepting that securing an owner for an unowned problem is itself the piece of work.
- C) Raise it with each of the three service owners separately.
- D) Propose a piece of research on the transitions.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Unowned problems are the characteristic failure of service-shaped organisations. This tests recognising the hard part is ownership rather than insight, and taking it to a forum with cross-service authority. Option A is least effective, correct and filed, changing nothing.

**Question 10 (harder) — Assurance failure the candidate signed off**

Six months ago the candidate assured a study whose screener excluded anyone who had contacted the support desk, systematically omitting users who had already struggled; the findings shaped a live service change. Ask what should be done.

- A) Add the case to your assurance guidance as a lesson.
- B) Notify the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are compromised and which stand, commission targeted follow-up with the excluded group, and examine why the assurance process did not catch it — then change the process.
- C) Commission follow-up research quietly and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Raise it with the head of profession and ask how to handle it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A live change is running on the findings. This tests precise scoping, targeted follow-up, and examining why the candidate's own assurance process did not catch it — the element specific to this level. Option C is least effective, concealing a known flaw while a live change proceeds.

**Question 11 (harder) — A capability you cannot buy**

The organisation cannot recruit experienced user researchers; posts stay vacant for months and teams are covered by junior staff working beyond their level. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Keep advertising and escalate the recruitment difficulty.
- B) Treat it as a capability design problem rather than a recruitment problem: restructure how research capacity is deployed so that experienced researchers lead across teams rather than being embedded singly, invest in developing the junior staff you have with structured supervision, and build routes in — apprenticeships, secondments, conversion from adjacent roles — while continuing to recruit.
- C) Lower the experience requirements in job adverts.
- D) Use agency researchers to fill the gaps.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Months of unfilled posts in a competitive market is not solved by more advertising. This tests whether the candidate redesigns around the constraint rather than lowering standards without changing support (C, setting people up to fail) or relying on capacity that leaves when a contract ends (D).

**Question 12 (harder) — Setting a standard that will be resisted**

The candidate intends to require all research outputs to disaggregate findings by access need, and anticipates resistance since teams will object that samples are too small. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Introduce the requirement and address objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is partly correct: with small samples, disaggregation cannot produce rates. Frame the requirement as reporting who was in the sample and what each group experienced, rather than producing group statistics — then pair it with the recruitment support that makes including those participants feasible.
- C) Introduce it as guidance rather than a requirement.
- D) Require it only for studies above a certain sample size.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The anticipated objection is a real methodological point, and a standard ignoring it will be resisted and technically wrong. This tests whether the candidate changes what is required (composition and experience rather than group rates) and pairs it with recruitment support. Option A is least effective, introducing a known-contestable requirement and arguing it case by case.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate changes a system, standard, or set of conditions rather than solving a problem through personal intervention alone.
- Note whether the candidate protects a colleague from having to hold a difficult position alone under senior pressure.
- Watch for whether the candidate calibrates escalation to genuine severity — physical or safeguarding risk immediately and in parallel; practice problems to the person first.
- Score for whether the candidate finds an owner for an unowned organisational problem, rather than filing it in a report where it changes nothing.
- Note whether an own or assured flaw is disclosed promptly, with precise scoping and the underlying process changed so it cannot recur the same way.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that reissues or restates a standard teams have already shown they will not follow.
- Missing when a candidate treats documenting a risk, with no action attached, as equivalent to managing it.
- Rewarding a candidate who solves a problem personally without building a mechanism that survives their attention.
- Accepting a candidate leaving a more junior colleague to hold a position under pressure the candidate should absorb.
- Missing when a candidate files a strategic insight rather than actively finding it an owner.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and systemic diagnosis across studies the candidate did not conduct, cross-programme evidence, and organisational recruitment practice; the arithmetic that supports board-level research argument — disaggregating a blended rate, interrogating a vendor's denominator, and reconstructing an implied unit value to check a business case; the discipline of reading governance frameworks, audit reports and board papers at exactly the strength the text supports; and the situational judgement that defines a strong lead user researcher — changing systems and conditions rather than acting through personal intervention, absorbing pressure downward, and finding an owner for a problem that belongs to no one.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answers consistently act on systems and conditions rather than instances. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
