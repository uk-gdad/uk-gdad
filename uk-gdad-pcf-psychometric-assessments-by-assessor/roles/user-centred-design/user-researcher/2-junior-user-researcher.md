# Junior User Researcher - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for junior user researcher. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A junior user researcher is embedded in a multidisciplinary team to carry out user research activities, with some practical experience behind them but needing regular guidance and training to produce their best work, and working in combination with a more senior researcher. That is a meaningful step up from a trainee: the role description says the candidate plans and conducts a user research project with support, understands when to use basic methods and how to apply them correctly, involves their team in research and in analysis, and presents clear findings colleagues can understand and use. The items are pitched accordingly — expecting a bit more than pure rule-following, asking the candidate to choose between methods and to judge how far a finding can be stretched, while still working in combination with a senior researcher, so the strongest answers rarely involve going it entirely alone.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate chooses a method to fit the actual research question rather than habit, and whether they state a finding at the strength the evidence supports rather than overreaching. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward competent independent action within the candidate's remit, combined with prompt honesty about anything beyond it — neither struggling in silence nor escalating everything.

It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance colleagues.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences: a discussion guide, an analysis grid, a participant tracker, a set of session notes, a short extract from a research plan or a service standard.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a similar level.

For a junior user researcher, this section is relevant because the core of the craft is disciplined noticing — that four participants hesitated at the same screen, that a discussion guide contains a leading question, that an analysis grid has a quote filed under a theme it does not really support, that a tracker shows two sessions booked without recorded consent. At this level the items expect a little more than mechanical rule-application: some ask the candidate to choose between reasonable options, or to judge how strongly a finding can be stated. There is still no memorisation required — every rule, definition and figure needed is inside the question — but judgement is being tested alongside accuracy.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, applying basic techniques for analysing research data and synthesising findings.
- **Error checking** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, explaining the importance of working to a research plan and following standards.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**. Knowing what evidence licenses — and refusing to go beyond it — is the difference between advocacy and assertion.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile research practices**, describing agile working and the roles in a multidisciplinary digital team.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **User research methods**, planning and conducting a project with support, and to **Inclusive research**.
- **Reading instructions exactly** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a discussion guide**

The team's guide-writing standard says questions must: be open rather than closed, avoid suggesting an answer, avoid asking about two things at once, and avoid jargon. Ask how many of the four standards this draft question breaks: "Did you find the streamlined evidence upload journey easy and quick to use?"

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** "Did you" invites a yes or no, breaking the open-question standard. "Streamlined" and "easy and quick" both suggest an answer. It asks about two attributes — easy and quick — in one question, leaving a participant who found it quick but confusing nowhere to go. And "evidence upload journey" is internal jargon a member of the public would not use. All four standards are broken, giving D. This tests whether the candidate completes the full check against every standard rather than stopping once dissatisfied.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a research standard**

The team's standard says: "Findings may be published on the internal research repository only if they have been peer reviewed by another user researcher, unless they have been marked as interim by the lead user researcher." A colleague says: "I published my findings this morning, and they were not peer reviewed." Ask which one must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) The findings were marked as interim by the lead user researcher.
- C) Either the findings were marked as interim, or the standard was breached.
- D) The findings are unreliable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is conditional with one stated exception, and the facts do not establish whether the exception was used. Option D introduces a claim about reliability the item never supports — findings can be unreviewed and still sound. Only C holds in every possible version of events. This tests whether the candidate recognises the "rule plus exception plus incomplete facts" pattern rather than assuming one branch, and whether they'd take the collegial route of checking rather than accusing.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition across an analysis grid**

Across eight sessions, difficulties mentioned: unclear eligibility wording 6; slow page loading 2; could not find contact details 5; confusing document formats 6; session timed out 1. Ask which pair of difficulties has the strongest combined claim to be the focus of the team's next design work.

- A) Slow page loading and session timeout
- B) Unclear eligibility wording and confusing document formats
- C) Could not find contact details and session timeout
- D) Slow page loading and could not find contact details

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Ranking by how many different participants raised each issue, unclear eligibility wording and confusing document formats each stand at six of eight — the highest pair. This tests whether the candidate ranks by breadth across participants rather than raw mention count, and whether they recognise that frequency is a strong signal but not the only one — a rare but locking-out problem can outrank a common irritation.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation with sessions running**

It is 9:00 am, sessions at 10:00, 11:30 and 14:00, senior researcher on leave. (1) the 10:00 participant asks to move to 10:30 for childcare; (2) a developer needs to know by 09:30 which prototype build to use; (3) yesterday's session notes are unwritten; (4) another team's researcher has asked for a discussion guide review this week. Ask what should be done first.

- A) Confirm the prototype build with the developer.
- B) Reply to the 10:00 participant.
- C) Write yesterday's notes while they are fresh.
- D) Review the other team's discussion guide.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 2 has the earlier hard deadline (09:30) and blocks someone else's work — if the wrong build deploys, all three of today's sessions are compromised — and it takes about a minute. Item 1's deadline is 10:00 and can be resolved five minutes later without harm. This tests whether the candidate ranks by deadline and blocking consequence, doing the quick unblocking task first before immediately turning to the participant.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking a participant tracker**

Four records required per session: signed consent, recorded verbal permission, access needs noted, incentive logged. Tracker: P1 all four; P2 missing incentive log; P3 missing verbal permission and access needs; P4 all four; P5 missing signed consent. Ask how many records are missing in total, and how many participants are affected.

- A) 3 records, 3 participants
- B) 4 records, 3 participants
- C) 4 records, 4 participants
- D) 5 records, 3 participants

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Missing records: P2 one, P3 two, P5 one — four total. Affected participants: P2, P3, P5 — three. This tests whether the candidate counts the two different things the question asks for separately, and whether they weight the gaps by seriousness rather than treating them as equivalent — a missing incentive log is administrative, a missing signed consent for a completed session is a governance issue needing escalation the same day.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Choosing between methods**

The team wants to know why users abandon an application at the evidence upload step; analytics show a 42% drop-off there, with two weeks and a modest budget. Ask which approach best fits the question.

- A) A survey sent to everyone who abandoned, asking why they stopped.
- B) Moderated usability sessions in which participants attempt the upload step with their own documents, plus a review of the analytics to select who to recruit.
- C) A/B testing two versions of the upload page.
- D) A card sort to understand how users categorise document types.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question is *why* people abandon — a question about behaviour and reasoning in context — which moderated sessions with participants' own documents can answer directly, since the upload problem may be about file formats, sizes or scanning rather than the interface. This tests whether the candidate matches the method to the question rather than to habit: option A yields unreliable recalled reasons from people unlikely to respond, option C shows which version performs better but not why, and option D answers a different question (categorisation) entirely.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction about strength of findings**

In a study with ten participants, seven struggled to understand the phrase "relevant supporting evidence." Ask which statement is best supported.

- A) Most users of the service do not understand the phrase.
- B) The phrase is confusing and must be changed.
- C) Seven of ten participants struggled with the phrase, which is a strong signal that it needs testing with clearer alternatives.
- D) The phrase is fine because three participants understood it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Ten participants is a solid qualitative sample and seven of ten a strong pattern, but this still describes participants rather than a user population, so option A overreaches. Option B jumps to a mandated solution and removes the team's design judgement. Option D ignores the majority signal. This tests whether the candidate states evidence accurately and proposes a proportionate next step — presenting clear findings colleagues can understand and use is a named skill, and pitching strength correctly is most of what that means.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Pattern recognition with a confound**

Six participants tested a new form: the four who completed it quickly all used a laptop, the two who struggled both used a phone. A colleague concludes: "The form works well on laptops and badly on phones." Ask for the most rigorous response.

- A) Agree — the pattern is clear and consistent.
- B) Note that device is a plausible explanation, but with two phone users it is a hypothesis to test rather than a finding, and other differences between those participants could also explain it.
- C) Reject the idea, since two participants cannot show anything.
- D) Recommend removing phone access until the form is fixed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is real and worth investigating, but two phone users is a thin base and the groups may differ in other ways — digital confidence, age, document format. This tests whether the candidate holds both truths at once — the pattern deserves attention, and it is not yet an established finding — rather than treating it as confirmed (A) or dismissing it (C), or recommending a drastic action on thin evidence (D).

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving mid-session**

Twenty minutes into a session, the candidate realises the prototype link sent to the participant is an older build missing the screens needed, with forty minutes remaining. Ask for the most sensible way to think about this.

- A) End the session and rebook, since the data will be worthless.
- B) Establish what the old build still allows, tell the participant honestly that the wrong version was sent, and either switch them to the correct link or adapt the remaining time to questions the old build can still answer.
- C) Carry on and report the findings as if they related to the new build.
- D) Say nothing and quietly change the topic to a general interview.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separating what has failed (specific screens) from what has not (the participant, their time, forty minutes), option B assesses what is salvageable, is honest, and takes the practical step of switching if quick. This tests whether the candidate avoids the worst of the four options — C corrupts the evidence base by misattributing findings to the wrong build, which nobody downstream may catch — and recognises that being straightforward with the participant costs nothing.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Reading a funnel for research focus**

Analytics: 10,000 users start, 8,500 complete eligibility, 5,100 complete evidence upload, 4,800 submit. Ask where research effort should be focused first, and why.

- A) The start of the journey, because 1,500 users are lost there.
- B) Evidence upload, because 3,400 users are lost there and it is the largest single drop.
- C) Submission, because it is the step closest to the outcome.
- D) All steps equally, since every loss matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Losses: start to eligibility 1,500; eligibility to upload 3,400; upload to submission 300. This tests whether the candidate calculates each step's loss before choosing rather than picking a smaller real loss (A), mistaking proximity to outcome for size of problem (C), or offering a sensible-sounding non-answer (D) — with finite time, the evidence should set the research agenda.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation when working alone**

Senior researcher off sick, at 10:00: (1) a 10:45 participant discloses a hearing impairment and asks about captions; (2) recruitment for next week closes at 12:00, two participants short; (3) a minister's office wants a one-page user needs summary by 16:00, via the delivery manager; (4) a designer wants twenty minutes today before building a screen tomorrow. Ask for the most sensible order.

- A) 3, 2, 1, 4
- B) 1, 2, 4, 3
- C) 2, 3, 1, 4
- D) 1, 3, 2, 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is 45 minutes away and concerns whether a participant can take part at all. Item 2 has a hard 12:00 deadline with an irreversible consequence. Item 4 is short with a real deadline of tomorrow. Item 3's 16:00 deadline is largely drafting work within the candidate's control. This tests whether the candidate ranks by deadline and consequence rather than by prestige (option A), pure clock order that lets the access question slide (option C), or placing a 16:00 drafting task ahead of a 12:00 hard cut-off (option D).

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about sampling**

Three rounds of research on a service used heavily by people with limited English have recruited only fluent English speakers, because recruitment ran through an online panel with an English-only screener and sessions were conducted in English without interpreters. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The sample is fine; the service is in English, so English speakers are the relevant users.
- B) The method itself excludes a key user group at three separate points — panel access, screener language and session language — so the findings cannot speak for those users, and fixing it requires changing recruitment routes, screener design and session delivery together.
- C) Add a note to the report acknowledging the limitation.
- D) Run more sessions through the same panel until some participants with limited English appear.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Exclusion is happening at three distinct points, explaining why three rounds produced the same profile. This tests whether the candidate identifies the full mechanism and the corresponding fixes at each point, rather than defending the status quo (A), documenting without addressing (C), or repeating a filtering method and expecting different results (D). A service delivered in English is still used by people with limited English, who are often the highest-value participants rather than an edge case.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate matches a research method to the actual question being asked, rather than to habit or comfort.
- Note whether the candidate states a finding at the strength the evidence supports, resisting the pull to generalise from participants to users.
- Watch for whether the candidate ranks a crowded morning by deadline and consequence rather than by who is asking.
- Score for whether the candidate holds a striking pattern as a hypothesis to test rather than a confirmed finding, when the underlying sample is thin.
- Note whether the candidate identifies a full, multi-point mechanism behind a systemic exclusion rather than a single cause.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a check that stops at the first fault rather than working through every standard named in the item.
- Missing when a candidate treats a striking small-sample pattern as an established finding.
- Rewarding a candidate who generalises "seven of ten participants" into "most users."
- Accepting a candidate who ranks by seniority rather than by deadline and consequence.
- Missing when a candidate corrupts or misattributes evidence rather than adapting honestly to a mid-session problem.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles the quantitative material this role produces. It is not a maths exam; it measures whether a candidate can read a table correctly, identify which figures matter, choose the right operation, and interpret the result without overstating it. Most platforms provide a calculator.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a small table, chart or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question. At this level the content stays modest — percentages, percentage change, proportions, averages, ratios, reading values from tables — but the expectation rises slightly to interpreting sensibly as well as calculating correctly: several items have a right arithmetic answer and a wrong conclusion attached to it.

Four habits carry the load. Establish what the question is actually asking before looking at the data. Identify the denominator before calculating anything. Estimate roughly, then calculate precisely, then compare the two. And sanity-check the result.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, applying basic analysis techniques.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Inclusive research**, since comparing groups of different sizes fairly is how accessibility gaps become visible.
- **Percentage change** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, showing an improvement accurately and honestly about its size.
- **Averages and spread** map to **User research methods**, reporting a study honestly.
- **Counting, ratios and budgeting** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, tracking the numbers in a plan.
- **Stating figures at the right strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

16 participants, 12 completed the core task without help. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 60%
- B) 67%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 12 ÷ 16 = 75%, giving C. This tests basic percentage calculation and whether the candidate sanity-checks with a rough estimate — 12 of 16 is clearly three quarters, catching a mis-typed calculation such as 12 ÷ 18 (giving B) or 12 ÷ 15 (giving D).

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruiting for no-shows**

No-show rate running at 20%, and 12 completed sessions are needed. Ask roughly how many participants should be booked.

- A) 13
- B) 14
- C) 15
- D) 18

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 20% do not attend, 80% do, so 12 ÷ 0.8 = 15, giving C. This tests whether the candidate recognises that adding 20% to the target (giving 14.4, tempting towards B) is not the same as allowing for 20% of bookings failing, since the 20% applies to the larger number — a genuinely useful calculation for planning recruitment.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a comparison table**

Completion of 20 participants: Task A 17, B 11, C 20, D 15. Ask which task had a completion rate of exactly 55%.

- A) Task A
- B) Task B
- C) Task C
- D) Task D

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 55% of 20 is 11, matching Task B. This tests whether the candidate converts the target percentage into a number of people first, faster than calculating four separate rates, and spots that with a total of 20, each participant is worth exactly 5 percentage points.

**Question 4 (easy) — Mean session length**

Six sessions lasted 55, 60, 45, 50, 65, and 45 minutes. Ask for the mean.

- A) 50 minutes
- B) 53 minutes
- C) 55 minutes
- D) 57 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The total is 320, divided by 6 gives 53.3, giving B. This tests basic averaging and whether the candidate uses an efficient method under time pressure — the deviation shortcut (guessing 55, summing differences of 0, +5, −10, −5, +10, −10 = −10, dividing by 6 and subtracting) reaches the same answer with smaller numbers.

**Question 5 (easy) — Ratio in a research plan**

A plan requires a 3:2 ratio of existing users to new users, with 20 participants needed. Ask how many should be new users.

- A) 6
- B) 8
- C) 12
- D) 14

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 3:2 ratio divides 20 into 5 shares of 4; new users take two shares (8), giving B. Option C answers the other half of the split. This tests whether the candidate writes down what each share represents before calculating and re-reads the question to confirm which group is being asked for.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups of unequal size**

18 of 24 non-assistive-technology participants completed a task; 3 of 8 assistive-technology participants completed it. Ask for the gap in completion rates, in percentage points.

- A) 15
- B) 25
- C) 37.5
- D) 40

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 18 ÷ 24 = 75%; 3 ÷ 8 = 37.5%; the gap is 37.5 points, giving C. This tests whether the candidate converts to rates before comparing rather than comparing raw counts of 18 and 3, which is meaningless across differently sized groups — and whether they notice the deliberate near-coincidence that 37.5 is both a rate and the gap, checking which quantity was actually asked for.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Task completion rose from 48 to 60 participants (same total each round). Ask for the percentage increase.

- A) 12%
- B) 20%
- C) 25%
- D) 60%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The difference is 12, divided by the original 48 gives 25%, giving C. Option B divides by the new value (12 ÷ 60), the single most common error in this question type. This tests whether the candidate fixes the rule that the denominator for percentage change is always where the change started.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a total**

15% of survey respondents abandoned an application for inability to upload a document, representing 54 people. Ask for the total number of respondents.

- A) 270
- B) 324
- C) 360
- D) 810

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 54 ÷ 0.15 = 360, giving C. This tests whether the candidate can work backwards from a percentage to a total, verifying by reversing (15% of 360 is 54) — a calculation that matters because stakeholders quote percentages far more often than base numbers.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the right denominator**

A funnel: 12,000 users start, 9,000 reach identity check, 6,300 reach evidence upload, 5,670 submit. Ask what percentage of users who reached evidence upload went on to submit.

- A) 47%
- B) 63%
- C) 76%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 5,670 ÷ 6,300 = 90%, giving D. Option A uses 12,000 as the denominator, answering an unasked question about the whole funnel. This tests whether the candidate identifies the group the question names before calculating — a trap with real consequences for how a finding gets communicated.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Weighted thinking**

Round one: 6 participants, mean score 4.0. Round two: 14 participants, mean score 3.0. Ask for the mean score across all 20 participants.

- A) 3.0
- B) 3.3
- C) 3.5
- D) 3.7

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Round one contributes 24 points (6 × 4.0), round two 42 (14 × 3.0); total 66 ÷ 20 = 3.3, giving B. Option C averages the two means directly, ignoring that round two had more than twice as many participants. This tests whether the candidate returns to totals rather than averaging averages from groups of different sizes — one of the most frequently mishandled calculations in real reporting.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentage change**

Completion rose from 25% to 40% after a redesign. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) Completion rose by 15%.
- B) Completion rose by 15 percentage points, a 60% relative increase.
- C) Completion rose by 60 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose by 37.5%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap is 15 percentage points; the relative increase is 15 ÷ 25 = 60%. Option B labels both correctly. This tests a distinction with real stakes: "completion rose 60%" and "completion rose 15 percentage points" describe the identical change, and the first sounds roughly four times more impressive — choosing which to report, and labelling it honestly, is an integrity question as much as a numerical one.

**Question 12 (harder) — When the average misleads**

Eight participants took 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, and 46 minutes. A colleague reports "the average task time was 9 minutes." Ask for the best response.

- A) The calculation is wrong; the average is 4 minutes.
- B) The calculation is right, but the mean is a poor summary here: seven of eight participants finished within 5 minutes, and one extreme case is pulling the figure up. Report the median of 4 minutes with the range, and describe the 46-minute session separately.
- C) The figure is fine as reported.
- D) The 46-minute session should be excluded as an anomaly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic (72 ÷ 8 = 9) is correct, ruling out A, but no participant took anywhere near 9 minutes. This tests whether the candidate recognises a mean distorted by an outlier and proposes the right fix — the median with the range, and the outlier described separately — rather than letting a misleading figure stand (C) or excluding an inconvenient data point (D), which is the most damaging answer since that session is probably the most interesting result in the dataset.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator before calculating, particularly in funnel or comparison items.
- Note whether the candidate returns to totals rather than averaging averages from groups of unequal size.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes a percentage-point change from a relative percentage change and labels both.
- Score for whether the candidate flags a mean distorted by an outlier and proposes reporting the distribution instead.
- Note whether the candidate sanity-checks a calculated answer against a rough estimate.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage change calculated by dividing by the new figure rather than the original.
- Missing when a candidate averages two means from groups of different sizes without weighting.
- Rewarding raw-count comparisons across groups of different sizes instead of converted rates.
- Accepting an over-recruitment calculation that adds a no-show percentage to the target rather than dividing by the attendance rate.
- Missing when a candidate excludes an inconvenient data point rather than reporting a distribution honestly.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. User research is document-driven work — service standards, ethics guidance, policy summaries, accessibility requirements, discussion guides, consent forms, stakeholder emails, and the words participants actually say — and reading any of it slightly wrong changes what a researcher does next.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone — a candidate's own knowledge of research ethics, data protection or the GOV.UK Service Manual must be set aside. Cannot Say deserves particular attention, since candidates avoid it, reaching for True or False to appear decisive, when it is very often correct. Small words carry the meaning: "all" is not "most", "may" is not "must", "should" is not "will", "subject to" and "unless" flag conditions that change everything downstream.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, following standards as written rather than as remembered.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, presenting clear findings colleagues can use without a smuggled-in inference.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **Inclusive research**.
- **Following written method descriptions exactly** maps to **User research methods**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Teams must publish a research plan before fieldwork begins. The plan must state the research questions, the methods to be used, and the recruitment approach. Where a team intends to research with participants who may be considered vulnerable, the plan must also include a safeguarding section, approved by the lead user researcher. Plans may be revised during fieldwork, provided that any revision is dated and the reason recorded."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "A research plan must be published before fieldwork starts."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly. True. This is a quick item — a fact stated plainly, tested plainly, worth answering confidently to bank time for harder items.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Every research plan must contain a safeguarding section."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage requires a safeguarding section only where the team intends to research with participants who may be considered vulnerable. The statement extends this to every plan, which the passage contradicts. False. This tests whether the candidate checks an absolute term ("every") against the condition the passage actually attached.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A plan revised in week two of fieldwork without a recorded reason complies with the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence permits revision only on condition that the revision is dated and the reason recorded; a revision without a recorded reason fails one of the two conditions. False. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes a failed condition (False) from an unmentioned matter (Cannot Say) — the structure tested more sharply in Question 4.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "The research plan must state the number of participants."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage lists three required items plus a conditional fourth; participant numbers are not among them, but a list of requirements is not automatically exhaustive, and the passage never says numbers are excluded. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate resists the instinct that a recruitment approach "usually implies" a number — implication is not the same as statement.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The evaluation found that participants recruited through community organisations were more likely to report difficulty with the online form than those recruited through the research panel. The evaluation team notes that the two groups differed in several respects, including age and prior use of digital services. Further work is planned for the autumn, funding permitting. The team recommends that recruitment for future rounds should draw on both routes."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Participants recruited through community organisations reported more difficulty than panel participants."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly. True. This tests whether the candidate respects the passage's careful "reported difficulty" rather than converting it into an objective claim about performance, such as "found it harder to use", which would shift the answer to Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The difference in reported difficulty was caused by the recruitment route."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage records an association and explicitly flags confounding differences (age, prior digital experience), so causation is not established. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate avoids treating an association as a cause when the passage itself names the confound.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "Further work will take place in the autumn."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The work is planned "funding permitting" — a conditional flag of the same family as "subject to" or "provided that." Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate treats a conditional plan as a certainty.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The evaluation team believes a single recruitment route is insufficient for future rounds."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Recommending both routes is, in substance, saying either alone would be inadequate. True. This tests whether the candidate recognises a legitimate restatement of the passage's position, distinguishing it from an added claim such as "the panel route is unreliable", which would be a Cannot Say.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Researchers should offer participants a choice of session format, including telephone and face-to-face options where practicable. Remote sessions must not be the only option offered for research on services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Incentives should be offered in a form the participant can readily use; vouchers requiring an online account are not suitable for all participants. Where a participant withdraws, any data already collected should be deleted unless it has been fully anonymised."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Remote sessions may sometimes be the only format offered."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The prohibition is specific — remote-only is barred for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion — implying it is not barred elsewhere. True. This tests whether the candidate reads the scope of a prohibition accurately, distinguishing "must not be the only option for X" from "must never be the only option."

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "Vouchers are never a suitable incentive."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says vouchers requiring an online account are not suitable for *all* participants — meaning some cannot use them, not that nobody can. False. This tests whether the candidate reads "not suitable for all" correctly rather than as "suitable for none", a construction test writers deliberately exploit.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "If a participant withdraws, all data collected from them must be deleted."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage requires deletion "unless it has been fully anonymised" and uses "should" rather than "must" — the statement drops the exception and hardens the verb. False. This tests whether the candidate checks both an exception clause and a verb strength before accepting an apparently faithful summary.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Remote research is generally inappropriate for government services.
- B) Participants should be able to take part, and be rewarded, in ways that work for them, with their withdrawal respected — and research practice should adapt accordingly.
- C) Incentives must be carefully chosen.
- D) Researchers must delete data when participants withdraw.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement. Options C and D are each true of a single sentence, and D drops the anonymisation exception. Option B captures all four elements: format choice, protection for people at risk of digital exclusion, usable incentives, and respect for withdrawal. This tests whether the candidate selects an answer broad enough to cover the whole passage and disciplined enough to add nothing.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate answers from the passage alone rather than from professional knowledge of ethics or data protection.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a failed condition ("provided that", broken) from an unmentioned topic (silent).
- Watch for whether the candidate treats an association the passage flags as confounded as established causation.
- Score for whether the candidate reads "not suitable for all" as "not suitable for some" rather than "suitable for none."
- Note whether the candidate selects a true but partial detail as a passage's main point.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer drawn from the candidate's own knowledge rather than from the passage.
- Missing when a candidate avoids Cannot Say and reaches for True or False to seem more decisive.
- Rewarding a candidate who accepts a hardened verb ("should" read as "must") as equivalent to the original.
- Accepting a claim of causation where the passage names a confound and reports only an association.
- Missing when a candidate treats a passage's silence on a topic as equivalent to a prohibition.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level, calibrated to a junior: embedded in a multidisciplinary team, carrying out research activities, with some practical experience, needing regular guidance, working in combination with a more senior researcher. The strongest answers usually involve competent independent action within remit, combined with prompt honesty about anything beyond it.

Four principles carry most scenarios: participants and users come first; the candidate acts within their remit and is transparent at its edges — do what is competently possible, then tell the right person promptly; problems are addressed directly, early, and with the person involved; and the strongest options both fix the immediate instance and improve the system.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Ethical judgement and participant care** map to **Inclusive research** and the ethical core of **User research methods**.
- **Working to a plan and surfacing deviations** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.
- **Involving colleagues in research and analysis** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** and **Agile research practices**.
- **Handling stakeholder requests, pressure and disagreement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Presenting findings honestly and usefully** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** and **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Knowing when to ask** maps to this role level, which states the candidate works in combination with a more senior researcher and needs regular guidance.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A participant becomes distressed**

Facilitating a session, the participant describes an application made after losing their job and becomes tearful. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Continue with the next task to help them move past it.
- B) Pause, acknowledge it gently, remind them they can stop or take a break at any time, and let them decide how to continue.
- C) End the session immediately.
- D) Offer advice about the benefits they might be entitled to.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Option B acknowledges what is happening, restores control to the participant, and lets them choose — many want to continue, and being asked rather than rescued is respectful. Option A steamrollers someone who has just shown distress. Option C removes agency in the opposite direction, though it becomes right for serious distress or an explicit request to stop. Option D is least effective, taking the candidate outside their role and competence — the right move is a prepared signposting list offered at the end, not improvised advice mid-session.

**Question 2 (easy) — Asked to use an unfamiliar method**

A senior researcher suggests running a diary study and asks the candidate to lead it, though they have only read about diary studies and never run one. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept, and work it out as they go.
- B) Accept, saying they have not run one before and asking for a review of their plan and a check-in after the first two days.
- C) Decline and suggest usability sessions instead, which they know well.
- D) Accept, then ask a peer in another team to plan it for them.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The role explicitly involves planning and conducting research with support, so option B is that description in action — accepting the opportunity, naming the gap honestly, and making two cheap, specific requests that catch the two points where diary studies most often go wrong. Option A risks a fortnight of unusable data. Option C is least effective, swapping a method chosen for the research question for one chosen for comfort. Option D outsources development and misrepresents the candidate's capability.

**Question 3 (easy) — An observer wants to join at short notice**

A policy colleague messages an hour before a session asking to observe; the consent form told the participant only "members of the research team" would be present. Ask what should be done.

- A) Let them join; observers are normal.
- B) Explain that the participant's agreement is needed, ask the participant at the start whether they are comfortable with an observer, and proceed accordingly — briefing the observer beforehand on staying muted.
- C) Refuse, since the consent form does not cover it.
- D) Let them join without telling the participant, to avoid making it awkward.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Consent has to be informed, and a participant who agreed to "the research team" has not agreed to a policy official watching. Option B asks the participant, gives a genuine chance to decline, and briefs the observer, since an unbriefed observer unmuting is a common way sessions get derailed. Option A skips the participant's agreement. Option C is over-rigid when a clear verbal request would suffice. Option D is least effective, a straightforward breach of trust with damage extending well beyond one session if discovered.

**Question 4 (easy) — Conflicting demands on the morning**

A senior researcher asks for the analysis grid this morning; the delivery manager then asks for help with recruitment instead, calling it urgent. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Do the recruitment, since the delivery manager is more senior.
- B) Do the analysis grid and tell the delivery manager they are unavailable.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask them and the senior researcher to confirm the priority.
- D) Attempt both and work through lunch.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two legitimate instructions have collided, and while resolving it is not the candidate's to impose, surfacing it promptly and helpfully is. Offering a specific option ("recruitment this morning, grid ready by three") turns the candidate from a bottleneck into a problem-solver. Option A ranks by seniority. Option B is abrupt and leaves an urgent need unmet. Option D is least effective, hiding the conflict and normalising over-commitment.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Findings a colleague dislikes**

Findings show a feature a designer has worked on for weeks caused problems for five of seven participants; the designer responds that the sample was too small and unrepresentative. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Concede that the sample was small and offer to redo the research.
- B) Acknowledge the limits of a seven-person sample honestly, then walk through what participants actually did rather than what they said, and propose testing again with a wider sample to check the pattern.
- C) Defend the research firmly and point out that five of seven is a strong signal.
- D) Take it offline and discuss with the senior researcher before responding.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The objection is partly legitimate and partly defensive. Option B concedes the real limit, shifts the conversation to observed behaviour rather than opinion, and proposes a further round without needing to sort the objection's motives apart. Option A capitulates and implies the research was worthless. Option C is factually right but risks the working relationship, and a defensive designer will not act on findings anyway. Option D avoids a conversation the candidate is capable of having; do B now, and debrief the senior researcher afterwards.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A gap in the research plan**

Halfway through fieldwork, the plan makes no provision for screen reader users and the prototype has never been tested with one, with four sessions remaining. Ask what should be done.

- A) Complete the four sessions as planned and note the gap in the report.
- B) Raise it with the senior researcher today, propose recruiting at least one screen reader user for the remaining sessions or a follow-up round, and check whether the prototype is testable with assistive technology at all.
- C) Test the prototype with a screen reader personally and report what is found as a finding.
- D) Pause the study until the gap is fixed.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Option B involves the person who can authorise a change, proposes a concrete and proportionate fix, and checks a prerequisite that could waste a participant's time if untrue. Option A is least effective, documenting a gap that there was still time to close. Option C confuses personal exploration with research — a non-daily-user's experience of a screen reader tells almost nothing about how a daily user would fare. Option D is disproportionate when four sessions can still yield value.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Asked for a percentage without the data**

At a show and tell, a senior stakeholder asks: "So what percentage of users can complete this?" The study was eight qualitative sessions. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Give an estimate based on the sessions — around 60%.
- B) Explain that qualitative sessions show what problems exist and why, not how common they are across the user base, describe what was found, and suggest analytics or a survey if a rate is needed.
- C) Say the answer is not known.
- D) Say the question cannot be answered.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's question is reasonable, just aimed at the wrong data. Option B explains the distinction in one sentence, delivers real value, and points at the method that would answer the actual question. Option A is least effective, manufacturing a statistic — "around 60% of users" — that will outlive every caveat once it reaches a slide. Options C and D are honest but unhelpful, and D is also wrong since the question is answerable with different data.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A colleague's practice concerns the candidate**

Observing a colleague's session, several leading questions are asked, then the debrief summarises the participant as having "loved the new design," which does not match what was observed. Ask what should be done.

- A) Correct them in the debrief in front of the team.
- B) Speak to them privately, describe the specific questions and the specific summary that concerned you, and ask how they saw it.
- C) Say nothing; it is not the candidate's session.
- D) Mention it to the senior researcher without speaking to the colleague.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Option B is private, specific and genuinely open — describing behaviour rather than character makes it possible to hear, and asking how they saw it allows for missing context. Option A embarrasses a colleague publicly, reliably producing defensiveness. Option C is least effective, letting an inaccurate summary stand where a design decision may be made on it. Option D bypasses a peer who has had no chance to respond, reasonable only if the pattern repeats after being raised, or the issue is serious.

**Question 9 (harder) — A safeguarding concern**

During a session about a housing service, a participant mentions in passing that they are currently sleeping in their car and have not eaten that day; the study is about the online form. Ask what should be done.

- A) Continue with the discussion guide, since the comment is not relevant to the research question.
- B) Acknowledge what they have said with care, check whether they want to continue, offer the support signposting information the team holds, and report the disclosure to the senior researcher straight after the session in line with the safeguarding process.
- C) Stop the session and call a support service on their behalf.
- D) Note it in the findings as evidence of user need and move on.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A disclosure like this outranks the research question absolutely. Option B acknowledges the person, restores their control by checking whether to continue, signposts without acting as an untrained support worker, and reports afterwards so the organisation's safeguarding process — not a junior researcher alone — carries the decision. Option A is least effective, treating a person's crisis as off-topic. Option C removes their agency. Option D converts distress into a data point.

**Question 10 (harder) — Pressure to change a finding**

A report states three of eight participants could not complete identity verification; a senior stakeholder asks for the finding to be removed from the summary slide "because it will derail the meeting" and moved to the appendix. Ask what should be done.

- A) Move it to the appendix as asked.
- B) Keep it in the summary, and offer to present it alongside a proposed next step so the meeting has something to decide rather than just a problem to react to.
- C) Refuse and say the finding stays where it is.
- D) Escalate immediately to the senior researcher without responding.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's worry about derailing the meeting is real, but hiding the finding is the wrong solution. Option B addresses the underlying worry directly by arriving with a proposed next step, converting confrontation into decision. Option A is least effective, burying a finding about identity verification — often where the most excluded users are lost — leading to a decision on an incomplete picture. Option C holds the line but offers nothing. Option D is worth doing as well, but B is within the candidate's competence as a first move.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of the candidate's own**

The candidate realises they used an old version of the discussion guide for the last three sessions, missing two questions the team agreed were essential. Ask what should be done.

- A) Add the two questions for the remaining sessions and say nothing about the first three.
- B) Tell the senior researcher and the team immediately, explain exactly which sessions are affected, and propose options — adding the questions now, and either a short follow-up with the three participants or noting the limitation clearly in the analysis.
- C) Contact the three participants personally to ask the missing questions.
- D) Note it in the report's limitations section at the end of the study.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three sessions are missing data the team specifically agreed mattered, affecting what the analysis can support. Option B reports promptly, states the scope precisely, and comes with options, turning an admission of error into a display of competence. Option A is least effective, concealing an inconsistency nobody else knows about. Option C is not unreasonable in principle but should not be taken unilaterally, given consent and burden implications. Option D delays disclosure until the point of least usefulness.

**Question 12 (harder) — Research being squeezed out**

Over three sprints, research findings have arrived after the relevant design decisions were already made, with polite responses but no change. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept it; the team's pace is not something a junior researcher can change.
- B) Bring specific examples to the senior researcher and to the team's retrospective, showing where earlier findings would have changed a decision, and propose a concrete change such as a short research slot before design decisions are locked.
- C) Work faster so findings arrive sooner.
- D) Stop doing research until the team commits to acting on it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This is a systemic problem needing evidence and a forum, not effort or protest. Option B supplies specific examples that make the cost visible, uses the team's own retrospective mechanism, and proposes a concrete process change. Option A gives up on something the role genuinely can influence. Option C treats a timing problem as a speed problem. Option D is least effective, punishing users rather than the process and damaging relationships needed for the fix.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate combines competent independent action within their remit with prompt, honest communication about what falls outside it.
- Note whether the candidate puts participant wellbeing, consent or safeguarding ahead of the research question or the schedule.
- Watch for whether the candidate addresses a problem directly and privately with the person involved.
- Score for whether the candidate's answer both resolves the immediate instance and proposes a system-level fix.
- Note whether the candidate reports their own mistake promptly, precisely, and with options attached, rather than concealing or delaying it.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that ranks a request by seniority rather than by consequence.
- Missing when a candidate either escalates everything or escalates nothing, rather than attempting what is within competence and flagging the rest.
- Rewarding silence or deference over a direct, private, non-accusatory conversation.
- Accepting a manufactured statistic ("around 60% of users") drawn from a small qualitative sample.
- Missing when a candidate conceals or delays disclosure of their own mistake rather than reporting it promptly with options.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and careful checking against the everyday materials of a research team — discussion guides, analysis grids, participant trackers, funnel data; the numeric habits that keep a study's reporting honest — finding the right denominator, distinguishing a percentage point from a percentage, weighting an aggregate correctly, and noticing when a mean conceals an outlier; the discipline of reading a standard or a stakeholder email exactly as written, including its exceptions, its qualifiers and its confounds; and the situational judgement that defines a strong junior user researcher — competent independent action within remit, prompt honesty about what falls outside it, and fixing both the instance and the system.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and the strongest answers consistently match method to question and state findings at the strength the evidence supports. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
