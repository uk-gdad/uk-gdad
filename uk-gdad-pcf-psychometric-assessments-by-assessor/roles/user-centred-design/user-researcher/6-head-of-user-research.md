# Head of User Research - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of user research. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A head of user research leads user researchers in an organisation and attracts and builds talent — an expert practitioner who defines and assures best practice, influences organisational strategy and priorities, and collaborates with colleagues across government. It is a role in which most of what a candidate does is exercised through other people, and their own hands-on research is now the smallest part of their contribution.

Four features shape the material below. First, the candidate is accountable for the quality of research produced by people they did not train, on services they have not seen, using evidence they will never inspect directly — making diagnostic reasoning, inferring the shape of a study from the traces it leaves in an output, a core competence. Second, the role produces strategic insight by synthesising across programmes, holding several incomplete and partly contradictory pictures in mind long enough to notice what they jointly imply. Third, the role negotiates with people who substantially outrank them, in forums where research is one input among many and rarely the loudest, so influence rests almost entirely on evidence being unassailable and judgement being trusted. Fourth, the role builds capability — the profession left behind matters more than any study assured.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate diagnoses a systemic mechanism behind evidence assembled at organisational distance, reads a distribution of failures diagnostically, and interrogates a quantitative claim (a vendor statement, a business case, a board paper) for what it actually establishes. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward action that changes a system or a set of conditions rather than solving a problem personally, absorbs organisational pressure that should not land on a more junior colleague, and finds an owner for a problem nobody currently owns.

## Advisory

This assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance colleagues.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-profession level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is almost entirely diagnostic and strategic: evidence assembled by other people for other purposes, standards applied unevenly across an organisation, findings that appear to conflict until what each measured is established, and prioritisation problems where the units are programmes and capabilities rather than tasks.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority.

Three things characterise this section at this level. First, reasoning at a distance — almost never seeing raw data, only a report, a slide, a claim in a board paper, a summary of a summary, so reading an output for the shape of the study behind it is a specific and trainable skill. Second, the interesting questions are usually about mechanisms rather than instances — when something goes wrong repeatedly, the distribution of failures is the informative feature, and reading distributions diagnostically is what separates organisational leadership from very senior practice. Third, prioritisation is genuinely hard because nothing dominates — programmes differ in time horizon, reversibility, population affected and political weight, and the discriminating factor is whether a candidate reasons from value of information rather than importance, urgency or visibility.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing evidence at a distance** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, assuring user research quality across the organisation.
- **Cross-programme synthesis** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, producing strategic insights to inform the organisation's roadmap.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to this role level's requirement to influence organisational strategy, and to **Agile research practices**.
- **Diagnosing systemic problems** maps to **User research methods** and **Analysis and synthesis** at organisational scale.
- **Reasoning about exclusion structurally** maps to **Inclusive research**, guiding organisations to adopt inclusive practices.
- **Reasoning about positions and incentives** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and collaborating with colleagues across government.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

The organisational standard requires every research output to state: research questions, method, sample composition including access needs, analysis approach, limitations, and date. An output states the research questions, method, analysis approach and date, describes its sample as "18 participants recruited through the central supplier," and has no limitations section. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four requirements are met. The sample description names a count and a source but nothing about composition or access needs, so that fails; limitations are absent, so that fails too — two unmet, giving B. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes provenance from composition — "18 from the central supplier" describes provenance, not composition, exactly what the standard exists to prevent being substituted. A strong candidate's answer also notes that access needs and limitations are the two most commonly omitted fields across organisations, pointing to a template fix rather than an instance fix.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

A standard states: "Research with participants who may be considered vulnerable requires an approved safeguarding protocol, unless the research is conducted entirely through an organisation covered by a standing partnership agreement." A directorate reports: "Our study with people experiencing homelessness ran last quarter without an approved protocol." Ask which must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) The study ran through a standing partnership organisation.
- C) Either the study ran entirely through a standing partnership organisation, or the standard was breached.
- D) Participants were exposed to risk.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** With the exception compound (entirely, and covered by a standing agreement) and the facts silent on whether it applied, only the disjunction is guaranteed. Option D is the distractor built for someone with this role's responsibilities — proceeding without a protocol is a governance failure, and whether anyone was actually exposed to risk is a separate empirical question the facts do not settle. This tests whether the candidate keeps the two apart, and reads "entirely" precisely: partial routing through a partnership does not qualify.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading a study from its output**

A report states: "The service is trusted. All 22 participants said they were confident their data would be handled properly." Recruitment was from the departmental panel, sessions held in departmental offices, and the confidence question asked by the facilitator as the closing item. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) Twenty-two participants is too small a base for a trust measure.
- B) Panel recruitment, departmental setting and a direct closing question from the facilitator all bias in the same direction towards socially desirable responding, so unanimity is evidence about the conditions rather than about the service.
- C) Confidence about data handling is not the same construct as trust.
- D) A validated trust scale should have been used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unanimity is the alarm, and this tests naming exactly what each condition removed and how they compound. Option C is legitimate and secondary; option A misdiagnoses size as the issue when 220 participants under identical conditions would produce the same result; option D leaves all three biases untouched — a directorate that swaps in a validated scale while keeping the panel and setting will produce the same finding with better packaging.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation across programmes**

Substantial capacity for one of four programmes this quarter: (1) Programme A chooses a service model next month, committing five years of build; (2) Programme B runs a live service with a persistent 30% drop-off affecting 400,000 users annually; (3) Programme C launches in five weeks with designs already signed off; (4) Programme D is preparing a spending review submission due in eight months. Ask which is strongest.

- A) B, because it affects the most users.
- B) A, because the decision is imminent, commits five years, and research is the only input that could still change it — giving it much the highest value of information.
- C) D, because spending review submissions shape long-term investment.
- D) C, because launching untested carries reputational risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Programme A is highest on both decision leverage and remaining time. This tests ranking by value of information rather than current scale (A) or profile (C, the profile trap) — Programme C is weakest despite sounding prudent, since signed-off designs five weeks from launch produce findings with nowhere to go.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-programme synthesis**

Three items in a month: a discovery finds people struggle to prove identity without a passport or driving licence; analytics show 45% drop-off at identity verification in another service; the contact centre reports identity queries as its highest call driver across five services. Ask for the strongest synthesis.

- A) Three teams have found usability problems at their identity steps.
- B) The organisation has a cross-cutting identity assurance problem concentrated among people without common photographic documents — a strategic capability question for the organisation's strategy rather than five separate service fixes.
- C) Identity verification is hard and should be simplified.
- D) The findings are unrelated.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a shared mechanism and reframing the unit of the problem as belonging in organisational strategy rather than five backlogs, where each team would build a partial workaround, leaving the excluded population excluded five times over. Option C is true and strategically useless.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Scope of a claim heading for strategy**

An insight report drawing on fourteen studies contains the headline "users across our services want proactive notification," supported by material from four studies, all of services with statutory deadlines. Ask for the most useful intervention.

- A) Remove the claim.
- B) Note that the evidence comes exclusively from deadline-driven services, so the claim over-reaches as written; narrow it, or test whether it holds where there is no deadline, before it informs investment.
- C) Commission more studies.
- D) Accept it; four studies is a reasonable base.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and the claim reaches further than the evidence goes, unusually consequential since strategy claims justify spending and, once in a strategy, get quoted for years. This tests preserving the finding while correcting its scope rather than accepting an over-generalised claim (D).

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Eight teams consistently report that research recruitment produces digitally confident participants, despite services serving many who are not; all eight use the central recruitment contract. Ask for the strongest diagnosis and response.

- A) Each team should supplement locally.
- B) The contract's supply model is structurally mismatched to the organisation's user base; document the pattern across all eight, specify what suitable supply would look like, take it to the contract owner, and enable partnership routes meanwhile.
- C) The teams need better screeners.
- D) Bring recruitment in-house.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eight teams with the same problem and supplier is one supply problem. This tests diagnosing at the level that produces a fix a contract owner can act on while unblocking teams immediately. Option C misdiagnoses, since a screener filters a pool that does not contain the participant type at all.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study under assurance used a prototype whose save-and-return function was broken throughout. Four findings: (i) participants could not locate eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned when interrupted; (iii) sessions ran long; (iv) participants misread the declaration. Ask which are compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; the fault was incidental.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, since inability to save and return plausibly produced both, while (i) and (iv) do not.
- D) Only (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A broken save function directly manufactures (ii) and plausibly extends (iii); content comprehension findings are untouched. This tests scoping precisely rather than wholesale condemnation (A), which at organisational scale teaches teams to conceal faults, leaving future assurance working from edited versions.

**Question 9 (harder) — Apparent contradiction between services**

Two teams researching adjacent services reach opposite conclusions about the same user group: one reports a preference for completing in one sitting, the other for saving and returning. Both studies appear sound. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Commission a third study.
- B) Establish what differs between the contexts before treating it as a contradiction — a preference about a fifteen-minute task and one about a three-week process involving third-party documents are answers to different questions, and the synthesis is that preference depends on whether the task can be completed with information the user already holds.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apparent contradictions between sound studies are usually context differences in disguise. This tests reaching a synthesis more valuable than either finding — a generalisable rule this role exists to extract and propagate across the organisation. Option C abdicates that synthesis.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

Accessibility assurance is required before public beta; fifteen of nineteen qualifying services complied, and the four that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced practitioners. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by people who know the requirement indicates a process that cannot be complied with under deadline conditions — investigate what it demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the four alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-beta gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests reading concentrated failure as diagnostic of a process rather than of conduct, while the remediation clause preserves that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unaddressed. Option D imposes friction on every compliant service to catch a localised failure.

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for an investment decision**

Evidence on investing in shared identity capability: qualitative findings from three services; analytics showing high drop-off across five; a supplier claim of 94% first-time verification success; a contact centre cost model. Ask for the most rigorous contribution.

- A) Present the internal evidence supporting investment.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that the supplier's 94% requires interrogation of its denominator and population before it can sit alongside internal drop-off data — and name the one or two unknowns that would most change the decision.
- C) Recommend investment on the converging evidence.
- D) Recommend further research before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests making the evidence base legible rather than advocating or delaying — noting that converging evidence can share a blind spot, and that a supplier's 94% likely excludes precisely the population driving the organisation's drop-off. Option D reflexively calling for more research is how heads of profession find themselves excluded from decisions.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your own organisation**

A year's research outputs show two teams consistently strong and six consistently weak; both strong teams contain researchers who came from the same previous employer. Ask for the most useful inference and action.

- A) The six weaker teams need training.
- B) Something transferable is likely operating in the two strong teams — a routine, a habit, a standard applied before circulation — and the highest-value action is to find out what it actually is by examining how they work, then build it into the organisation's standards and coaching rather than attributing the difference to individual talent.
- C) Recruit more researchers from that employer.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the six weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests treating a pattern as an opportunity to identify and propagate a routine rather than attributing it to individual pedigree (C, an unusable inference from a handful of people). Option D catches more problems and creates no capacity to prevent them.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate diagnoses a systemic cause behind evidence assembled at organisational distance, rather than reacting to the instance in front of them.
- Note whether the candidate reads a distribution of failures diagnostically (concentrated versus scattered) rather than treating each instance in isolation.
- Watch for whether the candidate reasons about prioritisation as value of information rather than importance, urgency, or visibility.
- Score for whether the candidate resolves an apparent contradiction between two sound sources by identifying what actually differs.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes what a claim (a vendor statement, converging evidence) actually covers before treating it as decisive.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an audit that catches wrong requirements but misses absent ones.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a participant count as a sample composition.
- Rewarding a reminder or training proposed as the response to a systemic, distribution-level problem.
- Accepting friction added for a compliant majority to address a small number of non-compliant, concentrated cases.
- Missing when a candidate condemns or excuses a flawed study wholesale rather than scoping the flaw precisely.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At this level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation — the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims, and in most rooms a head of profession enters, they may be the only person who will ask.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided. The figures a head of profession encounters are increasingly consequential and increasingly detached from anyone who could explain them — business cases, vendor claims, benefits realisation figures, performance metrics copied between documents for years without the definition being re-checked.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, assuring research quality across the organisation.
- **Aggregating across programmes** maps to synthesising findings into strategic insight.
- **Comparing rates between groups** maps to **Inclusive research**.
- **Judging what a measurement establishes** maps to **User research methods**, helping the organisation adopt and improve its methods.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where senior discussions must consider user needs, which requires the figures in them to mean what they appear to.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Across a programme, 45 of 60 participants completed the core task unaided. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 45÷60=75%. This tests basic percentage calculation — and, in a strong candidate's answer, asking whether aggregating 60 participants across a programme is meaningful, which requires comparable tasks and populations across the studies.

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruitment at scale**

Organisational no-show rate averages 20%; a programme needs 96 completed sessions. Ask for the number of bookings required.

- A) 112
- B) 115
- C) 120
- D) 124

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 96÷0.8=120. This tests correct application of an over-recruitment calculation, and whether the candidate notes that systematic under-recruitment at organisational scale does not distribute evenly — the quotas that fail first are access-need and under-represented-group participants, exactly the composition standards require.

**Question 3 (easy) — Disaggregated rates**

Completion: Digitally confident 36/40; Low digital confidence 12/24; Assistive technology users 4/16. Ask for the overall rate and the gap between the highest and lowest group rates.

- A) 60% overall; 50 point gap
- B) 65% overall; 65 point gap
- C) 65% overall; 50 point gap
- D) 60% overall; 65 point gap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Overall: 52÷80=65%. Group rates: 90%, 50%, 25%; gap 65 points. This tests not mistaking the overall figure for representative of any group — an organisational standard that does not require disaggregation by access need has a hole exactly the shape of the users least able to complain.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Thirteen sessions' task times in minutes: 5, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 19, 84. Ask which summary is most defensible.

- A) Mean 16.1 minutes
- B) Median 10 minutes, range 5 to 84, with the 84-minute case described separately
- C) Median 10 minutes
- D) Mean 16.1 minutes, range 5 to 84

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean exceeds eleven of thirteen observations; the median (10) describes the group well but alone conceals a session over eight times the typical duration — likely the most informative observation. This tests supplying centre, spread, and explicit treatment of an outlier.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four studies: 15 participants at 40%; 25 at 60%; 10 at 70%; 30 at 80%. Ask for the overall rate across all 80.

- A) 62%
- B) 65%
- C) 66%
- D) 68%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 6+15+7+24=52÷80=65%. This tests weighting by participant count rather than averaging the four rates directly (62.5%, option A) — and, in a strong candidate's answer, re-checking the total before it leaves their hands, since a plausible-looking figure attributed to the head of profession is the one nobody downstream will question.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 200,000 start, 150,000 pass identity, 90,000 upload evidence, 81,000 submit. Ask what proportion of those who passed identity went on to submit.

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 81,000÷150,000=54%. This tests identifying the denominator the question names; the funnel shows losses of 50,000 at identity and 60,000 between identity and upload, against 9,000 at submission.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A benefits claim**

A benefits paper states a change raised completion from 60% to 72%, describing it as "a 20% improvement." Ask whether the description is accurate.

- A) No; it is a 12% improvement.
- B) Yes as arithmetic — 12 ÷ 60 = 20% relative increase — but the expression is ambiguous, and the paper should state either "20% relative increase" or "12 percentage points."
- C) No; it is 12 percentage points, and calling it 20% is wrong.
- D) No; it should be 16.7%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relative increase (12÷60=20%) is defensible arithmetic; the problem is labelling ambiguity. This tests distinguishing an arithmetic error from a labelling one — requiring both figures as a standing convention costs no political capital, being a rule about documents rather than a challenge to a person.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A vendor claim**

A supplier states their identity product achieves "94% first-time verification success"; the organisation's rate, measured as verified users divided by users reaching the identity step, is 71%. Ask for the most important question before comparing.

- A) Whether the 94% is independently audited.
- B) What denominator the supplier uses — specifically whether it counts only users presenting acceptable documents, since excluding those without documents removes exactly the population driving your 29% failure.
- C) Whether the figure is current.
- D) Whether the supplier has other government clients.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two rates are comparable only if their denominators describe the same population. This tests identifying that a vendor figure likely excludes the very population driving internal failure, so adopting the product could leave the failure rate essentially unchanged while the vendor's metric and contract remain perfectly satisfied.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims a 5-percentage-point completion improvement on 2 million annual starts is worth £12 million a year. Ask for the implied value per additional completed application.

- A) £6
- B) £60
- C) £120
- D) £240

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 0.05×2,000,000=100,000 additional completions; £12,000,000÷100,000=£120. This tests reconstructing an implied unit value in seconds to check a business case's plausibility, enabling a specific, answerable challenge that accuses nobody.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A board paper reports "average time to complete: 11 minutes" for a service the candidate's evidence shows most users take over forty minutes across several sittings to complete. Ask for the likely explanation and the right intervention.

- A) The board paper is wrong and should be corrected.
- B) The metric almost certainly measures single sessions rather than end-to-end journeys, so multi-sitting completions are excluded or counted only for their final short visit, and abandoned sessions may be included — ask for the definition and the distribution, and propose an end-to-end measure alongside it.
- C) The research sample was atypical.
- D) The figures cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Session-based analytics and journey-based reality diverge systematically for any multi-sitting service, always flattering it. This tests naming the mechanism and proposing a constructive intervention rather than picking a fight over a figure that is probably calculated correctly.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A directorate proposes measuring whether a redesign improved completion using 50 moderated sessions before and 50 after; baseline is 62%, a 5-point improvement considered meaningful. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 100 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 50 per group is two or three participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate through live analytics across the full user base and spending the 100 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 80 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Fifty per group cannot distinguish a five-point difference from chance. This tests redirecting to the right instrument — option D is the most damaging, since caveated findings from underpowered comparisons get cited without their caveats, and an organisation accepting one such comparison will be arguing against the precedent for the rest of a tenure.

**Question 12 (harder) — A claim already in a board paper**

A board paper states: "Design research confirms 93% of users can complete the service independently," traced to a study of 43 participants (40 completed), recruited from the departmental panel; the board meets in four days. Ask for the most important response.

- A) Ask for the figure to be removed.
- B) Go to the paper's author, explain that 43 panel-recruited participants cannot support a population rate, propose the accurate statement — 40 of 43 panel participants completed unaided — and offer what the organisation does have if the board needs a rate; then confirm the correction is made before the paper is tabled.
- C) Raise it at the board.
- D) Note it for the next assurance cycle.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A qualitative sample from a self-selected panel has become a population statistic informing a board decision. This tests going to the author with a specific replacement statement and confirming the correction lands — the step most often omitted, leaving an agreed correction never made.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies what a claim (a vendor statement, a benefits figure, a board metric) actually covers before treating it as comparable to other evidence.
- Note whether the candidate insists on disaggregation rather than accepting a single blended rate.
- Watch for whether the candidate reconstructs an implied unit value to sanity-check a business case's plausibility.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change and flags an ambiguous label.
- Note whether the candidate corrects a flawed figure already in a decision-facing document by going to the author with a specific replacement and confirming it lands, rather than raising it in the most public and least recoverable setting.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an average of rates that is not weighted by the underlying counts.
- Missing when a candidate compares a vendor rate to an internal rate without checking their denominators describe the same population.
- Rewarding a qualitative sample converted into a population rate without challenge.
- Accepting an underpowered comparison endorsed "with caveats" as an adequate answer.
- Missing when a candidate raises a correction to a board paper at the board itself rather than with the author beforehand.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. At this level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, organisational standards, audit reports, cross-government guidance, ministerial correspondence, board papers, and contracts — a misread exception clause becomes an organisation-wide compliance failure, and a misread audit finding becomes advice given confidently to a permanent secretary.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to mark True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, and expertise actively costs marks at this level. Small words carry the load — "all" is not "most", "consider whether" is not "recommend", "unless" and "provided that" govern everything around them.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, guiding the organisation in using evidence.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Inclusive research**, noticing when a framework never mentions access needs.
- **Reading for absence** maps to organisational-scale ethical consequences.
- **Reading cross-government prose accurately** maps to collaborating with counterparts across government.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Organisations must submit an annual research assurance return to the cross-government profession board, unless all research within the organisation has been conducted under a centrally managed programme. Returns must confirm that ethics review was completed where required, and must list any studies where it was not. Where an organisation identifies a study conducted without required ethics review, it must notify the board within ten working days of identifying it, separately from the annual return. Returns are published across government; notifications are not."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Every organisation must submit an annual research assurance return."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception for organisations where all research was conducted under a centrally managed programme. This tests catching an absolute-term-versus-exception pattern — note "all", not "most", material for Question 3.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Notifications of missed ethics review are published across government."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states returns are published and notifications are not. This tests reading past the point a sentence appeared to finish — the operative distinction sits after a semicolon.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "An organisation where nineteen of twenty studies ran under a centrally managed programme must submit a return."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where *all* research ran under a centrally managed programme; nineteen of twenty does not satisfy that. This tests carrying "all" forward and catching partial satisfaction of an exception treated as full satisfaction.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Statement: "A study conducted without required ethics review must be notified within ten working days of the study concluding."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Notification is required within ten working days of *identifying* the study, not of release or conclusion. This tests establishing what starts the clock, since misreading a trigger produces either unnecessary alarm or false comfort when reported to a cross-government board.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The cross-government review of research assurance found considerable variation between departments. Five departments operate peer review before publication; the remainder rely on line management sign-off. Departments with peer review reported fewer instances of findings being challenged after publication, although the review did not examine whether those findings were more robust. The review recommends that the profession board consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Some departments do not operate peer review before publication."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Five departments operate peer review and the remainder rely on line management sign-off. A straightforward baseline.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Peer review produces more robust findings."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer challenges and explicitly states robustness was not examined. This tests resisting a substitution of professional intuition for what the text supports — at this level, exactly the substitution that produces a cross-government recommendation resting on a finding a review declined to make.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common assurance approach."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the board "consider whether" a common approach would be proportionate, including concluding it would not be. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X."

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "Line management sign-off is a weaker form of assurance than peer review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports one outcome difference while declining to assess robustness, making no judgement about relative strength. This tests noticing a document's implied stance (mentioned first, favourable association) is not the same as its assertions.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Research findings must not be altered to reflect the preferences of stakeholders. Presentation may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. Where a researcher is asked to alter findings, the matter must be raised with the head of profession, who is accountable for the integrity of research outputs. Where findings cannot reach decision-makers before a decision is taken, the reason must be recorded; records are reviewed annually."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Findings may be presented differently to different audiences."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The second sentence permits adapting presentation provided substance is unchanged. This tests recognising that a statement weaker than the passage's full provision is still True.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A researcher asked to alter findings must refuse."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage requires escalation to the head of profession, not personal refusal. This tests distinguishing a duty to escalate from a duty to refuse — since the passage names this role as accountable, this is the protection this role is meant to provide.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "A decision taken before findings were available breaches the requirements unless the reason is recorded."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage requires the reason to be recorded where findings cannot reach decision-makers in time — it treats late findings as an expected occurrence requiring honesty, not a breach that recording excuses. This tests reading the structure precisely; organisations that adopt the breach-and-excuse reading get concealment instead.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Research findings must never be changed.
- B) The substance of research must remain independent of stakeholder preference, with adaptation permitted in form, escalation provided where independence is pressured, accountability located with the head of profession, and honesty required where timing fails.
- C) The head of profession is accountable for research integrity.
- D) Reasons must be recorded when findings arrive late.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary spanning the whole passage without inventing anything — A overstates the conditional prohibition into an absolute one, and C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads past the apparent end of a sentence, particularly after a semicolon, for an operative qualification.
- Note whether the candidate carries an absolute qualifier ("all", "entirely") forward to a scenario stated several lines later.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes a duty to escalate from a duty to personally refuse or comply.
- Score for whether the candidate reads a conditional prohibition as conditional rather than absolute.
- Note whether the candidate notices a document's implied stance is not the same as its assertions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False from the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate treats partial satisfaction of a compound or absolute exception as full satisfaction.
- Rewarding "consider whether X" read as "recommends X."
- Accepting fewer challenges read as equivalent to greater robustness.
- Missing when a candidate reads a rule about outcomes and a rule about conduct as the same obligation.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. A head of user research leads user researchers in an organisation, attracts and builds talent, defines and assures best practice, influences organisational strategy and priorities, and collaborates across government.

Three features distinguish this level. Almost nothing a head of profession does is executed by them personally — an option solving a problem through personal intervention is usually second-best, since it does not survive their absence or scale past their calendar. Authority is largely informal, so options leading with instruction, mandate or escalation score poorly — not because escalation is wrong, but because it is capital spent rather than a tool used freely. And a head of profession absorbs pressure downwards — when a director leans on a researcher, the researcher should not be left holding that line.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Ethics and safeguarding at organisational scale** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.
- **Assurance across boundaries** maps to assuring research quality across the organisation.
- **Talent and capability** map to this role level's requirement to attract and build talent, and to leading a community of practice.
- **Influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Organisational advocacy** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, ensuring senior discussions consider user needs.
- **Cross-government collaboration** maps to this role level's requirement to collaborate with colleagues across government.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A researcher under pressure from a director**

A lead researcher reports that a director has asked them to remove an accessibility finding from a board summary because it "muddies the message." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell them to leave it in and cite your instruction.
- B) Agree with them how the finding should be presented, including mitigation; offer to attend; and take the request itself up with the director yourself, since a director asking for findings to be removed is a matter at your level.
- C) Contact the director and instruct them not to make such requests.
- D) Raise it with the permanent secretary's office.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the finding survives, and the researcher emerges more capable and less exposed. This tests taking the request up with the director personally — a power differential this role exists to absorb. Option A is least effective, leaving them defending by proxy, teaching nothing.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard nobody follows**

An organisational research standard is widely ignored, described by researchers as unrealistic. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Reissue it with a message from the candidate.
- B) Review recent outputs with researchers to establish which requirements are genuinely unworkable and which are merely unclear, revise it with them, and pair it with a lightweight routine — such as peer review — that gives compliance a moment of application and delivers something researchers want anyway.
- C) Introduce mandatory compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the standard.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard everybody ignores has already failed. This tests revising with researchers to produce authorship, paired with a routine giving compliance a moment of application. Option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act.

**Question 3 (easy) — Building the pipeline**

The organisation cannot recruit experienced user researchers; posts sit vacant for months, teams covered by junior staff working beyond their level. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Keep advertising and escalate the recruitment difficulty.
- B) Treat it as capability design rather than recruitment: redeploy experienced researchers to lead across teams instead of embedding singly, invest in structured supervision for the junior staff you have, and build entry routes — apprenticeships, secondments, conversion from adjacent roles — while continuing to recruit.
- C) Lower the stated experience requirements.
- D) Fill gaps with agency researchers.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Months of unfilled posts in a competitive market is not solved by more advertising. This tests redesigning around the constraint rather than lowering the bar without changing support (C, setting people up to fail) or relying on capacity that leaves when a contract ends (D).

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate not managed by the candidate has released research whose central claim is unsupported by the evidence, cited in a spending submission. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct the record with the submission's author.
- B) Speak to the researcher and their lead first, set out specifically what the evidence supports, and agree how the correction is made and communicated to those already relying on it — with a clear next step if they decline.
- C) Raise it at the departmental board.
- D) Note it for the annual assurance return.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The relationship needs to survive years of continued assurance. This tests going to the people responsible first and communicating to those already relying on the claim. Option D is least effective by a distance, a record rather than an intervention, while a spending submission is moving now.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Non-compliance concentrated in one place**

Ethics review is required for research with vulnerable participants; studies in one directorate repeatedly proceeded without it, always under ministerial deadline pressure, always by experienced researchers. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the breaches and require retrospective review.
- B) Address the specific breaches, and separately investigate the review process itself — what it demands, how long it takes, whether a fast route exists — on the reasoning that experienced researchers who know the requirement and breach it under pressure are telling you the process cannot be complied with when time is short.
- C) Remind all researchers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-fieldwork checkpoint for every study.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The concentration is the most informative feature. This tests handling both layers — remediation and process investigation. Option C is least effective, the weakest available response to any compliance failure.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want the candidate's research capacity in the same quarter — one has a failing live service, the other a ministerially visible new programme — both raised with the director general. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what research would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and the director general together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the ministerial programme.
- D) Ask the director general to decide.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests making a trade-off explicit and simultaneously visible to prevent parallel-negotiation dynamics, and making a recommendation rather than presenting neutral options. Option A is least effective, splitting constrained capacity producing two efforts too thin to inform either decision.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Evidence against a public commitment**

Research across programmes shows a publicly announced channel closure will substantially disadvantage users without reliable digital access; colleagues propose framing it as "opportunities for future enhancement." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the framing; the commitment is public.
- B) Report accurately and do the constructive work alongside — quantify who is affected, specify what assisted provision would mitigate it, and present finding and mitigation together so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report starkly and let the organisation respond.
- D) Escalate before acting.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The proposed framing protects the organisation from discomfort while the disadvantage occurs anyway. This tests keeping the finding intact and making it actionable with quantification and mitigation. Option A is least effective, the precise failure professional standards exist to prevent.

**Question 8 (harder) — A serious safeguarding risk in another team's fieldwork**

A team is researching a domestic abuse support service, recruiting through the service's own contact list, running video sessions with no protocol for confirming a participant is alone. Ask what should be done.

- A) Raise it with the researcher and offer to help design a safer approach.
- B) Intervene immediately: contact the researcher and their lead, set out the specific risks — that contact via the service's list may reach people whose devices are monitored, and that a video session without an aloneness protocol may expose a participant to danger during the call — press for fieldwork to pause until safeguards are in place, and inform the relevant director in parallel.
- C) Report it to the data protection officer.
- D) Raise it at the next community of practice.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The potential harm is severe, immediate and physical. This tests intervening now, pressing for a pause, and informing in parallel rather than sequentially — setting and assuring safeguarding standards is explicitly this role's accountability. Option D is least effective by a wide margin, deferring a live physical-safety risk to a scheduled forum.

**Question 9 (harder) — An unowned problem**

Synthesising across programmes, the candidate concludes the organisation's largest user-facing problem is in transitions between four services that each work adequately alone, with no director owning it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Report it in your annual synthesis.
- B) Build the case with evidence from all four services, quantify the affected population using contact centre and analytics data, identify who would need to own it, and take it to a forum with cross-service authority — accepting that securing an owner for an unowned problem is itself months of work.
- C) Raise it with each of the four service owners separately.
- D) Propose research on the transitions.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Unowned problems are the characteristic failure of service-shaped organisations. This tests recognising the hard part is ownership rather than insight, building a case a cross-service forum can act on. Option A is least effective, correct and filed, changing nothing.

**Question 10 (harder) — Cross-government collaboration**

Three departments are each independently procuring user research recruitment services, all facing the same difficulty reaching digitally excluded participants; the candidate has no authority over the other two. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Focus on your own department's procurement.
- B) Convene the three departments' research leads, establish whether the requirement is genuinely common, and if so propose a shared specification or joint approach to market — accepting that you are building consensus rather than exercising authority, and that a well-evidenced shared requirement is more persuasive than three separate ones.
- C) Raise it with the cross-government profession board and ask them to mandate a common approach.
- D) Share your specification with the other two departments for information.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three departments solving one problem separately is the waste cross-government collaboration exists to prevent. This tests calibrating action to the authority actually held — convening is available, mandating is not. Option A is least effective, accepting solvable waste and leaving the other two departments to reproduce the same difficulty.

**Question 11 (harder) — Assurance failure you signed off**

Nine months ago, a study was assured whose screener excluded anyone who had contacted the support desk, omitting users who had already struggled; the design is live and its findings shaped a service change. Ask what should happen.

- A) Add it to your assurance guidance as a lesson.
- B) Notify the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are compromised and which stand, commission targeted follow-up with the excluded group, and examine why the assurance process did not catch it — then change the process.
- C) Commission follow-up quietly and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Ask your director general how to handle it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A live change is running on the findings. This tests precise scoping, targeted follow-up, and, distinctively at this level, examining why the candidate's own assurance process did not catch it. Option C is least effective, concealing a known flaw while a live change proceeds.

**Question 12 (harder) — A standard that will be resisted**

The candidate intends to require all research outputs to disaggregate findings by access need, and anticipates resistance since teams will object that samples are too small. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Introduce the requirement and handle objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is partly correct: small samples cannot produce group rates. Frame the requirement as reporting who was in the sample and what each group experienced, rather than producing group statistics — and pair it with the recruitment support that makes including those participants feasible.
- C) Introduce it as guidance rather than a requirement.
- D) Require it only above a certain sample size.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The anticipated objection is a real methodological point, and a standard ignoring it will be resisted and technically wrong. This tests changing what is required (composition and experience rather than group rates) and pairing it with recruitment support. Option A is least effective, introducing a known-contestable requirement and arguing it case by case.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate changes a system, standard, or set of conditions rather than solving a problem through personal intervention alone.
- Note whether the candidate absorbs pressure downward rather than leaving a more junior colleague to argue alone.
- Watch for whether the candidate finds an owner for an unowned organisational problem, rather than filing it in a report where it changes nothing.
- Score for whether an own or assured flaw is disclosed promptly, with precise scoping and the underlying process changed so it cannot recur the same way.
- Note whether the candidate engages a standard's strongest true objection before introducing it, rather than introducing it and arguing case by case.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that reissues or restates a standard teams have already shown they will not follow.
- Missing when a candidate treats documenting a risk, with no action attached, as equivalent to managing it.
- Rewarding a candidate who solves a problem personally without building a mechanism that survives their attention.
- Accepting a candidate leaving a more junior colleague to hold a position under pressure the candidate should absorb.
- Missing when a candidate files a strategic insight rather than actively finding it an owner.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and systemic diagnosis across research outputs, cross-programme evidence, and organisational recruitment practice; the arithmetic that supports board-level research argument — disaggregating a blended rate, interrogating a vendor's denominator, and reconstructing an implied unit value to check a business case; the discipline of reading governance frameworks, audit reports and board papers at exactly the strength the text supports, especially conditional prohibitions and deadline triggers; and the situational judgement that defines a strong head of user research — changing systems and conditions rather than acting through personal intervention, absorbing pressure downward, and finding an owner for a problem that belongs to no one.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answers consistently act on systems and conditions rather than instances. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
