# Junior Service Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for junior service designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A junior service designer explains design decisions, works collaboratively, has responsibility for a service, works independently after being given direction by more senior designers, and independently identifies user issues and important needs. That mix of independence and direction shapes this material: candidates analyse and synthesise evidence themselves rather than only reading someone else's findings, so items ask what evidence supports rather than only spot patterns in it; they prototype at different fidelities and iterate based on successive rounds of research, so there are questions about method choice and what an iteration can and cannot tell you; and they have responsibility for a service, so the prioritisation and judgement items assume they will act rather than only observe.

Service design insists on looking at the whole thing — the journey across channels, across organisations, including the weeks where nothing visible happens and the people who fall out at the edges. A great deal of what this material measures is that habit of zooming out, applied to a table of numbers or a paragraph of policy prose instead of a wall of sticky notes.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate judges the strength of a claim as well as noticing it — deciding how strongly a finding can be stated, choosing between reasonable methods, or identifying which of two plausible explanations the evidence actually supports. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward genuine independent action within remit, combined with prompt, honest disclosure of anything beyond it, and zooming out to the whole journey rather than addressing only the step in front of them.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract shapes — a service blueprint, a set of journey steps, a design standard, analytics, research findings, a channel comparison, an extract from a team's design principles.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

For a junior service designer the core of the craft is disciplined noticing applied at the scale of a whole journey — that a blueprint shows a step users described completely differently in research, that the "quick" route takes three weeks once a postal wait is included, that a form asks for something the service already holds, that two channels have been designed by different teams to work in incompatible ways. What rises at this level, compared with a trainee's, is the expectation to judge as well as notice — several questions ask a candidate to decide how strongly a finding can be stated, choose between reasonable methods, or identify which of two plausible explanations the evidence actually supports.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining evidence relevant to users or a service, such as web analytics data.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Evidence-based design** and **Design communication**: clearly explaining design decisions. A decision explained on an overreaching claim does not survive its first challenge.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**: ensuring a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations, with support.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Iterative design**: applying iterative design principles and agile methodologies.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Iterative design** and **Designing for everyone**: working with others to deliver services meeting the needs of all users.
- **Seeing the whole journey** maps to **Designing strategically**: aligning design work to a team's wider goals.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a service blueprint**

Blueprint standard: every step must record the user action, the frontstage service response, the backstage process, and the duration. Four steps: Step 1 — complete; Step 2 — user action, frontstage, duration all present, backstage missing; Step 3 — user action, backstage, duration present, frontstage missing; Step 4 — user action, frontstage, backstage present, duration missing. Ask how many required items are missing in total.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 2 is missing backstage (one), Step 3 is missing frontstage (two), Step 4 is missing duration (three). This tests counting items, not steps, since the question asks for items — and, in a strong candidate's answer, noting that Step 3's missing frontstage response during a three-week wait is a design finding rather than only a documentation gap: a user waiting three weeks with nothing happening in front of them is a user who telephones, or gives up.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Rule: "A change to a live service may be deployed only if it has been through design review, unless it is a content correction approved by the content lead." A colleague reports: "The change went live yesterday and it did not go through design review." Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The rule was broken.
- B) The change was a content correction approved by the content lead.
- C) Either the change was an approved content correction, or the rule was broken.
- D) The change was risky.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional rule with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, yields only the disjunction. This tests deducing only what the facts guarantee — option D introduces an unsupported judgement about risk, since an unreviewed content correction may be entirely safe, and the accurate, collegial move at this level is asking which route was used rather than assuming the worst.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across evidence sources**

Three sources on the same service: research with ten participants found seven struggled to understand the eligibility wording; analytics show only 3% of users leave at the eligibility page; the contact centre reports eligibility as its second-highest call topic. Ask for the strongest interpretation.

- A) The research is unrepresentative; the analytics show few people have a problem.
- B) The sources measure different things — the analytics measure leaving, not understanding — and the contact centre volume corroborates the research: users are proceeding past the page without understanding it, and phoning to find out.
- C) The analytics and research contradict each other and cannot be reconciled.
- D) The eligibility page should be rewritten immediately.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A user who does not understand the criteria but continues anyway produces no abandonment signal at all. This tests naming what each evidence source actually measures — the contact centre data is the pivot, corroborating the research using exactly the kind of behavioural data at scale that sceptical colleagues find credible. Option D is probably the right eventual action and skips the analysis that would justify and shape it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a sprint**

Capacity for one substantial piece of work this sprint: (1) a new eligibility screen goes into build in five days, nothing designed yet; (2) a live journey has a 35% drop-off at document upload, unchanged for months; (3) a policy team wants exploratory work on a concept for next year; (4) the team wants to revisit a screen designed six weeks ago after minor copy changes. Ask which should be prioritised.

- A) Item 2, because it affects the most users.
- B) Item 1, because it is the only one where design work can still change what gets built inside the window, while items 2 and 3 remain valuable but are not time-boxed in the same way.
- C) Item 3, because early-stage work has the highest leverage.
- D) Item 4, because unverified changes are a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 has a hard five-day window in which design can still shape what gets built; item 2 matters greatly but has persisted for months, so deferring costs comparatively little. This tests ranking by whether work can still change a decision, not scale alone (A, the most common error, leaving teams perpetually researching problems they cannot yet act on).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Choosing a prototype fidelity**

A team needs to test whether users understand a new eligibility concept before committing to build, with one week available. Ask which approach best fits.

- A) A high-fidelity interactive prototype of the full journey.
- B) A low-fidelity prototype — sketches or a simple clickable outline — focused on the eligibility explanation, because the question is about comprehension of a concept rather than about interaction detail, and low fidelity is faster to make and easier to change between sessions.
- C) A written description read aloud to participants.
- D) A live A/B test of two versions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question at stake — comprehension of a concept — does not require working interactions, real data or polished visuals, and high fidelity can actively work against it, since participants respond to polish and are more reluctant to criticise something finished. This tests matching fidelity to the actual question — option D cannot run without something built and would show which version performed better without showing why.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about what a finding supports**

In research with twelve participants, eight failed to notice a "save and return" link. Ask which conclusion is best supported.

- A) 67% of users do not notice the link.
- B) The link's discoverability is a substantial problem, evidenced across most participants, warranting design work and a follow-up check on whether the change helps.
- C) The link should be moved to the top of the page.
- D) The link is invisible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eight of twelve is a strong qualitative signal, stated at that strength, with a named follow-up. This tests resisting a jump to one specific fix (C, when the cause could be position, wording, visual weight, or the user's mental model) and resisting overreach in both directions — a population percentage (A) or "invisible" (D, overstating "not noticed").

**Question 7 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a constraint**

A service requires users to upload a document; users are predominantly over 70 and many are not confident online. A team proposes phone-only upload, simplest to build. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The team is right; most people have smartphones.
- B) Smartphone-only upload assumes a suitable phone, data, somewhere appropriate to photograph a document, and the confidence to do it — assumptions that fail disproportionately in this user group, so the design would exclude a substantial share of the service's core users; the useful next step is to establish how many, and to identify an alternative route such as post or assisted digital.
- C) The team should add a postal route.
- D) The team should test the upload with older users first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Phone-only upload bundles four assumptions, each failing disproportionately in this population, and because the affected group is the core user base, the exclusion is the main case. This tests naming the mechanism and its scale — option A treats a majority statistic as sufficient, precisely the reasoning producing inaccessible public services, and option C names a solution without the analysis that would justify it.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading analytics to focus design work**

Journey: 20,000 start, 17,000 pass eligibility, 10,200 upload evidence, 9,690 submit. Ask where design attention should go first.

- A) The start, because 3,000 users are lost there.
- B) Evidence upload, because 6,800 users are lost between eligibility and upload — more than twice any other step.
- C) Submission, because it is closest to the outcome.
- D) All steps equally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eligibility-to-upload loses 6,800, more than twice either other step (3,000 and 510). This tests calculating every step's loss before choosing where to look — as rates, that step loses 40% of those reaching it, against 15% and 5% elsewhere, and quantitative data sets the agenda for the qualitative work that follows.

**Question 9 (harder) — Distinguishing a pattern from an artefact**

Six participants tested a new journey: the four who completed easily all used desktop, the two who struggled both used a phone. A colleague concludes the journey works on desktop and fails on mobile. Ask for the most rigorous response.

- A) Agree; the split is consistent.
- B) Note that device is a plausible explanation but rests on two phone users, and those two may differ in other relevant ways — digital confidence, or whether they had their documents to hand — so it is a hypothesis to test rather than a finding, and the obvious next step is to recruit more phone users.
- C) Reject it; two participants show nothing.
- D) Recommend removing mobile access until the journey is fixed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A perfect split like this should prompt investigation, and this tests holding two things together — the split is plausible, and two phone users is a thin base for a conclusion — rather than promoting it to a finding (A) or dismissing it entirely (C, which discards a genuine signal). Option D proposes a drastic action on thin evidence that would remove access from users who may have no alternative.

**Question 10 (harder) — Prioritising a genuine trade-off**

Three weeks available: either map the whole end-to-end journey at moderate depth, or work in detail on the two steps where analytics show the largest losses, which the team is about to rebuild with no plans to change anything else. Ask which is stronger.

- A) The whole-journey map, because end-to-end understanding is always more valuable.
- B) The detailed work on two steps, because that is the only surface the team can act on in this cycle, and depth there will produce something robust enough to guide a rebuild — while a map of screens nobody will change cannot influence anything now.
- C) The whole-journey map, because focusing on two steps risks losing context.
- D) Split the time evenly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Design value is a function of what a team can act on, and here that surface is two steps. This tests applying a general preference (end-to-end understanding) against the actual situation — option C raises a real risk with a straightforward mitigation (capturing enough context around the two steps), while option D produces both a map too shallow to be useful and detail too thin to guide the rebuild, the characteristic failure of splitting a constrained resource down the middle.

**Question 11 (harder) — Reconciling contradictory evidence**

A satisfaction survey of 1,500 users reports 78% positive; twelve research sessions found substantial frustration, with nine participants describing significant difficulty. Ask how these should be reconciled.

- A) Trust the survey; 1,500 responses outweigh twelve sessions.
- B) Trust the sessions; observed behaviour beats self-report.
- C) Examine what each measures and who answered: satisfaction surveys are usually served at the end of a journey, so people who abandoned rarely see them, and they often ask about the outcome rather than the process — so a service can be effective and punishing at once, and the gap between the two is itself a finding.
- D) Average them and report moderate satisfaction.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests resisting the instinct to declare a winner on a general heuristic (A, B) and instead identifying that a service can be effective and punishing simultaneously — the gap between "I got what I needed" and "that was hard" is itself worth reporting. Option D is the worst answer, averaging incommensurable measures to produce a number describing nothing.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in work you have already shared**

A journey map shared a fortnight ago, against which the team has planned two sprints, is found to omit an offline step in which many users telephone the contact centre after receiving a letter. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Update the map and mention it at the next stand-up.
- B) Tell the team immediately, explain exactly what was missing, and flag which planning decisions may have been based on the incomplete picture — then update the map and, since the omitted step is a large volume of calls, propose looking at why the letter generates them.
- C) Update the map quietly.
- D) Note it in the retrospective.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two sprints have been planned against an incomplete picture, so correction alone is insufficient. This tests flagging downstream impact, the step most often skipped, and, distinctively for service design, recognising that a letter reliably generating calls is a finding, not just an omission — a defect worth investigating in its own right, not deferred to a retrospective (D).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate judges how strongly a finding can be stated, not only whether they notice it.
- Note whether the candidate names what each of two evidence sources actually measures before treating an apparent contradiction as real.
- Watch for whether the candidate ranks work by whether it can still change a decision, rather than by scale alone.
- Score for whether the candidate applies a general preference (such as end-to-end mapping) against the actual constraints of the situation.
- Note whether the candidate treats a striking but small-sample split as a hypothesis to test rather than a finding.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting participants converted into a population percentage.
- Missing when a candidate declares a winner between two data sources rather than reconciling what each measures.
- Rewarding a solution prescribed before the underlying problem is established.
- Accepting a fix prescribed before the underlying problem is established.
- Missing when a candidate ranks work by scale rather than by whether it can still change a decision.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles the numbers this role produces. Not a maths exam — no equations, calculator usually provided, arithmetic deliberately simple where one is not. What is measured is whether a candidate reads a table correctly, identifies which figures matter, chooses the right operation, and interprets the result without overstating it.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a small table, chart, or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question, time per item tight. What rises at this level is interpretation — several items pair a correct calculation with a wrong conclusion, and telling them apart is the test. A candidate who can compute a percentage but not say whether it means anything is not much use in a prioritisation meeting; one who can say "that rate is measured against the wrong group, so it understates the problem" is genuinely valuable.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading analytics accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing and clearly explaining evidence such as web analytics data.
- **Comparing rates between groups and channels** maps to **Designing for everyone**: a gap between groups is often the first numerical trace of exclusion.
- **Measuring whether an iteration worked** maps to **Iterative design**: iterating and improving a design based on successive rounds of research.
- **Elapsed journey time** maps to **Designing strategically**: understanding the service as users experience it, including the waiting nobody designed.
- **Explaining figures honestly** maps to **Design communication**: clearly explaining design decisions.
- **Working with researchers and analysts** maps to **Evidence-based design**: using evidence to inform, develop and test a design idea.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Of 320 users who started a journey, 240 completed it. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 240÷320=75%. This tests basic percentage calculation, checkable by estimating three quarters first — and, in a strong candidate's answer, distinguishing "75% of users who started completed" (accurate) from "75% of users completed" (which quietly changes the denominator).

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off measured correctly**

A step is reached by 2,400 users and passed by 1,800. Ask for the drop-off rate.

- A) 25%
- B) 33%
- C) 75%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Drop-off 600÷2,400=25%. This tests measuring against those who arrived, not those who continued (option B, 600÷1,800, overstating the drop-off) — comparing two steps computed with different denominators makes the comparison meaningless.

**Question 3 (easy) — Channel comparison**

Started/Completed: Online 6,000/4,800; Telephone 1,200/1,080; Post 800/320. Ask which channel has the lowest completion rate, and what it is.

- A) Online, 80%
- B) Post, 40%
- C) Telephone, 90%
- D) Post, 60%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Post is 320÷800=40%, lowest of the three (80%, 90%, 40%). This tests calculating rates correctly, and, in a strong candidate's answer, noting online loses more people in absolute terms (1,200 versus post's 480) while post's 40% rate signals something badly broken — and postal users are frequently those who cannot use the online channel, making the failure rate a likely inclusion problem.

**Question 4 (easy) — Elapsed journey time**

Journey: complete form (25 minutes), wait for reference (3 working days), post evidence (5 minutes plus 2 working days in transit), assessment (4 weeks), decision letter (2 working days). Ask roughly how long the journey takes from the user's point of view.

- A) About 30 minutes
- B) About 4 weeks
- C) About 5 to 6 weeks
- D) About 3 months

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Adding the waits (3+2 working days, plus 4 weeks, plus 2 working days) gives roughly five and a half weeks; the half hour of active time is negligible. This tests seeing the whole elapsed journey rather than only active time — option A is the instructive trap, exactly how a service comes to be described internally as "a half-hour application" while the person experiencing it waits a month and a half.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Did the iteration work?**

Before a change, 480 of 800 users completed a step. After, 630 of 900. Ask whether completion improved, and by how much in percentage points.

- A) Yes, by 150 users
- B) Yes, by 10 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 16.7 percentage points
- D) No, the rate is unchanged

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Before 60%, after 70%: 10 points. This tests converting to rates because totals differ — option A compares raw counts across differently sized groups, crediting the design with an increase partly caused by more traffic arriving.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing unequal groups**

Of 500 users under 65, 425 completed online. Of 150 over 65, 45 completed. Ask for the gap in completion rates, in percentage points.

- A) 30
- B) 45
- C) 55
- D) 85

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Under-65s 85%; over-65s 30%; gap 55 points. This tests converting to rates before comparing — options A and D offer the two group rates as if they were the gap. A gap this size would justify investigating what in the online journey older users cannot get past.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Weekly completed applications rose from 350 to 434 after a redesign. Ask for the percentage increase.

- A) 19%
- B) 24%
- C) 84%
- D) 124%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (434−350)÷350=24%. This tests dividing by the original value — option A divides by 434, the commonest error, understating every improvement described this way.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a base**

15% of users abandon at the identity step, equal to 2,700 people a month. Ask how many reach the identity step monthly.

- A) 3,105
- B) 4,050
- C) 18,000
- D) 40,500

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 2,700÷0.15=18,000. This tests working back from a percentage and a count to the base, verifiable by reversing — establishing the base in ten seconds is what lets a candidate judge whether a quoted figure deserves the weight it is being given.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

Journey: 25,000 start, 20,000 pass eligibility, 12,000 upload evidence, 10,800 submit. Ask what percentage of those who uploaded evidence went on to submit.

- A) 43%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 10,800÷12,000=90%. This tests identifying the group the question names as the denominator; the funnel also shows where to look — 8,000 lost between eligibility and upload, against 1,200 at submission.

**Question 10 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion rose from 25% to 40% after a change. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) Completion rose 15%.
- B) Completion rose 15 percentage points, a 60% relative increase.
- C) Completion rose 60 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose 37.5%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Gap 15 points; relative 15÷25=60%. This tests correctly labelling both figures — "rose 60%" and "rose 15 percentage points" describe the identical change, with the first sounding four times more impressive, and quoting both without prompting is a cheap way to be trusted with numbers.

**Question 11 (harder) — An average that hides a bimodal pattern**

Time on a page averages 40 seconds; research showed participants either glancing for a few seconds or reading carefully for two to three minutes. Ask for the most useful response.

- A) The average is wrong.
- B) The average is probably correct and uninformative: it sits between two clusters and describes neither, so the useful request is for the distribution rather than the mean — which will show whether the page has two distinct audiences behaving differently.
- C) The research participants were unrepresentative.
- D) The page needs to be shorter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 40-second average is entirely consistent with two clusters, neither near 40. This tests recognising a mean landing in the gap between two distinct behaviours and requesting the distribution — the single most useful thing to ask an analytics colleague — rather than leaping to a fix that could harm the careful readers (D).

**Question 12 (harder) — What a small comparison can show**

Baseline completion is 65%, and a 5-point improvement would be meaningful; a colleague suggests testing with 20 participants before and 20 after. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 40 participants is reasonable.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 20 per group is one participant and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users, and spending the 40 sessions on understanding why users succeed or fail, which sessions do uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 40 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With 20 per group a 5-point difference is a single participant, indistinguishable from chance. This tests redirecting to the right instrument for each question rather than merely objecting (C moves towards adequacy without approaching it) — option D is the most damaging response, since a reported difference from an underpowered comparison invites the team to act on noise, and caveats do not travel — the number does.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator before calculating.
- Note whether the candidate converts to rates before comparing groups or channels of different sizes.
- Watch for whether the candidate recognises a mean landing between two behavioural clusters and asks for the underlying distribution.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change.
- Note whether the candidate adds up waiting as well as active time when estimating a whole journey's real elapsed duration.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a drop-off or completion rate calculated against the wrong denominator.
- Missing when a candidate compares raw counts before and after a change without accounting for different totals.
- Rewarding a journey duration estimate that counts only active user time and ignores waiting.
- Accepting an underpowered comparison endorsed as adequate to detect the effect size in question.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a mean without checking for a bimodal or clustered distribution behind it.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. Service design runs on documents — service standards, accessibility requirements, policy summaries, operational procedures, letters sent to users, contracts with suppliers, and the words users say — and reading any of them slightly wrong changes what gets designed.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say — regardless of what a candidate otherwise knows. Cannot Say deserves particular attention because candidates avoid it, reaching for True or False to seem decisive when the honest answer is that the passage does not settle it. Small words carry the meaning — "all" is not "most," "may" is not "must," "encouraged" is not "required" — a distinction that matters more in this discipline than most, since a great many design opportunities hide in the gap between what a policy actually requires and what everyone assumes it requires.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**: ensuring a design meets appropriate standards, with support.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing and clearly explaining evidence.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: explaining problems and design ideas.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: aligning to organisational goals expressed in documents full of "should" and "where practicable."
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**: noticing a procedure never mentions people without internet access has a direct human consequence.
- **Understanding written feedback and direction** maps to **Designing together**: feedback only works if understood as given.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "All new public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before launch. Where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, the team must publish an accessibility statement setting out the gap and the plan to close it. Existing services must meet the standard within 18 months of this policy taking effect. Internal-facing tools are outside the scope of this policy, although teams are encouraged to apply the same standard."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Internal tools must meet the accessibility standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Internal tools are outside the policy's scope, and teams are only "encouraged," not required, to apply the same standard. This tests catching the two-part structure common in policy prose: an exclusion followed by a softer aspiration — reading only one half produces the wrong answer.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "A service that cannot meet one requirement has breached the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage provides a route through this: publish an accessibility statement setting out the gap and the plan to close it. This tests recognising a rule that anticipates a case and defines honesty about it rather than prohibiting it outright — the breach would be failing to publish the statement, not failing the requirement itself.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service launched three years ago must now meet the standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The 18-month deadline is relative to when the policy took effect, which the passage never states. This tests checking whether a deadline's reference point is actually given — a service launched three years ago could predate or postdate the policy, and the passage does not say which.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Statement: "A team that publishes an accessibility statement has met the policy's requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Publishing a statement is required where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, but the passage does not say publishing alone discharges every requirement, nor that it is insufficient. This tests resisting supplying a link the passage does not make — reasoning that "the passage provides a route" therefore means taking the route equals full compliance is exactly the overreach this section penalises.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The service review found that applicants using the assisted digital route completed applications more slowly than online applicants, and required an average of two contacts with the service. The review notes that assisted digital users differed from online users in several respects, including age and first language. The review did not assess the quality of the completed applications. It recommends that the service team consider whether additional guidance would reduce the number of contacts."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Assisted digital applicants required an average of two contacts with the service."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, about assisted digital users specifically. This tests the baseline reading — a statement broadening the claim to "applicants generally" would overstate the passage and be Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The assisted digital route causes applications to take longer."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage names two confounds (age, first language) that could equally explain the difference. This tests treating declared confounds as a warning against inferring causation — if the cause is the route's design, fix the route; if the cause is language, fix the content — treating an association as a cause is a reliable way for a team to spend a quarter improving the wrong thing.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "Applications submitted through the assisted digital route are of lower quality."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states explicitly that quality was not assessed. This tests treating declared silence as establishing nothing — inferring lower quality from slower completion and more contacts is exactly the overreach this item is built to catch, and could plausibly run the other way.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "The review recommends providing additional guidance."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the team "consider whether" additional guidance would help, including concluding it would not. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X" — a distinction that pervades real review documents.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Teams should offer users a choice of channel wherever practicable. A digital channel must not be the only option for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Where a team withdraws a channel, it must assess the impact on affected users and record the assessment; the record is reviewed annually. Users who need assistance must be able to reach a person by telephone during published service hours."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Users must be able to reach a person at any time."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage specifies "who need assistance" and "during published service hours," both dropped by the statement. This tests catching dropped qualifiers — a statement can be assembled entirely from the passage's own vocabulary and still say something the passage does not.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A team that withdraws a channel without recording an impact assessment has breached the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The requirement is unconditional: withdrawal requires assessing and recording impact. This tests distinguishing a rule with an escape route (Passage A, Question 2) from an unconditional rule, where failing it straightforwardly is a breach.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "A digital-only service for a general-population service breaches the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The prohibition is scoped to services used "predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion," which a general-population service does not obviously meet — but the softer "should offer a choice wherever practicable" has not necessarily been discharged, since whether offering a choice was practicable is not stated. This tests holding two provisions in mind at once rather than resolving the case with only one of them.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Digital services are unsuitable for many users.
- B) Users should be able to reach services through a channel that works for them, with digital-only ruled out where exclusion is likely, channel withdrawals assessed and recorded, and human help available during service hours.
- C) Teams must record an impact assessment when withdrawing a channel.
- D) Users must be able to speak to a person.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary broad enough to cover every requirement without inventing anything — A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement, and C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest, with D also dropping the service-hours and "who need assistance" qualifications.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a passage before concluding what a provision requires, rather than stopping at an early sentence.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a rule with an escape route (failure is not automatically a breach) from an unconditional rule.
- Watch for whether declared confounds are treated as a genuine limit on what can be concluded about causation.
- Score for whether the candidate catches a dropped qualifier in a statement.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary covering the whole passage rather than one true detail promoted above the rest.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate reads "encouraged" as "required."
- Rewarding an association read as causation despite a declared confound in the passage.
- Accepting "consider whether X" read as "recommends X."
- Missing when a candidate assumes an escape route exists where a requirement is in fact unconditional.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and specifically whether a candidate's instincts match what the profession considers good practice at this level. A junior service designer explains design decisions, works collaboratively, has responsibility for a service, works independently after being given direction, and independently identifies user issues and important needs — so the strongest answers involve genuine independent action within remit combined with prompt honesty about anything beyond it.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Users come first, specifically the users who are struggling most — whole journeys fail people even when every individual part looks acceptable, and noticing that is what this discipline is for. Act within remit and be transparent at its edges: at this level a candidate is expected to make design decisions, run a design session, handle routine stakeholder conversations, and identify user needs themselves, but not to overrule a policy requirement, commit the organisation, or absorb a serious accessibility problem without telling anyone — the pattern is attempt, then tell. Address problems directly and early, with the person involved. And zoom out — the distinctively service-design signature of a strong answer, since asking what happens before and after a step is usually stronger than addressing only the step itself.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Explaining and defending design decisions** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Protecting inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Working with others' perspectives** maps to **Designing together**.
- **Using evidence honestly** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Working in cycles and adapting** maps to **Iterative design**.
- **Knowing where design sits** maps to **Designing strategically** and **Leading design**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A live problem you did not cause**

A caseworker reports the new form is producing cases they cannot process, and has been handling them manually for three days; the candidate designed the form section. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Explain that the section was based on research and ask them to raise a ticket.
- B) Thank them, ask for specific examples of what users are entering, look at whether the design invites that misunderstanding, and take a proposed fix to a senior designer quickly — while asking how many cases are affected so the scale is understood.
- C) Wait for the next research round to confirm the problem.
- D) Ask for the section to be reverted.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A caseworker reporting the same problem repeatedly is high-quality free evidence, and gathering the specifics usually reveals the misunderstanding immediately. This tests treating unwelcome evidence as a diagnostic opportunity and understanding scale — option A is least effective, defending a design against evidence it is not working, the fastest way to lose both the design and the caseworker's willingness to raise anything next time.

**Question 2 (easy) — A gap in a journey nobody has noticed**

Reviewing a journey, nothing appears to have been designed for what happens if a decision letter does not arrive. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Assume the operational team has a process and move on.
- B) Find out what actually happens today by asking the operational team, and if nothing exists, raise it with a senior designer as a gap with a proposed approach.
- C) Design a solution and add it to the journey map.
- D) Note it in the next retrospective.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A letter that does not arrive leaves a person with no decision, no notification and often no obvious route to ask. This tests establishing what happens today before treating it as a gap, since an unmapped process may exist — option A is least effective, since "someone must have thought about it" is precisely how these gaps survive for years.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting direction**

A senior designer asked for a blueprint finished today; a delivery manager then asks for the day spent preparing tomorrow's show and tell, calling it urgent. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Do the show and tell preparation; the delivery manager is more senior.
- B) Do the blueprint and tell the delivery manager you are unavailable.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask both to confirm the priority.
- D) Attempt both and work late.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Surfacing the conflict promptly with a concrete option — "I could prepare the show and tell this morning and have the blueprint by four" — turns the candidate into part of the solution. This tests offering a specific workable alternative rather than bare escalation — option D is least effective, hiding the conflict, normalising over-commitment, and meaning the team never learns it is asking for two days of work in one.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A user problem outside your service**

Research shows users routinely fail at a step belonging to another department's service, before reaching this one; the team says it is out of scope. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept it; the team cannot fix another department's service.
- B) Document the problem and its effect on users with evidence, raise it with a senior designer and ask about a route to the other department — and separately, consider what the current service can do to reduce the harm, such as explaining the earlier step or handling people who arrive without having completed it.
- C) Contact the other department directly yourself.
- D) Note it in the findings and move on.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A user's journey does not respect organisational boundaries even though budgets do. This tests documenting and finding a legitimate route, plus asking what the candidate's own service can do meanwhile — option A is least effective, since "out of scope" describes a budget, not a person's experience.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to remove inconvenient detail**

A journey mapped to appear thirty minutes actually takes six weeks because of two waiting periods; a stakeholder asks for the waits to be removed because "they are not design." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Remove them.
- B) Explain that the waits are the largest part of the user's experience and that removing them makes the map describe something users would not recognise — and offer a visual treatment, such as showing them greyed out or compressed, so the design steps stay legible.
- C) Refuse and send the map unchanged.
- D) Produce two versions.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's underlying concern — legibility — is legitimate, and this tests holding the substance while addressing it: a map omitting six weeks of waiting is not a map of the user's experience, and waiting is exactly where users give up or telephone. Option D is least effective, since two versions of the truth means the convenient one gets used.

**Question 6 (moderate) — An assumption in the room**

Someone in a design session says "we don't need to worry about people without smartphones — everyone has one now," while research shows a significant minority currently apply by post. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go; the team has decided the direction.
- B) Bring the specific fact into the room — that a significant minority currently apply by post — and ask what the team thinks should happen to those users, so the decision is made knowingly rather than by assumption.
- C) Argue that all services must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately with your senior designer afterwards.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A specific fact dissolves an assumption more readily than a principle. This tests contributing arithmetic or a fact that keeps the decision honest — option A is least effective, letting a factually wrong assumption stand when the fact to correct it was available.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Feedback that stings**

A senior designer says a blueprint is "confusing and doesn't show the offline channel," though the candidate believes it does. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Point out where the offline channel appears.
- B) Ask them to show you where they got lost, on the reasoning that if an experienced designer cannot find the offline channel then the blueprint is not communicating it — then fix that.
- C) Accept the criticism and redo it.
- D) Ask for the feedback in writing to consider later.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Both things can be true — the channel is on the blueprint, and the blueprint does not communicate it. This tests converting feedback into information rather than winning the argument — option A is least effective, leaving the artefact exactly as confusing and teaching the room that feedback is effortful.

**Question 8 (moderate) — An accessibility problem found late**

Ten days before launch, a key step is found incompletable with a screen reader. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with a senior designer and delivery manager, describe precisely what cannot be done and by whom, and ask for a decision — while offering any interim route that would let affected users complete the journey another way.
- C) Try to fix it yourself in the ten days.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A step incompletable by screen reader means some people cannot use the service, and weighing a delay against exclusion is a decision for people with authority. This tests looking for an interim route so the choice is not simply launch-or-delay — option A is least effective, sending a genuine blocker to a backlog where it will remain unfixed.

**Question 9 (harder) — A requirement you doubt**

A policy colleague says a service must ask for three years of address history "because the policy requires it," and research shows the question causes significant abandonment. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Design the question as well as possible and accept it.
- B) Share the abandonment evidence, ask what the requirement is for and where it comes from, and explore whether the underlying need could be met another way — while being ready to accept the constraint if it turns out to be fixed.
- C) Challenge the requirement and ask for it to be removed.
- D) Escalate to your senior designer without speaking to the policy colleague.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** "The policy requires it" is sometimes exactly true, sometimes a summary satisfiable another way, and sometimes an inherited assumption. This tests asking "what is this for?" rather than "must we?" — option C is least effective, a head-on challenge without understanding the basis, which will not succeed and makes the next conversation harder.

**Question 10 (harder) — Findings the team does not want**

Research shows a feature the team has built over six weeks causes problems for eight of ten participants, with five minutes at the show and tell. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Lead with the positives and mention the difficulties briefly.
- B) Present what happened clearly and without blame — what participants tried, where they got stuck, what they said — and frame the next step as understanding why, inviting the team into the diagnosis.
- C) Present the failure rate starkly so the team takes it seriously.
- D) Ask your senior designer to present it instead.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Delivery determines whether a difficult finding gets acted on or defended against, and ending on an invitation into the diagnosis brings people who built the feature into finding the cause. Option A is least effective, softening an eight-in-ten failure so six weeks of effort continues down the same path.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

A blueprint shared a fortnight ago, against which the team has planned two sprints, is found to omit a step where many users telephone the contact centre after receiving a letter. Ask what should happen.

- A) Update it and mention it at stand-up.
- B) Tell the team immediately, explain exactly what was missing, flag which planning decisions may have relied on the incomplete picture, and note that a letter reliably generating calls is itself a finding worth investigating.
- C) Update it quietly.
- D) Raise it in the retrospective.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Two sprints have been planned against an incomplete picture, so correction alone is insufficient. This tests flagging downstream impact and recognising the omission as a finding, not merely a documentation gap — option C is least effective, leaving downstream consequences unexamined and risking discovery by someone else.

**Question 12 (harder) — Design work that never lands**

For three sprints, design work has arrived after the relevant build decisions were made; the team is appreciative but nothing changes. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept it; the pace is set by the product manager.
- B) Bring specific examples to a senior designer and to the team's retrospective, showing where earlier design input would have changed a decision, and propose a concrete change such as a design checkpoint before stories are committed.
- C) Work faster.
- D) Stop producing design work until the team commits to using it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This is a process problem needing a process forum, evidence, and a concrete proposal. This tests bringing specific examples and a named change to the team's own improvement mechanism — option D is least effective, punishing users and the team rather than the process, and costing the relationships needed to change it.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate takes genuine independent action within remit and discloses anything beyond it promptly.
- Note whether the candidate zooms out — considering the step before and after, or the whole journey — rather than addressing only the artefact in front of them.
- Watch for whether the candidate asks what a stated policy or technical constraint actually is before designing around it.
- Score for whether own errors are disclosed promptly, with downstream impact flagged.
- Note whether the candidate brings specific evidence or a concrete proposal to a recurring process problem rather than accepting it or withdrawing effort.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that ranks by seniority of the requester rather than by consequence.
- Missing when a candidate defends a design against evidence rather than examining it in light of the evidence.
- Rewarding a response treating "out of scope" as a sufficient answer about a user's actual experience.
- Accepting an accessibility statement offered as a way to launch with a known blocker.
- Missing when a candidate delays disclosure of their own error rather than raising it and its downstream impact immediately.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and error checking across service blueprints, evidence sources, and prototype fidelity choices; the arithmetic that makes design judgements defensible — measuring drop-off against the correct denominator, converting a defect into a specific number of affected people, and adding up waiting time as well as active time when estimating a journey's real duration; the discipline of reading service standards, accessibility requirements and review documents at exactly the strength the text supports, especially distinguishing a rule with an escape route from an unconditional one; and the situational judgement that defines a strong junior service designer — genuine independent action within remit, prompt honest disclosure beyond it, and zooming out to the whole journey before deciding.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
