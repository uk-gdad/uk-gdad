# Service Designer - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for service designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A service designer is a confident, competent designer who develops designs from evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes, is trusted to make good decisions, recognises when to ask for further guidance, contributes to design concepts, and interprets evidence-based research and incorporates it into their work. At this level a candidate is rarely choosing between a right answer and a wrong one, and usually between defensible options under incomplete information, competing constraints and real consequences.

Three features of the role shape this material. First, a candidate is trusted to make good decisions, so the strongest answers do not escalate everything and do know which decisions belong to someone else. Second, the role now designs strategically — identifying risks and opportunities and aligning work to organisational objectives as well as team goals — which turns prioritisation from an administrative question into a design one. Third, the role runs design sessions, engages the right people, and gives and receives constructive feedback, so several items concern getting good thinking out of a room rather than out of the candidate alone.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate reasons about second-order and forward-looking consequences (risk and opportunity) rather than only immediate problems, and for whether they can identify the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence while stating its limits. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a candidate who exercises real professional judgement, holds a position under pressure when evidence warrants it, and escalates only what genuinely needs authority they do not hold.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At this level the materials get messier and the reasoning less mechanical: evidence supporting two incompatible readings, service problems whose cause is genuinely ambiguous, design constraints that turn out to be negotiable and others that do not, and prioritisation problems with no dominant option.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

Two things distinguish this section at this level. First, a candidate is expected to reason about risk and opportunity rather than only about problems — forward-looking reasoning about what is likely to go wrong and where something is available that nobody has taken, with several items turning on a second-order consequence rather than an immediate one. Second, comfort with irreducible uncertainty: a number of questions have no clean answer, only a best one, and the discriminating factor is whether a candidate can identify the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence and state its limits, rather than collapsing prematurely into confidence.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining evidence relevant to users or a service.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Design communication**: clearly explaining design decisions using appropriate tools and methods.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**: ensuring a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations.
- **Prioritisation and risk reasoning** map to **Designing strategically**: identifying risks and opportunities and using design to address them, and aligning to organisational objectives.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Iterative design**: prototyping at different fidelities and iterating across successive rounds of research.
- **Reasoning about people and positions** maps to **Designing together**: identifying and engaging the right people throughout the design process.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing a design against a standard**

Standard: every transactional page must use words users would use, say what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid asking for information already held, and offer a way to get help. A page: "Your application has been received. Your case will be triaged by the assessment team. Please re-enter your National Insurance number to confirm" — the service already holds it, and there is no contact information. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 4
- D) 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Triaged" is internal terminology (fails); it does say something about what happens next (arguably met); no timescale is given (fails); it re-requests held information (fails); no route to help (fails). Four unmet. This tests checking each requirement precisely — and, in a strong candidate's answer, noticing that the requirement most often missed when auditing is the one about something absent (a missing help route) rather than something present and wrong.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Rule: "A service may be released to public beta only with sign-off from the service owner, unless it is replacing an existing service on a like-for-like basis and the change has been assessed as low risk." A colleague reports: "It went to public beta last week without service owner sign-off." Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The rule was breached.
- B) The release was a like-for-like replacement assessed as low risk.
- C) Either it was a low-risk like-for-like replacement, or the rule was breached.
- D) The release was risky.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** With the exception compound (like-for-like *and* low risk) and the facts silent on whether it applied, only the disjunction is guaranteed. This tests reading a compound exception precisely — option D conflates "not assessed as low risk" with "risky," different claims, and a candidate trusted to make good decisions would establish which route was taken before forming a view.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Competing explanations**

Completion is 74% for users arriving from a GOV.UK guidance page, 46% for users arriving directly from a search engine. A product manager proposes redesigning the form. Ask for the most rigorous analysis.

- A) Agree; the form is clearly failing direct arrivals.
- B) The two groups differ in preparation rather than necessarily in what they encounter: users who came via guidance have generally learned what is required and gathered documents, while direct arrivals may be discovering the requirements mid-application — so the highest-value intervention may be at the point of entry rather than inside the form.
- C) A 28-point gap is too large to be explained by anything but the form.
- D) Direct entry to the form should be blocked.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Referral route is a marker for preparation, one of the most consequential confounds in service analytics — a difference in preparation can account for much of the gap without the form itself being worse for anyone. This tests naming the confound and drawing a practical, cheaper implication (a "what you'll need" step) rather than acting on an unexamined correlation (A) or mistaking effect size for causal certainty (C).

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritising by what can change**

Capacity for one substantial piece of work this quarter: (1) a service model decision is taken next month, committing three years of build; (2) a live service has a 32% drop-off at one step, unchanged for months; (3) pre-launch usability testing on screens already signed off, launching in four weeks; (4) a director wants design support for a business case due in six months. Ask which is strongest, and why.

- A) Item 2, because it affects the most users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decision is imminent, commits three years, and design input is the only thing that could still change it — giving it far the highest value.
- C) Item 4, because the director is the most senior sponsor.
- D) Item 3, because launching untested is a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 scores highest on decision leverage and remaining time, and a service model choice is exactly where design shifts the answer rather than decorating it. This tests ranking by how much a decision can still change, not sponsor seniority (C, a live risk at this level since directors ask directly) — item 3 sounds responsible and is weakest, since testing signed-off screens four weeks from launch produces findings with nowhere to go.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Risk reasoning**

A team proposes replacing a paper form with an online-only service, projecting a 30% cost reduction; 12% of applications currently arrive on paper. Ask for the most complete risk analysis.

- A) The risk is that 12% of users cannot apply.
- B) The immediate risk is exclusion of the 12%, but the second-order risks matter as much: those users will contact the service by other routes, so contact centre volume will rise; some will apply late or not at all, which may shift cost rather than remove it; and if the paper cohort skews towards people with the greatest need, the policy outcome is affected as well as the service metric.
- C) There is no significant risk; 88% of users are unaffected.
- D) The projected saving is probably overstated.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests following consequences beyond the first order — removing a channel does not remove the people who used it, and the final clause (if the paper cohort skews towards greatest need) is the most important, since the service may be shedding cost by shedding exactly the people it exists to serve. Option C treats a majority as sufficient.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Strength of a finding**

Ten of fourteen participants failed to notice a "save and return" link. Ask which conclusion is best supported.

- A) 71% of users do not notice the link.
- B) Discoverability of the link is a substantial problem, evidenced across most participants and varied user groups, warranting design work and a follow-up measure of whether the change helps.
- C) The link should be moved to the top of the page.
- D) The link is invisible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Ten of fourteen is a strong qualitative signal, stated at that strength with a named follow-up. This tests resisting a jump to one specific fix (C, when the cause could be position, wording, visual weight, or mental model) and resisting overreach in both directions — a population percentage (A) or "invisible" (D, overstating "not noticed").

**Question 7 (moderate) — Designing under a constraint**

Research and design work is needed for a service used mainly by people over 75, many not online; the organisation's recruitment supplier works exclusively through an online panel, and procurement for a new supplier takes eight weeks, with five available. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Use the online panel and note the limitation.
- B) Work within the five weeks using routes that do not need procurement — partnering with organisations already working with older people, running sessions in community venues or by telephone, and arranging incentives that do not require an online account — while starting procurement in parallel for future rounds.
- C) Delay until a suitable supplier is procured.
- D) Recruit through colleagues' older relatives.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The constraint is procurement, not recruitment, and this tests separating the two — partnering with charities, day centres and libraries is a legitimate route independent of a supplier contract, and starting procurement in parallel fixes the structural problem for next time. Option A produces findings about a different population from the one the service serves, and a limitation note does not repair that.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A relied-upon study used a prototype whose "back" function was broken throughout. Four findings: (i) participants could not locate the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned after taking a wrong turn; (iii) tasks took longer than expected; (iv) participants misread the declaration wording. Ask which findings are compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; it was a minor fault.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, because inability to go back plausibly caused both the abandonment after wrong turns and the extended durations, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only finding (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A broken back function directly manufactures abandonment and inflated task times; content comprehension findings are largely untouched. This tests scoping precisely rather than wholesale condemnation (A), which discards sound findings — someone who could not locate the eligibility criteria could not locate them with a working back button either.

**Question 9 (harder) — Spotting the opportunity**

40% of users who abandon at the evidence step return within seven days and complete; the team treats this as a success and focuses on the 60% who do not. Ask for the strongest observation.

- A) Agree; the 60% is the real problem.
- B) The 40% who return are also evidence of a problem — they are making two or more attempts at something that should take one, which costs them time and generates repeat contact — and understanding why they had to leave and what let them come back successfully is likely the fastest route to helping the 60% too.
- C) The 40% should be excluded from the drop-off figure.
- D) The service should send reminders to everyone who abandons.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A returning user is a user who could not finish first time, almost always because a document was not to hand. This tests treating a comfortable number as still needing investigation, and identifying the natural experiment inside it — whatever let the returners come back is likely the mechanism that would help the 60%. Option C would conceal the phenomenon entirely.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reconciling contradictory evidence**

A satisfaction survey of 2,000 users reports 81% positive; fourteen research sessions found substantial frustration, with eleven participants describing significant difficulty; the contact centre reports the service as its third-highest call driver. Ask how this should be synthesised.

- A) Trust the survey; 2,000 responses outweigh fourteen sessions.
- B) The sources measure different populations and constructs: satisfaction surveys are typically served at the end of a journey so people who abandoned rarely see them, and they often ask about outcome rather than process — while the contact centre volume is behavioural evidence at scale that corroborates the sessions. The picture is a service most people complete, many find hard, and a substantial minority need help with.
- C) The sources conflict irreconcilably.
- D) The service is performing well with minor usability issues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests noting who each source sees and what it measures — a completion-end survey reaches only people who got there, while contact centre volume is behavioural evidence at scale corroborating the sessions using exactly the kind of data sceptical colleagues find credible. Option D is the comfortable reading that ignores the contact centre, the most telling figure in the set.

**Question 11 (harder) — A constraint that may not be one**

A policy colleague states a service must collect three years of address history "because the policy requires it"; research shows the question causes 18% of users to abandon. Ask for the most productive line of enquiry.

- A) Accept the requirement and design the question as well as possible.
- B) Ask what the requirement is for and where it is written, on the reasoning that the underlying need — most likely identity verification or fraud prevention — may be satisfiable by other means, and that knowing the need rather than the question opens design options that arguing about the question never will.
- C) Challenge the policy requirement and ask for it to be removed.
- D) Propose making the question optional.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "The policy requires it" may be exactly true, a compressed account of a need satisfiable differently, or an inherited practice unrevisited. This tests asking "what is this for?" rather than "must we?" — option D proposes a solution that may quietly break the requirement it was meant to accommodate, and option C is a head-on challenge without understanding the basis.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in your own work**

Three weeks after a design decision was supported, the research behind it is found to have recruited only users who had previously completed the service successfully. Ask what should happen.

- A) Note it as a limitation in the next report.
- B) Tell the team and the decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are affected and which still stand, and propose targeted follow-up with users who did not complete — since those are the people the study could not see and the ones the design most needs to serve.
- C) Quietly commission follow-up research and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Say nothing; the design decision was reasonable anyway.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A sample of successful completers cannot see the failures the design was meant to address. This tests prompt disclosure, precise scoping (some conclusions may still stand) rather than minimising or catastrophising, and proposing the fix. Option D is least defensible, substituting a guess for the evidence originally relied on.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reasons forward about risk and opportunity, not only about immediate problems.
- Note whether the candidate identifies the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence while explicitly stating its limits.
- Watch for whether the candidate scopes a methodological flaw precisely rather than treating it as total or trivial.
- Score for whether the candidate ranks priority by how much a decision can still change and how much time remains, not by sponsor seniority.
- Note whether the candidate names a confound before accepting an apparent cause, particularly where the confound points at a cheaper intervention.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting participants converted into a population percentage.
- Missing when a candidate treats effect size as proof of causal certainty.
- Rewarding an analysis that stops at the first-order consequence and misses the second-order one.
- Accepting a partially satisfied compound exception as if it were fully satisfied.
- Missing when a candidate ranks priority by the seniority of the requester rather than decision leverage.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles the quantitative material this role produces and consumes. At this level the emphasis shifts from calculating to interrogating — candidates still need to compute rates correctly, but the harder items ask whether a figure means what somebody claims, often the question only the candidate in the room is positioned to ask.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or short paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided. Distractors are built from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value used as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting, raw counts compared across unequal groups.

Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority — a number in a slide is treated as fact and a design argument as a preference, regardless of the rigour behind either. Being able to ask "what is the denominator, and who could not have appeared in this data?" is a substantial part of the value this role adds.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading analytics accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining evidence such as web analytics data.
- **Comparing rates between groups** maps to **Designing for everyone**: a gap between groups is often the first numerical trace of exclusion.
- **Measuring whether an iteration worked** maps to **Iterative design**.
- **Quantifying risk and opportunity** maps to **Designing strategically**: identifying risks requires knowing how many people are involved.
- **Explaining figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Interrogating others' claims** maps to **Designing together**: giving and receiving constructive feedback, including on evidence.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Of 480 users who started, 360 completed. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 360÷480=75%. This tests basic percentage calculation, checkable by estimating three quarters first — and, in a strong candidate's answer, distinguishing "75% of users who started completed" from "75% of users completed," which quietly shifts the denominator.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off against the right base**

A step is reached by 3,600 users and passed by 2,700. Ask for the drop-off rate.

- A) 25%
- B) 33%
- C) 75%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Drop-off 900÷3,600=25%. This tests measuring against those who arrived, not against those who continued (option B) — if two steps are computed with different denominators, the resulting ranking of where to focus can simply be wrong.

**Question 3 (easy) — Channel rates**

Started/Completed: Online 12,000/9,600; Telephone 2,400/2,160; Paper 1,600/640. Ask which channel has the lowest completion rate, and how many users it loses.

- A) Paper, 40% rate, 960 users lost
- B) Online, 80% rate, 2,400 users lost
- C) Paper, 60% rate, 640 users lost
- D) Telephone, 90% rate, 240 users lost

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Rates: online 80%, telephone 90%, paper 40%; paper loses 960. This tests calculating rates correctly, and, in a strong candidate's answer, giving both readings — online loses more people by volume (2,400) while paper's rate signals something badly broken, and paper users are frequently those who cannot use the online channel, making the failure rate an inclusion problem.

**Question 4 (easy) — Elapsed journey time**

Journey: form (30 minutes), wait for reference (3 working days), post evidence (2 working days transit), assessment (5 weeks), decision letter (2 working days). Ask roughly how long the journey is from the user's perspective.

- A) About 30 minutes
- B) About 5 weeks
- C) About 6 to 7 weeks
- D) About 3 months

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Adding the waits (3+2 working days, plus 5 weeks, plus 2 working days) gives roughly six and a half weeks; the half hour of active time is negligible. This tests seeing the whole elapsed journey rather than only active time — option A is the instructive trap, exactly how a service comes to be described internally as "a half-hour application."

**Question 5 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before: 720 of 1,200 users completed a step. After: 950 of 1,400. Ask whether completion improved, and by how much in percentage points.

- A) Yes, by 230 users
- B) Yes, by about 8 percentage points
- C) Yes, by about 13 percentage points
- D) No, the rate fell

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Before 60%, after ≈68%: about 8 points. This tests converting to rates because totals differ — option A compares raw counts across differently sized groups, crediting the design with an increase partly caused by more traffic arriving.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups**

Of 800 users under 65, 680 completed online. Of 240 over 65, 72 completed. Ask for the gap in percentage points.

- A) 30
- B) 45
- C) 55
- D) 85

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Under-65s 85%; over-65s 30%; gap 55 points. This tests converting to rates before comparing — options A and D offer the two group rates as if they were the gap. A gap this size would justify investigating what in the online journey older users cannot get past.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Weekly completions rose from 640 to 800. Ask for the percentage increase.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 80%
- D) 125%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (800−640)÷640=25%. This tests dividing by the original value — option A divides by 800, the commonest error, understating every improvement described this way.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Quantifying an exclusion risk**

240,000 applications a year, 9% arriving on paper; the team proposes closing the paper channel. Ask how many people a year would need another route.

- A) 2,160
- B) 21,600
- C) 26,700
- D) 216,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 0.09×240,000=21,600. This tests converting a percentage into an affected-people count — "9% of applications" sounds like a rounding error, "21,600 people a year" does not, and the number sets up the next question: how many will telephone, apply late, or not apply at all.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

Journey: 40,000 start, 32,000 pass eligibility, 19,200 upload evidence, 17,280 submit. Ask what proportion of those who uploaded evidence went on to submit.

- A) 43%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 17,280÷19,200=90%. This tests identifying the group the question names as the denominator; the funnel also shows losses of 12,800 between eligibility and upload against 1,920 at submission — establishing that in fifteen seconds redirects a team's attention in the meeting rather than a week later.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A dashboard reports "average time on the eligibility page: 25 seconds," and a product manager concludes the page is easy; the candidate's sessions showed participants spending two to three minutes reading it. Ask for the likely explanation and the right response.

- A) The dashboard is wrong and should be disregarded.
- B) The mean probably mixes two populations — people who bounce within a few seconds and people who stay and read — so it describes neither, and the right response is to ask for the distribution rather than to dispute the figure.
- C) The session participants were unusually slow.
- D) The two measures cannot be compared.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A mean time on page combining bouncing and reading behaviours drags the figure below the experience of anyone actually engaging, describing nobody. This tests naming the mechanism and proposing a constructive intervention rather than picking a fight over a figure that is probably calculated correctly and interpreted wrongly.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion rose from 45% to 54% after a redesign. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) A 9% increase.
- B) A 9 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 20% relative increase.
- C) A 20 percentage point increase.
- D) A 16.7% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Gap 9 points; relative 9÷45=20%. This tests labelling both figures — "completion rose 20%" and "rose 9 percentage points" describe the same change, and being the person who calmly asks for both is a cheap way to keep an organisation's evidence honest.

**Question 12 (harder) — What a small comparison can establish**

Baseline is 60%, a 6-point improvement would be meaningful, and a colleague proposes 25 moderated sessions before and 25 after. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 50 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 6 point difference on 25 per group is between one and two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users, and spending the 50 sessions on understanding mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 40 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Twenty-five per group cannot distinguish a six-point difference from chance. This tests redirecting to the right instrument for each question — option D is the most damaging, since a reported difference from an underpowered comparison invites the team to act on noise, and caveats do not travel with the number.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator and the population it represents before calculating.
- Note whether the candidate converts to rates before comparing groups or channels of different sizes.
- Watch for whether the candidate adds up waiting time as well as active time when estimating a whole journey's real elapsed duration.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change.
- Note whether the candidate questions a metric's own construction (e.g. what population a mean actually describes) rather than accepting it or disputing it outright.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a drop-off or completion rate calculated against the wrong denominator.
- Missing when a candidate compares raw counts before and after a change without accounting for different totals.
- Rewarding a journey duration estimate that counts only active user time and ignores waiting.
- Accepting an underpowered comparison endorsed as adequate to detect the effect size in question.
- Missing when a candidate disputes a figure outright rather than asking for its definition or distribution.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. In a role that runs on documents — service standards, accessibility requirements, policy summaries, operational procedures, letters sent to users, supplier contracts — reading accuracy is the substrate everything else rests on. A misread requirement produces a design that fails an audit; a misread finding produces a design that fails a user.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to mark True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, and a candidate's own professional knowledge is the main hazard at this level — the more standards they have read, the more readily they supply the clause they expect. Cannot Say deserves particular attention because candidates avoid it, reaching for True or False to seem decisive.

A great many design opportunities live in the gap between what a policy actually requires and what everyone assumes it requires, and reading the requirement precisely is how a candidate finds that gap.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**: ensuring a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: explaining problems and design ideas.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: knowing which constraints are hard tells a candidate how much room they have.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**: noticing a procedure never mentions people without internet access has a direct consequence.
- **Reading feedback and direction accurately** maps to **Designing together**: giving and receiving constructive feedback.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "All new public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before going into public beta. Where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, the team must publish an accessibility statement setting out the gap and the plan to close it. Services already in live operation must meet the standard within 18 months of this policy taking effect. Internal-facing tools are outside the scope of this policy, although teams are encouraged to apply the same standard."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Internal tools must meet the accessibility standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Internal tools are outside scope, and teams are only "encouraged," not required. This tests catching the two-part structure common in policy prose — exclusion followed by soft aspiration — misreported in both directions.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service that cannot meet one requirement has breached the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage anticipates this and provides a route: publish an accessibility statement. This tests recognising a rule that anticipates a case and defines honesty about it rather than prohibiting it outright — the breach would be failing to publish, not failing the requirement.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service that entered public beta last year without meeting the standard breached the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage never states when the policy took effect, so whether a service entering beta last year was covered at the time cannot be determined. This tests checking whether a deadline's reference point is actually given.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "Publishing an accessibility statement is sufficient for a service to comply with this policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage requires a statement where a requirement cannot be met, but does not say publishing discharges every other obligation, nor that it is insufficient. This tests resisting supplying a link the passage does not make — reasoning that "the passage provides a route" therefore means full compliance is exactly the overreach this section penalises.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The review found that applicants using the assisted digital route completed more slowly than online applicants and required an average of 2.3 contacts with the service. The review notes that assisted digital users differed from online users in several respects, including age and first language. The review did not assess whether the completed applications were of comparable quality. It recommends that the service team consider whether additional guidance would reduce the number of contacts."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Assisted digital applicants required more than two contacts on average."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 2.3 is more than two, stated about assisted digital users specifically. This tests the baseline reading — a claim broadened to "applicants generally" would overstate the passage and be Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The assisted digital route causes applications to take longer."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage names two confounds (age, first language) that could equally explain the difference. This tests treating declared confounds as a warning against inferring causation — the design consequence differs entirely depending on the actual cause.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "Assisted digital applications are of lower quality."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states explicitly that quality was not assessed. This tests treating declared silence as establishing nothing — inferring lower quality from slower completion and more contacts is exactly the overreach this item catches.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "The review recommends providing additional guidance."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the team "consider whether" additional guidance would help, including concluding it would not. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X," a distinction that pervades real review documents.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Teams should offer users a choice of channel wherever practicable. A digital channel must not be the only option for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Where a team withdraws a channel, it must assess the impact on affected users and record the assessment; records are reviewed annually. Users who need assistance must be able to reach a person by telephone during published service hours."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Users must be able to reach a person at any time."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage specifies "who need assistance" and "during published service hours," both dropped by the statement. This tests catching dropped qualifiers — a statement can be assembled entirely from the passage's own vocabulary and still say something the passage does not.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A team that withdraws a channel without recording an impact assessment has breached the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The requirement is unconditional. This tests distinguishing a rule with an escape route (Passage A, Question 2) from an unconditional rule, where failing it straightforwardly is a breach.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "A digital-only design for a general-population service breaches the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The hard prohibition is scoped to services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion, which a general-population service does not obviously meet — but the softer "should offer a choice wherever practicable" has not necessarily been discharged, since practicability is not stated. This tests holding two provisions in mind at once rather than resolving the case with only one.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Digital services are unsuitable for many users.
- B) Users should be able to reach services through a channel that works for them, with digital-only ruled out where exclusion is likely, withdrawals assessed and recorded, and human help available during service hours.
- C) Teams must record an impact assessment when withdrawing a channel.
- D) Users must be able to speak to a person.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary spanning the whole passage without inventing anything — A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement, and C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest, with D also dropping both qualifications.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a passage before concluding what a provision requires, resisting the pull of their own professional expertise.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a rule with an escape route from an unconditional rule.
- Watch for whether declared confounds or declared gaps in what was assessed are treated as genuine limits on what can be concluded.
- Score for whether the candidate resists treating one compliance route as equivalent to full compliance.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary covering the whole passage rather than one true detail promoted above the rest.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False from the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate reads "encouraged" as "required."
- Rewarding an association read as causation despite a declared confound.
- Accepting "consider whether X" read as "recommends X."
- Missing when a candidate assumes an escape route exists where a requirement is in fact unconditional.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. A service designer is a confident, competent designer trusted to make good decisions, who recognises when to ask for guidance, contributes to design concepts, plans and runs design sessions, engages the right people, and gives and receives constructive feedback. What distinguishes this level from a junior one: a junior designer escalates a policy question, while this level is expected to have the conversation, understand the constraint, and escalate only what genuinely needs authority they do not hold.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Users come first, specifically the users struggling most. Protect the integrity of the evidence and the design rationale — options that inflate, soften or mislabel findings are close to automatically wrong, however reasonable the motive. Address problems directly, early, with the person involved. And fix the instance, then the system — spotting the systemic version of a recurring problem, and doing something about it, is a marker of the seniority this role is working towards.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Explaining and defending design decisions** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Protecting inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Running sessions and engaging the right people** maps to **Designing together**.
- **Giving and receiving feedback** maps to **Designing together**, named in both directions.
- **Using evidence honestly** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Risk, opportunity and alignment** map to **Designing strategically**.
- **Supporting colleagues** maps to **Leading design**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A design session that is producing one voice**

Twenty minutes into a session, two senior stakeholders dominate and four invited caseworkers have not spoken. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it run; the stakeholders know the service well.
- B) Change the structure — ask everyone to write for two minutes, then go round the table, or split into pairs — so the quieter participants contribute without anyone being told to stop talking.
- C) Ask the stakeholders directly to let others speak.
- D) Follow up with the caseworkers separately afterwards.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The session is producing one perspective when it was convened for several. This tests adapting a session's format rather than confronting individuals — option A is least effective, wasting the caseworkers' time and producing a design based on how senior people imagine the service works.

**Question 2 (easy) — Feedback you disagree with**

A senior designer says a service blueprint is "confusing and doesn't show the offline journey," though the candidate believes it does. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Point out where the offline journey appears.
- B) Ask them to show you where they got lost, on the reasoning that if an experienced designer cannot find it then the blueprint is not communicating it — then fix that.
- C) Accept the criticism and redo it.
- D) Ask for written feedback to consider later.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Both things can be true — the offline journey is on the blueprint, and the blueprint does not communicate it. This tests converting feedback into information rather than winning the argument — option A is least effective, leaving the artefact exactly as confusing and teaching the room that feedback is effortful.

**Question 3 (easy) — A live problem in something you designed**

Caseworkers report that a form section the candidate designed is producing cases they cannot process, and have been handling them manually. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Explain that the section was research-based and ask them to raise a ticket.
- B) Thank them, ask for specific examples of what users are entering and how many cases are affected, diagnose whether the design invites the misunderstanding, and propose a fix quickly.
- C) Wait for the next research round.
- D) Ask for the section to be reverted.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Caseworkers seeing the same wrong answer repeatedly is high-quality free evidence. This tests gathering specifics and understanding scale — option A is least effective, defending a design against evidence it is not working, costing both the design and the caseworkers' willingness to raise anything next time.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Scope creep in a design session**

Halfway through a session on the application journey, a stakeholder asks to also cover a proposed new feature "since everyone's here." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Cover it; it is efficient use of the time.
- B) Explain that adding an unrelated topic risks both the session's original purpose and the quality of thinking on the new one, and propose either a short separate session or adding it to the next one with proper preparation.
- C) Refuse, since the agenda is agreed.
- D) Cover it briefly at the end if there is time.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Session time is finite and an unprepared topic gets thin, unstructured attention. This tests redirecting rather than refusing a legitimate but mistimed request — option D is least effective, an unprepared topic discussed by a tired room producing conclusions too weak to use.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to soften a design position**

A journey mapped as thirty minutes actually takes six weeks; a stakeholder asks for the waiting periods to be removed from the map. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Remove them.
- B) Explain that the waits are the largest part of the user's experience and their removal would make the map describe something users would not recognise — then offer a visual treatment, such as compressing or greying them, so the design steps stay legible.
- C) Refuse and send it unchanged.
- D) Produce two versions.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's underlying concern — legibility — is legitimate. This tests holding the substance while addressing it: a map omitting six weeks of waiting is not a map of the user's experience. Option D is least effective, since two versions of the truth means the convenient one gets used.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A requirement you doubt**

A policy colleague says a service must collect three years of address history because the policy requires it; research shows the question causes 18% abandonment. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Design the question as well as possible and accept it.
- B) Share the abandonment evidence, ask what the requirement is for and where it is written, and explore whether the underlying need could be met another way — while being ready to accept the constraint if it turns out to be fixed.
- C) Challenge the requirement and ask for it to be removed.
- D) Escalate to your senior designer without speaking to the policy colleague.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** "The policy requires it" may be exactly true, satisfiable differently, or unrevisited. This tests asking "what is this for?" rather than "must we?" — option C is least effective, a head-on challenge without understanding the basis.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Findings the team does not want**

Research shows a feature built over two months causes problems for nine of twelve participants, with five minutes at the show and tell. Ask for the most effective approach.

- A) Lead with the positives and mention the difficulties briefly.
- B) Present what happened clearly and without blame — what participants tried, where they got stuck, what they said — and frame the next step as understanding why, inviting the team into the diagnosis.
- C) Present the failure rate starkly so the team takes it seriously.
- D) Ask your senior designer to present it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Delivery determines whether a difficult finding gets acted on or defended against. This tests keeping the focus on observed behaviour and inviting the builders into the diagnosis — option A is least effective, softening a nine-in-twelve failure so two months of effort continues down the same path.

**Question 8 (harder) — An accessibility blocker close to launch**

Two weeks before launch, a key step is found incompletable with a screen reader. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with the delivery manager and service owner, describe precisely what cannot be done and by whom, ask for an explicit decision, and offer any interim route that would let affected users complete the journey another way.
- C) Attempt the fix yourself in the two weeks.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A step incompletable by screen reader means some people cannot use the service, and weighing a delay against exclusion is a decision for people with authority. This tests securing an explicit decision and an interim route — option A is least effective, sending a genuine blocker to a backlog where it will remain unfixed.

**Question 9 (harder) — A problem that belongs to someone else**

Research shows users routinely fail at a step owned by another department, before reaching this service; the team says it is out of scope. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept it; you cannot fix another department's service.
- B) Evidence the problem and its effect on your users, find a route to raise it with the other department through someone with standing to do so, and separately design what your own service can do to reduce the harm — such as explaining the earlier step, or handling people who arrive without having completed it.
- C) Contact the other department directly yourself.
- D) Document it in the findings and move on.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A user's journey does not respect organisational boundaries even though budgets do. This tests evidencing the problem, finding a legitimate route, and designing what can be done meanwhile — option A is least effective, since "out of scope" describes a budget, not a person's experience.

**Question 10 (harder) — Consensus that would harm users**

A team has converged on a solution everyone likes, which the evidence indicates will not work for the users who struggle most, and reopening it reverses a decision people are relieved to have made. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go; consensus has value.
- B) Raise it once, clearly and specifically, showing what the evidence indicates will happen to those users and what a mitigation might look like — then if the team proceeds, ensure the risk is recorded and propose measuring the outcome after release.
- C) Escalate to the service owner over the team's head.
- D) Keep raising it until the team changes course.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests threading a professional obligation to say the thing against the need to keep working with the same people — raising once with evidence and a mitigation, then documenting the risk and proposing measurement if the decision stands. Option A is least effective, since consensus that harms the users who struggle most is not a value.

**Question 11 (harder) — A flaw in your own work**

Research relied on for a design decision now in build is found to have recruited only users who had previously completed the service. Ask what should happen.

- A) Note it as a limitation next time.
- B) Tell the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are affected and which still stand, and propose targeted follow-up with users who did not complete.
- C) Quietly commission follow-up and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Say nothing; the decision was reasonable anyway.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A sample of successful completers cannot see the failures the design was meant to address, and something is already being built on it. This tests prompt disclosure with precise scoping — option D is least effective, substituting a guess for the evidence originally relied on, and concealment being what is remembered if it emerges later.

**Question 12 (harder) — Design work that never lands**

Across three sprints, design work has consistently arrived after build decisions were taken; the team is appreciative and nothing changes. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept it; the pace is the product manager's call.
- B) Bring specific examples to the team's retrospective showing where earlier design input would have changed a decision, propose a concrete change such as a design checkpoint before stories are committed, and secure your senior designer's or the delivery manager's support for it.
- C) Work faster so design arrives sooner.
- D) Stop producing design work until the team commits to using it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This is a process problem needing a forum, evidence, and a proposal with organisational backing. This tests bringing specific examples and securing support from someone with influence over planning — option D is least effective, punishing users and the team rather than the process.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate exercises real professional judgement and holds a position the evidence supports under pressure.
- Note whether the candidate escalates only what genuinely needs authority they do not hold, having first engaged directly with the person who can resolve it.
- Watch for whether the candidate fixes both the instance and the underlying system (a process, a session format) rather than the instance alone.
- Score for whether an own or relied-upon flaw is disclosed promptly, with precise scoping of what is and is not affected.
- Note whether the candidate protects the integrity of a design rationale, resisting a response that inflates, softens or mislabels a finding.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that defends a design against evidence rather than examining it in light of the evidence.
- Missing when a candidate treats "out of scope" as a sufficient answer about a user's actual experience.
- Rewarding an accessibility statement offered as a way to launch with a known blocker.
- Accepting consensus treated as a terminal value when it harms the users who struggle most.
- Missing when a candidate escalates over a colleague's head as a first move rather than using the direct route.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and risk reasoning across service standards, competing explanations, and second-order consequences; the arithmetic that makes design judgements defensible — measuring drop-off against the correct denominator, converting a defect into a specific number of affected people, and adding up waiting time as well as active time when estimating a journey's real duration; the discipline of reading service standards, accessibility requirements and review documents at exactly the strength the text supports, especially distinguishing a rule with an escape route from an unconditional one; and the situational judgement that defines a strong service designer — exercising real professional judgement, escalating only what needs authority not held, and fixing the instance and the system together.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answers are consistently the ones honest about the limits of what the evidence shows. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
