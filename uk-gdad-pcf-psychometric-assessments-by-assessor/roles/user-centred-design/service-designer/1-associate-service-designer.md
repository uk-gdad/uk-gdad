# Associate Service Designer - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for associate service designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the everyday thinking a particular job actually uses. For an associate service designer that means noticing when a journey map and a set of research findings disagree, spotting that a service step assumes something users do not have, reading a design standard carefully and applying it, working out what a set of numbers about a service is really telling you, and making sensible decisions when something in a design session goes sideways.

An associate service designer is a trainee in an entry-level position, working under supervision, expected to show design aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. Nobody at this level is expected to have mapped a cross-government service or negotiated with a policy team — what is being tested is exactly what the role summary names: aptitude, potential, and understanding. Every rule, definition and figure needed appears inside the question; nothing needs to be memorised.

Service design is unusual in that its subject is the whole journey — not a screen, not a page, but everything a person has to do, across digital and offline channels, sometimes across several organisations, to get something done. That means the thinking constantly has to zoom out: where does this person come from before they reach us, what happens after they submit, who else is involved, what happens to the person who cannot do this step at all. A surprising amount of what this material measures is precisely that habit of looking at the whole thing rather than the part in front of you.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and verbal items for whether the candidate concludes only what a stated rule or set of facts guarantees, resisting a plausible-but-unproven answer, and for whether they consider the whole journey — including waiting time and offline steps — rather than only the part immediately in front of them. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a response that acts within remit and is transparent at its edges — attempting what can competently be done and promptly telling the right person about what cannot — over either silent struggling or reflexive escalation.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking this role genuinely demands, using practical workplace materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences — a journey map, a set of service steps, a design standard, a list of research findings, a channel comparison, a short extract from a team's design principles.

Typical format: an online test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with questions clustering into pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a similar level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

For an associate service designer, so much of the early value of the role comes from noticing — that a journey map shows a step users described completely differently in research, that a service assumes everyone has a bank account, that two teams have designed the same letter in two incompatible ways, that the "quick" route through a service takes three weeks if a document has to arrive by post. Each is a small act of comparison or deduction, and each protects the quality of the design.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Evidence-based design**: identifying common sources and types of data. Recognising that several findings describe the same underlying problem is the first step in using evidence at all.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**: describing different access needs and ways to meet them. Spotting that a design step excludes somebody is error checking with a human consequence.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Evidence-based design**: framing ideas as design hypotheses to be tested, which means knowing exactly what evidence supports.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Iterative design**: working in short cycles means constantly deciding what to do first and being able to explain why.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Designing together** and **Design communication**.
- **Seeing the whole journey** maps to **Designing strategically**: aligning design work to a team's wider goals.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a journey map**

A team rule: every journey map step must record what the user does, what the service does in response, and how long the step takes. Four steps: Step 1 — user completes online form, confirmation email sent, 15 minutes; Step 2 — user uploads evidence, no service response recorded, 10 minutes; Step 3 — user waits for decision, case assessed, 3 weeks; Step 4 — user receives letter, no service response, no duration recorded. Ask how many steps are missing at least one required item.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 2 is missing a service response; Step 4 is missing both a service response and a duration. Two steps are incomplete. This tests distinguishing how many steps are incomplete from how many items are missing — there are three missing items across two steps, and the question asks about steps, not items (the trap in C) — a distinction that matters at work too, since "two steps need finishing" and "three pieces of information are missing" prompt different conversations.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a service rule**

Rule: "A service change may go live only if it has been tested with users, unless it is a correction to published content approved by the service owner." A colleague reports: "The change went live this morning, and it was not tested with users." Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The team broke the rule.
- B) The change was a content correction approved by the service owner.
- C) Either the change was an approved content correction, or the rule was broken.
- D) The change will cause problems for users.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is conditional with one permitted exception, and the given facts do not establish whether it applies. This tests deducing only what the facts guarantee — option D introduces an unsupported claim about consequences, since an untested change is not automatically harmful, though it may be.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition across research findings**

Six sessions about a licensing service. Difficulties mentioned: P1 — did not know which documents were needed; P2 — documents, fee confusing; P3 — could not find the phone number; P4 — documents; P5 — documents, phone number; P6 — fee confusing. Ask which difficulty is most widely shared.

- A) Not knowing which documents were needed
- B) Finding the fee confusing
- C) Not being able to find the phone number
- D) All three are equally common

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Documents affected four of six participants (P1, P2, P4, P5); the other two issues affected two each. This tests counting participants affected rather than mentions, and, in a strong candidate's answer, recognising that spotting a pattern is not the same as explaining it — the documents issue could stem from unclear guidance, a hidden page, or genuinely variable requirements, each implying a different fix.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation in a design week**

Monday morning, four items: (1) a live page tells users the wrong fee, and people may pay the wrong amount today; (2) a senior designer asked for a rough journey map by Wednesday; (3) an 11:00 design session needing printed personas; (4) a newsletter about a cross-government design community event. Ask what should happen first.

- A) Start the journey map while the morning is quiet.
- B) Raise or fix the wrong fee on the live page.
- C) Print the personas for the 11:00 session.
- D) Read the newsletter before the week gets busy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A wrong fee causing people to pay the wrong amount today outranks everything else. This tests weighing impact and urgency together — harm to users outranks internal commitments, and internal commitments outrank nice-to-haves — and, in a strong candidate's answer, noting that at this level the right first action may be alerting a senior designer or content owner rather than fixing it alone, but either way it comes first.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking against instructions**

A senior designer requested exactly three changes to a service map: (i) add the offline channel to step 2, (ii) correct step 4's duration from two days to two weeks, (iii) remove the duplicate step 6. The returned map shows all three changes plus an unrequested colour scheme change throughout. Ask how many of the changes made were requested.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three requested changes were made; the colour change was extra. This tests distinguishing what was requested from what was done — delivering exactly what was agreed and flagging anything else is the discipline, since unrequested changes are how inconsistencies creep into shared artefacts, and a map whose colours no longer match the team's other maps is a small but real cost.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about a service journey**

Rules: evidence must be submitted within 28 days of applying; a decision is issued within 10 working days of evidence being received; applicants may request one extension of 14 days to the evidence deadline. An applicant applied 1 March, requested an extension, and submitted evidence on 10 April. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The applicant missed the deadline.
- B) The applicant submitted within the extended deadline.
- C) The applicant will receive a decision by 20 April.
- D) The application is invalid.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The original deadline (29 March) extended by 14 days becomes 12 April; 10 April is within it. This tests working dates through step by step — option C is tempting but wrong, since the decision comes within 10 *working* days, not calendar days, so the exact date depends on weekends and bank holidays. Checking whether a rule says days or working days matters, since service journeys routinely mix the two and users routinely misread them, itself a design problem worth noticing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across channels**

Completion: Online 6,400/8,000; Telephone 900/1,000; Post 400/1,000. Ask which channel most clearly needs design attention, and why.

- A) Online, because it has the most incomplete applications in absolute terms.
- B) Post, because only 40% of postal applications complete, far below the other channels.
- C) Telephone, because it has the fewest applications.
- D) None; the overall completion rate is acceptable.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Online completes 80%, telephone 90%, post 40%. This tests converting to rates before comparing channels of different volumes — option A is a reasonable-sounding trap, since online loses more people in absolute terms (1,600 against post's 600), and a strong candidate's answer would note both matter, while a 40% completion rate signals something badly broken, and postal users are often those who cannot use the online channel, making their failure rate a likely inclusion problem rather than mere inefficiency.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking against a design standard**

Standard: a service page must use words users would use, say what happens next, state how long the step takes, and not ask for information already held. Page: "Your application has been received. Your case will be triaged by the assessment team. Please confirm your National Insurance number" — the service already holds it. Ask how many parts of the standard the page breaks.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Triaged" is internal terminology (fails); it does say something about what happens next (arguably met); it gives no timescale (fails); it re-requests held information (fails). Three broken. This tests checking each requirement precisely — a better version, "We have received your application. We will check it and write to you within 10 working days," is shorter and costs nothing to write correctly.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving in a design session**

Twenty minutes into a design session, two senior stakeholders dominate and three invited frontline caseworkers have not spoken; the most junior person present has noticed this. Ask for the most sensible way to think about it.

- A) Let it run; the stakeholders are senior and know the service.
- B) Recognise that the session's value depends on the caseworkers' knowledge, and use a structural technique — such as asking everyone to write ideas on cards for two minutes, then going round the table — to change who is speaking without confronting anyone.
- C) Interrupt and ask the stakeholders to let others speak.
- D) Speak to the caseworkers separately afterwards instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests recognising the room is producing one perspective and reaching for a structural rather than confrontational fix — available even to the most junior person present, which is exactly why it fits this level. Option A wastes the caseworkers' time and produces a design based on how senior people imagine the service works; option C risks direct conflict with senior stakeholders as a trainee when a gentler alternative exists.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Deduction about what evidence supports**

In research with nine participants, six said they would prefer updates by text message rather than letter. Ask which conclusion is best supported by this evidence alone.

- A) Most users of the service prefer text messages.
- B) The service should replace all letters with text messages.
- C) Six of nine participants preferred text messages, which is worth exploring further — including with users who may not have a mobile phone.
- D) Letters are unpopular and should be phased out.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Nine participants is a useful qualitative sample but does not support a population-wide claim (A), and B and D turn a preliminary finding into a service-wide decision — D additionally invents a claim about letters being unpopular that preferring one channel does not establish. This tests stating evidence at its actual strength, and, in a strong candidate's answer, naming that a channel decision is never only about preference — it is about who becomes unable to use the service if you change it.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation with competing pressures**

Senior designer away; at 10:00: (1) a caseworker reports the new form generates cases they cannot process, handling them manually; (2) a 14:00 design review with the candidate's part of the map unfinished; (3) a content designer needs confirmation of step names by 12:00 to publish guidance; (4) a colleague's prototype needs review this week. Ask for the most sensible order.

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — finish your own commitments first.
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — understand the live problem, unblock the content designer, then your own map, then the review.
- C) 3, 2, 1, 4 — deadlines in time order.
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — as listed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is a live problem affecting a colleague's ability to work and real users being handled by workaround, understandable and passable in fifteen minutes; item 3 has a hard 12:00 deadline and blocks somebody; item 2 can absorb the earlier interruptions. This tests ranking by consequence and deadline together, rather than by ownership (A, the classic trap since a candidate's own deadlines feel most pressing precisely because they are theirs) or strict time order alone (C, which places a 12:00 deadline behind an unfolding live problem costing someone hours today).

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about inclusion**

A service requires applicants to upload a photograph of a document, used heavily by people in temporary accommodation and people over 75. A team proposes phone-only upload with no alternative, simpler to build, and most people have a smartphone. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The team is right; most people have smartphones, and the alternative routes add cost.
- B) A single channel with no alternative excludes anyone without a suitable phone, without data, without somewhere private to photograph a document, or without the confidence to do it — and these overlap heavily with the service's main user groups, so the design would fail exactly the people it most needs to serve.
- C) The team should add a postal route as well.
- D) The team should test the phone upload with older users first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Phone-only upload bundles four assumptions, each failing disproportionately in this population, and because the affected group is the core user base rather than an edge case, the exclusion is the main case. This tests naming the mechanism and its scale — option A treats a majority statistic as sufficient, precisely the reasoning that produces inaccessible public services, and option C names a solution without the analysis that would justify it.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate checks every stated rule against every item precisely, rather than stopping at the first fault found.
- Note whether the candidate deduces only what given facts guarantee, resisting a plausible but unproven conclusion.
- Watch for whether the candidate converts raw counts to rates before comparing channels or groups of different sizes.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes a count of failing items from a count of failing requirements.
- Note whether the candidate considers the whole journey, including waiting periods and offline steps, rather than only the part directly in front of them.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that stops at the first problem found rather than completing a full count.
- Missing when a candidate counts mentions instead of participants affected.
- Rewarding a comparison of raw counts across channels of different sizes.
- Accepting "could be true" as though it were "must be true" in a deduction item.
- Missing when a candidate treats a majority statistic as sufficient justification for excluding a minority.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how well a candidate works with the numbers this role produces. Not a maths exam — no equations to solve, calculator usually provided, arithmetic deliberately simple where one is not. What is measured is whether a candidate reads a table correctly, picks the right numbers out of a cluttered display, chooses the right operation, and interprets the result sensibly.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item showing a small table, chart, or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question. Service design is full of numbers even though it is not a quantitative discipline — how many people drop out at each step, comparing phone against online, how long a journey actually takes when a two-week wait sits in the middle of it, how many people a channel closure would affect. At associate level the content is deliberately modest: percentages, fractions, proportions, simple averages, ratios, and reading values from tables.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**: identifying common sources and types of data. Journey analytics, channel volumes, and completion rates all arrive as tables.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Evidence-based design** and **Designing for everyone**: comparing completion between user groups or channels is how exclusion becomes visible in numbers.
- **Averages** map to **Iterative design**: understanding how long a step actually takes tells you whether an iteration improved anything.
- **Reading a journey end to end** maps to **Designing strategically**: understanding the whole service, including the waiting times nobody designed.
- **Interpreting figures cautiously** maps to **Design communication**: a decision justified by a misread number is hard to defend twice.
- **Spotting an implausible number** maps to **Evidence-based design**: framing hypotheses well starts with trusting the right data.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Of 250 people who started an application, 200 completed it. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 200÷250=80%. This tests basic percentage calculation, checkable by estimating first — clearly more than three quarters and less than everything.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off between steps**

A journey shows 1,200 users reaching the evidence step and 900 continuing past it. Ask how many dropped off, and what percentage of those reaching the step that is.

- A) 300 users, 25%
- B) 300 users, 33%
- C) 900 users, 75%
- D) 300 users, 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Drop-off 1,200−900=300; 300÷1,200=25%. This tests measuring drop-off against the number who arrived, not the number who continued (the denominator error in option B, using 900) — reporting the wrong one describes the step as a third worse than it is.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a channel table**

Completion: Online 3,400/4,000; Telephone 450/500; Post 200/500. Ask which channel has a completion rate of exactly 85%.

- A) Online
- B) Telephone
- C) Post
- D) None of them

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Online is 3,400÷4,000=85%. This tests reading a table efficiently — a faster route with round totals is asking what 85% of each total would be, which matches immediately for online.

**Question 4 (easy) — Average handling time**

Five telephone calls lasted 6, 9, 4, 11, 10 minutes. Ask for the mean.

- A) 7 minutes
- B) 8 minutes
- C) 9 minutes
- D) 10 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum 40÷5=8. This tests basic averaging, checkable with a shortcut from a round guess: differences from 8 are −2, +1, −4, +3, +2, summing to zero, confirming the mean.

**Question 5 (easy) — Total journey time**

Journey steps: complete the form (20 minutes), wait for a reference number (2 working days), upload evidence (15 minutes), wait for assessment (3 weeks), receive the decision letter (2 working days in the post). Ask roughly how long the whole journey takes.

- A) About 35 minutes
- B) About 3 weeks
- C) About 4 weeks
- D) About 6 weeks

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Adding the waiting periods (roughly 3 weeks plus 4 working days) gives just under four weeks; the 35 minutes of active user time is negligible by comparison. This tests seeing the whole journey rather than only active user time — option A is the instructive trap, counting only what the user does, exactly how services come to be described internally as "a twenty-minute application" when the person experiencing it waits a month.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups fairly**

Of 400 users under 65, 340 completed a form online. Of 200 over 65, 60 completed. Ask which statement is correct.

- A) Both groups completed at similar rates.
- B) The rate was 85% for under-65s and 30% for over-65s.
- C) Under-65s completed 280 more applications, so the difference is modest.
- D) The groups cannot be compared because they are different sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 340÷400=85%; 60÷200=30%. This tests converting to rates before comparing groups of different sizes — option D wrongly claims incomparability when converting to rates is precisely the correct method, and a 55-point gap this large would be a serious inclusion signal, pointing at something in the online journey older users cannot get past.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

After a redesign, weekly completed applications rose from 480 to 600. Ask for the percentage increase.

- A) 20%
- B) 24%
- C) 25%
- D) 120%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** (600−480)÷480=25%. This tests dividing by the original value, not the new one — option A divides by 600, the commonest error, which under-reports every improvement described this way.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a total**

20% of applicants abandon at the identity step, equal to 1,400 people a month. Ask how many people reach the identity step each month.

- A) 1,680
- B) 2,800
- C) 7,000
- D) 28,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 1,400÷0.2=7,000. This tests working back from a percentage and a count to the base, verifiable by reversing the calculation — establishing the base in ten seconds is what lets a candidate judge whether a quoted percentage deserves the weight it is being given.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Ratio in service volumes**

A service receives applications online:telephone in a 4:1 ratio, 3,500 a month total. Ask how many arrive by telephone.

- A) 700
- B) 875
- C) 2,800
- D) 1,400

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 5 shares of 700 each; telephone takes 1 share = 700 (online takes 4 = 2,800). This tests writing down what each share represents before calculating — option C answers the wrong half of the split, the trap for a candidate who calculates correctly and reads the question carelessly; 700 applications a month is far from negligible, displacing 8,400 people a year if the channel closed.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Choosing the right denominator**

Journey: 10,000 start, 8,000 pass eligibility, 6,000 upload evidence, 5,400 submit. Ask what percentage of those who uploaded evidence went on to submit.

- A) 54%
- B) 67%
- C) 68%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 5,400÷6,000=90%. This tests identifying the group the question names as the denominator — option A uses 10,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked, and telling a team "90% of people who upload evidence go on to submit" is a completely different message from "54% of all starters finish," pointing at different parts of the service.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Before a change, 30% of users completed a step unaided; afterwards, 45% did. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) Completion rose by 15%.
- B) Completion rose by 15 percentage points, which is a 50% increase.
- C) Completion rose by 50 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose by 33%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 45−30=15 points; 15÷30=50% relative. This tests correctly labelling both figures — "rose 50%" and "rose 15 percentage points" describe the identical change, with the first sounding more than three times as impressive, and being precise about which is meant is part of explaining a design decision honestly.

**Question 12 (harder) — When an average hides something**

Seven users' times to complete a step, in minutes: 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 7, 62. A colleague reports "the average was 13 minutes." Ask for the best comment.

- A) The calculation is wrong; the average is 5 minutes.
- B) The calculation is right, but the mean is misleading here: six of seven users finished within seven minutes and one extreme case is pulling the figure up. Report the median of 5 minutes with the range, and describe the 62-minute case separately.
- C) The figure is fine as reported.
- D) The 62-minute case should be excluded as an anomaly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 91÷7=13, arithmetically correct but describing no actual user, since six finished within seven minutes and one took 62. This tests recognising an outlier skewing a mean and reporting the median instead — and, in a strong answer, treating the outlier as the most interesting finding rather than excluding it (D, the most damaging option), since it is likely a lead worth investigating rather than noise.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads a numeric threshold or figure precisely rather than rounding toward a convenient answer.
- Note whether the candidate measures drop-off against the correct denominator (those who arrived, not those who continued).
- Watch for whether the candidate converts raw counts to rates before comparing groups or channels of different sizes.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change.
- Note whether the candidate adds up waiting time as well as active time when estimating a whole journey's duration.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a drop-off or completion rate calculated against the wrong denominator.
- Missing when a candidate compares raw counts across channels of different sizes.
- Rewarding a journey duration estimate that counts only active user time and ignores waiting.
- Accepting "percentage" and "percentage points" used interchangeably without distinguishing them.
- Missing when a candidate excludes an outlier rather than reporting and investigating it.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. Service design is a role that runs on documents — service standards, accessibility guidance, policy summaries, operational procedures, letters sent to users, and the words users themselves say. Getting any of those slightly wrong changes what gets designed.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say — regardless of what a candidate otherwise knows. Cannot Say deserves particular attention because candidates avoid it, reaching for True or False to seem decisive when the honest answer is that the passage does not settle it. Small words carry the meaning — "all" is not "most," "may" is not "must," "should" is not "will" — a distinction that matters more in this discipline than most, since distinguishing what a policy actually requires from what everyone assumes it requires is frequently where the best design opportunities are hiding.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**: accessibility requirements are written documents.
- **Distinguishing statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**: framing ideas as hypotheses.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: explaining design decisions.
- **Understanding qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: knowing whether a requirement is mandatory or recommended.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **Designing for everyone**: noticing a procedure never mentions people without internet access has a direct consequence.
- **Following written direction exactly** maps to **Designing together**: feedback only works if understood as given.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "All new public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before launch. Where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, the team must publish an accessibility statement explaining the gap and the plan to close it. Services launched before this policy came into effect must meet the standard within 18 months. Internal-facing tools are not covered by this policy, although teams are encouraged to apply the same standard."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "New public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before launch."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly in the first sentence. This tests the baseline reading, answerable quickly and confidently.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Internal tools must meet the accessibility standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Internal tools are not covered, and teams are only "encouraged" — not required — to apply the same standard. This tests catching the two-part structure common in policy prose: an exclusion followed by a softer aspiration, and reading only one half produces the wrong answer.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service that cannot meet one requirement has breached the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage provides a route through this: publish an accessibility statement explaining the gap and plan. This tests recognising a rule that anticipates a case and defines honesty about it rather than prohibiting it outright — the actual breach would be failing to publish the statement, not failing the requirement itself.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service launched two years ago must now meet the standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The 18-month deadline is relative to when the policy took effect, which the passage never states. This tests checking whether a deadline's reference point is actually given — a service launched two years ago could have launched before or after the policy came into effect, and the passage does not say which.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The review of the licensing service found that most applicants completed the online journey without help, but that applicants using the postal route took considerably longer and were more likely to submit incomplete applications. The review team notes that postal applicants differed from online applicants in several respects, including age and prior use of the service. The review recommends that the service team consider whether additional support for postal applicants is needed. Funding for any changes has not been agreed."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Postal applicants were more likely to submit incomplete applications than online applicants."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, respecting the passage's comparative framing ("more likely" rather than "usually"). A statement claiming postal applicants "usually" submit incomplete applications would overstate the passage and be Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The postal route causes applications to be incomplete."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage names two confounds (age, prior use) that could equally explain the difference. This tests treating declared confounds as a warning against inferring causation — the design consequence differs entirely depending on the actual cause, and treating an association as a cause is a reliable way for a team to spend months improving the wrong thing.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "Additional support for postal applicants will be provided."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The review recommends only "consider whether" support is needed, and funding has not been agreed. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from a firm decision to act — a phrase that recurs constantly in real review documents and is very frequently reported afterwards as though it were a firm recommendation.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "Most online applicants did not need help to complete the journey."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "Completed without help" and "did not need help to complete" describe the same claim. This tests recognising a rephrasing that changes the words but not the meaning is still supported — a statement like "online applicants found the journey easy" would add an experience claim the passage does not establish, and would be Cannot Say.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Teams should offer users a choice of channel wherever practicable. A digital channel must not be the only option for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Where a service withdraws a channel, the team must assess the impact on affected users and record the assessment. Users who need assistance must be able to reach a person, by telephone or in person, during published service hours."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "A digital-only service is always prohibited."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The prohibition is scoped to "services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion," and the statement's "always" goes beyond that. This tests reading the scope of a prohibition accurately — a rule forbidding something in defined circumstances is telling you about the undefined circumstances too.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A team that withdraws a channel without recording an impact assessment has breached the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The requirement is unconditional: withdrawal requires assessing and recording impact. This tests distinguishing a rule with an escape route (Passage A, Question 3, where failing the requirement was not itself the breach) from an unconditional rule, where failing it straightforwardly is the breach.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "Users must be able to reach a person at any time."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage specifies "during published service hours" and "who need assistance," both dropped by the statement. This tests catching dropped qualifiers — a statement can be built entirely from the passage's own vocabulary and still say something the passage does not.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Digital services are unsuitable for many users.
- B) Users should be able to access services through a channel that works for them, with digital-only ruled out where exclusion is likely, changes assessed for impact, and human help reachable during service hours.
- C) Teams must record impact assessments when withdrawing a channel.
- D) Users must be able to speak to a person.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary broad enough to cover every requirement without inventing anything — A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement, and C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest, with D also dropping the service-hours qualification.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a passage before concluding what a provision requires, rather than stopping at an early sentence.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a rule with an escape route (failure is not automatically a breach) from an unconditional rule.
- Watch for whether declared confounds are treated as a genuine limit on what can be concluded about causation.
- Score for whether the candidate catches a dropped qualifier ("during published service hours," "who need assistance") in a statement.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary covering the whole passage rather than one true detail promoted above the rest.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate reads "encouraged" as "required."
- Rewarding an association read as causation despite a declared confound in the passage.
- Accepting "consider whether X" read as "recommends X" or "will do X."
- Missing when a candidate selects a true-but-narrow detail as the main point rather than a summary covering the whole passage.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and specifically whether a candidate's instincts align with what the profession considers good practice at this level — the same scenario has different best answers for a trainee and for a head of profession. An associate service designer who redesigns a journey unilaterally is overstepping; one who never raises anything is under-performing.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Users come first, specifically the users who are struggling most — service design exists because whole journeys fail people even when every individual part looks fine. Act within remit and be transparent at its edges — the pattern is attempt, then tell. Address problems directly and early, with the person involved. And zoom out — the distinctively service-design signature of a strong answer, since asking what happens before and after a step is usually stronger than addressing only the step itself.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Putting users first, especially excluded users** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Explaining and defending a design position** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Working with others' perspectives** maps to **Designing together**.
- **Using evidence honestly** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Working within a cycle** maps to **Iterative design**.
- **Understanding where design sits** maps to **Leading design** and **Designing strategically**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — You do not understand the task**

A senior designer asks for "a service blueprint for the renewals journey by Thursday." The candidate knows roughly what a blueprint is but has never made one. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say yes, then search online and attempt it alone.
- B) Say yes, and add that this has not been done before and half an hour of guidance would be really useful before starting.
- C) Say it cannot be done because of no training.
- D) Say yes, and quietly ask another associate to help rather than telling the senior.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Asking for guidance at this level, given a role that works under supervision, is doing the job correctly. This tests combining willingness with a specific, small, easy-to-grant request — option C is least effective, declining a development opportunity and handing the work back with nothing offered; option D leaves the senior with an inaccurate picture of the candidate's skills.

**Question 2 (easy) — You notice something in a journey map**

A journey map used for months shows users receiving a decision letter but says nothing about what happens if the letter does not arrive. Ask for the most sensible response, from the most junior person on the team.

- A) Say nothing; the map has been used for months and someone would have noticed.
- B) Raise it at the next team session or with a senior designer, describing the gap and asking what does happen in that case.
- C) Add the missing step to the map yourself.
- D) Note it for the retrospective in three weeks.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A letter that does not arrive leaves a person with no decision and often no obvious way to ask — a genuine service failure risk. This tests raising it as a question rather than a correction, leaving room for the process existing but unmapped — option A is least effective, since "someone would have noticed" is how gaps survive for years, and being new is precisely why the candidate can see it.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

A senior designer asked for a channel comparison finished this morning; a delivery manager then asks for slides for tomorrow's show and tell instead, calling it urgent. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Do what the delivery manager asked; they are more senior.
- B) Do what the senior designer asked and tell the delivery manager you are busy.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask both to confirm the priority.
- D) Try to do both by working through lunch.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Surfacing the conflict promptly with a concrete option — "I could do the slides this morning and the comparison after lunch" — turns the candidate into part of the solution. This tests offering a specific workable alternative rather than bare escalation — option D is least effective, hiding the conflict, setting an unsustainable expectation, and meaning nobody learns the team is over-committing the candidate.

**Question 4 (easy) — A design decision you disagree with**

A team decides to remove a "save and come back later" feature to simplify a form; research shows several users completed the journey over multiple sittings because they had to find documents. Ask for the most effective response, from the most junior person in the room.

- A) Say nothing; the decision has been made.
- B) Raise the specific evidence — that several participants completed over multiple sittings because they needed to find documents — and ask how those users would manage without the feature.
- C) Raise it privately with a senior designer afterwards.
- D) Accept the decision but note your disagreement in the team channel.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Bringing evidence the room may not be weighing is the useful contribution, phrased as a question rather than a challenge. This tests raising relevant evidence at the moment a decision is being made — option A is least effective, since a decision made without evidence that was available in the room is a worse decision, and seniority does not make the evidence less relevant.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A user problem outside your service**

Research shows users of a service routinely fail at a step belonging to a different department's service, before ever reaching this one; the team says it is out of scope. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept it; the team cannot fix another department's service.
- B) Document the problem and its effect on users clearly, share it with a senior designer, and ask whether there is a route to raise it with the other department — while considering whether the current service can reduce the harm, for example by explaining the earlier step better.
- C) Contact the other department yourself.
- D) Add a note about it in the research findings and move on.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A user's journey does not respect organisational boundaries even though budgets do. This tests documenting and finding a legitimate route, plus asking what the candidate's own service can do meanwhile — option A is least effective, since "out of scope" describes a budget, not a user's experience.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pressure to simplify the story**

A journey map shows a service that appears to take twenty minutes actually taking about five weeks because of two waiting periods; a stakeholder asks for the waiting periods to be removed because "they are not part of the design." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Remove them; the stakeholder knows what the map is for.
- B) Explain that the waiting periods are the largest part of the user's experience and that removing them would make the map describe something users do not recognise — and offer to show them differently, for example greyed out, so the design steps remain clear.
- C) Refuse and send the map unchanged.
- D) Make two versions, one with and one without.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's underlying concern — legibility — is legitimate, and this tests addressing it while holding the substance: a map omitting five weeks of waiting is not a map of the user's experience, and waiting is exactly where users give up or phone the contact centre. Option A is least effective, since once waiting is invisible nobody designs for it.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Something you designed is not working**

A form section drafted by the candidate is live, and caseworkers report users consistently entering the wrong information. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Wait for the next research round to confirm the problem.
- B) Thank the caseworkers, ask for specific examples of what users are entering, look at whether the wording invites that misunderstanding, and take a proposed fix to a senior designer quickly.
- C) Explain to the caseworkers that the section was based on research.
- D) Ask for the section to be reverted to the previous version.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Caseworkers seeing the same wrong answer repeatedly is high-quality free evidence, and gathering the specifics usually reveals the misunderstanding immediately. This tests treating unwelcome evidence as a diagnostic opportunity — option C is least effective, defending a design against evidence it is not working, the fastest way to lose both the design and the caseworkers' willingness to raise anything next time.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Feedback that stings**

A senior designer says a journey map is "confusing and does not show the offline channel at all," though the candidate believes it does. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Explain why they are wrong and point to where the offline channel appears.
- B) Ask them to show you where they got lost, since if an experienced designer cannot find the offline channel, the map is not communicating it clearly enough — then fix that.
- C) Accept the criticism silently and redo the map.
- D) Ask for the feedback in writing to consider later.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Both things can be true — the channel is on the map, and the map does not communicate it. This tests converting feedback into information rather than winning the argument — option A is least effective, leaving the map exactly as confusing and teaching the room that feedback is effortful.

**Question 9 (harder) — An accessibility problem discovered late**

Two weeks before launch, a key journey step is found to be incompletable with a screen reader. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with a senior designer and delivery manager, describing precisely what cannot be done and by whom, and ask for a decision — while offering any interim option that would let affected users complete the journey another way.
- C) Try to fix it yourself in the two weeks available.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A step incompletable by screen reader means some people cannot use the service, and weighing a delay against exclusion is a decision for people with authority, though raising it precisely and now is entirely the candidate's to do. This tests looking for an interim route so the choice is not simply launch-or-delay — option A is least effective, sending a genuine blocker to a backlog where it will remain unfixed.

**Question 10 (harder) — Being asked to design around a constraint you doubt**

A policy colleague says a service must ask for three previous addresses "because the policy requires it," and research shows this causes many users to abandon the journey. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Design the question as well as possible and accept the requirement.
- B) Share the evidence about abandonment, ask specifically what the requirement is for and where it comes from, and explore with them whether the underlying need could be met differently — while being ready to accept the constraint if it is genuinely fixed.
- C) Challenge the policy requirement directly and ask for it to be removed.
- D) Escalate to your senior designer without speaking to the policy colleague.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** "The policy requires it" is sometimes exactly true, sometimes a requirement satisfiable differently, and sometimes an inherited assumption. This tests asking "what is this for?" rather than "must we?", since knowing the underlying need (identity verification, fraud prevention) opens alternatives — option C is least effective at this level, challenging a policy requirement head-on as a trainee without understanding its basis.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

A journey map produced by the candidate, used by the team for a fortnight to plan work, is found to show the wrong order for two steps. Ask what should happen.

- A) Correct it quietly and say nothing.
- B) Tell the team straight away, explain exactly which steps were wrong and what the correct order is, and flag any decisions made in the last fortnight that may have relied on the error.
- C) Correct it and mention it at the next retrospective.
- D) Wait to see whether anyone noticed.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A shared artefact has been wrong for a fortnight and people have planned against it, so correction alone is insufficient. This tests flagging downstream impact, the step most often skipped because it is uncomfortable — option D is least effective, since waiting means the error persists and, if discovered later, invites the question of why it was not raised sooner.

**Question 12 (harder) — Designing for people the team does not think about**

A team designing a service mostly for working-age adults hears someone say "we don't need to worry about people without internet access — this is a digital service," while research shows a significant minority currently apply by post. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go; the team has decided the scope.
- B) Bring the specific fact into the room — that a significant minority currently apply by post — and ask what the team thinks should happen to those users, so the decision is made knowingly rather than by assumption.
- C) Argue that all services must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately with your senior designer afterwards.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A specific fact dissolves an assumption more readily than a principle. This tests contributing a fact that keeps the decision honest, an effective form of challenge available even to the most junior person in the room — option A is least effective, letting a factually wrong assumption stand when the fact to correct it was available.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate attempts what can be competently handled and promptly tells the right person about what cannot, rather than struggling silently or escalating reflexively.
- Note whether the candidate zooms out — considering the step before and after, or the whole journey — rather than addressing only the artefact immediately in front of them.
- Watch for whether the candidate asks what a stated policy or technical constraint actually is before designing around it.
- Score for whether own errors are disclosed promptly, with downstream impact flagged.
- Note whether the candidate brings a specific fact or measured value rather than an assertion of principle when challenging an assumption.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that ranks by seniority of the requester rather than by consequence.
- Missing when a candidate stays silent about a defect out of newness ("someone would have noticed").
- Rewarding a design defended against evidence rather than examined in light of it.
- Accepting "out of scope" as a sufficient answer about a user's actual experience.
- Missing when a candidate delays disclosure of their own error rather than raising it and its downstream impact immediately.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and error checking across journey maps, service pages, and channel comparisons; the arithmetic that makes design judgements defensible — measuring drop-off against the correct denominator, converting a defect into a specific number of affected people, and adding up waiting time as well as active time when estimating a journey's real duration; the discipline of reading service standards, accessibility guidance and review documents at exactly the strength the text supports, especially distinguishing a rule with an escape route from an unconditional one; and the situational judgement that defines a strong associate service designer — raising problems early with a measured value attached, zooming out to the whole journey, and disclosing an own mistake promptly with its downstream impact.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answer is very often the one that asks for guidance rather than acting alone. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
