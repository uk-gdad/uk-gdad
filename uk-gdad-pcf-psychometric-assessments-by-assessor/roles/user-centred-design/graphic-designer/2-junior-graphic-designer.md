# Junior Graphic Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for junior graphic designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the thinking skills and workplace judgement that predict success in a role, rather than IQ tests dressed up in work clothes. For a junior graphic designer, that means the assessments in this guide use the real materials of the job — layout grids, spacing scales, colour palettes, type choices, icon sets, design patterns and components, analytics reports, and feedback from design crits — and the real decisions the role makes: explaining a design decision clearly, spotting a user issue, checking work against accessibility standards with support, and knowing when to ask a more senior designer for direction.

A junior graphic designer works independently after being given direction, and is trusted to identify user issues and important needs. That takes sharp eyes and clear thinking: checking that content is legible and readable, spotting when spacing breaks a grid, when a colour fails a contrast check, or when an icon does not match the pattern library, reading research findings and analytics with researchers and analysts, and explaining design decisions to teammates. Cognitive, numeric, verbal, and situational judgement assessments measure exactly these abilities.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and verbal items for whether the candidate concludes only what a stated rule or set of facts guarantees, and for whether visual and numeric judgements are backed by a measured value rather than an impression. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a response that explains a decision by the problem it solves rather than by preference, and that raises a problem — a contrast failure, an unclear brief, an own mistake — as early as possible rather than waiting.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking skills the role actually demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles: a spacing scale, a draft page layout, an icon set, a component library, a set of design principles, reasoned about quickly and accurately.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a similar level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

The core professional value of a graphic designer is visual and structural thinking — holding rules in mind (grids, spacing systems, type scales, colour palettes, naming conventions) and noticing instantly when something breaks them, and reasoning about what follows from a design decision: if body text grows, what happens to the line length; if a pattern changes, which pages are affected. At junior level the focus is the day-to-day checking, spotting, and reasoning tasks done with support from senior designers.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Iterative design**, especially using and iterating design patterns and components. Spotting when a button, card, or icon breaks the established pattern is one of the most valuable things a junior designer does when checking work across a service.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Design communication** and **Designing strategically**. Explaining design decisions clearly is presenting a chain of reasoning: the evidence, the principle, the decision.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**: ensuring a design meets appropriate standards, for example accessibility regulations, with support, means methodically comparing artefacts against rules.
- **Prioritisation** maps to identifying user issues and important needs, and working independently after being given direction — ranking competing issues by impact on users, not by which is easiest or loudest.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Evidence-based design** and working with researchers or analysts: problem-solving questions give tangled information and ask for the clear next step, exactly as research findings arrive in the job.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a spacing scale**

The team's design system uses a spacing scale based on multiples of 8 pixels: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, and so on. A draft page layout uses these spacing values between components: 16px, 24px, 30px, 40px, 8px. Ask which value breaks the spacing scale.

- A) 16px
- B) 30px
- C) 40px
- D) 8px

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 16, 24, 40 and 8 are all multiples of 8; 30 is not (8×3=24, 8×4=32). This tests checking every value against a stated rule methodically — a stray 30px gap looks tiny in isolation but inconsistent spacing makes pages feel subtly untidy and makes components harder to reuse. The reliable method is to derive the rule first, then test each item against it, rather than scanning the whole list hoping something jumps out.

**Question 2 (easy) — Pattern recognition in icon naming**

The icon library names files using the pattern "icon-{category}-{name}" in lower case, e.g. "icon-navigation-arrow". Five new icons: icon-navigation-chevron, icon-action-download, Icon-action-upload, icon-status-warning, icon-status-error. Ask which file name breaks the naming pattern.

- A) icon-action-download
- B) Icon-action-upload
- C) icon-status-warning
- D) icon-status-error

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern requires lower case throughout; "Icon-action-upload" begins with a capital I. This tests character-by-character checking rather than a quick skim — on some systems the capitalised and lower-case names are treated as different files, which can break links in prototypes and production code, so this kind of slip matters more than it looks.

**Question 3 (easy) — Logical deduction from a design rule**

Design guidance states: "All body text must use the standard type size unless the content designer approves an exception." A page under review has body text that does not use the standard type size. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The page breaks the design guidance.
- B) The content designer approved an exception.
- C) Either the content designer approved an exception, or the page breaks the guidance.
- D) The page is hard to read.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule allows exactly one exception; nothing given establishes whether it applies, so neither A nor B is guaranteed, and D adds information the facts never gave. This tests concluding only what the facts guarantee — a candidate who would check for an approved exception before flagging a page as non-compliant is showing the same evidence-first discipline good design communication depends on.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Error checking against accessibility rules**

Three rules: (i) body text at least 16px; (ii) text colour meets the minimum contrast standard against its background; (iii) links visually distinct from ordinary text in more than colour alone. A draft: body text 16px, passes contrast; caption text 14px, passes contrast, marked "supporting text, not body text"; links same size and colour as body text, underlined; button label 18px, fails contrast. Ask how many of the three rules the draft breaks.

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rule (i) is not broken — the 14px item is explicitly not body text; rule (ii) is broken by the button label; rule (iii) is satisfied because the underline distinguishes the link by more than colour. This tests error checking against the rule as stated rather than as expected — the caption tempts a second false failure, and the underlined link (same colour as body text) tempts an unwarranted flag. Over-reporting undermines trust in the issues a candidate does raise.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Prioritisation of design issues**

Four issues on a live page, in the order a senior designer asked them fixed: (1) an error message fails the colour contrast standard, so some users with low vision may not read it; (2) a footer icon is the older version, pre-dating the last pattern update; (3) a page heading uses a spacing value 4px off the spacing scale; (4) a decorative image is missing its rounded corners. Ask for the most defensible fix order.

- A) 3, 2, 4, 1 — quickest jobs first
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4 — user-blocking accessibility issue first, then pattern consistency, then visual polish
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4 — pattern consistency is the design system's top rule
- D) 4, 3, 2, 1 — visible cosmetic issues damage trust most

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Issue 1 can prevent some users from reading an error message at the exact moment they most need help, so it comes first; issue 2 is a genuine consistency problem that can confuse users, so it reasonably comes next; issues 3 and 4 are visual polish that block nobody. This tests ranking by user impact rather than convenience (A), rather than putting consistency above access (C, which inverts designing for everyone), and rather than confusing visibility with importance (D).

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction about component changes**

Four statements: (i) the card component will be updated next sprint; (ii) every page using the card component must be re-checked after the update; (iii) the service homepage uses the card component; (iv) the contact page does not use the card component. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The contact page will not need any re-checking next sprint.
- B) The service homepage must be re-checked after the update.
- C) The contact page will not change next sprint.
- D) The homepage re-check will find problems.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chaining (i), (ii) and (iii) guarantees the homepage must be re-checked. This tests distinguishing "the rule doesn't require X" from "X won't happen" — A and C both overreach on the contact page, and D predicts an outcome nothing supports. A strong candidate scopes their checking honestly: which pages a change definitely commits them to reviewing, without claiming the rest are untouched.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a design specification**

Type scale: heading 1 = 32px, heading 2 = 24px, heading 3 = 19px, body = 16px. A draft table: page title (h1) 32px; section heading (h2) 24px; sub-heading (h3) 16px; body paragraph 16px; caption (body) 19px. Ask how many rows contain an error against the type scale.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The sub-heading (should be 19px) and the caption (should be 16px) are both wrong — the values have swapped between the two rows. This tests catching a transposition error, which is easy to miss because both values individually look legal (each appears somewhere in the scale). The reliable method is checking each row against the scale definition, not against a memory of what looks right.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with analytics evidence**

Analytics on a form page: 1,000 users start each week; 900 complete page one; 450 complete page two; 430 complete page three and finish. Ask where design effort should focus first.

- A) Page one, because it loses 100 users — the first drop matters most.
- B) Page two, because it loses 450 of the 900 who reach it — half of them.
- C) Page three, because it is closest to the end, where drop-outs are most costly.
- D) Nowhere yet — 430 completions is a healthy number.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Page one loses one in ten; page two loses exactly half of those who reach it, by far the steepest drop; page three loses a small fraction. This tests working through a funnel by proportion rather than position (A confuses "first" with "biggest"; C uses journey position rather than evidence) and comparing a completion count against the number who started rather than judging it in isolation (D).

**Question 9 (moderate) — Prioritisation with direction from a senior designer**

Direction given Monday: "This week your priority is the icon refresh for the renewals service. Fit other requests around it." By Wednesday: (1) the icon refresh, about two days remaining; (2) a content designer's request for a quick banner graphic "by Friday if possible"; (3) an invitation to Thursday's design crit, where the icon work could get useful feedback; (4) an email from another team requesting a team poster, no deadline. Ask for the most sensible plan.

- A) Do the poster first as a warm-up, then the icons.
- B) Continue the icon refresh, attend the crit to test the icons, fit the banner in around them, and politely defer the poster.
- C) Pause the icons to finish the banner and poster, then return to icons next week.
- D) Skip the crit to maximise heads-down icon time, and decline the banner.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The crit directly improves the priority work rather than distracting from it; the banner is small with a soft deadline; the poster has no deadline and sits outside the stated priority, so deferring it with a stated reason is the professional move. This tests honouring stated direction while using judgement on the margins — A ignores the direction entirely, C pauses the stated priority for two unprioritised requests, and D protects icon time but wastes the best feedback opportunity of the week and abruptly declines a reasonable small request.

**Question 10 (harder) — Multi-step deduction about page layout**

Rules: (i) if a page has a sidebar, the main content column must be narrow; (ii) if the main content column is narrow, tables wider than four columns must become stacked lists; (iii) a guidance page must include a six-column data table displayed as a table, a fixed stakeholder requirement. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The guidance page must not have a sidebar.
- B) The stakeholder requirement must be dropped.
- C) The six-column table must become a stacked list.
- D) The main content column must be narrow.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Working backwards from the fixed requirement: the table must stay a table, so the column cannot be narrow (else rule ii forces a stacked list), so the page cannot have a sidebar (else rule i forces a narrow column). This tests reasoning backwards from a constraint that cannot move, then eliminating whatever conflicts with it — B wrongly assumes a contradiction when removing the sidebar resolves everything, and C and D each directly contradict the chain.

**Question 11 (harder) — Pattern recognition across a component audit**

Primary button style across five pages, recorded as (colour, corner style, label case): page 1 (green, rounded, sentence case); page 2 (green, rounded, sentence case); page 3 (green, square, sentence case); page 4 (green, rounded, Title Case); page 5 (blue, rounded, sentence case). The standard is the majority style per attribute. Ask how many pages fully match the standard.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The majority standard is (green, rounded, sentence case); pages 1 and 2 match on all three attributes, while pages 3, 4 and 5 each fail on a different single attribute. This tests deriving the rule attribute by attribute first, then testing each item against the whole rule rather than one attribute — a common structure in real audits, since different pages are usually built by different people at different times.

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving under conflicting evidence**

Three pieces of evidence on a start page, with direction to improve the start rate: (i) analytics show 40% of users click a small help link instead of the large start button; (ii) a usability test with five participants found three said the start button "looked like an advert" and hesitated; (iii) a stakeholder emails that the start button "looks great, don't change it." Ask for the strongest next step.

- A) Change nothing — the stakeholder is happy, and five test participants is too few to matter.
- B) Redesign the start button so it looks less like an advert, then measure whether help-link clicks fall and the start rate rises.
- C) Remove the help link so users have no choice but the start button.
- D) Ask for a larger usability test before touching the design, since evidence is conflicting.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The analytics show what is happening and the usability test suggests why (banner blindness) — complementary quantitative and qualitative evidence, not conflicting evidence, with the stakeholder's view a preference rather than user evidence. This tests forming a hypothesis from converging evidence, changing the design, and testing the result — the iterative design method — rather than weighing one opinion above two evidence sources (A), punishing users instead of fixing the design (C), or delaying action the existing evidence already supports (D).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate derives a rule or standard first and tests every item against it, rather than skimming for errors that jump out.
- Note whether the candidate concludes only what stated facts guarantee, resisting an answer that quietly overreaches.
- Watch for whether the candidate ranks issues by user impact rather than by ease or visibility.
- Score for careful row-by-row and rule-by-rule checking on specification and audit items, since transposition and single-attribute errors are designed to reward exactly this.
- Note whether the candidate treats converging quantitative and qualitative evidence as complementary rather than as conflicting.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a design opinion offered in place of a stated rule's actual requirement.
- Missing when a candidate concludes more than the facts allow — "the rule doesn't require it" read as "it won't happen".
- Rewarding prioritisation by ease rather than user impact.
- Accepting an answer that counts errors against memory rather than against the stated rule.
- Missing when a candidate treats two complementary evidence sources as contradictory.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately a candidate works with the numbers this role produces. Not a maths exam: no algebra, no trigonometry. Instead, tables, charts, and short data descriptions from realistic work situations, needing percentages, ratios, averages, simple rates, and careful chart reading.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions, each showing a small data set with four or five answer options, a basic on-screen calculator usually provided. Scoring compares against a norm group, some platforms adapt difficulty, and speed and accuracy are usually both reported. Early questions are typically single-step; later ones chain two or three steps.

Modern graphic design work is quietly full of numbers: colour contrast is a ratio with numeric accessibility thresholds; type and spacing live on numeric scales; layouts are built on grids with column widths and gutters measured in pixels; analytics arrive as percentages and counts; usability findings arrive as "3 of 5 participants". At junior level, the bar is reading a table without misreading a row, taking a percentage without slipping a decimal, comparing two ratios fairly, and noticing when a number cannot be right.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and rates** map to **Evidence-based design**: analysing and clearly explaining web analytics data means handling completion rates, drop-off percentages, and week-on-week changes without error.
- **Ratios** map to **Designing for everyone**: colour contrast is a ratio (such as 4.5:1), and ensuring a design meets appropriate standards with support means comparing measured ratios against required thresholds correctly.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Design communication** and **Evidence-based design**: extracting the right numbers from the right rows before explaining evidence to others.
- **Averages and simple summaries** map to working with researchers or analysts: usability timings, satisfaction scores, and test results usually arrive as averages, and knowing what an average does and does not tell you matters.
- **Grid and dimension arithmetic** maps to **Iterative design**: prototyping and building layouts on grids involves quick, reliable arithmetic with column widths, gutters, margins, and image dimensions.
- **Proportional scaling** maps to the craft core of the role: resizing images and graphic elements without distortion is ratio arithmetic, done many times a day.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading an analytics table**

Weekly visits: Start page 5,200 this week / 5,000 last week; Help page 1,300 / 1,300; Contact page 800 / 1,000. Ask which page had fewer visits this week than last week.

- A) Start page
- B) Help page
- C) Contact page
- D) None of them

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Start page rose by 200, Help page was unchanged, Contact page fell by 200. This tests careful row-by-row reading — the commonest error on easy table questions is answering from the wrong row or column under time pressure, so a strong candidate reads and states each comparison deliberately rather than scanning.

**Question 2 (easy) — A simple percentage from analytics**

The start page received 5,200 visits this week; 1,300 visitors clicked the green "Start now" button. Ask what percentage of visitors clicked the button.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 1,300÷5,200=0.25=25%. This tests basic percentage calculation, sense-checkable since 1,300 is exactly a quarter of 5,200 (1,300×4=5,200) — sense-checking catches decimal slips that would produce an answer like 2.5% or 250%.

**Question 3 (easy) — Percentage change after an iteration**

Before a design iteration, 400 users a week completed a form; afterwards, 500 did. Ask for the percentage increase.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 80%
- D) 100%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (500−400)÷400=25%. This tests dividing by the original value rather than the new one — option A divides by 500, the classic trap, which under-reports every improvement reported to a team.

**Question 4 (easy) — Scaling an image proportionally**

A photo is 1,200px wide by 800px tall, scaled down to 600px wide, keeping proportions. Ask how tall it will be.

- A) 200 pixels
- B) 300 pixels
- C) 400 pixels
- D) 500 pixels

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Scale factor 600÷1,200=0.5; 800×0.5=400px. This tests applying a single scale factor to both dimensions rather than subtracting or scaling only one dimension (the trap in option A, 800−600=200) — getting this wrong produces stretched or squashed images.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Colour contrast ratios**

Requirement: normal text at least 4.5:1, large text at least 3:1. Measured: body paragraph (normal) 4.6:1; caption (normal) 4.2:1; page heading (large) 3.2:1; hero banner text (large) 2.9:1. Ask how many elements fail.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Caption fails against 4.5:1, hero banner fails against 3:1; body paragraph and page heading pass. This tests matching each measurement to the threshold for its own text type — a common slip is testing large text against 4.5:1, which would wrongly fail the heading too.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Grid arithmetic**

A 960px page uses a 12-column grid with 11 gutters of 20px each and no outer margins. Ask how wide each column is.

- A) 55 pixels
- B) 60 pixels
- C) 61.7 pixels
- D) 80 pixels

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total gutter width 11×20=220; remaining width 960−220=740; per column 740÷12≈61.7. This tests the n−1 gutter rule — option D (960÷12=80) forgets gutters entirely, and option B wrongly assumes 12 gutters instead of 11, since columns side by side always have one fewer gap than the number of columns.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Averages from a usability test**

Five participants' task times: 40, 50, 45, 55, 210 seconds. Ask for the mean, and whether it might be misleading.

- A) 80 seconds; misleading because one very slow time pulls the mean far above the typical time
- B) 80 seconds; not misleading, because the mean always represents the typical user
- C) 50 seconds; misleading because five participants is too few to calculate a mean
- D) 210 seconds; the slowest user defines the experience

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** (40+50+45+55+210)÷5=80, but four of the five times sit between 40 and 55 seconds, with one outlier nearly four times longer. This tests recognising when a single outlier skews a mean above what most people actually experienced — the median (50) describes the typical experience better, and a strong candidate would flag the outlier for separate investigation rather than let it define the summary.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratios in a colour palette**

Guidance: primary, secondary, and accent colours should be used in the ratio 6:3:1 by area. A draft of 900 total colour-area units uses 500 primary, 300 secondary, 100 accent. Ask which colour is furthest from its target.

- A) Primary
- B) Secondary
- C) Accent
- D) All three match the guidance

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 10 parts of 900 = 90 per part; targets are 540/270/90; actuals are 500/300/100, gaps of 40/30/10 — primary's gap is largest. This tests the standard ratio method (total the parts, find the unit value, scale up, compare) and reading the question stem precisely — it asks for the furthest from target, not the one over target.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reading a bar chart honestly**

A chart shows this month's completion bar (82%) towering over last month's (78%) because the vertical axis starts at 75% rather than 0%. A stakeholder says completion "more than doubled." Ask for the accurate description.

- A) Completion rose by 4 percentage points, from 78% to 82%.
- B) Completion doubled, as the bars show.
- C) Completion rose by 50%, matching the bar heights.
- D) The data cannot be interpreted from a truncated chart.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 82−78=4 percentage points (about a 5% relative change); the truncated axis exaggerates the visible bar heights, misleading the reader, but the labelled values remain readable — so D overstates the problem. This tests reading the labelled values rather than the bar heights, and matters doubly for this role: as a reader of evidence and as someone who may design charts, where axis choices are a design decision with honesty implications.

**Question 10 (harder) — Two-step funnel percentages**

2,000 users start a two-step journey; 70% complete step one; of those, 60% complete step two. Ask how many complete the whole journey, and what overall percentage that is of starters.

- A) 840 users; 42%
- B) 1,200 users; 60%
- C) 1,400 users; 70%
- D) 600 users; 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 2,000×0.70=1,400; 1,400×0.60=840; 840÷2,000=42% (or directly 0.70×0.60=0.42). This tests chaining two percentages correctly rather than stopping after one step (C), applying the second rate to the original base instead of the reduced one (B), or adding the rates.

**Question 11 (harder) — Comparing proportions fairly**

Version A: 320 of 400 users completed a task. Version B: 405 of 540 completed. Ask which performed better on completion rate.

- A) Version B, because 405 completions is more than 320.
- B) Version A, because its completion rate is 80% versus 75% for B.
- C) They performed equally.
- D) Version B, because it was tested on more users, making it more reliable.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 320÷400=80%; 405÷540=75%. This tests converting to rates before comparing groups of different sizes — option A compares raw counts across unequal groups, and option D confuses sample stability with which observed rate is actually higher.

**Question 12 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

An accessibility audit found 20% of pages had a contrast failure; after fixes, 15% do. Two draft summary sentences: (i) "Failures fell by 5%"; (ii) "Failures fell by a quarter." Ask which assessment of the sentences is correct.

- A) Only (i) is accurate.
- B) Only (ii) is accurate.
- C) Both are accurate if "%" in (i) means percentage points; (ii) is the relative change.
- D) Neither is accurate.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The absolute change is 20−15=5 percentage points; the relative change is 5÷20=25%. This tests distinguishing percentage points from a relative percentage change — the two figures tell very different stories about the same underlying fix, and precise wording ("failures fell from 20% of pages to 15%, a drop of one quarter") is the numeric twin of legibility: both exist so a reader cannot misunderstand.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads a numeric threshold at its precise boundary rather than rounding toward a convenient answer.
- Note whether the candidate completes both stages of a two-stage calculation rather than stopping at the first.
- Watch for whether the candidate converts raw counts to rates before comparing groups of different sizes.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change.
- Note whether a mean skewed by an outlier is recognised and flagged rather than reported as if representative.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage change divided by the wrong base.
- Missing when a candidate compares raw counts across groups of unequal size.
- Rewarding grid arithmetic that forgets gutters or miscounts the number of gaps.
- Accepting "percentage" and "percentage points" used interchangeably without distinguishing them.
- Missing when a candidate trusts a truncated chart's bar heights over its labelled values.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment checks whether a candidate can work out exactly what a piece of writing establishes — not what it implies, and not what the candidate already knows to be true.

For a junior graphic designer the material is the writing that surrounds design work: accessibility standards, a design system's guidance, brand and style rules, a brief from a service manager, and research findings from a user researcher.

The usual format gives a passage and statements to mark True, False, or Cannot Say — True where the passage establishes it, False where the passage contradicts it, and Cannot Say where the passage settles it neither way. Cannot Say is a confident, correct answer rather than an admission of defeat, and a well-built test includes several.

Two things matter for scoring. First, design standards are written with precise conditions — a contrast requirement applies at a stated ratio, to a stated kind of content, with stated exceptions, and reading past a condition changes what the rule requires. Second, a candidate's own design knowledge is the main hazard here: a candidate may know a 3:1 ratio is too low for body text, but if the passage does not say so, the answer comes from the passage alone.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading accessibility standards precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**, ensuring a design meets appropriate standards with support.
- **Reading design system guidance** maps to working independently after direction from more senior designers.
- **Reading briefs** maps to identifying user issues and important needs.
- **Reading research findings** maps to understanding what research does and does not establish.
- **Explaining accurately** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Reading collaboratively-produced documents** maps to working collaboratively as part of a service.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Text and images of text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against their background. Large-scale text must have a ratio of at least 3:1, where large-scale means at least 24 pixels, or at least 18.66 pixels if bold. This requirement does not apply to text that is purely decorative, text that is part of an inactive interface component, or text within a photograph containing significant other visual content. Logotypes are exempt. Interface components and meaningful graphics must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent colours."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: Body text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly, and body text is neither large-scale nor covered by any listed exemption. This is the baseline reading that the following, more complicated items build on.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: Text at 20 pixels bold counts as large-scale text.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Large-scale is defined as at least 24px, "or at least 18.66px if bold"; 20 clears the second threshold. This tests reading both halves of a two-part definition — checking only 20 against 24 and stopping there is a common error that produces the wrong answer here.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: The border of a text input must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage sets 3:1 for "interface components and meaningful graphics," and a border is a component, not text. This tests catching when a candidate applies the text threshold universally, which would wrongly flag a compliant border built to 3:1 or 4.5:1 alike as failing an unstated requirement.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Statement: The label on a greyed-out button must meet the 4.5:1 ratio.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage exempts "text that is part of an inactive interface component," and a greyed-out button is inactive. This tests setting aside professional instinct — low-contrast disabled states are a genuine usability concern many teams choose to exceed regardless — in favour of what the passage actually requires; both can be true at once, and the item asks only about the stated requirement.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The design system provides three button styles: primary, secondary, and warning. Guidance states that a page should contain no more than one primary button, which should be used for the main action the user is expected to take. Secondary buttons may be used for alternative actions. The warning style should be reserved for actions that are difficult or impossible to reverse, such as deleting a record. Teams departing from the design system should record the reason. The design system is updated quarterly."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: The design system provides three button styles.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. A straightforward baseline, worth reading carefully anyway since the following items turn on the guidance attached to each style.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: A page must never contain two primary buttons.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says a page "should" contain no more than one primary button — a strong recommendation a team may depart from with a recorded reason, which the passage explicitly provides for — not an absolute "must never." This tests the should-versus-must distinction, the single most reliable source of lost marks in this format.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: A "cancel subscription" action should use the warning style.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The warning style is for actions "difficult or impossible to reverse," and whether cancelling this particular subscription is difficult to reverse is not stated — it could go either way depending on the service. This tests resisting an answer that matches the general feel of an action rather than the stated criterion.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: A team that departs from the design system is required to record the reason.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says teams departing "should record the reason," not "must" — and the passage has already shown it distinguishes "may," "should" and (implicitly) "must." This tests reading modal verbs precisely, particularly under a statement worded more strongly ("required") than the source text.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Research with 24 participants tested two versions of a service start page. Version A used an illustration above the start button; version B used no illustration. Participants using version B located the start button more quickly, with a median time of 3.2 seconds against 5.1 seconds for version A. Three participants using version A did not find the start button within the time allowed. Participants were recruited from existing users of the service. The research team noted that no participants used screen magnification."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: Participants using version B found the start button faster than those using version A.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly with figures: median 3.2 seconds against 5.1. This tests the baseline reading; note the passage reports medians rather than means, the appropriate statistic for timing data.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: The illustration caused participants to take longer to find the start button.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes an association between version and timing, not a cause — the two versions could differ in other ways the passage does not mention. This tests resisting a well-evidenced and very plausible inference in favour of what the passage actually establishes — the discipline the format is specifically testing.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: The findings apply to people who have never used the service before.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Participants were "recruited from existing users of the service," so the findings speak to existing users; whether the same pattern holds for first-time users is not established. This tests checking who was in a study before generalising a finding beyond them.

**Question 12 (harder)**

Statement: The illustration causes problems for people using screen magnification.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage notes no participants used screen magnification — a declared gap in coverage, not evidence of no problem or a problem. This tests treating silence about a group as a reason to find out, never as grounds to conclude either way.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads both halves of a two-part definition (e.g. 24px or 18.66px if bold) rather than stopping at the first.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes "must," "should," and "may" precisely — this decides more items than any other single skill.
- Watch for whether the candidate checks whether a threshold applies to text or to a component before applying it.
- Score for whether the candidate treats a stated association as an association, not a cause, even where the inference is plausible.
- Note whether the candidate checks who was in a piece of research before generalising its finding, and treats a declared evidence gap as a gap rather than an answer.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting the text contrast ratio applied to a button, border, or icon component.
- Missing when a candidate reads "should" as "must."
- Rewarding an answer drawn from the candidate's own accessibility knowledge rather than from the passage.
- Accepting a well-evidenced cause marked True when the passage establishes only an association.
- Missing when a candidate generalises a finding past the participants who produced it, or reads "not tested" as "no problem."

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment presents realistic situations from the working life of a junior graphic designer and asks what the candidate would do. The situations are ordinary; the question is which response an experienced designer would choose.

For a junior graphic designer the situations involve working to direction from more senior designers, explaining decisions, working with people from other disciplines, raising accessibility concerns, and receiving critique.

Items typically ask for the most effective and least effective response, or a rating of each. Scoring is against a key built from experienced practitioners — professional consensus, not personal taste.

Three principles carry most items. Design decisions need reasons, not preferences: the difference between "it looked better" and "I increased the spacing because the two groups were being read as one list" is the difference between a decision a team can discuss and one they can only agree or disagree with. Accessibility is a fact, not a matter of taste: a design that fails a standard should be flagged early — the candidate is not expected to own accessibility at this level, only to notice and raise it. Asking is expected: a junior designer works under direction, so a clarifying question is the arrangement working properly, not a sign of difficulty.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Explaining design decisions** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Working to direction** maps to working independently after being given direction by more senior designers.
- **Raising accessibility issues** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Working across disciplines** maps to working collaboratively and having responsibility as part of a service.
- **Spotting user problems** maps to identifying user issues and important needs.
- **Receiving critique** maps to developing practice within a team.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Explaining a choice**

In a design review, a candidate is asked why a particular type size was chosen. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say it looked right at that size.
- B) Explain what the size is doing — the reading distance and device designed for, the hierarchy it establishes against the surrounding text, and the minimum being worked to.
- C) Say it matches the design system.
- D) Offer to change it if the reviewer prefers something else.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A reason connected to the problem it solves can be discussed and improved, while a preference can only be agreed or disagreed with. This tests explaining design decisions properly — option D is least effective, treating a question as a complaint and teaching the reviewer that the candidate's decisions are not considered; option C is a partial answer that names a source without a reason.

**Question 2 (easy) — A brief you do not fully understand**

A senior designer gives direction for a piece of work, and part of it is unclear. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Ask them to clarify the part that is unclear before starting.
- B) Make a start and ask if stuck.
- C) Interpret it the way that seems most sensible and show the result.
- D) Ask a colleague what they think it means.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A two-minute question at the start is the cheapest it will ever be, and working to direction means clarifying it is the process working properly. This tests asking rather than guessing — option C risks a full piece of work built on a wrong guess, putting the senior designer in the position of rejecting finished work; option D asks someone who is also guessing.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Spotting a contrast problem**

A candidate notices a colour combination in a colleague's design fails contrast requirements. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Mention it with the measured ratio and the required ratio, and suggest a nearby colour that passes.
- B) Mention that the contrast looks low.
- C) Leave it; it is the colleague's design.
- D) Raise it in the next design review.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Bringing the measured value removes the question of taste entirely, and a working alternative makes it collaborative rather than critical. This tests raising a specific, measured observation promptly — option C is least effective, since a failure that reaches build costs far more to fix, and noticing without saying helps nobody; option D delays something that takes a minute now.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Critique on your work**

In a critique session, several people suggest substantial changes to work a candidate has spent days on. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Listen, ask questions to understand each point, and take notes without defending each choice in the moment.
- B) Explain the reasoning behind each decision as it comes up.
- C) Agree to make all the changes.
- D) Ask whether the critique is really necessary at this stage.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Gathering everything first and deciding afterwards keeps a review a review rather than a negotiation, and asking questions is how a strong point is told from a passing preference. This tests receiving critique without defensiveness — option D is least effective, reading as defensiveness and reliably reducing what people offer next time; option B has a place for genuine misunderstandings but costs the session as a general posture.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A stakeholder's colour request**

A service manager asks for a colour from their team's branding, which fails contrast against the planned background. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Use the colour; the service manager has asked.
- B) Explain that the combination would fail the accessibility requirement, show the measurement, and offer options — a darker version of the same colour, a different background, or the colour used somewhere it is not carrying meaning.
- C) Explain that the colour cannot be used.
- D) Use it and flag it for accessibility testing later.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Offering three routes keeps what the stakeholder actually wants — the brand present — while meeting the requirement. This tests substituting rather than refusing outright — option D is least effective, knowingly building something non-compliant and hoping testing catches it, which is how accessibility problems reach users; option C reaches the right answer but invites reading accessibility as an obstacle rather than a constraint to design within.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Research that contradicts your design**

A user researcher reports that people are not noticing an element the candidate designed. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Ask the researcher what people were trying to do and where their attention was.
- B) Redesign the element to be more prominent.
- C) Ask how many participants there were.
- D) Ask to see the recordings.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** "People did not notice it" is a fact about attention, not a diagnosis; understanding what people were doing and where their attention was determines whether the fix is the element, its position, or the surrounding content. This tests engaging with an unwelcome finding rather than deflecting it — option C is least effective, reading as defensiveness and the fastest way to be left out of future research; option B jumps to a solution before the problem is understood.

**Question 7 (harder) — A mistake in something already published**

A candidate realises a graphic they produced, now live, has an error, and nobody has mentioned it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell the team now, say what the error is, and offer a corrected version.
- B) Correct it quietly and replace the file.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone notices.
- D) Mention it at the next team meeting.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Prompt disclosure lets someone else assess whether the error matters more than it appears — it might appear in printed material or a partner's service the candidate is unaware of. This tests disclosing an own error promptly and with its scope — option C is least effective, since a delayed discovery by someone else, possibly a user, looks worse than the error itself; option B removes anyone's chance to check the wider impact.

**Question 8 (harder) — Direction you disagree with**

A senior designer directs a layout the candidate believes will be harder to read. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Ask about it — describe the perceived reading problem and ask what might be missing.
- B) Do it as directed; the senior designer is more experienced.
- C) Produce both versions and let them choose.
- D) Do it a different way and explain afterwards.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The "what am I missing?" framing is easy to ask and easy to answer, and quite often reveals a constraint the candidate did not know about; equally, senior designers get things wrong and would rather be asked. This tests raising a disagreement rather than silently complying or silently overriding — option D is least effective, delivering something other than what was asked for without saying so, wasting the review and damaging trust for no benefit a question would not have got; option B may be right but skips a chance to learn why.

**Question 9 (harder) — Working with a content designer**

A content designer asks for a heading much longer than the layout was designed around. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Ask what the heading needs to do and work out together whether the layout or the wording should give.
- B) Redesign the layout to fit it.
- C) Ask them to shorten it.
- D) Let it wrap and accept the result.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A content designer's heading length usually rests on their own evidence, so treating it as a shared problem tends to produce the best result — often a slightly shorter heading and a slightly more flexible layout. This tests collaborating across disciplines rather than assuming one side absorbs the whole problem — option D is least effective, letting an unplanned wrap decide the outcome by not deciding; B and C each unilaterally assume the other side should give way.

**Question 10 (harder) — Being asked for an estimate**

A candidate is asked how long a set of graphics will take, having not done this type of work before. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Give a range, say what is unsure, and offer a firmer figure after looking at one example properly.
- B) Give a best single guess.
- C) Say it cannot be estimated.
- D) Give a generous figure to be safe.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A range with stated uncertainty gives the asker a real sense of scale and a cheap route to a firmer answer. This tests honest, useful estimation — option D is least effective, since padding looks harmless but quietly distorts everyone else's planning, and consistently finishing early teaches people to discount future estimates; option C leaves the asker with nothing to plan around.

**Question 11 (harder) — An inconsistency across a service**

A candidate notices two parts of a service use different icon styles; neither part is their own work. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Raise it with the team, showing the two examples and asking which is intended.
- B) Change one to match the other.
- C) Leave it; neither is the candidate's work.
- D) Note it for a future consistency review.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Inconsistency across a service is something users genuinely notice, making a service feel unreliable even when nothing is broken, and asking which is intended allows for the possibility that one is a deliberate exception. This tests identifying and raising a user issue outside one's own work — option B is least effective, changing another team's work without asking, which may undo a deliberate decision and is discourteous; option D is slower than a two-minute conversation.

**Question 12 (harder) — Too much work**

A candidate has been given more work than can plausibly be finished this week. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say so now, explain which pieces are at risk, and ask which matter most.
- B) Do their best and see how far they get.
- C) Work extra hours to finish it.
- D) Say something at the end of the week if it has not been finished.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Raising it while there is still time to act gives whoever is planning a chance to reprioritise, and asking which matters most means any slippage lands on the piece the team would have chosen. This tests transparency about capacity early enough to matter — option D is least effective, since information arriving after the week cannot change anything; option C hides a planning problem behind unsustainable extra hours.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate explains a design decision by the problem it solves rather than by preference.
- Note whether the candidate brings a measured value when raising an accessibility or contrast issue, rather than an impression.
- Watch for whether the candidate asks clarifying questions before acting on an unclear brief, rather than guessing and building.
- Score for whether the candidate discloses an own error promptly, and states its likely scope.
- Note whether the candidate raises a capacity or priority conflict early enough for someone to act on it, rather than late.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a design decision justified by preference rather than by the problem it solves.
- Missing when a candidate offers to change a design as soon as it is questioned, rather than explaining the reasoning first.
- Rewarding a full piece of work built on an unclarified guess about the brief.
- Accepting a response that knowingly builds a failing design and defers the fix to later testing.
- Missing when a candidate changes another team's work without asking, or discloses their own error late rather than promptly.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and error checking against spacing systems, type scales, contrast standards and component audits; the arithmetic that makes design judgements defensible — reading thresholds at their exact boundary, chaining percentages correctly, comparing rates rather than raw counts, and recognising when a mean hides an outlier; the discipline of reading accessibility standards, design system guidance and research findings at exactly the strength the text supports, especially the difference between "should" and "must"; and the situational judgement that defines a strong junior graphic designer — explaining decisions by the problem they solve, and raising problems early rather than waiting.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
