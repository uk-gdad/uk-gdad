# Content Strategist - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for content strategist. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A content strategist is an expert practitioner who takes an overview of content and how it is produced: shaping the architecture of content around user needs, designing governance that delivers strategy and policy intent, negotiating with senior stakeholders to unblock progress, and directing and critiquing the work of content teams. Professional value rests on structured thinking at scale — seeing patterns across hundreds of pages, spotting where a workflow breaks down, reasoning about what a governance rule implies in an edge case, and weighing competing stakeholder demands against user needs and strategic intent.

Because this is a senior, expert-practitioner level, the assessment reflects that seniority — governance design, strategy evaluation, cross-team interdependencies, coaching junior colleagues, and influencing directors, not day-to-day editing tasks. The situational judgement material assumes managing relationships and interdependencies with other content roles, working with lead content designers to organise teams, and delivering through others.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate deduces only what given rules or evidence guarantee, interrogates a quantitative claim rather than accepting it at face value, and treats governance rules mechanically even when they are imperfect — the critique of an imperfect rule belongs in a different item, not in overriding it with experience. Situational judgement items consistently reward a response that treats adoption as a design problem rather than a compliance problem, computes whether a governance benefit exceeds its distributed cost, and subjects the candidate's own structures and processes to the same scrutiny applied to anyone else's.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles — taxonomies, governance frameworks, publishing workflows, content audit spreadsheets, journey maps, and strategy documents.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

For a content strategist, cognitive ability is particularly relevant because core professional value is structured thinking about content at scale — holding many moving parts in mind at once: which taxonomy terms map to which user needs, which workflow stages depend on which approvals, where a governance rule contradicts established practice, and what follows logically when one element of a content model changes.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **User-centred content design**: recognising recurring structures — duplicated pages, inconsistent taxonomy terms, repeated journey breakpoints — across large and messy content estates.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic thinking**: reasoning validly from governance rules, style standards, and policy intent to their consequences in specific cases.
- **Error checking** maps to **User-centred content design** and designing governance, workflows and taxonomies: spotting inconsistencies between artefacts.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile working**: rapidly ranking what matters most against user impact, deadlines, and strategic intent.
- **Problem solving** maps to **User focus** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: decomposing tangled cross-team content problems and recommending next steps.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a taxonomy**

A taxonomy naming pattern: parent topics are plural nouns; child topics append ": specific service area"; grandchild topics append a further colon and task. A draft: (1) Visas; (2) Visas: Work visas; (3) Visas: Work visas: Extend your visa; (4) Work visas: Visas: Skilled worker route; (5) Visas: Study visas. Ask which entry breaks the naming pattern.

- A) Entry 2
- B) Entry 3
- C) Entry 4
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Entry 4 inverts the hierarchy — "Work visas" is a child of "Visas," so a grandchild entry must read "Visas: Work visas: Skilled worker route." This tests spotting a broken naming convention that destroys findability and machine-readability once other teams tag content against the broken terms — a consistency check exactly like assuring the quality of a more junior colleague's work.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from governance rules**

Governance rule: "Every page in the benefits journey must be reviewed within 12 months of its last review date unless it has been formally archived." A page is in the benefits journey and has not been reviewed for 14 months. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The team has breached the governance framework.
- B) Page X has been formally archived.
- C) Either page X has been formally archived, or the framework has been breached.
- D) Page X contains out-of-date information.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is conditional with a stated exception, and the given facts do not establish which branch applies. This tests deducing only what the information guarantees — option D introduces an unsupported claim about content accuracy, since an unreviewed page is not automatically an inaccurate one.

**Question 3 (easy) — Spotting the broken workflow step**

Publishing workflow: (1) draft; (2) peer review; (3) subject matter expert factcheck; (4) 2i quality review; (5) publish, with the added rule: "Any change made after step 3 must be re-factchecked before publishing." A process log shows the step 4 reviewer rewrote a paragraph about eligibility criteria, and the page was published. Ask what has gone wrong.

- A) Nothing — the 2i reviewer is senior, so their changes need no further check.
- B) The page skipped peer review.
- C) The page needed to return to step 3 for re-factcheck after the step 4 rewrite, and did not.
- D) The subject matter expert should have done the 2i review.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rewrite occurred after step 3, requiring re-factcheck before publishing — seniority is irrelevant, since the rule contains no exemption. This tests applying a rule mechanically to a process log, and, in a strong candidate's answer, recognising eligibility criteria as exactly the content where an unfactchecked rewrite creates real user harm through a plausible-sounding but silently wrong paraphrase.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Error checking a content audit**

Audit rows: UN1 (Check eligibility) → Eligibility checker page, Team Red; UN2 (Apply online) → Application start page, Team Blue; UN3 (Track application) → Status tracker page, Team Green; UN1 (Check eligibility) → Eligibility guidance page, Team Blue. Strategy rules: (i) each user need is met by exactly one piece of content; (ii) Team Red owns all eligibility content. Ask how many rows are inconsistent with the strategy.

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Row 4 breaches both rules at once — it duplicates UN1's content and assigns eligibility content to the wrong team — while rows 1–3 breach neither rule on their own; removing row 4 restores full consistency. This tests locating the minimal correction rather than flagging every downstream symptom as a separate defect, mirroring real audit review.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a governance backlog**

Monday morning, four items: (1) a design authority meeting Wednesday needing an assessment of a new content type, blocking two teams; (2) a mentee's first content model needing feedback by Friday; (3) a director's undated email asking for "a quick view" of how a restructure would affect content ownership; (4) a quarterly taxonomy health review due in two weeks. Ask for the most defensible sequencing.

- A) 3, 1, 2, 4 — directors outrank all other stakeholders
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — the blocking assessment first, then the strategic request, then coaching, then routine review
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — strict deadline order
- D) 2, 1, 3, 4 — developing others always comes first

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is both urgent and high impact (blocking two teams); item 3 has no stated deadline but is strategically significant, sponsored by a director, and exactly what this role exists to answer, with a brief scoping reply managing expectations early; item 2 matters but has a Friday deadline; item 4 has slack. This tests sequencing by impact-weighted urgency rather than a single dimension — option C wrongly treats the undated director request as lowest priority purely for lacking a date, confusing "no deadline" with "no urgency."

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across journey data**

Eligibility-stage abandonment across four comparable service areas: Area A 12%, Area B 14%, Area C 41%, Area D 13%. Ask for the most reasonable first interpretation of Area C's figure.

- A) Area C's eligibility content is badly written and should be rewritten immediately.
- B) The figure is an outlier that warrants investigation — it may reflect a measurement difference, a genuinely harder eligibility rule, or a content problem.
- C) Areas A, B and D are losing too few users and their eligibility content is too permissive.
- D) The figures cannot be compared because the areas are different services.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three areas cluster at 12–14%; Area C sits at roughly three times the cluster, an outlier and a prompt for inquiry rather than a conclusion. This tests resisting a leap to a rewrite (A) before checking innocent explanations — a common one being that Area C's eligibility content correctly self-screens out more users, meaning high abandonment is the content doing its job, not failing at it.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Logical deduction about workflow sequencing**

Migration plan: (i) content cannot migrate before taxonomy mapping is complete; (ii) mapping cannot start until the content audit finishes; (iii) editor training must occur in the migration month or the month immediately before it. The audit finishes end of March; mapping takes one full month. Ask for the earliest month editor training may begin.

- A) February
- B) March
- C) April
- D) May

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Mapping occupies April (completing end of April); migration cannot occur before May; training must fall in May or April. This tests working forward through a dependency chain to find the earliest feasible point — the tempting error anchors on the migration month (May) rather than tracing the full chain from the audit's completion.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits chain for a content strategy**

Business case chain: "Consolidate 400 duplicate pages into 120 → users find the right page faster → reduced calls to the contact centre → improved staff wellbeing in the contact centre." Ask which link is logically weakest as drawn.

- A) Consolidation → users find the right page faster
- B) Users find the right page faster → reduced contact centre calls
- C) Reduced contact centre calls → improved staff wellbeing
- D) All links are equally strong

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The first two links have plausible mechanisms; the third changes category without one — fewer calls could equally lead to headcount reduction or higher call complexity per remaining adviser (easy calls disappear first). This tests spotting a category error in a causal chain, central to directing the evaluation of strategies, since a strategy promising the wrong benefit is judged a failure even when the content work succeeds.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

Three governance workshops (V, T, W) across Monday–Friday, one per day: V must precede both T and W; the head of content is available only Monday and Thursday and must attend V; T requires two full working days of synthesis after V; W must be the day immediately after T. Ask which day T must take place.

- A) Tuesday
- B) Wednesday
- C) Thursday
- D) Friday

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** V on Thursday would push T beyond the week, so V must be Monday; two synthesis days (Tuesday, Wednesday) put T's earliest day at Thursday; testing Friday for T would push W to Saturday, invalid — so T must be Thursday, W Friday. This tests fixing the most constrained variable first (V, pinned by availability) and propagating consequences through boundary cases.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Deduction from conflicting governance principles**

Framework: (P1) content under 10 views/month for six consecutive months must be archived; (P2) statutory content remains published regardless of usage; (P3) any exception to an archiving decision must be recorded in the exceptions register. A statutory page has had 4 views/month for eight months, remains published, with no exceptions register entry. Ask which statement best describes the situation.

- A) The framework has been breached because the page should have been archived under P1.
- B) The framework has been breached because the exception is unrecorded under P3.
- C) Whether the framework has been breached depends on whether P2 operating automatically counts as an "exception to an archiving decision" requiring registration — the framework is ambiguous and needs clarifying.
- D) The framework has not been breached and needs no change.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** P2 correctly overrides P1's archiving trigger for statutory content, ruling out A; whether that override counts as a registrable "exception" is genuinely undetermined by the text, ruling out both B and D as assumed interpretations. This tests recognising a genuine drafting ambiguity rather than resolving it by assumption — an ambiguity surfacing on one page today will surface on hundreds in the next audit.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Applied problem solving across interdependent teams**

Team Red proposes renaming "Payments" to "Money" based on user research; Team Blue's service auto-generates navigation from term names; Team Green has 200 pages tagged "Payments" and a three-week content freeze for a legal deadline. Ask for the soundest first move.

- A) Approve the rename now — user research evidence beats operational convenience.
- B) Reject the rename — stability of shared infrastructure beats one team's research.
- C) Validate the research finding across all three teams' users, model the impact on Blue's navigation and Green's freeze, and schedule any rename after Green's freeze with Blue's navigation tested.
- D) Let Team Red use "Money" locally while the shared taxonomy keeps "Payments".

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A shared taxonomy is shared infrastructure, and C sequences the decision correctly — validating generalisability, assessing each dependency's impact, and scheduling around the freeze, without refusing the change outright. This tests routing a change through appropriate governance rather than acting on one team's evidence alone (A) or forking the taxonomy (D), the worst long-term outcome since divergent local terms destroy the cross-service consistency this role exists to maintain.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Detecting the flawed inference in a strategy paper**

A strategy paper argues: "78% of users who contacted the helpline had first visited the website. Therefore the website is failing to answer users' questions, and we should invest in rewriting the most-visited pages." Ask for the most important flaw in this reasoning.

- A) The survey percentage is too low to matter.
- B) The inference ignores the users who visited the website and did not need to call — the data says nothing about the website's overall success rate.
- C) Rewriting pages is always less effective than restructuring navigation.
- D) Helpline users may be lying about visiting the website.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The statistic conditions on callers only — by construction, the population for whom every channel had already failed — so it cannot establish the website's overall success rate, which could be 95% or 40% and remain consistent with the same figure. This tests catching a base-rate error that misdirects investment, since rewriting the most-visited pages targets pages selected by traffic, not by actual failure.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate applies a stated governance rule mechanically to a scenario, even where the rule is imperfect, rather than substituting professional judgement for it.
- Note whether the candidate locates the minimal correction that restores consistency in an audit or error-checking item, rather than flagging every downstream symptom separately.
- Watch for whether the candidate treats an anomalous or outlier figure as a prompt for investigation rather than an immediate verdict.
- Score for whether prioritisation reasons from impact-weighted urgency across all items, distinguishing "no stated deadline" from "no urgency."
- Note whether the candidate catches a category error or an unstated mechanism in a causal or benefits chain.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a "must be true" answer that is merely plausible rather than guaranteed by the stated rules.
- Missing when a candidate flags every symptom in an audit as a separate defect rather than the minimal correcting change.
- Rewarding an immediate diagnosis or fix proposed for an outlier figure before any investigation.
- Accepting a prioritisation ranked by seniority of the requester or the presence of a stated deadline alone.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a causal chain or business case without checking each link's actual mechanism.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures a candidate's ability to interpret, manipulate, and draw sound conclusions from numerical information of the kind this role actually produces and consumes — content performance data, audit statistics, publishing throughput, survey results, readability metrics, and the resourcing arithmetic behind team plans.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 35 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, presenting a table, chart description, or short data narrative, calculator and rough paper usually permitted.

At this level the emphasis shifts from computing figures to interrogating them — catching the percentage applied to the wrong base, the average that hides a bimodal distribution, and the trend that is really a definition change.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages, ratios and rates** map to **User-centred content design**: review coverage rates, failure-demand percentages, and satisfaction scores, with the exact denominator known.
- **Averages and distributions** map to **Agile working**: understanding when a mean is informative and when it conceals variation that changes the decision.
- **Trend interpretation** maps to **Strategic thinking** and **User focus**: separating seasonality, level shifts, and definitional changes from genuine trends.
- **Resource and capacity arithmetic** maps to working with lead content designers to organise and manage teams.
- **Data-based challenge** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing senior stakeholders often turns on redoing their arithmetic politely but correctly.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Review coverage percentage**

Estate of 1,250 pages, governance requires review within 12 months; 950 pages meet the standard. Ask what percentage of the estate is out of compliance.

- A) 76%
- B) 24%
- C) 30%
- D) 32%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (1,250−950)÷1,250=24%. This tests fixing the denominator as the whole estate — option A is the compliance rate, the classic complement error, and stating clearly which way round a figure runs matters when presenting it to a board.

**Question 2 (easy) — Publishing throughput**

Pages published over four sprints: 18, 22, 17, 23. Ask for the mean per sprint.

- A) 19
- B) 20
- C) 21
- D) 22

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 80÷4=20. This tests basic averaging, checkable via symmetry (−2, +2, −3, +3 around 20), and, in a strong answer, noting a mean alone says nothing about consistency worth checking before committing a team to a forecast.

**Question 3 (easy) — Ratio of content types**

Guidance to transactional pages in the ratio 5:2, 350 pages total. Ask how many are transactional.

- A) 100
- B) 140
- C) 250
- D) 70

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 5+2=7 parts; 350÷7=50; 2×50=100. This tests the standard ratio method — option C is the guidance count, the read-the-wrong-half error, and the reliable method is always totalling the parts, sizing one, then multiplying.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Percentage change in failure demand**

Failure demand calls fell from 4,800 to 4,080 while total calls fell from 32,000 to 30,000. Ask which statement is correct.

- A) Failure demand fell 15% in absolute terms, and from 15% to 13.6% as a share of all calls.
- B) Failure demand fell 15% in absolute terms, and its share of all calls was unchanged.
- C) Failure demand fell 7.2% in absolute terms, and from 15% to 13.6% as a share of all calls.
- D) Failure demand rose as a share of calls because total calls fell.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Absolute change 15%; share last quarter 4,800÷32,000=15%; share this quarter 4,080÷30,000≈13.6%. This tests checking both absolute change and share when a denominator also moves — option B wrongly assumes an unchanged share without recomputing, and option D gets the general direction of a shrinking-denominator effect right but the specific arithmetic wrong.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average satisfaction**

Journey X: 82% satisfied, 3,000 responses. Journey Y: 76%, 1,500 responses. Journey Z: 90%, 500 responses. Ask for the overall satisfaction rate.

- A) 83%
- B) 80%
- C) 81%
- D) 79%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Weighted: (2,460+1,140+450)÷5,000=81%. This tests weighting by response count rather than averaging percentages unweighted (A, the trap, letting a small high-scoring journey drag the figure up) — weighting is what keeps a board-level roll-up honest.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reading a trend with a definition change**

Monthly views: Jan 210,000, Feb 205,000, Mar 208,000, Apr 265,000, May 262,000, Jun 267,000, with mobile app webviews added to tracking starting in April. A stakeholder claims: "Views jumped 27% in April — the March redesign worked." Ask for the soundest response.

- A) Agree — the April rise coincides with the redesign.
- B) Point out that the measurement definition changed in April, so the jump cannot be attributed to the redesign without separating webview traffic; the flat pattern before and after the step suggests a level shift, not growth.
- C) Argue the redesign failed because May fell slightly.
- D) Average the six months and report 236,000 views as the stable monthly figure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The series is flat before and flat after a step matching the definition change exactly — the signature of a measurement change, not a content effect, since a genuine redesign benefit would more plausibly show continuing growth. This tests refusing to read a definition change as a behaviour change — option A commits the coincidence-equals-cause error.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Capacity versus backlog**

Backlog of 300 pages; 4 designers each review 15 pages/sprint; 20 new pages arrive per sprint. Ask how many sprints to clear the backlog.

- A) 5
- B) 6
- C) 7.5
- D) 15

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Net clearance = (4×15)−20=40/sprint; 300÷40=7.5. This tests using the net rate rather than gross capacity alone (A ignores arrivals) — a backlog is a flow problem, and if arrivals rose to 60/sprint the backlog would never clear regardless of starting size.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Consolidation business case arithmetic**

Merging 400 duplicate pages into 120; maintenance costs 1.5 hours/page/quarter at £45/hour; the consolidation project costs 350 hours one-off. Ask after how many complete quarters cumulative saving exceeds the one-off cost.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 280 pages eliminated × 1.5 hours = 420 hours/quarter saved (£18,900), against a £15,750 one-off cost — exceeded within the first quarter. This tests computing payback correctly, and, in a strong answer, choosing units that cancel (hours rather than pounds) to shortcut the arithmetic.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Sample sizes and confident claims**

Comprehension task: Round 1 (old page) 6 of 10 completed; Round 2 (new page) 8 of 10 completed. A stakeholder claims: "The rewrite improved comprehension by 33%." Ask for the most important critique.

- A) The improvement is 20 percentage points, not 33%, so the claim is arithmetically wrong.
- B) With samples of 10, the difference between 6/10 and 8/10 is well within the range of chance variation, so the data cannot support a confident causal percentage claim at all — though it is encouraging and worth testing at scale.
- C) The claim should say 25% because 8 ÷ 6 is not a valid calculation.
- D) Comprehension tasks are qualitative, so no numbers should ever be reported.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The 33% relative-change arithmetic is actually defensible; the deeper problem is that with n=10, two additional successes fall well within ordinary sampling variation. This tests separating an arithmetic critique from an inferential one — option D overcorrects, since counts like "8 of 10" are legitimately reportable with appropriate caveats about uncertainty.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Interpreting a two-way table**

1,200 pages: High traffic — 380 in date, 120 overdue (500 total); Low traffic — 460 in date, 240 overdue (700 total). Ask which statement is supported.

- A) Most overdue pages are high traffic.
- B) A high-traffic page is more likely to be in date (76%) than a low-traffic page (about 66%), and overdue high-traffic pages, though a minority, number 120.
- C) Low-traffic pages are reviewed more reliably than high-traffic pages.
- D) 30% of high-traffic pages are overdue.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** High traffic in date 380÷500=76%; low traffic in date 460÷700≈66%. This tests computing correct conditional (column) percentages — option A miscounts (120 high vs 240 low overdue, so most overdue pages are low traffic), and D likely comes from 360÷1,200, the overall rate rather than the high-traffic-conditioned rate.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Compound change over multiple periods**

Traffic fell 10%, rose 15%, then fell 5% over three quarters. A stakeholder claims traffic is level with pre-migration volumes since −10+15−5=0. Ask, from a 100,000-session baseline, for the actual third-quarter volume, and whether the stakeholder is right.

- A) 100,000 — the stakeholder is right.
- B) 98,325 — the stakeholder is wrong; traffic is about 1.7% below pre-migration levels.
- C) 101,750 — traffic is above pre-migration levels.
- D) 95,000 — traffic is 5% down.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 100,000×0.90×1.15×0.95=98,325. This tests recognising that percentage changes compound multiplicatively, not additively — the stakeholder's additive shortcut overstates recovery, an error that grows fast with larger swings.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator before computing a percentage, especially when it has changed between periods.
- Note whether the candidate weights an average by group size rather than averaging percentages unweighted.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes a definitional or measurement change in a metric from a genuine behavioural trend.
- Score for whether a small-sample or underpowered finding is reported at its actual level of confidence, neither overclaimed nor dismissed entirely.
- Note whether percentage changes across multiple periods are compounded multiplicatively rather than added.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage computed against the wrong denominator, especially in a two-way table or where the base changed between periods.
- Missing when a candidate averages group rates unweighted rather than by volume.
- Rewarding a step change in a metric attributed to a behavioural cause without ruling out a measurement or definition change.
- Accepting a confident precise percentage claim drawn from a small sample.
- Missing when a candidate adds sequential percentage changes rather than compounding them.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment tests whether a candidate can determine exactly what a piece of written material establishes. For a content strategist this is unusually close to the work itself — reading policy intent, governance documents, style guidance, research findings and stakeholder correspondence, and turning them into structures other people will rely on.

The commonest format presents a passage followed by statements to be marked True, False or Cannot Say. True means the passage establishes it, False means the passage contradicts it, Cannot Say means it settles neither — regardless of professional knowledge, which is the main hazard, since a strong candidate will frequently know how governance usually works while the passage in front of them is silent on exactly that point.

Three specifics matter particularly: governance documents allocate authority in language that looks interchangeable and is not (approves, endorses, is consulted, may direct, is informed); policy intent and policy text are different things, and reading an aspiration as a commitment is how content strategies acquire promises nobody made; and taxonomy and definition prose is precise by necessity, with a term defined in one place governing everywhere it appears.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading governance precisely** maps to developing governance, processes and workflows based on strategy and policy intent.
- **Reading definitions and taxonomies** maps to shaping and structuring the architecture of content.
- **Reading policy intent** maps to **Strategic thinking**: evaluating strategies and policies against business requirements.
- **Reading stakeholder correspondence** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Reading research** maps to **User focus** and **User-centred content design**.
- **Reading for quality assurance** maps to assuring the quality of content from more junior colleagues.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "The content governance board approves changes to the departmental content model and to the top two levels of the taxonomy. Changes to lower levels of the taxonomy are made by the content strategist for the relevant domain, who must notify the board where a change creates a new term. The board may direct that a lower-level change be brought to it for approval where in its view the change affects more than one domain. The head of content endorses the board's decisions and may return a decision for reconsideration once, with reasons; a decision returned and reaffirmed by the board stands. The board meets every six weeks."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Changes to the top two levels of the taxonomy require approval by the content governance board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests the baseline reading, and, in a strong answer, noting the passage immediately sets up a second, different arrangement for lower levels that later questions turn on.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: "The head of content can overturn a decision of the content governance board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The head of content has a single power of return, not a veto, and the final clause exists precisely to close this inference. This tests distinguishing endorsement-with-a-return-power from an override — knowing the difference determines whose agreement is actually needed when negotiating a change through.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A content strategist making a lower-level taxonomy change that does not create a new term has no obligation to notify the board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The notification duty is qualified to changes creating a new term. This tests reading a conditional obligation correctly, and, in a strong answer, noting this says nothing about the board's separate cross-domain call-in power.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "The board can require a lower-level change to come to it for approval only where the change creates a new term."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The call-in power is conditioned on affecting more than one domain, a different and independent test from creating a new term. This tests catching a condition attached to one mechanism (notification) imported into a different one (call-in) — the commonest misreading of governance text, worth being alert to since it is also the commonest way governance written by this role gets misapplied.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The department's content strategy states that content will be organised around user tasks rather than around organisational structure. Where an existing content set cannot be reorganised without disrupting a live service, the reorganisation may be deferred until the service's next major release. The strategy anticipates that the majority of guidance content will be task-organised within two years. Content owned by arm's length bodies is out of scope. Teams are encouraged to align local taxonomies with the departmental model and to record where they diverge. The strategy is reviewed annually."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Content owned by arm's length bodies is within the scope of the strategy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Directly contradicted. This tests reading a scope exclusion carefully — the sentences most often skimmed, and the ones determining what a strategy actually covers.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The majority of guidance content will be task-organised within two years."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The strategy "anticipates" this — a forecast, establishing that the forecast exists rather than that the outcome will occur. This tests catching the specific error strategy documents most reliably produce: reading a document's expectation as a fact about the world.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "A team must record where its local taxonomy diverges from the departmental model."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Teams are "encouraged" to align and record — a single verb governing both — not required. This tests recognising that a deliberate word choice ("encouraged," alongside "may be deferred" and "is out of scope" elsewhere) is not an accident.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "A content set that would disrupt a live service if reorganised will be reorganised at the service's next major release."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes a permission to defer up to that point, not an obligation for reorganisation to then happen. This tests resisting the natural completion of a deferral into an obligation — exactly the kind of drafting weakness worth noticing in a real strategy.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "A study of 36 participants tested two navigation structures for a guidance section. Structure A grouped content by the life event prompting the user's visit; structure B grouped content by the type of document. Participants using structure A located the correct guidance more often, at 78% against 53%. Participants who described themselves as familiar with the subject showed no difference between structures. The study did not measure how long participants took. Participants were recruited from people who had contacted the department's helpline in the previous month. The research team recommends structure A."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: "Participants using structure A located the correct guidance more often than those using structure B."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly with figures. This tests the baseline reading, and, in a strong answer, carrying forward the immediate qualification for familiar users that the next question turns on.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "Structure A is better for users who are already familiar with the subject."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "No difference" is absence of a detected difference, not evidence A is better — nor worse. This tests distinguishing a null finding from a positive one, a distinction that survives when a stakeholder challenges the recommendation.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: "The findings apply to the general population of people seeking this guidance."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Participants were recruited from recent helpline callers — a group whose channels had already failed. This tests checking who was studied before generalising a finding, most important when the finding is one a candidate likes.

**Question 12 (harder)**

Statement: "Structure A takes users less time than structure B."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly states time was not measured. This tests noticing what a study did not measure and reproducing that boundary when citing it — and, in a strong answer, noting the intuitive answer is not even clearly right, since a life-event structure could plausibly take longer while still producing better outcomes.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes different governance authority verbs (approves, endorses, may direct, is consulted) precisely.
- Note whether the candidate checks a scope-exclusion or scoping clause carefully rather than skimming past it.
- Watch for whether the candidate reads a forecast ("anticipates") as a forecast rather than an established commitment.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes a null research finding ("no difference") from a positive one ("better").
- Note whether the candidate checks who was recruited into a study before generalising its findings.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own governance or content strategy experience rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate imports a condition attached to one governance mechanism into a different one.
- Rewarding a strategy's stated projection ("anticipates," "expects") treated as an established outcome.
- Accepting a permission to defer completed into an obligation the passage never actually creates.
- Missing when a candidate turns "no difference for group X" into "better for group X."

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment presents realistic scenarios and asks a candidate to identify effective and ineffective responses. For a content strategist the scenarios involve senior stakeholders with competing interests, governance that people find inconvenient, taxonomies that outlive the assumptions they were built on, and content teams influenced but frequently not managed.

The commonest formats ask for the most and least effective response, or for ratings. Scoring is against a key derived from experienced practitioners in comparable roles.

Three patterns run through the strong answers at this level. First, governance is paid for by everyone who works within it every day, so the useful question about a process is whether the benefit exceeds the distributed cost, and this role is usually the only person who will compute that. Second, adoption is a design problem rather than a compliance problem — a workflow that is better and harder to follow loses to one that is worse and easier, every time. Third, this role works almost entirely through influence, and that only works if people believe the same standards apply to the candidate's own structures as to theirs.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Governance design** maps to developing governance, processes, and workflows.
- **Stakeholder negotiation** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Taxonomy judgement** maps to shaping and structuring the architecture of content.
- **Quality assurance and coaching** maps to **User-centred content design**.
- **Prioritisation and delivery** maps to **Agile working**.
- **Evidence judgement** maps to **User focus**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A stakeholder wants their own section**

A senior stakeholder asks for a top-level site section dedicated to their directorate's work. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Explain that content is organised by user task, not by organisational structure.
- B) Find out what they need that is not currently happening — usually that their content is hard to find, or that they cannot show anyone what their directorate has published — and propose how to meet that within a task-based structure, because the underlying need is nearly always achievable and the top-level section almost never is.
- C) Agree to the section; the stakeholder is senior.
- D) Offer a landing page instead.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The requested structure is a solution arrived at before speaking to this role; the underlying need (findability, visibility) has answers that do not require restructuring the site. This tests recovering the actual need behind a structural request — option C is least effective, granting a top-level section on seniority establishes a precedent every other directorate will invoke, degrading the structure permanently.

**Question 2 (easy) — A workflow nobody follows**

A content review workflow introduced six months ago is widely ignored. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Remind teams of the workflow.
- B) Find out why before doing anything, because a workflow ignored by capable people for six months is usually unworkable, unclear, or more expensive than the alternative — and each of those has a different fix.
- C) Add it to the governance board's assurance criteria.
- D) Withdraw it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Widespread non-compliance over six months is evidence about the workflow, not about discipline. This tests diagnosing before enforcing — option A is least effective, a reminder about an unworkable process buying a fortnight of compliance and teaching everyone that governance here is advisory.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Two teams, two taxonomies**

Two content teams in the same domain have developed different taxonomies for overlapping material, and both defend theirs. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Decide which is better and require both to adopt it.
- B) Get them together and establish what each taxonomy was built to do, because two teams defending structures usually have two different sets of user needs that were never compared — and put the user encountering both in the room, since neither team sees that from inside their own material.
- C) Let each keep its own; both work locally.
- D) Escalate to the content governance board.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Moving both parties from positions to requirements resolves this, and naming the user who encounters both structures supplies the argument neither team is making alone. This tests moving disputing parties from positions to underlying needs — option C is least effective, accepting a permanent user-facing incoherence to avoid a conversation, which compounds over time.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Governance that is slowing delivery**

A delivery manager reports that content governance is delaying releases. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Explain that governance exists for good reasons.
- B) Find out where the delay actually sits — a board that meets every six weeks, a review step with one available reviewer, an approval required for changes that do not need it — because the delay is nearly always in a queue rather than in the review itself, and queues can usually be removed without losing the control.
- C) Reduce the governance requirements.
- D) Ask them to plan further ahead.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Governance processes are rarely slow because the reviewing takes long — they are slow because work waits for a meeting, a person, or a slot, and that wait can usually be removed without losing control. This tests locating the queue rather than the review as the cause — option D is least effective, accepting the delay as permanent and putting its cost on delivery.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Research that undercuts your structure**

Research suggests a content architecture designed last year is not matching how users think about the domain. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Present the finding and what it means, including the cost of restructuring and the cost of leaving it, and let the decision be made explicitly with both in view.
- B) Present the finding and recommend restructuring.
- C) Ask for further research before raising it.
- D) Note it for the next annual review.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Giving decision-makers both costs, framed as a live choice, allows a change of position without it reading as a verdict on the original work. This tests presenting a finding against one's own past work honestly and promptly — option D is least effective, deferring a finding about how users think to an annual review, a year of people not finding things, and reads as burying evidence about one's own design.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Quality assurance on a junior colleague's work**

A junior content designer's work has a sound structure but terminology inconsistent with the taxonomy. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Correct the terms yourself before it publishes.
- B) Show them the inconsistencies against the taxonomy, explain why the consistency matters for findability and reuse, and ask them to make the changes — because the reason is the transferable part, and a colleague who understands why will catch the next instance unaided.
- C) Tell them which terms to change.
- D) Approve it; the structure is what matters.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Explaining why taxonomy consistency matters transfers a principle rather than a list, making the correction stick for future work. This tests coaching by transferring reasoning — option A is least effective, removing the work and teaching nothing, guaranteeing the same corrections indefinitely.

**Question 7 (harder) — Competing senior stakeholders**

Two directors want incompatible things from the same content set, and both have escalated. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Establish what each needs the content to achieve rather than what structure they want, and look for an arrangement serving both — and if the needs are genuinely incompatible, put the choice, with its consequences for users, to whoever is accountable for the content set.
- B) Propose a compromise structure combining both.
- C) Take the decision yourself on user-centred grounds.
- D) Escalate to the content governance board.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Incompatible requests are frequently compatible needs, and testing that first, then naming who decides if the conflict is genuinely real, prevents recurrence. This tests distinguishing genuine conflict from apparent conflict — option B is least effective, a structure combining two incompatible approaches typically acquiring the weaknesses of both and the coherence of neither.

**Question 8 (harder) — A taxonomy that has aged**

A taxonomy designed three years ago no longer fits how services are organised, and teams are working around it. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Defend it; the workarounds are the problem.
- B) Treat the workarounds as evidence and say plainly that the original assumptions no longer hold — what has changed, what the workarounds cost, and what revising it would take — because your visible willingness to have your own structures revisited is what makes it safe for anyone to raise the next one.
- C) Require teams to stop working around it.
- D) Revise it to match what teams are doing.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Reading workarounds as information about the structure rather than about compliance, and handling the fact that this was one's own design openly, is what makes future challenges to other people's structures credible. This tests treating workarounds as evidence — option C is least effective, enforcing a taxonomy people cannot work within, producing nominal compliance and hidden shadow structures.

**Question 9 (harder) — Being asked to commit to a timeline**

A programme director asks for a firm date for a full content restructure, with estimates carrying wide uncertainty. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Commit to the outcome and to the early milestones precisely, give the later stages as ranges with the uncertainty stated, and propose a point at which the timeline firms up.
- B) Commit to the date; a programme needs a date.
- C) Commit with heavy caveats.
- D) Decline until the scope is clearer.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Giving the certainty that genuinely exists (early stages, the destination) without manufacturing certainty that does not, with a review point, gives the director what they actually need. This tests structured honest commitment under uncertainty — option C is least effective, caveats rarely being heard while the date is, committing to the number while feeling like a hedge.

**Question 10 (harder) — Evidence against your own process**

Data shows a content review process introduced by the candidate consumes significant time across five teams and has not measurably improved content quality. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Improve the measurement; content quality is hard to capture.
- B) Publish the finding, including that it is your process, and narrow it to where the benefit is demonstrable or withdraw it — because five teams paying weekly for an unmeasurable benefit is a real cost, and withdrawing your own process on evidence makes every remaining one more credible.
- C) Retain it; some benefits do not show in data.
- D) Reduce its scope quietly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The cost is certain and distributed; the benefit is unmeasured, and publishing the finding and narrowing or withdrawing openly builds credibility. This tests publishing and acting on evidence against one's own process — option C is least effective, an unfalsifiable claim that keeps every unproductive process in place indefinitely.

**Question 11 (harder) — A content team you do not manage**

A content team in another directorate publishes material conflicting with the departmental model, with no authority over them. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Approach them directly, show what the conflict causes for users moving between the two content sets, and offer help aligning — because you are asking for cooperation and the case has to be worth their effort, not merely correct.
- B) Raise it with the content governance board.
- C) Raise it with their director.
- D) Correct the affected content yourself.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Without authority, the only currency is a case worth acting on plus offered help, lowering the cost of agreeing. This tests building cooperation without formal authority — option D is least effective, editing another team's content without asking, discourteous, likely to undo decisions made for unknown reasons, and ending any prospect of cooperation.

**Question 12 (harder) — Prioritising with a team**

A content team with more requests than capacity is trying to serve all of them partially. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Help them establish what each request is actually for and what happens if it waits, then support them in doing fewer things completely and telling the rest explicitly what that means for them.
- B) Ask them to work faster.
- C) Advise them to prioritise by stakeholder seniority.
- D) Escalate the capacity problem.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Partial delivery on everything often serves nobody, since content half-restructured is worse than content left alone; naming the requests that will not be served lets those people replan. This tests doing fewer things completely rather than everything partially — option B is least effective, treating a prioritisation problem as an effort problem and guaranteeing the same position next quarter.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate recovers the underlying need behind a structural or process request rather than accepting or refusing it as stated.
- Note whether the candidate reads long-standing non-compliance as evidence about a process or structure, rather than about the people failing to follow it.
- Watch for whether the candidate computes or estimates whether a governance process's benefit exceeds its distributed cost before defending or changing it.
- Score for whether the candidate applies the same scrutiny to their own past structures, taxonomies and processes as to anyone else's.
- Note whether a response builds cooperation or a case worth acting on where the candidate has no formal authority, rather than escalating prematurely or acting unilaterally.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a structural or process request granted on the requester's seniority alone.
- Missing when a candidate reminds people of a rule or workflow they already know and evidently cannot follow, rather than diagnosing why.
- Rewarding a compromise structure combining two genuinely incompatible approaches.
- Accepting enforcement of a taxonomy or process that capable teams are visibly working around.
- Missing when a candidate acts unilaterally on content or a team outside their authority rather than building a cooperative case.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through structural reasoning — reading a set of inconsistencies for the cause that generates them, following governance rules through several stacked conditions, and distinguishing what a content set is from what it is organised around; the arithmetic that turns a content argument into a decision — volumes and proportions weighted correctly, the cost of a process multiplied across the teams and frequency that actually bear it, and percentage changes compounded rather than added; the discipline of reading governance, strategy and research documents at exactly the strength the text supports, since endorsement is not veto and a forecast is not a commitment; and the situational judgement that defines this role — designing structures everyone else lives inside, with limited authority to compel and considerable ability to make things worse, where the strong answers found the need behind a request, read workarounds as evidence, and subjected the candidate's own taxonomy and process to the same scrutiny applied to anyone else's.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several cognitive and situational items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, particularly around what evidence actually establishes and whose decision a governance conflict belongs to. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
