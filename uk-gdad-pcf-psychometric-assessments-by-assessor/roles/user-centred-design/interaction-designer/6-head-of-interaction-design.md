# Head of Interaction Design - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of interaction design. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A head of interaction design is an expert practitioner with broad industry experience who defines and assures best practice, influences both design and organisational strategy, collaborates with counterpart colleagues across government, and focuses on ensuring the right conditions and environment for designers to work effectively. That last element distinguishes this role from every level below it: the primary output is no longer design, nor even the assurance of design, but the conditions under which good design becomes the normal result.

Four features shape this material. First, a candidate is accountable for design quality produced by people they did not train, on services they have not seen, judged from outputs rather than the work behind them — diagnostic reasoning at a distance is a core competence. Second, the role works at the level of shared assets and systems — design systems, component libraries, standards, assurance routines, tooling and the conditions of work — decisions paid for many times over, in maintenance, in the expectations they set, and in the accessibility characteristics they propagate. Third, the role influences people who substantially outrank the candidate, in forums where design is one input among many, so influence rests on evidence being unassailable and judgement being trusted. Fourth, the role builds capability — the practice left behind matters more than any component assured.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate diagnoses a systemic mechanism behind evidence assembled at a distance, reads a distribution of failures diagnostically, and interrogates a quantitative claim (a conformance statement, a business case, a board paper) for what it actually establishes. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward action that changes a system or set of conditions rather than solving a problem personally, absorbs organisational pressure that should not land on a more junior colleague, and finds an owner for a problem nobody currently owns.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-profession level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is almost entirely diagnostic and strategic: evidence assembled by other people for other purposes, standards applied unevenly, findings that appear to conflict until what each measured is established, and prioritisation problems whose units are programmes and shared assets.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority.

Three things characterise this section at this level. First, reasoning at a distance — almost never seeing the work itself, only an output, a component, a claim in a board paper, a summary of a summary. Second, the interesting questions concern mechanisms — when something goes wrong repeatedly, the distribution of failures is the informative feature. Third, the multiplier — a component adopted by fifteen services carries its defects and virtues fifteen times, and is far harder to change than to introduce.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing at a distance** maps to this role level's requirement to define and assure best practice.
- **Cross-service synthesis** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and using evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Systemic diagnosis** maps to **Iterative design** and **Leading design**: iterating and measurably improving design maturity.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Designing strategically**: ensuring design is part of organisational strategy and included early enough to inform decisions.
- **Diagnosing structural exclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**: guiding organisations to adopt inclusive practices and mitigating the influence of privilege and power.
- **Reasoning about positions and incentives** maps to **Design communication**: gaining support from senior leaders and communicating across organisational boundaries.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Standard: every component entry must state the problem it solves, when not to use it, keyboard behaviour, assistive technology behaviour, known limitations, and the date last reviewed. An entry states the problem, keyboard behaviour, and date; describes assistive technology behaviour as "tested and works"; has no "when not to use" or known limitations. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Problem, keyboard behaviour and date are present; "tested and works" addresses assistive technology behaviour, even thinly, as written; "when not to use" and known limitations are both absent. Two unmet. This tests applying the standard as written — and, in a strong candidate's answer, noticing that "when not to use" and known limitations are the two most commonly omitted fields in component documentation everywhere, pointing to a fix in the template and review prompt rather than this one entry.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Rule: "A custom component may enter a live service only with design assurance sign-off, unless it replaces an existing custom component on a like-for-like basis and has been assessed as low risk." A directorate reports shipping one without sign-off. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) It was a low-risk like-for-like replacement.
- C) Either it was a low-risk like-for-like replacement, or the standard was breached.
- D) The component should be withdrawn.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** With the exception compound (like-for-like *and* low risk) and the facts silent on whether it applied, only the disjunction is guaranteed; D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true. This tests reading a compound exception precisely, and framing a follow-up as an enquiry rather than an accusation, since an accusation withdrawn costs standing that is hard to replace.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading a study from its output**

An output states: "Users find the new autocomplete easy — all 24 participants completed the task without difficulty." The facilitator demonstrated the component first, participants came from the departmental panel, and all used a laptop with a mouse. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) Twenty-four participants is too small a base.
- B) The demonstration, the panel recruitment and the mouse-only conditions each remove a distinct source of the difficulty this kind of component characteristically produces — first encounter, unfamiliarity, and keyboard or assistive technology operation — so the result describes an unusually favourable case rather than the component's usability.
- C) "Easy" is not a measurable construct.
- D) A think-aloud protocol should have been used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unanimity is the alarm, and this tests naming exactly what each condition removed. Option A misdiagnoses validity as size — 240 participants under identical conditions produce the same finding; option D leaves all three conditions in place, and a team adding think-aloud while keeping the demonstration gets the same answer and believes it more.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

Substantial capacity for one thing this quarter: (1) an identity check pattern is being decided next month, reused across eight services; (2) a live service has a persistent 30% drop-off at one step, affecting 500,000 users a year; (3) a service launches in five weeks with designs signed off; (4) a spending review submission due in eight months. Ask which is strongest.

- A) Item 2, because it affects the most users.
- B) Item 1, because the decision is imminent, will be reused eight times, and design input is the only thing that could still shape it — and once adopted, changing a pattern becomes a coordination problem across eight teams rather than a design problem.
- C) Item 4, because spending review submissions shape long-term investment.
- D) Item 3, because launching untested carries reputational risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack. This tests weighting by value of information and reuse rather than current scale — item 3 sounds prudent and is weakest, since signed-off designs five weeks out means findings arrive with nowhere to go; item 4 is the profile trap, since spending review work attracts attention that is not decision leverage.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

Three items reach a candidate in a month: a team reports users struggling to prove identity without a passport or driving licence; a second service shows 45% drop-off at identity verification; the contact centre reports identity queries as its highest call driver across five services. Ask for the strongest synthesis.

- A) Three teams have found usability problems at their identity steps.
- B) The organisation has a cross-cutting identity assurance problem concentrated among people without common photographic documents — a strategic capability question rather than five separate interaction fixes, and one where five teams each building a workaround produces five partial solutions and leaves the same population excluded five times over.
- C) Identity verification is hard and should be simplified.
- D) The findings are unrelated.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a shared mechanism and reframing the unit of the problem, then drawing the consequence — five local workarounds are actively harmful, not merely wasteful, since each will be partial in a different way and the excluded population remains excluded in variations nobody can then fix centrally. Option C is true and strategically useless.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Scope of a claim heading for a standard**

A proposal for a new organisational pattern argues "users expect to save and return at any point," supported by four studies all of services taking more than two weeks. Ask for the most useful intervention.

- A) Reject the pattern.
- B) Note that the evidence comes exclusively from multi-week services, so the claim over-reaches; narrow the pattern's stated applicability, or test whether the expectation holds in short services, before it becomes something every team is expected to implement.
- C) Commission more studies.
- D) Accept it; four studies is a reasonable base.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and the claim built on it reaches further than the evidence goes, unusually consequential since it is destined to become an organisational pattern with an implied obligation. This tests preserving the finding and narrowing its stated scope — the fix belongs exactly in the field Question 1 identified as most commonly omitted.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Eight teams keep building custom components where Design System components would serve, each with a plausible local reason. Ask for the strongest diagnosis and response.

- A) Review and challenge each proposal.
- B) Eight teams doing the same thing for plausible local reasons is one systemic cause with eight rationalisations; establish which of the realistic candidates it is — the components are genuinely missing a needed variant, teams do not know what exists, or adopting is harder than building fresh — and address that, because the corresponding remedies are entirely different.
- C) Introduce a rule requiring approval for any custom component.
- D) Accept it; teams know their own needs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eight teams behaving identically for plausible reasons is the signature of a systemic cause. This tests naming the realistic candidates, each implying a different remedy — option A wins each argument while the behaviour continues; option D accepts eight sets of accessibility defects and eight maintenance burdens.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study under assurance used a prototype whose "back" function was broken. Four findings: (i) participants could not locate the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned after a wrong turn; (iii) sessions ran long; (iv) participants misread the declaration. Ask which are compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; the fault was incidental.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, since inability to go back plausibly produced both, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A broken back function directly manufactures abandonment and inflates durations; content comprehension findings are largely untouched. This tests scoping precisely rather than wholesale condemnation (A), which at organisational scale teaches teams to stop declaring faults — leaving future studies with problems quietly omitted.

**Question 9 (harder) — Apparent contradiction between teams**

Two teams reach opposite conclusions about the same control: one finds users prefer a single combined date field, the other three separate inputs. Both studies appear sound. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Commission a third study.
- B) Establish what differs between the contexts before treating it as a contradiction — a date of birth is recalled and transcribed while an appointment date is chosen from a known range, and the synthesis is that the right control depends on whether the user already holds the value.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apparent contradictions between sound studies are usually context differences in disguise, and this tests asking what each participant was actually doing. The synthesis is more valuable than either finding, extracting a generalisable rule this role exists to propagate. Option C abdicates that synthesis.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

Accessibility assurance is required before public beta; fifteen of nineteen services complied, and the four that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced practitioners. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by people who know the requirement indicates a process that cannot be complied with under deadline conditions — investigate what it demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the four alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-beta gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests reading concentrated failure as diagnostic of a process rather than of conduct, while the remediation clause preserves that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unaddressed. Option D imposes friction on fifteen compliant services to catch four — how organisational burden accumulates.

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for an adoption decision**

Evidence on adopting a third-party component library: an internal audit finding accessibility defects in four components; a supplier claim of full WCAG conformance; two teams reporting faster delivery; a maintenance cost model. Ask for the most rigorous contribution.

- A) Oppose adoption on the basis of the audit.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that conformance claims typically cover components in isolation under default configuration rather than as composed and customised, which is where defects arise — and name the one or two unknowns that would most change the decision.
- C) Support adoption on the delivery evidence.
- D) Recommend further audit before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests making the evidence base legible rather than advocating or delaying, identifying why a conformance claim and an audit can both be accurate about different scopes. Option D reflexively calling for more evidence is how heads of profession find themselves excluded from decisions.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your own organisation**

A year's outputs show two teams consistently producing accessible components and six that do not; both strong teams include a developer who previously worked in accessibility consultancy. Ask for the most useful inference and action.

- A) The six weaker teams need accessibility training.
- B) Something transferable is likely operating in the two strong teams — a routine, a check, a habit of testing in a particular mode — and the highest-value action is to find out what it actually is by watching how they work, then build it into the organisation's standards and tooling rather than attributing the difference to individual expertise.
- C) Recruit more people with accessibility backgrounds.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the six weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests treating a pattern as an opportunity to identify and propagate a routine rather than attributing it to individual pedigree (C, unusable as an inference from two people). Option D catches more defects and creates no capacity to prevent them.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate diagnoses a systemic cause behind evidence assembled at organisational distance, rather than reacting to the instance in front of them.
- Note whether the candidate reads a distribution of failures diagnostically (concentrated versus scattered) rather than treating each instance in isolation.
- Watch for whether the candidate weighs a shared-asset decision by how many services will reuse it.
- Score for whether the candidate resolves an apparent contradiction between two sound sources by identifying what actually differs.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes what a claim (conformance statement, benefits figure) actually covers before treating it as contradicting other evidence.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an audit that catches wrong requirements but misses absent ones.
- Missing when a candidate applies a standard more strictly or loosely than its own wording supports.
- Rewarding a reminder or training proposed as the response to a systemic, distribution-level problem.
- Accepting friction added for a compliant majority to address a small number of non-compliant, concentrated cases.
- Missing when a candidate condemns or excuses a flawed study wholesale rather than scoping the flaw precisely.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At this level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation — the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims, and in most rooms a head of profession enters, they may be the only person who will ask.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided. The figures a head of profession encounters are increasingly consequential and increasingly detached from anyone who could explain them — business cases, vendor conformance claims, benefits figures, performance metrics copied between documents for years without the definition being re-checked.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to this role level's requirement to define and assure best practice.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and using evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Comparing rates between modes of operation** maps to **Designing for everyone**: guiding organisations to adopt inclusive practices.
- **Judging what a measurement establishes** maps to **Evidence-based design**: embedding hypothesis-driven design.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**: gaining support from senior leaders.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **Designing strategically**: design must inform decisions early, which requires the figures in those decisions to mean what they appear to.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 12,000 users who reached a page, 3,000 triggered a validation error on one field. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 3,000÷12,000=25%. This tests basic percentage calculation — and, in a strong candidate's answer, asking whether the field belongs to a shared pattern, in which case fixing it multiplies in value by the number of adopting services.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off against the right base**

A page is reached by 16,000 users and passed by 12,000. Ask for the drop-off rate.

- A) 25%
- B) 33%
- C) 75%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Drop-off 4,000÷16,000=25%. This tests measuring against arrivals, not against those who continued — a single line in a standard specifying the denominator is among the cheapest quality interventions available, and one of the few that fixes a whole class of error permanently.

**Question 3 (easy) — Disaggregated rates**

Completion: Mouse users 24,300/27,000; Keyboard-only 1,050/2,100; Screen reader 225/900. Ask for the overall completion rate and the gap between the highest and lowest group rates.

- A) 85% overall; 50 point gap
- B) 85% overall; 65 point gap
- C) 90% overall; 65 point gap
- D) 88% overall; 40 point gap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Overall: 25,575÷30,000≈85%. Group rates: 90%, 50%, 25%; gap 65 points. This tests not mistaking the dominant group's rate for the overall rate (C, the characteristic error when one group is 90% of the sample) — the aggregate of 85% describes no group and conceals that screen reader users fail three times in four.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Thirteen sessions' task times in seconds: 12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 28, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 300. Ask which summary is most defensible.

- A) Mean 49.2 seconds
- B) Median 28 seconds, range 12 to 300, with the 300-second case described separately
- C) Median 28 seconds
- D) Mean 49.2 seconds, range 12 to 300

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean exceeds eleven of thirteen observations; the median (28) describes the group well but alone conceals a session ten times the typical duration — likely the most informative data point. This tests supplying centre, spread, and explicit treatment of an outlier.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four studies of a component: 20 participants at 40%; 30 at 60%; 10 at 70%; 40 at 80%. Ask for the overall rate across all 100.

- A) 62%
- B) 65%
- C) 66%
- D) 68%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 8+18+7+32=65÷100=65%. This tests weighting by participant count rather than averaging the four rates directly (62.5%, option A) — and, in a strong candidate's answer, re-checking the total before it leaves their hands, since at this level a plausible-looking figure travels on the candidate's authority and nobody downstream will question it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 200,000 start, 150,000 pass identity, 90,000 upload evidence, 81,000 submit. Ask what proportion of those who passed identity went on to submit.

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 81,000÷150,000=54%. This tests identifying the denominator the question names; the funnel shows losses of 50,000 at identity and 60,000 between identity and upload, against 9,000 at submission.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A benefits claim**

A benefits paper states a change raised completion from 60% to 72%, describing it as "a 20% improvement." Ask whether the description is accurate.

- A) No; it is a 12% improvement.
- B) Yes as arithmetic — 12 ÷ 60 = 20% relative increase — but the expression is ambiguous, and the paper should state either "20% relative increase" or "12 percentage points."
- C) No; it is 12 percentage points and calling it 20% is wrong.
- D) No; it should be 16.7%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relative increase (12÷60=20%) is defensible arithmetic; the problem is labelling, since many readers would read "20% improvement" as a rise to 80%. This tests distinguishing an arithmetic error from a labelling ambiguity — requiring both figures as a standing convention costs no political capital, being a rule about documents rather than a challenge to a person.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A conformance claim**

A supplier states their component library is "fully WCAG 2.2 AA conformant." An internal audit found defects in four components. Ask for the most important question before treating these as contradictory.

- A) Whether the audit was conducted competently.
- B) What the conformance claim actually covers — most such claims are established for components in isolation under default configuration, while defects typically arise once components are composed, customised and placed in a real page with real focus management, so both statements can be accurate about different things.
- C) Whether the claim is current.
- D) Whether the supplier has other government clients.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Conformance claims and internal audits routinely disagree without either being wrong, and this tests identifying that scope, not conflict, explains it. Options A, C and D produce the appearance of scrutiny without touching the actual discrepancy, which is worse than asking nothing because it satisfies the room and closes the question.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims a 5-percentage-point completion improvement on 2 million annual starts is worth £12 million a year. Ask for the implied value per additional completed application.

- A) £6
- B) £60
- C) £120
- D) £240

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 0.05×2,000,000=100,000 additional completions; £12,000,000÷100,000=£120. This tests reconstructing an implied unit value in seconds to check a business case's plausibility, enabling a specific, answerable challenge that accuses nobody.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A board paper reports "average time to complete: 11 minutes" for a service the candidate's evidence shows most users take over forty minutes across several sittings to complete. Ask for the likely explanation and the right intervention.

- A) The board paper is wrong and should be corrected.
- B) The metric almost certainly measures single sessions rather than end-to-end journeys, so multi-sitting completions are excluded or counted only for their final short visit, and abandoned sessions may be included — ask for the definition and the distribution, and propose an end-to-end measure alongside it.
- C) The research sample was atypical.
- D) The figures cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Session-based analytics and journey-based reality diverge systematically for any multi-sitting service, always flattering it. This tests naming the mechanism and proposing a constructive intervention rather than picking a fight over a figure that is probably calculated correctly — how heads of profession acquire a reputation for obstruction and are invited to fewer meetings.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A directorate proposes measuring whether a redesign improved completion using 50 moderated sessions before and 50 after; baseline is 62%, a 5-point improvement meaningful. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 100 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 50 per group is two or three participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate through live analytics across the full user base and spending the 100 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 80 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Fifty per group cannot distinguish a five-point difference from chance. This tests redirecting to the right instrument — option D is the most damaging at organisational level, since caveated findings from underpowered comparisons get cited without their caveats, and an organisation that accepts one such comparison sets a precedent argued against for the rest of a tenure.

**Question 12 (harder) — A claim already in a board paper**

A board paper states: "Testing confirms 93% of users can complete the service independently," traced to a study of 43 participants (40 completed), all recruited through the departmental panel, all using a mouse; the board meets in four days. Ask for the most important response.

- A) Ask for the figure to be removed.
- B) Go to the author, explain that 43 panel-recruited mouse users cannot support a population rate — and that the mouse-only condition specifically excludes the modes where failure concentrates — propose the accurate statement, offer what the organisation does have if a rate is needed, and confirm the correction is made before the paper is tabled.
- C) Raise it at the board.
- D) Note it for the next assurance cycle.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A qualitative sample from a self-selected panel has become a population statistic informing a board decision, and the mouse-only condition compounds it by excluding the modes where failure concentrates. This tests going to the author with a specific replacement statement and confirming the correction lands — the step most often omitted, leaving an agreed correction never made.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies what a claim (a conformance statement, a benefits figure, a board metric) actually covers before treating it as contradicting other evidence.
- Note whether the candidate insists on disaggregation by mode of operation rather than accepting a single blended rate.
- Watch for whether the candidate reconstructs an implied unit value to sanity-check a business case's plausibility.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change and flags an ambiguous label.
- Note whether the candidate corrects a flawed figure already in a decision-facing document by going to the author with a specific replacement and confirming it lands, rather than raising it in the most public and least recoverable setting.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an average of rates that is not weighted by the underlying counts.
- Missing when a candidate treats a conformance claim and an audit as a straight contradiction rather than checking their scopes.
- Rewarding a qualitative sample converted into a population rate without challenge.
- Accepting an underpowered comparison endorsed "with caveats" as an adequate answer.
- Missing when a candidate raises a correction to a board paper at the board itself rather than with the author beforehand.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. At this level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, organisational standards, audit reports, cross-government guidance, technical specifications, board papers, and contracts — a misread exception clause becomes an organisation-wide compliance failure, and a misread audit finding becomes advice given confidently to a permanent secretary.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to mark True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, and expertise actively costs marks at this level. Small words carry the load — "all" is not "most", "consider whether" is not "recommend", "unless" and "provided that" govern everything around them.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**: guiding the organisation in using evidence.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: gaining support from senior leaders and communicating strategy across boundaries.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: ensuring design is included early enough in policy, technology and delivery processes.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**: noticing a framework never mentions a mode of operation has consequences at scale.
- **Reading cross-government prose accurately** maps to collaborating with counterparts across government.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Organisations must submit an annual design assurance return to the cross-government profession board, unless all design work within the organisation has been delivered under a centrally managed programme. Returns must confirm that accessibility assurance was completed where required, and must list any services where it was not. Where an organisation identifies a service released without required accessibility assurance, it must notify the board within ten working days of identifying it, separately from the annual return. Returns are published across government; notifications are not."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Every organisation must submit an annual design assurance return."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception for organisations where all design work was delivered centrally. This tests catching an absolute-term-versus-exception pattern — note the exception says "all", not "most", material for Question 3.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Notifications of missed accessibility assurance are published across government."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states returns are published and notifications are not. This tests reading past the point a sentence appeared to finish — the operative distinction sits after a semicolon, and governance prose places qualifications there with striking regularity.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "An organisation where nineteen of twenty services were delivered under a centrally managed programme must submit a return."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where *all* design work was delivered centrally; nineteen of twenty does not satisfy that. This tests carrying "all" from the first sentence forward and catching a recurring organisational error — partial satisfaction of an exception condition is non-compliance.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service released without required accessibility assurance must be notified within ten working days of release."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Notification is required within ten working days of *identifying* the service, not of release — the two triggers can be months apart. This tests establishing what starts the clock, since misreading a trigger produces either unnecessary alarm or false comfort when reported to a cross-government board.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The cross-government review of component libraries found considerable variation between departments. Five departments maintain a shared internal library; the remainder rely on team-level components. Departments with a shared library reported fewer accessibility defects reaching live, although the review did not examine whether their components were more accessible when tested independently. The review recommends that the profession board consider whether a common approach to component governance would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Some departments do not maintain a shared internal component library."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Five departments maintain one and the remainder rely on team-level components, establishing directly that some do not. A straightforward baseline.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Shared component libraries produce more accessible components."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer defects reaching live and states explicitly that whether components were more accessible when tested independently was not examined. This tests resisting a substitution of professional intuition for what the text supports, since a shared library might simply concentrate review rather than produce better components — and at this level such a substitution produces a cross-government recommendation resting on a finding a review declined to make.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The review recommends introducing common component governance."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the board "consider whether" a common approach would be proportionate, including concluding it would not be. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X."

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "Team-level components are a weaker approach than a shared library."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports one outcome difference while declining to assess component quality, making no judgement about relative strength. This tests noticing a document's implied stance (mentioned first, favourable association) is not the same as its assertions.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Design decisions must not be altered to reflect the preferences of stakeholders where doing so would misrepresent the evidence. Presentation may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. Where a designer is asked to alter a design rationale, the matter must be raised with the head of profession, who is accountable for the integrity of design outputs. Where design input cannot reach decision-makers before a decision is taken, the reason must be recorded; records are reviewed annually."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Design rationale may be presented differently to different audiences."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The second sentence permits adapting presentation provided substance is unchanged, and the statement claims only that different presentation is permitted. This tests recognising that a statement weaker than the passage's full provision is still True.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A designer asked to alter a design rationale must refuse."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage requires escalation to the head of profession, not personal refusal. This tests distinguishing a duty to escalate from a duty to refuse — the escalation route exists precisely so an individual is not left to refuse alone, and since the passage names this role as accountable, it is the protection this role is meant to provide.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "Altering a design rationale at a stakeholder's request always breaches the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The prohibition is conditional — "where doing so would misrepresent the evidence" — and presentation adaptation is expressly permitted. This tests reading a conditional prohibition as conditional rather than absolute; read as absolute, the clause makes every stakeholder edit suspect, and standards read as unworkable absolutes get ignored entirely.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Design rationales must never be changed.
- B) The substance of design reasoning must not be misrepresented to suit stakeholders, with adaptation permitted in form, escalation provided where independence is pressured, accountability located with the head of profession, and honesty required where timing fails.
- C) The head of profession is accountable for design integrity.
- D) Reasons must be recorded when design input arrives late.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary spanning the whole passage without inventing anything — A overstates the conditional prohibition into an absolute one, and C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads past the apparent end of a sentence, particularly after a semicolon, for an operative qualification.
- Note whether the candidate carries an absolute qualifier ("all", "entirely") forward to a scenario stated several lines later.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes a duty to escalate from a duty to personally refuse or comply.
- Score for whether the candidate reads a conditional prohibition as conditional rather than absolute.
- Note whether the candidate notices a document's implied stance is not the same as its assertions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False from the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate treats partial satisfaction of a compound or absolute exception as full satisfaction.
- Rewarding "consider whether X" read as "recommends X."
- Accepting fewer defects reaching live read as equivalent to more accessible components.
- Missing when a candidate reads a conditional prohibition as an absolute one, or vice versa.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. A head of interaction design is an expert practitioner who defines and assures best practice, influences design and organisational strategy, collaborates with counterparts across government, sets a vision for design across a whole organisation, supports a culture of learning and experimentation, and focuses on ensuring the right conditions for designers to work effectively.

Three features distinguish this level. Almost nothing a head of profession does is executed by them personally — an option solving a problem through personal intervention is usually second-best, since it does not survive their absence or scale past their calendar. Authority is largely informal, so options leading with instruction, mandate or escalation score poorly — not because escalation is wrong, but because it is capital spent rather than a tool used freely. And a head of profession absorbs pressure downwards — when a director leans on a designer, the designer should not be left holding that line.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Defining and assuring best practice** maps to this role level's core accountability.
- **Conditions and culture** map to **Leading design**: supporting a culture of learning and experimentation and improving design maturity.
- **Influence with senior leaders** maps to **Design communication** and **Designing strategically**: including design early enough to inform decisions.
- **Cross-government collaboration** maps to collaborating with counterparts and to **Designing together**.
- **Organisational inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**: guiding organisations to adopt inclusive practices.
- **Embedding evidence practice** maps to **Evidence-based design**: guiding the organisation in using evidence to improve services.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A designer under pressure from a director**

A lead designer reports that a director has asked them to remove an accessibility finding from a board summary because it "muddies the message." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell them to leave it in and cite your instruction.
- B) Agree with them how the finding should be presented, including mitigation; offer to attend; and take the request itself up with the director yourself, since a director asking for findings to be removed is a matter at your level.
- C) Contact the director and instruct them not to make such requests.
- D) Raise it with the permanent secretary's office.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the finding survives, and the lead designer emerges more capable and less exposed. This tests taking the request up with the director personally — a power differential this role exists to absorb. Option A is least effective, leaving them defending by proxy, teaching nothing.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard nobody follows**

An organisational component standard is widely ignored, described by designers as unrealistic. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Reissue it with a message from the candidate.
- B) Review recent components with designers to establish which requirements are genuinely unworkable and which merely unclear, revise it with them, and pair it with a lightweight routine — a keyboard and screen reader pass before merge — that gives compliance a moment of application and produces something designers and developers want anyway.
- C) Introduce mandatory compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the standard.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard everybody ignores has already failed. This tests revising with designers to produce authorship, paired with a routine giving compliance a moment of application. Option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act while changing only the date.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for designers**

Designers across the organisation report being pulled into delivery firefighting, no time for critique, rarely seeing each other; three have left in eight months. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Discuss workload with each designer's delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as structural: establish a protected routine bringing designers together, negotiate with delivery leadership for the time by showing what the current pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how designers are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Three departures alongside no critique and no professional contact is a structural pattern, and this role explicitly focuses on the conditions for designers. This tests addressing deployment directly, since components and pattern decisions made in isolation diverge and nobody notices for a year. Option D is least effective, reproducing the attrition on a lag in a small recruitment market.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate not managed by the candidate has shipped a custom component whose focus behaviour traps keyboard users, now in four services. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Advise the four services to stop using it.
- B) Speak to the designer and their lead first with a precise reproduction, agree how the fix will be made and communicated to the four services already using it, and set a clear next step if they decline.
- C) Raise it at the departmental board.
- D) Note it for the annual assurance return.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The relationship needs to survive years of continued assurance. This tests going to the people responsible first with a precise reproduction and communicating to affected services. Option D is least effective by a distance, an annual return being a record, not an intervention, while keyboard users cannot use four services today.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Non-compliance concentrated in one place**

Accessibility assurance was missed on four services, all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced designers. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Remediate the four, and separately investigate the assurance process — what it demands, how long it takes, whether a fast route exists — on the reasoning that experienced designers who know the requirement and miss it under pressure are telling you the process cannot be complied with when time is short.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-release gate for every service.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The concentration is the most informative feature. This tests handling both layers — remediation and process investigation. Option C is least effective, the weakest available response to any compliance failure.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want the candidate's design capacity in the same quarter — one has a failing live service, the other a ministerially visible new programme — both raised with the candidate's own director general. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what design work would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and the director general together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the ministerial programme.
- D) Ask the director general to decide.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests making a trade-off explicit and simultaneously visible to prevent parallel-negotiation dynamics, and making a recommendation rather than presenting neutral options. Option A is least effective, splitting constrained capacity producing two efforts too thin to inform either decision.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A technology decision already made**

A technology directorate has adopted a component library whose date picker, modal and custom select fail the standard; five services already use it. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Require them to stop using the library.
- B) Establish precisely which components fail and how, propose a shortlist to avoid or wrap with compliant alternatives, and a route to remediate the five existing services — framing it as making their choice workable rather than reversing it.
- C) Escalate to the director general.
- D) Accept it and document the risk.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A library is almost never uniformly bad. This tests converting an unwinnable argument into a tractable list. Option D is least effective, documenting a risk with nothing following it, how an exclusion becomes permanent while everyone feels it has been handled.

**Question 8 (harder) — Inclusion practice that is not improving**

After a year of guidance, templates and talks, components still routinely ship with keyboard defects. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Produce clearer guidance and repeat the training.
- B) Stop adding guidance and find out what is actually preventing it — most likely that nobody checks before merge, or that checking is manual and slow, or that defects are found only in an audit months later when fixing is expensive — then fix that blocker, most plausibly by putting a keyboard and screen reader pass into the routine where code is already reviewed.
- C) Introduce a mandatory accessibility checklist.
- D) Report the failure to the director general.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A year of guidance that has not changed practice is evidence the barrier is not knowledge. This tests diagnosing when and how checking happens and putting a check where work already flows. Option A is least effective, repeating an intervention that demonstrably did not work.

**Question 9 (harder) — An unowned problem**

Synthesising across services, the candidate concludes the organisation's largest interaction problem is inconsistency between services at the same moments — different controls, error behaviour, navigation — for users who move between them, and no director owns cross-service consistency. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Report it in the annual synthesis.
- B) Build the case with evidence from the affected services, quantify the affected population using contact centre and analytics data, identify who would need to own it, and take it to a forum with cross-service authority — accepting that securing an owner for an unowned problem is itself months of work.
- C) Raise it with each service owner separately.
- D) Propose research on cross-service journeys.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Unowned problems are the characteristic failure of service-shaped organisations. This tests recognising the hard part is ownership rather than insight, building a case a cross-service forum can act on. Option A is least effective, correct and filed, changing nothing.

**Question 10 (harder) — Cross-government collaboration**

Three departments are independently building the same complex address-entry component, each solving it differently, none confident about assistive technology behaviour; the candidate has no authority over the other two. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Focus on your own department's version.
- B) Convene the three departments' heads of design, establish whether the requirement is genuinely common, and if so propose one properly tested shared component or a joint contribution to the Design System — accepting that you are building consensus rather than exercising authority, and noting that one well-tested component is more achievable than three adequately tested ones.
- C) Ask the cross-government profession board to mandate a common component.
- D) Share your version with the other two for information.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three departments building one complex component three ways is the waste cross-government collaboration exists to prevent. This tests calibrating action to the authority actually held — convening is available, mandating is not. Option A is least effective, accepting solvable waste and leaving three inadequately tested components in public services.

**Question 11 (harder) — Assurance failure you approved**

A pattern approved nine months ago, now used in seven services, is found to assume errors appear on the same page as the field — breaking in the two services using it across multiple pages. Ask what should happen.

- A) Note it for the next pattern review.
- B) Notify all seven services now, scope precisely which two are affected and which five are not, propose a fix to the pattern and an interim workaround for the two, and examine why the approval did not test against a multi-page case — then change the approval process.
- C) Amend the pattern documentation quietly.
- D) Advise the two services to stop using the pattern.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A pattern in seven services means a flaw is potentially seven defects, and precise scoping prevents unnecessary alarm for the five unaffected. This tests notifying, scoping, fixing, and, distinctively at this level, changing the approval process itself. Option C is least effective, amending documentation without telling the two affected services, worse than silence because it will be cited as evidence the issue was handled.

**Question 12 (harder) — A standard that will be resisted**

The candidate intends to require every component entry to document its keyboard and assistive technology behaviour and known limitations, and anticipates resistance since teams cannot fully test assistive technology behaviour. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Introduce the requirement and handle objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is correct: comprehensive assistive technology testing is genuinely beyond most teams. Frame the requirement as documenting what was tested, with what, and what was not — so a truthful "tested with one screen reader on one browser; not tested with voice control" satisfies it — and pair it with access to a shared testing capability for what teams cannot cover.
- C) Introduce it as guidance rather than a requirement.
- D) Require it only for components used by more than one service.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The anticipated objection is true, and a standard ignoring it will be resisted and technically unreasonable at once. This tests changing what is required (documenting what was and was not tested) and pairing it with a shared testing capability. Option A is least effective, introducing a known-contestable requirement and arguing it case by case.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate changes a system, standard, or set of conditions rather than solving a problem through personal intervention alone.
- Note whether the candidate absorbs pressure downward rather than leaving a more junior colleague to argue alone.
- Watch for whether the candidate finds an owner for an unowned organisational problem, rather than filing it in a report where it changes nothing.
- Score for whether an own or approved flaw is disclosed promptly, with precise scoping and the underlying process changed so it cannot recur the same way.
- Note whether the candidate engages a standard's strongest true objection before introducing it, rather than introducing it and arguing case by case.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that reissues or restates a standard teams have already shown they will not follow.
- Missing when a candidate treats documenting a risk, with no action attached, as equivalent to managing it.
- Rewarding a candidate who solves a problem personally without building a mechanism that survives their attention.
- Accepting a candidate leaving a more junior colleague to hold a position under pressure the candidate should absorb.
- Missing when a candidate files a strategic insight rather than actively finding it an owner.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and systemic diagnosis across component standards, patterns, and cross-organisational evidence; the arithmetic that supports board-level design argument — disaggregating a blended rate, distinguishing a conformance claim's scope from an audit's, and reconstructing an implied unit value to check a business case; the discipline of reading governance frameworks, audit reports and board papers at exactly the strength the text supports, especially conditional prohibitions and deadline triggers; and the situational judgement that defines a strong head of interaction design — changing systems and conditions rather than acting through personal intervention, absorbing pressure downward, and finding an owner for a problem that belongs to no one.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answers consistently act on systems and conditions rather than instances. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
