# Trainee Data Scientist - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

Data science is a broad and fast-moving field spanning maths, statistics, software engineering and communications. Data scientists will often work as part of a multidisciplinary team, using data and analytics to inform and achieve organisational goals. A trainee data scientist is inquisitive, curious about data and keen to learn and develop.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it measures how well
someone does the thing, on the evidence in front of you, not how often they
do it or how they feel about it.

**Worked example — Applied maths, statistics and scientific practices.** Someone who freezes when shown a simple summary table and cannot say what
the average or the spread of the numbers tells you, and who has never
attempted to inspect or visualise a dataset, sits at **0 — None**. Someone
who, with prompting, can produce a basic set of summary statistics for a small
dataset and read off a correlation coefficient, but needs a colleague to check
whether their interpretation is right before it goes anywhere, sits at
**1 — Beginner**. Someone who works through the same task unprompted,
produces a simple chart alongside the statistics, correctly explains in plain
English what the numbers show, and can name at least one relevant accuracy
metric without being asked, sits at **2 — Intermediate**. Notice that the
difference between these points is not how confident someone seems — it is
whether the interpretation on the page is actually correct, and whether they
needed help getting there.

## About this role level

A **data scientist** works in a broad, fast-moving field spanning maths,
statistics, software engineering and communication. Data scientists are
inquisitive: they explore and visualise data, make recommendations to address
complex problems and inform strategic and operational decisions, use data
ethically and appropriately, and are innovative and adaptable. They explore
existing and new data using statistical tools and techniques such as machine
learning and predictive analytics, and they find patterns in data and turn
them into insight for their organisation. Data scientists typically work as
part of a multidisciplinary team.

This assessment covers the **trainee data scientist** — the first of six
levels in the data scientist ladder:

1. **Trainee data scientist** (this level)
2. Associate data scientist
3. Data scientist
4. Principal data scientist
5. Lead data scientist
6. Head of data science

A trainee data scientist is inquisitive, curious about data, and keen to learn
and develop. At this role level, the framework expects someone who:

- has a basic understanding of software development and analytical methods
- interprets statistical outputs effectively
- is aware of how data science techniques can be used, and has a basic
  knowledge of key techniques such as machine learning
- works as part of a multidisciplinary team to develop data science solutions
  and outputs
- prepares and manipulates data
- presents effectively
- is aware of ethical considerations
- is aware of the technologies used

The framework names eight skills for this level, covered in full in the
competency matrix below: Applied maths, statistics and scientific practices;
Data engineering; Data ethics and privacy; Data science innovation; Delivering
business impact; Developing data science capability; Programming and build
(data science); and Understanding product delivery.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Applied maths, statistics and scientific practices

**What the framework expects at this level**

The trainee shows an understanding of the benefits of applied mathematics and
statistics, and can use that knowledge to carry out data science tasks. They
carry out general analysis techniques for inspecting, exploring and
visualising data. They interpret statistical output effectively and
accurately. They show an awareness of different performance and accuracy
metrics used for statistical assessment and validation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own accountability list calls out
"interpret statistical outputs effectively" specifically for this level, so
some demonstrated ability is expected on day one — but the level's framing
throughout is basic technique performed with support, not independent
statistical judgement, which belongs to the level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what a mean, a median or a measure of spread tells
  you about a dataset, and has not attempted to inspect, explore or visualise
  data using any technique.
- 1 — Beginner: With guidance, produces a basic summary of a dataset (counts,
  averages, spread) and can read off a simple statistical output such as a
  correlation coefficient or a p-value, but needs someone to check the
  interpretation before it is used.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently produces basic descriptive statistics and a
  simple visualisation for a dataset, correctly explains what the numbers mean
  in plain English, and can name at least one accuracy or performance metric
  relevant to the task.
- 3 — Advanced: Chooses an appropriate analysis technique for a given question
  without being told which one, interprets the output accurately including its
  limitations, and can explain the trade-off between two different metrics to
  a non-technical colleague.
- 4 — Expert: Designs the statistical approach for a piece of analysis from
  first principles, spots subtle misinterpretations in someone else's
  statistical output, and coaches colleagues on which metric fits which
  problem.

**Evidence to gather**

- A short guided task: give you a small, anonymised dataset and try to produce two or three summary statistics and one simple chart, then
  talk through what each one shows.
- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you looked at a set
  of numbers and worked out what was going on. What did you check first, and
  how did you know your reading of it was right?"
- A document to review: any written analysis, coursework or exercise you have produced, checking whether your statistical claims match what
  the underlying numbers actually show.
- A scenario to observe: show two summary statistics for the same dataset that
  point in different directions (for example, a mean and a median that differ
  noticeably) and ask what that difference suggests.

### Skill: Data engineering

**What the framework expects at this level**

The trainee can explain why they need engineering support to design and
provide products for the organisation. They can explain why data needs
cleansing and preparing before it is used in data science products. They can
explain different architectures, including cloud and on-premise, and different
data manipulation and transformation tools.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 0 — None. Every bullet the framework lists for this skill at this
level is about explaining a need, not performing engineering work, and the
level's own accountabilities only ask the trainee to be "aware of the
technologies used". A trainee who arrives with no prior exposure to data
architecture or engineering concepts is still within what this level expects;
being able to give a confident, accurate explanation of why engineering
support matters is itself the beginner behaviour, and moves someone to 1.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why raw data usually needs cleaning before it is used,
  and has no working sense of the difference between a cloud and an
  on-premise setup.
- 1 — Beginner: Can give a simple, mostly correct account of why data needs
  cleansing and preparing, and knows that different storage and processing
  architectures exist, but the explanation needs correcting or filling out by
  someone more experienced.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains, unprompted and accurately, why a data science
  product needs engineering support, what "cleansing and preparing" data
  actually involves, and can name at least one cloud and one on-premise option
  along with a data transformation tool.
- 3 — Advanced: Can talk through the trade-offs between two architectural
  choices for a given piece of work, and knows enough to raise the right
  questions with an engineering colleague rather than simply asking for help.
- 4 — Expert: Advises others on which architecture and which data preparation
  approach suits a given problem, and is a point of reference the team turns
  to when scoping engineering support.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Why might data need to be cleaned before
  you can use it in a project? Can you give me an example from something
  you've worked on or studied?"
- A short scenario: describe a simple data source (a spreadsheet export, a
  set of sensor readings) and ask what preparation it would likely need before
  it could be analysed, and why.
- A document to review: any coursework, project write-up or portfolio note
  that touches on where data came from or how it was stored.
- Something to ask a referee: whether you have asked sensible
  questions of engineering colleagues rather than treating data preparation as
  someone else's problem to ignore.

### Skill: Data ethics and privacy

**What the framework expects at this level**

The trainee explains the importance of using data ethics and privacy
appropriately in their work, and identifies appropriate channels to discuss
ethical issues, with support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The accountability is explanatory and support-seeking
rather than independent judgement — the framework's own level description says
the trainee is "aware of ethical considerations" — but it is a named
requirement, so some demonstrated understanding is expected from the start
rather than developed later.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not considered that data work raises ethical or privacy
  questions, and would not know who to ask if something felt wrong with a
  piece of data or a task.
- 1 — Beginner: Can say, in general terms, why data ethics and privacy matter,
  and knows they should raise a concern with someone senior, but needs
  prompting to notice an ethical issue in a specific piece of work.
- 2 — Intermediate: Notices, unprompted, when a task touches personal or
  sensitive data or raises a fairness question, explains clearly why it
  matters, and knows the right channel to raise it through without being told.
- 3 — Advanced: Raises ethical or privacy concerns proactively and early in a
  piece of work, weighs up more than one consideration (for example, utility
  against privacy), and helps colleagues think the issue through.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes how the team handles data ethics and privacy day to day,
  and is trusted to make the initial call on a genuinely ambiguous case.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Have you ever come across a piece of data
  work that felt like it raised a privacy or fairness question? What did you
  do about it, or what would you do?"
- A short scenario: describe a simple dataset that includes personal details
  (for example, customer records) and ask what you would check before using
  it, and who you would ask if unsure.
- A document to review: any note, write-up or reflection you have
  produced that touches on data protection, consent or fairness.
- Something to ask a referee: whether you have, in practice, flagged
  a concern rather than working around it quietly.

### Skill: Data science innovation

**What the framework expects at this level**

The trainee adopts an inquisitive and curious approach to data. They are
receptive to learning about data science and its techniques. They are unafraid
to ask questions and to discover what there is to learn.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This skill is not a technical competency to be
built up gradually — it is the defining disposition of the level itself: the
framework's own one-line description of a trainee data scientist is someone
"inquisitive, curious about data and keen to learn and develop". A trainee who
is not yet visibly curious and willing to ask questions is missing something
central to the level, not something they are still building towards.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Waits to be told what to do, rarely asks a question about data or
  a technique, and shows no sign of exploring beyond the task in front of
  them.
- 1 — Beginner: Asks occasional questions when prompted or when clearly stuck,
  but does not follow up on interesting things they notice in the data or the
  work.
- 2 — Intermediate: Regularly asks questions about data, techniques or
  decisions without being prompted, follows up on something unexpected they
  spot in a dataset, and can point to something they taught themselves
  recently.
- 3 — Advanced: Actively seeks out new techniques and ideas beyond what the
  immediate task requires, and shares what they learn with the rest of the
  team unprompted.
- 4 — Expert: Sets a visible standard of curiosity that others in the team
  pick up, and is someone colleagues bring an unusual or puzzling dataset
  to first.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about something you got curious
  about recently, in data or otherwise, and followed up on your own."
- Direct observation: during a guided exploration task, note whether you asks questions about the data unprompted or only answers what is
  put directly in front of you.
- A document to review: a CV, portfolio or personal project that shows
  self-directed exploration rather than only coursework.
- Something to ask a referee: whether you asks questions in team
  settings, or tends to stay quiet even when something seems worth asking
  about.

### Skill: Delivering business impact

**What the framework expects at this level**

The trainee shows an understanding of the organisation and the benefits data
science brings to it. They develop data science products as part of a team.
They create basic visuals and presentations to communicate data science
effectively. They show knowledge of data and analysis and can discuss how to
align them to meet user needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The bullets describe contributing to team output and
producing basic communication materials, not owning impact independently —
consistent with the level accountability that the trainee "presents
effectively" as part of a wider team effort, under support.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what their organisation uses data science for, and has
  not produced a chart, table or short write-up to communicate a finding.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe, in simple terms, why the organisation values
  data science, contributes small pieces to a team product under close
  direction, and produces basic charts or slides with help tidying them up.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes a defined piece of a team's data science
  product with limited direction, produces clear basic visuals and a short
  presentation unaided, and can explain in a discussion how a piece of
  analysis connects to what users actually need.
- 3 — Advanced: Shapes a small piece of a product's direction based on what
  they have found in the data, and presents findings persuasively to a mixed
  technical and non-technical audience.
- 4 — Expert: Is trusted to identify where data science can create impact
  before being asked, and their presentations are used as the model others
  are pointed to.

**Evidence to gather**

- A work sample: ask you to produce a short (one or two slide)
  summary of a simple dataset aimed at a non-technical reader.
- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a piece of analysis or a
  project you worked on. Who was it for, and how did you explain what you'd
  found to you?"
- A document to review: any presentation, poster or written summary you have produced.
- A scenario to observe: describe a simple user need (for example, "the team
  wants to know which service pages people give up on") and ask how you
  would start turning that into something you could show someone.

### Skill: Developing data science capability

**What the framework expects at this level**

The trainee shows an awareness of various sources of training and
self-directed learning for data science. They create a continuous professional
development (CPD) plan with support from their manager. They demonstrate a
basic understanding of key data science techniques, such as machine learning,
and how they can be used.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Every part of this skill is explicitly supported at
this level — "with support from your manager" — so the framework itself sets
the expectation at foundational rather than independent, matching the level
accountability of having "a basic knowledge" of key techniques.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has no CPD plan and cannot name a source of data science training
  or learning, and shows no sign of thinking about their own development.
- 1 — Beginner: Names at least one or two ways to learn data science (a
  course, a book, a community), and has started a CPD plan with their
  manager's help, though it needs prompting to keep it current.
- 2 — Intermediate: Maintains their own CPD plan with only light input from
  their manager, actively uses more than one learning source, and can give a
  basic, broadly accurate explanation of a technique such as machine learning
  and when it might be used.
- 3 — Advanced: Seeks out learning ahead of what the current task needs,
  brings back what they have learned and applies it to real work, and can
  explain more than one technique clearly enough for a colleague to follow.
- 4 — Expert: Actively helps other trainees find good learning sources and
  build their own CPD plans.

**Evidence to gather**

- A document to review: any CPD plan or personal development log you keeps, even an early or informal one.
- A structured interview question: "How have you been building your data
  science knowledge? What have you learned recently, and where did that come
  from?"
- A short scenario: ask you to explain, in plain terms, what machine
  learning is and give one example of when it might be used.
- Something to ask a referee: whether you have followed through on
  learning goals set with a manager or tutor, rather than letting you lapse.

### Skill: Programming and build (data science)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The trainee shows a basic understanding of software development principles
and can write simple scripts under supervision.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording — "basic understanding"
and "under supervision" — states the bar directly, and this matches the level
accountability of having "a basic understanding of software development and
analytical methods".

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never written a script or piece of code to process or explore
  data, even with help.
- 1 — Beginner: With supervision, writes a simple script to load, filter or
  summarise a dataset, though it needs review before use and basic concepts
  like version control or testing are unfamiliar.
- 2 — Intermediate: Writes a simple script unaided, follows a team's basic
  coding conventions when shown them, and can fix a straightforward error in
  their own code without help.
- 3 — Advanced: Writes small, reasonably well-structured scripts
  independently, uses version control as a matter of course, and can read and
  adapt someone else's simple script.
- 4 — Expert: Writes robust, reusable code for data science tasks and reviews
  other people's scripts constructively.

**Evidence to gather**

- A short scripted exercise: ask you to write a small script (in a
  language of your choice) that loads a simple dataset and produces one
  summary output, working alongside you or reviewing it afterwards.
- A structured interview question: "Tell me about the last time you wrote a
  script or some code to work with data. What did it do, and did anyone check
  it over?"
- A document to review: any code sample, notebook or repository you
  can show, even from coursework or a personal project.
- Direct observation: watch how you approaches a small coding task —
  whether you check your own output, and how you respond when something
  does not work first time.

### Skill: Understanding product delivery

**What the framework expects at this level**

The trainee manages their own contribution to tasks so that it fits in with
the work of their wider team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The expectation is narrow and self-focused — fitting
one's own contribution around the team's work — rather than understanding or
influencing how the product as a whole gets delivered, which belongs to later
levels.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Works on their own tasks without reference to what the rest of the
  team is doing, and misses deadlines or hand-offs that affect other people's
  work.
- 1 — Beginner: With reminders, adjusts their own work to fit around the
  team's plan and lets others know when something is going to be late.
- 2 — Intermediate: Keeps their own tasks broadly aligned with the team's plan
  without needing reminders, and flags early, unprompted, when something might
  affect someone else's work.
- 3 — Advanced: Actively coordinates their contribution with what the rest of
  the team needs, adjusting their own priorities when the team's plan
  changes.
- 4 — Expert: Helps shape how the team sequences its work, not just their own
  part of it.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a time your part of a task
  depended on someone else's, or theirs on yours. How did you keep things
  lined up?"
- A scenario to observe: during a team exercise, note whether you
  checks in about dependencies or works in isolation until asked.
- Something to ask a referee: whether you reliably lets the team
  know about delays or changes to your own work in good time.
- A document to review: any evidence of planning or task tracking you have contributed to, such as a shared task board or sprint note.
