# Technical Writer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A technical writer takes a user-centred approach to making complicated technical concepts easier to understand for a specialist audience. In this role, you will focus on specialist content and software documentation, and write for technologists such as developers, technical architects, and technology leaders. A technical writer is an expert practitioner with a deep understanding of technology and the needs of technologists.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This rates how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available — not how often
they do it. A worked example, using this level's skill of User-centred content design:

- **3 — Advanced**: they independently identified where a content journey needed
  fixing, made the fix, and reviewed their own content on a regular cycle.
- **4 — Expert**: they took responsibility for assuring the quality of content written
  by more junior colleagues, coached one of them through a specific improvement, and
  encouraged a continuous improvement mindset across the team as a result.

A technical writer's baseline for this skill, below, is 4.

## About this role level

**The role: technical writer.** A technical writer takes a user-centred approach to
making complicated technical concepts easier to understand for a specialist audience.
The role focuses on specialist content and software documentation, writing for
technologists such as developers, technical architects and technology leaders.

**The level: technical writer.** The framework describes this level as an expert
practitioner with a deep understanding of technology and the needs of technologists.
It is the first of two levels the framework publishes for this role, below lead
technical writer. At this level, you take a user-centred approach to explaining how to
use government products and services to a technical audience, focusing on specialist
content and software documentation; write blog posts for or on behalf of the technical
community; and engage with and contribute to the cross-government technical writing
and content design communities.

**The skills this level names**, in the summary's order: Agile working; Prototyping;
Stakeholder relationship management; Strategic thinking; Technical understanding
(user-centred design); User focus; and User-centred content design. Four of these —
Agile working, Prototyping, Strategic thinking, Technical understanding (user-centred
design), and User focus — carry text identical to the lead technical writer level
above; Stakeholder relationship management and User-centred content design are worded
differently at the level above, and that is where the real step up between the two
levels of this role sits.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a technical writer to identify and compare the best processes or
delivery methods to use, including measuring and evaluating outcomes; help the team
decide the best approach; and help teams manage and visualise outcomes, prioritise
work, and adhere to an agreed minimum viable product (MVP), priorities and scope.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Identifying and comparing delivery methods, and helping a team
decide and manage its approach, is independent, judgement-driven contribution to how a
team works, consistent with an "expert practitioner" baseline for this entry-to-the-
role level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not compared delivery methods for a piece of technical writing work.
- 1 — Beginner: has followed a delivery process someone else chose.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped a team evaluate one delivery approach, with guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: independently identifies and compares delivery methods, measures and
  evaluates outcomes, and helps a team manage its outcomes, priorities and scope
  against an agreed MVP.
- 4 — Expert: is someone a team turns to when its agreed way of working is not
  producing the outcomes it needs.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about comparing two delivery approaches
  for a documentation project and recommending one to the team."
- Ask for an example of helping a team keep to an agreed scope when pressure built to
  expand it.
- Ask a referee whether you have shaped how a team measured or visualised its
  delivery outcomes.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a technical writer to understand when to use a specific prototyping
technique or method, and to show the value of prototyping to the team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's own wording here — "understand" and "show
the value" — is lighter than the "identify", "direct" and "lead" language used for this
level's other skills, so this is the one skill in the matrix where the honest baseline
sits below Advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not used a prototyping technique in documentation work.
- 1 — Beginner: has followed a prototyping technique someone else chose for a
  documentation deliverable.
- 2 — Intermediate: understands when to use a specific prototyping technique, such as a
  draft documentation structure or a sample API reference, and has shown a team the
  value of prototyping before committing to a full piece of content.
- 3 — Advanced: independently chooses between more than one prototyping technique for
  documentation and advises others on the choice.
- 4 — Expert: is someone other writers ask which prototyping technique fits an
  unusual documentation problem.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you prototyped a piece of
  documentation, such as a draft structure or a sample reference, before writing it in
  full. Why that approach?"
- Ask for an example of showing a team the value of prototyping a piece of content
  before committing to it.
- Ask for an example prototype document, if available.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a technical writer to work with the team to develop and maintain an
understanding of stakeholders; work with the team to develop and implement stakeholder
communications strategies; identify and resolve issues, influence stakeholders and
manage relationships effectively; and build long-term strategic relationships,
communicating clearly and regularly with stakeholders.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Resolving issues, influencing stakeholders, and building
long-term strategic relationships is independent relationship management, though the
"work with the team" framing keeps this below the fully directive language used at the
lead technical writer level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not identified or resolved an issue with a stakeholder independently.
- 1 — Beginner: has attended a stakeholder conversation led by someone more senior.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped develop an understanding of one stakeholder, with
  support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently works with the team to understand and communicate with
  stakeholders, identifies and resolves issues, influences stakeholders, and builds a
  long-term strategic relationship with at least one.
- 4 — Expert: directs the stakeholder relationship strategy for a team, not just their
  own relationships.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about identifying and resolving an issue
  with a stakeholder that could have derailed a documentation project."
- Ask for an example of a long-term stakeholder relationship you built and how you
  maintained it.
- Ask a referee about your influence over a difficult stakeholder
  relationship.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a technical writer to lead the design and implementation of
strategy, directing the evaluation of strategies and policies to ensure business
requirements are being met.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Lead the design and implementation" and "directing the
evaluation" are explicit leading-and-directing, mastery-level expectations, and this
wording is identical at the lead technical writer level above — the framework holds
both levels of this role to the same high bar for this specific skill.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not led the design of a strategy or directed its evaluation.
- 1 — Beginner: has contributed to a strategy that someone else led.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped evaluate one strategy or policy against business
  requirements, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: has led part of the design of a strategy independently, without yet
  directing its full evaluation.
- 4 — Expert: leads the design and implementation of a strategy, and directs the
  evaluation of strategies or policies to check business requirements are being met.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about leading the design of a strategy —
  for documentation, content, or how technical writing supports a wider goal."
- Ask for an example of directing the evaluation of a strategy or policy against
  business requirements.
- Look back at a strategy document you led, if available.

### Skill: Technical understanding (user-centred design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a technical writer to demonstrate knowledge of the technologies
used to build and operate digital services, and to collaborate closely with colleagues
in different digital disciplines.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Demonstrating knowledge of the technologies behind a digital
service, and collaborating closely across digital disciplines, is independent
technical grounding appropriate to writing credibly for developers, technical
architects and technology leaders. This wording is identical at the lead technical
writer level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot demonstrate knowledge of the technologies behind a digital service
  they have written about.
- 1 — Beginner: has a surface-level understanding of one technology, gained secondhand.
- 2 — Intermediate: demonstrates knowledge of one technology used to build or operate a
  service, with some support from technical colleagues.
- 3 — Advanced: independently demonstrates knowledge of the technologies used to build
  and operate a digital service, and collaborates closely with colleagues across
  different digital disciplines to check accuracy.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted by technical colleagues to write documentation without close
  review, because their technical understanding is not in doubt.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about documenting a technology you had to
  learn from scratch. How did you build enough understanding to write about it
  credibly?"
- Ask for a piece of documentation that demonstrates technical depth, and try to
  explain a technical decision it describes.
- Ask a referee — ideally a developer or architect — whether you trusted your technical understanding.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a technical writer to give direction on which tools or methods to
use; demonstrate experience meeting the needs of users across a variety of channels;
bring insight and expertise in how user needs have changed over time to ensure they are
met by the business; and apply strategic thinking to provide the best service for the
end user.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Give direction on which tools or methods to use" is
teach-and-guide-others, mastery-level ownership, and this wording is identical at the
lead technical writer level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not given direction on tools or methods to meet a user need.
- 1 — Beginner: has followed direction someone else gave on which tool or method to
  use.
- 2 — Intermediate: has met a user need across one channel, with support choosing the
  method.
- 3 — Advanced: independently meets user needs across a variety of channels, and
  brings insight into how those needs have changed over time.
- 4 — Expert: gives other writers direction on which tools or methods to use, and
  applies strategic thinking to shape the best overall service for the end user.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you directed which tool or
  method a colleague should use to meet a user need, and why."
- Ask for an example of how user needs for your technical audience changed over time,
  and how you adapted.
- Ask a referee whether you have shaped a strategic decision about the
  overall service for a technical audience.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a technical writer to show a deep understanding of end-to-end
journeys and how content is affected within them; identify where journey fixes or
content improvements need to be made; take responsibility for assuring the quality of
content from more junior colleagues, coaching and guiding them to improve; ensure that
content is regularly reviewed and evaluated, contributing to continuous improvement and
iteration; encourage a continuous improvement mindset in teams and more junior content
colleagues; and deliver through others, directing and critiquing their work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Assuring the quality of more junior colleagues' content, coaching
them, and delivering through others by directing and critiquing their work, is
explicit teach-and-guide-others, mastery-level ownership.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not assured the quality of another colleague's content.
- 1 — Beginner: has received a review of their own content from someone more senior.
- 2 — Intermediate: has identified one journey fix or content improvement, with
  support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently shows a deep understanding of end-to-end journeys,
  identifies where fixes are needed, and reviews their own content on a regular cycle.
- 4 — Expert: takes responsibility for assuring the quality of content from more
  junior colleagues, coaches and guides them, encourages a continuous improvement
  mindset in the team, and delivers through others by directing and critiquing their
  work.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about coaching a more junior colleague to
  improve a specific piece of content. What did you change, and why?"
- Ask for an example of identifying a content improvement across an end-to-end
  journey, not just one page or document.
- Ask a referee about your role in reviewing or critiquing other people's
  content.
