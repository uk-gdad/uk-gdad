# Junior DevOps Engineer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

Commonly referred to as ‘DevOps engineers’, development operations engineers support the development and operation of software through tools, environments and practices. A junior DevOps engineer learns on the job by building software components.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example — Systems design.** At Beginner (1), someone could
describe having watched the team design part of a larger system, without
having contributed themselves. At Intermediate (2) — this level's baseline —
they can describe assisting, as part of a team, in designing a component of a
larger system: naming the component, what it needed to do, and how their
contribution fitted into the wider design. At Advanced (3), above this
level's baseline, they could describe producing a design largely on their
own, which is more independence than a junior engineer is expected to show.

## About this role level

Development operations (DevOps) engineers — commonly called "DevOps
engineers" — support the development and operation of software through tools,
environments and practices. The role is responsible for underpinning good
development processes, including managing tools and testing environments,
central code control, maintaining development standards, and writing software
that automates systems.

This assessment covers the **Junior DevOps engineer** level, the second step
of the role's ladder. The full ladder, in order, is: Apprentice DevOps
engineer, Junior DevOps engineer (this level), DevOps engineer, Senior DevOps
engineer (technical and management track), Lead DevOps engineer (technical
and management), and Principal DevOps engineer (technical and management).

A junior DevOps engineer learns on the job by building software components.
At this level, a person is expected to:

- assist in the building of a complex story
- work under supervision
- be involved in implementation but not planning

This level names nine skills: Availability and capacity management,
Information security, Modern development standards, Programming and build
(software engineering), Prototyping, Service support, Systems design, Systems
integration, and User focus.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior DevOps engineer is expected to be able to explain availability and
capacity management processes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The wording matches the apprentice level's, and this
level's own accountabilities keep someone working under supervision, so
explanatory understanding remains the reasonable expectation.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe what availability or capacity management covers.
- 1 — Beginner: can explain what availability and capacity management
  processes are for, in their own words.
- 2 — Intermediate: can describe how a specific service they have worked near
  is monitored for availability or capacity, and why that monitoring matters.
- 3 — Advanced: not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd explain why a team tracks a service's capacity before
  it becomes a problem.
- Ask about a service you have been near — even briefly — and what you
  noticed about how its availability was tracked.

### Skill: Information security

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior DevOps engineer is expected to use information security practices
and available security controls to contribute to protecting solutions and
services.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is a step up from the apprentice level's
"explain" wording to actually using practices and controls, matching the
junior's move into building software components, albeit still under
supervision.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot name or use a basic security control on a task they are
  given.
- 1 — Beginner: can name a security control relevant to their work but has
  not yet applied one themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: has applied an existing security control (for example,
  using a secrets manager instead of hard-coding a credential) while building
  a component, under supervision.
- 3 — Advanced: independently spots where a security control is missing from
  a piece of work and raises it, without being prompted.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a security control you have applied while
  building something, and what it protected against.
- Give a short code sample with an obvious security gap (such as a
  hard-coded password) and ask what you would do about it.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior DevOps engineer is expected to explain the most important principles
of modern development standards and how they apply to their work, and to
apply those principles under guidance.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework asks for both explanation and
guided application, which sits above pure explanation and matches "knows the
basics and can perform tasks with some guidance."

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot name a development standard their team follows.
- 1 — Beginner: can explain a development standard in general terms but has
  not applied it themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: has applied a specific standard (such as a commit message
  convention or a code style rule) to their own work, with guidance available.
- 3 — Advanced: applies standards consistently without needing to be
  reminded, and can explain why each one exists.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Look back at a piece of code or a commit history you have produced, and
  check whether it follows a stated standard.
- Think about how you'd explain, for one standard you follow, what problem it
  prevents.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior DevOps engineer is expected to design, code, test, correct and
document simple programs or scripts under the direction of others.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is a step up from the apprentice level's
"basic knowledge" to actually producing simple programs or scripts, matching
the level's "assist in the building of a complex story" accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot produce a simple script or program even with direction.
- 1 — Beginner: can follow step-by-step direction to write a very small piece
  of code, with heavy support.
- 2 — Intermediate: can design, code, test, correct and document a simple
  script or program under general direction, needing occasional guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: can do the same work with minimal direction, and helps
  correct a peer's simple mistakes.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Set a small, well-scoped coding task and observe your process:
  do you test what you wrote, and can you explain what it does?
- Ask to see a simple script or program you built recently, and walk through
  how you tested and documented it.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior DevOps engineer is expected to understand when to use a specific
prototyping technique or method, and to show the value of prototyping to
their team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework still asks for understanding and
demonstrating value rather than running prototyping activities, so the
baseline stays close to the apprentice level's.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot say when a prototype would help versus building the real
  thing straight away.
- 1 — Beginner: can identify, for a simple example, whether a quick prototype
  would help before committing to a full build.
- 2 — Intermediate: has explained to a colleague or in a team discussion why
  a specific prototype was worth building, and been understood.
- 3 — Advanced: not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give a short scenario (a new feature idea) and ask whether you would
  prototype it first, and why.
- Ask about a time you helped a team decide to build a rough version of
  something before the real one.

### Skill: Service support

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior DevOps engineer is expected to help fix service faults following
agreed procedures, and to carry out maintenance tasks on service support
infrastructure.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is more independent than the apprentice's
"help under direction" — following agreed procedures without needing each
step spelled out — while still short of leading an investigation.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot follow an agreed fault-fixing procedure even when given
  one.
- 1 — Beginner: can follow an agreed procedure step by step with someone
  checking their work as they go.
- 2 — Intermediate: can follow an agreed procedure to help fix a fault, and
  carry out routine maintenance tasks, with only occasional check-ins.
- 3 — Advanced: adapts a procedure sensibly when a fault does not quite match
  the documented steps, and flags this to the team.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd walk through a real fault you helped fix, focusing
  on which agreed procedure you followed.
- Ask a referee whether you carries out routine maintenance tasks
  reliably without close supervision.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior DevOps engineer is expected to assist, as part of a team, in the
design of components of larger systems.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. "Assist... as part of a team" is a supporting
contribution, not an independent one, so the baseline sits at the low end of
the scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not contributed to designing any part of a system, even
  alongside others.
- 1 — Beginner: has taken part in a team discussion about designing a system
  component, contributing a small idea or observation.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped produce part of a component's design (for
  example, sketching how two parts connect) under a more senior person's
  direction.
- 3 — Advanced: not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a system component you helped design, and
  what specifically you contributed.
- Ask a referee whether you contributes useful ideas in design
  discussions, even in a supporting role.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior DevOps engineer is expected to explain the process and principles of
integrating systems, and to describe the challenges of designing, building
and testing interfaces between systems.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Explanation and description are asked for, not
hands-on integration work, matching this level's implementation-not-planning
accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot explain what it means for two systems to be integrated.
- 1 — Beginner: can explain, in general terms, what systems integration
  involves and why interfaces between systems are tricky to get right.
- 2 — Intermediate: can describe a specific integration challenge they have
  seen (for example, two systems disagreeing on a data format) and what made
  it hard.
- 3 — Advanced: not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd explain, using a simple example, why connecting two
  systems together is harder than it sounds.
- Ask about any integration work you have observed, and what challenge stood
  out to you.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior DevOps engineer is expected to show an awareness or understanding of
user experience analysis and its principles, and to explain the purpose of
user stories and the focus on user needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Awareness and explanation are asked for, not
conducting research or writing user stories independently.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot explain why a team writes user stories or considers user
  needs before building something.
- 1 — Beginner: can explain, in simple terms, what a user story is for and
  why user needs matter to the work.
- 2 — Intermediate: can point to a specific user need that shaped a piece of
  work they were involved in, and explain the connection.
- 3 — Advanced: not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd explain, in your own words, what a user story is and
  why your team uses you.
- Ask about a piece of work you built, and whether you can connect it back
  to a user need.
