# Lead developer (management) - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A software developer designs, runs and improves software that meets user needs. A lead developer leads development on one or a small number of related project teams.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale.
This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well someone does the thing,
on the evidence available — not a frequency scale, and it is not about how
often they do it or how they feel about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**A worked example**, using Development process optimisation, one of the skills
this level names:

- **2 — Intermediate**: someone can describe, when asked, a process
  problem their team had and a change that was tried, but the change was
  proposed and driven by someone else — a tech lead or a delivery manager —
  and they were one contributor among several.
- **3 — Advanced**: someone can describe a process inefficiency they
  identified themselves, a change they implemented, and a measurable result
  (fewer rollbacks, a shorter lead time), but the change was scoped to their
  own team.
- **4 — Expert**: someone can describe setting a policy or standard that
  other teams adopted, leading a small group of experts through implementing
  it, and evaluating whether it delivered the intended service improvement —
  evidence at programme, not team, scale.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs — writing clean, secure code following
a test-driven approach, and creating code that is open by default and easy for
others to reuse.

**This level: lead developer (management).** The framework describes a lead
developer as someone who leads development on one or a small number of related
project teams. At this role level, you will:

- guide the ways in which the team works
- be proficient in a wide range of technical systems
- be involved in identifying appropriate technology and approaches, deciding
  when software should be written
- contribute to the developer community
- provide technical leadership, coaching and mentoring your team
- promote knowledge sharing and adoption of good practice

**This is the management track.** The framework defines two roles at this
seniority — a technical one and a management one — and this document assesses
the management track. Its sibling, the technical track, is
[Lead developer](6-lead-developer.md), assessed in a separate document at the
same level in the ladder. The two share every accountability above; where they
differ is in three of the ten skills, set out below.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. Senior developer
5. Senior developer (management)
6. Lead developer
7. **Lead developer (management)** — you are here
8. Principal developer
9. Principal developer (management)

**How this level's skill wording differs from the technical track.** Two
skills read more senior here than at the technical Lead developer level:
Availability and capacity management moves from managing service components to
ensuring correct implementation of standards and initiating remedial action,
and Development process optimisation moves from working under guidance to
analysing current processes, leading a team of experts, and setting policy and
standards. One skill reads *less* senior here than at the technical track:
Programming and build stays at the "collaborate... use the agreed
specifications... medium-to-high complexity" wording seen at developer and
senior developer level, rather than the technical lead developer's "maintain
technical responsibility for all stages... set team-based standards". This is
what the framework's own text says at this level — it is not smoothed into a
clean progression here, and the relevant skill block below says so plainly.

**The skills this document assesses**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to ensure availability and capacity management
standards and procedures are correctly implemented across the services your
team runs, to identify capacity issues before or as they arise, to implement
whatever changes those issues require, and to initiate remedial action rather
than waiting for it to be requested.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is markedly more accountable wording than "manage
service components to ensure they meet business needs and KPIs" — it is about
owning the standard, not just meeting a target set by someone else, and
initiating action rather than following a runbook. A lead should be able to do
this independently across a small number of teams; a baseline of 4 would ask
for evidence of doing it at a scale this level's accountabilities do not yet
claim.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never been responsible for whether a service meets its
  availability or capacity targets.
- 1 — Beginner: can describe what an availability or capacity standard is, but
  has only followed one written by someone else, under close supervision.
- 2 — Intermediate: has monitored capacity for a service and escalated a
  problem to someone else to fix, but has not owned the fix.
- 3 — Advanced: has identified a capacity issue independently, implemented the
  required change, and can describe how they confirmed it actually resolved
  the issue.
- 4 — Expert: has written or substantially revised an availability or capacity
  standard that other teams now follow, and coaches other leads on
  implementing it correctly.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific example of a capacity issue you identified yourself,
  what you did, and how you knew it worked.
- Ask to see an availability or capacity standard or runbook you wrote or
  materially updated.
- In a structured interview, present a scenario — a service is intermittently
  breaching its error budget — and try to talk through how you would
  diagnose and remediate it.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to analyse your team's current development
processes, identify and implement opportunities to optimise them, lead and
develop a team of experts to deliver service improvements, and help evaluate
and establish requirements for implementing changes by setting policy and
standards.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This wording is unambiguously senior: analysing
processes, leading a team of experts, and setting policy and standards are all
things done independently and taught to others, not things done under
guidance. A lead in the management track should be expected to meet this in
full.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never analysed or changed a development process beyond
  following what their team already does.
- 1 — Beginner: can point to a small suggestion they made about a process,
  implemented by someone else.
- 2 — Intermediate: has implemented a process change proposed by someone else,
  with support.
- 3 — Advanced: has independently analysed a process problem, proposed and
  implemented a fix, and measured whether it worked.
- 4 — Expert: has set a policy or standard for how a process should work, led a
  small group of people through implementing it, and can describe how they
  evaluated the requirements for the change before committing to it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a process problem you analysed yourself, what
  policy or standard you set, and who else adopted it.
- Ask to see a document — a process standard, a retrospective write-up, an
  improvement proposal — you authored or led.
- Ask a referee what changed about how the team worked because of this
  person, specifically.

### Skill: Information security

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to design solutions and services with security
controls built in, specifically engineered to mitigate the security threats
relevant to what you are building.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The wording is the same as at developer and senior
developer level — designing controls in, rather than leading others in
security practice — so a lead in the management track should meet it
independently but the wording does not itself claim expert-level, teach-others
mastery.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never designed a security control into a solution; treats
  security as someone else's concern.
- 1 — Beginner: can name common threats relevant to their team's work, but has
  not designed a mitigation themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: has implemented a security control someone else designed.
- 3 — Advanced: has independently designed a security control into a solution,
  specifically engineered against a named threat, and can explain the
  trade-off it made.
- 4 — Expert: is sought out by other teams to review the security design of
  their solutions, and has changed how their organisation approaches a class of
  threat.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific solution you designed, what threat it was engineered
  against, and what trade-off you made.
- Ask to review a design document or architecture decision record with a
  security section you wrote.
- In a structured interview, present a scenario with a plausible security gap
  and try to identify it and propose a control.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to identify and apply modern development standards
to support complex projects and programmes, and to lead others in applying
them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Lead others in applying modern development standards"
is explicit teach-and-set-direction language, matching what a lead in the
management track should be expected to do across their team.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: does not know what "modern development standards" means for their
  technology area.
- 1 — Beginner: can name a standard, but has only applied one someone else
  selected.
- 2 — Intermediate: applies standards to their own work reliably, with
  occasional guidance on which to choose.
- 3 — Advanced: independently identifies which standards a complex piece of
  work needs and applies them without support.
- 4 — Expert: has introduced a standard to their team or a wider group, and can
  describe how they coached someone else through adopting it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a standard you introduced to your team, and how
  you got it adopted.
- Ask to see a coding standard, style guide, or technical RFC you authored.
- Ask a referee whether this person is the one others go to for "how should
  we be doing this" questions.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to collaborate with others when necessary to review
specifications, and to use agreed specifications to design, code, test and
document programs or scripts of medium-to-high complexity, using the right
standards and tools.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is the anomaly flagged in `## About this role
level`: the wording here is the same as developer and senior developer level —
independent delivery against agreed specifications — not the technical lead
developer's "maintain technical responsibility for all stages... set
team-based standards". A baseline of 4 would be asking for more than this
level's own text supports; 3 reflects genuinely independent, reliable delivery
without over-crediting a leadership dimension the framework does not claim
here.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot deliver a program or script from an agreed specification
  without significant support.
- 1 — Beginner: delivers small pieces of a specification under close
  direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: delivers medium-complexity work against a specification
  with some guidance on approach.
- 3 — Advanced: independently designs, codes, tests and documents work of
  medium-to-high complexity from an agreed specification, using the right
  standards and tools without prompting.
- 4 — Expert: takes technical responsibility for a whole development project
  end to end and sets team-based standards for how others build — a step
  beyond what this level's own text claims, useful context if evidence shows
  it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a work sample: a piece of medium-to-high complexity code you built
  from a specification, and walk through the design choices.
- Think about how you'd review a specification and flag gaps or ambiguities before
  building against it.
- Ask a referee whether this person's delivered code needs significant rework
  after review.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to approach prototyping as a team activity, actively
soliciting prototypes and testing them with others, to establish design
patterns and iterate them, and to use a variety of prototyping methods,
choosing the most appropriate for the situation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Establish design patterns and iterate them" is
independent, standard-setting practice within a team, without yet claiming the
organisation-wide "share best practice and coach others" language seen at
principal level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never run or contributed meaningfully to a prototyping
  exercise.
- 1 — Beginner: has taken part in a prototyping exercise someone else ran.
- 2 — Intermediate: has run a simple prototype with support on method choice.
- 3 — Advanced: independently chooses the right prototyping method for a
  problem, runs it as a team activity, and establishes a design pattern from
  the result.
- 4 — Expert: coaches other teams on which prototyping method suits their
  problem, and has established a pattern now reused beyond their own team.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a prototype you ran, why you chose that method, and
  what design pattern came out of it.
- Ask to see an artefact from a prototyping exercise you led — sketches, a
  spike, a design pattern document.
- In a structured interview, present a new, ambiguous problem and ask which
  prototyping method you would choose and why.

### Skill: Service support

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to identify, locate and fix service faults.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is short, plain wording, matched consistently
across senior and lead levels — the expectation is independent, reliable fault
resolution, not yet the coach-others dimension the framework reserves for
other skills at this level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never diagnosed or fixed a service fault unsupervised.
- 1 — Beginner: fixes faults only when told exactly what to do.
- 2 — Intermediate: fixes straightforward faults with some support on
  diagnosis.
- 3 — Advanced: independently identifies, locates and fixes faults across a
  service they know well, including ones with an unclear root cause.
- 4 — Expert: is someone other teams call in for the hardest faults, and has
  changed how the team diagnoses faults going forward.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a fault you diagnosed and fixed without being told
  what was wrong.
- Ask to see an incident record or postmortem you wrote.
- Ask a referee how often this person needs help locating the root cause of a
  fault.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to design systems characterised by medium levels of
risk, impact, and business or technical complexity; to select appropriate
design standards, methods and tools and ensure they are applied effectively;
and to review the systems designs of others to ensure appropriate technology
choice, efficient use of resources, and integration across multiple systems and
technologies.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Review the systems designs of others" is a genuine
step up from designing alone — it asks for judgement about someone else's
work, which fits a lead reviewing their team's designs, without yet claiming
the "control system design practice within an enterprise architecture" scope
seen at principal level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not designed a system beyond a small, low-risk component.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes to a system design under close direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: designs a system of managed complexity with support on
  standard and tool choice.
- 3 — Advanced: independently designs systems of medium risk and complexity,
  selects the right standards and tools, and reviews a colleague's design for
  technology choice and resource use.
- 4 — Expert: sets the design standards a wider group of teams uses, and their
  reviews materially change how other leads approach systems design.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a system design of medium complexity you produced, and the
  standards and tools you chose and why.
- Ask to see written feedback you gave on a colleague's systems design.
- In a structured interview, present a design with a resourcing inefficiency
  and try to review it.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to define the integration build, co-ordinate build
activities across systems, and understand how to undertake and support
integration testing activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Co-ordinate build activities across systems" implies
working across more than one system or team boundary independently, without
yet claiming a coach-others or organisation-wide dimension.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not defined or co-ordinated an integration build.
- 1 — Beginner: has taken part in an integration build defined by someone
  else.
- 2 — Intermediate: co-ordinates a straightforward integration with support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently defines the integration build for a piece of
  work spanning multiple systems, and co-ordinates the activity across them.
- 4 — Expert: has designed the integration approach for a programme spanning
  many systems and teams, and other leads follow the pattern they set.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of an integration build you defined and co-ordinated
  across more than one team or system.
- Ask to see an integration test plan or build definition you wrote.
- Ask a referee how much of the co-ordination on a cross-system piece of work
  was actually driven by this person.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to collaborate with user researchers and represent
users internally, explain the difference between user needs and the desires of
the user, champion user research to focus on all users, prioritise and define
approaches to understanding the user story while guiding others in doing so,
and offer recommendations on the best tools and methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Guiding others" is the marker here — this level is
expected not just to practise user focus well but to help the rest of the team
do it, which fits a lead's coaching accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never engaged with user research findings in their work.
- 1 — Beginner: reads user research someone else produced, but does not act on
  it independently.
- 2 — Intermediate: uses user research to inform their own work, with support
  distinguishing needs from wants.
- 3 — Advanced: independently represents user needs in technical decisions,
  and has coached a colleague on distinguishing a user need from a stated
  desire.
- 4 — Expert: champions user research across teams beyond their own, and has
  changed how a wider group approaches understanding the user story.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a technical decision you changed because of user
  research, and how you explained that to a colleague.
- Ask to see a case where you distinguished a stated user desire from an
  underlying need, in your own words.
- Ask a referee whether this person coaches others on user focus, or only
  practises it yourself.
