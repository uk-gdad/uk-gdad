# Lead Product Manager - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A product manager makes sure that their products provide value and achieve the right outcomes by balancing user and business needs. A lead product manager is responsible for a product portfolio or end-to-end service, and the delivery of product quality and value. They work with teams to identify, implement and improve strategies and tactics for their products. They are a member of the management team for their product area and provide product leadership and direction across multiple teams.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill below is rated on the same five-point scale. This is a
**proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale — it measures how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example, using Agile and Lean practices.** Someone rated
**2 — Intermediate** can run a well-formed sprint or Kanban cycle for one
team using a template someone else designed, but struggles to explain why
the approach fits this particular product's risk profile. Someone rated
**3 — Advanced** designs and adapts the working approach for their own team,
justifies the trade-offs, and changes tack when a retrospective shows the
current approach is not working. Someone rated **4 — Expert** does all
of that across several teams at once, is sought out by other product
managers for advice on which approach to use, and has visibly introduced a
new way of working that other teams then adopted. Look for the evidence at
the level you are scoring, not the title someone happens to hold.

## About this role level

A **product manager** makes sure that their products provide value and
achieve the right outcomes by balancing user and business needs. In this
role, a product manager works with multidisciplinary teams, bringing
together their views, user needs and business needs, to frame the problem;
works with multidisciplinary teams to provide the right outcomes for users
and the organisation; makes decisions on priorities for their teams; and
ensures teams comply with appropriate standards.

The role has five levels, run in order of seniority:

1. Associate product manager
2. Product manager
3. Senior product manager
4. **Lead product manager — you are assessing this level**
5. Head of product

A **lead product manager** is responsible for a product portfolio or an
end-to-end service, and for the delivery of product quality and value across
it. They work with teams to identify, implement and improve strategies and
tactics for their products, sit as a member of the management team for their
product area, and provide product leadership and direction across multiple
teams. At this role level, a lead product manager will:

- manage people and manage products
- maintain the high-level roadmap
- be involved in a range of programme management activities relevant to
  their product portfolio
- be involved in recruiting product managers at all levels and contractors
- provide leadership in their programmes and across the local community,
  working closely with the head of product, where one exists

A lead product manager is assessed against nine skills, in this order:
Agile and Lean practices, Applying user-centred insights, Creating value for
money, Life cycle management, Managing product outcomes, Product
leadership, Product management, Stakeholder relationship management, and
Strategic ownership.

Six of these nine skills — Agile and Lean practices, Applying user-centred
insights, Life cycle management, Product management, Stakeholder
relationship management, and Strategic ownership — carry the same published
wording at head of product, the level above. That does not mean the
baseline is the same: a head of product carries that skill across the whole
product community and represents it across government, where a lead product
manager carries it across a portfolio and a set of programmes. The other
three skills — Creating value for money, Managing product outcomes, and
Product leadership — are worded differently at head of product, reflecting
a step up in scope from portfolio ownership to organisation-wide and
cross-government responsibility.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile and Lean practices

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead product manager coaches and leads teams in Agile and Lean good
practice, creates and tailors the right working approach for a team, and
challenges, evaluates and iterates that approach through the product's life
cycle. They experiment with new and innovative ways of working to improve
delivery across the organisation, and act as a recognised expert and
advocate for Agile and Lean approaches.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. A lead product manager is expected to tailor and
coach an approach across several teams in their portfolio and to keep
adjusting it as the work changes; that is independent, portfolio-level
practice, one step below the organisation-wide, sought-after expertise that
head of product's identical wording implies at that level's larger scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not led a team's choice of working approach; can describe
  Agile or Lean terms but has not applied them to real delivery.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a working approach someone else set up, attending
  ceremonies and using the artefacts without adapting them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Runs a standard Agile or Lean cycle for one team
  competently, but leans on a template and needs support to change it when
  it stops fitting.
- 3 — Advanced: Designs and adapts the working approach for one or more
  teams in their portfolio, explains the trade-offs behind it, and changes
  it visibly in response to a retrospective or a delivery problem.
- 4 — Expert: Is sought out by other product managers for advice on working
  approach, has introduced a new way of working that spread beyond their own
  teams, and is recognised across the organisation as an authority on
  applying Agile and Lean thinking to product delivery.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd walk through a time you changed a team's working
  approach mid-cycle, and what evidence told you it needed to change.
- Look back at a retrospective output or a working agreement you wrote or
  significantly reshaped.
- Ask a referee whether other product managers have come to this person for
  advice on ways of working, and for a specific example.

### Skill: Applying user-centred insights

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead product manager advocates for a range of effective research
approaches across the organisation, coaches others in making decisions that
meet user needs across a range of channels, advocates for the continuous use
of user insights in teams, and uses user insights to make strategic
decisions that provide the best user experience.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This level asks for independent, cross-team use of
user insight to shape strategic product decisions across a portfolio, which
is a strong, independent grasp of the skill rather than the organisation-wide
advocacy role head of product's identical wording implies at that larger
scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not used user research to inform a product decision;
  relies solely on stakeholder opinion or assumption.
- 1 — Beginner: Reads research findings when given them, but does not seek
  them out or question a decision that ignores them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Asks for research before a decision on their own
  product, but does not yet coach other teams to do the same.
- 3 — Advanced: Coaches product managers on other teams in their portfolio
  to use research and insight properly, and has changed a strategic product
  decision because the evidence pointed the other way.
- 4 — Expert: Is the organisation's go-to advocate for user-centred
  decision making, has shaped how research is commissioned or used
  organisation-wide, and other senior leaders cite their judgement on user
  evidence.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a strategic product decision you changed after
  seeing user research that contradicted the original plan.
- Look back at a roadmap or business case and try to point to where user
  insight shaped a specific choice.
- Ask a referee for an example of you coaching another product manager on
  using research well.

### Skill: Creating value for money

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead product manager advocates for user needs while balancing user value
against cost and value for the organisation. They determine the benefit of a
product and persuade others it is the right product to pursue, write or
contribute to business cases, communicate business-value propositions, and
help others make value-based decisions that influence the direction of
development.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The level's own wording is about persuading others
and contributing to business cases within a portfolio — independent,
credible practice — rather than the ownership of a business case across its
whole life cycle and organisation-wide value strategy that head of
product's distinct wording at that level describes.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not written or contributed to a business case, and cannot
  explain how a product's cost relates to its value.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe a product's costs and benefits when asked, but
  has not built the argument themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes sections to a business case with support,
  and can explain value for money for their own product with guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Writes or substantially shapes a business case
  independently, persuades sceptical stakeholders that a product is the
  right investment, and helps other product managers frame value-based
  arguments.
- 4 — Expert: Owns a business case across its full life cycle, builds the
  organisational case for a value-for-money approach to product strategy,
  and is trusted to make the call when value and cost genuinely conflict.

**Evidence to gather**

- Look back at a business case or value proposition you wrote or led,
  and ask what you cut and why.
- Think about how you'd describe a time you persuaded a sceptical senior stakeholder
  that a product was worth the investment, and what evidence you used.
- Ask a referee for an example of you helping another product manager make
  a value-based prioritisation call.

### Skill: Life cycle management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead product manager leads and coaches teams through different phases of
the product life cycle, ensures effective support for the product or service
and its users, predicts, prevents, mitigates and manages potential risks at
different phases of that life cycle, and contributes to the assessment of
other teams, providing guidance and support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Leading and coaching teams through the life cycle
and managing its risks across a portfolio is strong, independent practice; a
shade below the whole-community coaching remit that head of product's
identical wording carries at organisation and cross-government scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not led a product through a full life cycle phase change,
  such as moving from discovery into live.
- 1 — Beginner: Understands the phases of the life cycle but has followed
  someone else's decisions about when to move between them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Manages the life cycle of their own product with
  support, spotting some risks but needing help to prioritise them.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads several teams through life cycle transitions,
  anticipates and mitigates risk before it lands, and formally contributes
  to another team's service assessment.
- 4 — Expert: Is the reference point other leads bring life cycle risk
  decisions to, and has changed how the organisation runs a phase gate or
  assessment because of what they identified.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a product you took through a life cycle transition (for
  example, beta to live) and what risk you caught before it became a
  problem.
- Review notes or an outcome from a service assessment you contributed to.
- Ask a referee whether this person is asked to advise other teams on life
  cycle risk, and for a specific instance.

### Skill: Managing product outcomes

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead product manager leads product teams to create effective methods for
measuring product outcomes, advocates for data-driven decision making,
helps others prioritise to make better choices, uses data to drive
continuous improvement in quality and user experience, and identifies
opportunities to share and connect product data.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This level's wording is about leading teams within a
portfolio to build measurement methods and helping others prioritise with
data — strong, independent leadership — rather than head of product's
distinct, wider wording about influencing senior stakeholders and
connecting data across government.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not defined how success would be measured for a product
  they owned.
- 1 — Beginner: Reports metrics someone else set up, without shaping what
  is measured.
- 2 — Intermediate: Defines outcome measures for their own product with
  support, and uses them occasionally to justify a decision.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads several teams to build shared, effective outcome
  measures, helps other product managers use data to prioritise, and has
  changed a product's direction because the data showed it should.
- 4 — Expert: Has built an organisation-wide approach to measuring product
  outcomes that other portfolios now use, and is someone senior
  colleagues ask to sanity-check a data-driven prioritisation call.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe an outcome measurement framework you built
  or led for more than one product or team.
- Look back at a dashboard, metric set, or outcome report you created or
  commissioned.
- Ask a referee for an example of this person helping another team
  prioritise using data rather than opinion.

### Skill: Product leadership

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead product manager seeks opportunities for reuse and learning across
their organisation, represents product management in their area in
conversations with senior stakeholders, protects teams from unnecessary
distractions and requests to change direction, coaches and manages others in
product management, and leads some product management community activities
and participates in cross-government communities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This level is a member of the management team for its
product area and is explicitly accountable for people management, senior
stakeholder representation, and community leadership — full mastery, though
scoped to a local area and community rather than head of product's
organisation-wide co-ordination of the whole profession.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not represented product management to a senior stakeholder
  or protected a team from an unhelpful change of direction.
- 1 — Beginner: Attends senior stakeholder conversations but does not yet
  speak for product management or shield the team from noise.
- 2 — Intermediate: Represents their own product to senior stakeholders
  with support, and occasionally pushes back on a distracting request.
- 3 — Advanced: Reliably represents the product area to senior
  stakeholders, coaches one or two other product managers, and has visibly
  protected a team from a disruptive change of direction.
- 4 — Expert: Manages and coaches other product managers as part of their
  job, leads local community activities, is trusted by senior stakeholders
  to speak for the whole product area, and takes part in cross-government
  community work.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of protecting a team from an unhelpful request or
  distraction, and what the consequence would have been if you had not.
- Ask a referee — ideally someone you coach or manage — what coaching from
  this person actually looks like.
- Review evidence of participation in a cross-government or cross-organisation
  product community, such as a talk, a working group, or a shared write-up.

### Skill: Product management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead product manager sets the strategy and vision for their products in
collaboration with others, creates a prioritised roadmap and uses it to
discuss product strategy at all levels, plans prioritised work across
multiple teams while minimising dependencies and risk, supports multiple
teams in delivering quality outcomes the right way, and coaches and mentors
others in product management.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Setting strategy for a portfolio, planning across
multiple teams, and coaching others is the core of the role at this level —
full, independent mastery — even though the scope is a portfolio rather than
head of product's whole-organisation remit under the same published
wording.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not set a product strategy or roadmap that other teams
  worked to.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a roadmap someone else set, without shaping the
  strategy behind it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Builds a roadmap for their own product with support,
  and can explain its priorities when asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Sets strategy and a prioritised roadmap across more than
  one team independently, and manages dependencies between them without
  much support.
- 4 — Expert: Sets and defends strategy for a whole portfolio, plans
  prioritised work across multiple teams while actively minimising
  dependency risk, and coaches and mentors other product managers as a
  routine part of the job.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd walk through a portfolio-level roadmap you built,
  including a dependency you identified and managed between teams.
- Think about how you'd describe a strategy discussion at a senior level and how you
  defended a prioritisation call.
- Ask a referee you have coached or mentored what specifically changed in
  your practice as a result.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead product manager directs the stakeholder relationship strategy for
their teams, ensures stakeholders' objectives are set and supports teams in
meeting them, and influences and negotiates with senior stakeholders to
resolve issues and enable progress.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Directing a stakeholder strategy and personally
negotiating with senior stakeholders to unblock progress is full, mastery
level practice at this level, scoped to the lead's own portfolio and
programmes rather than head of product's identical wording carried across
the entire organisation.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not negotiated directly with a senior stakeholder to
  resolve a blocking issue.
- 1 — Beginner: Attends stakeholder meetings but relies on someone more
  senior to do the negotiating.
- 2 — Intermediate: Manages day-to-day stakeholder relationships for their
  own product with occasional support at a senior escalation.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently negotiates with senior stakeholders to
  resolve a specific issue, and sets clear objectives for a stakeholder
  relationship.
- 4 — Expert: Directs the stakeholder relationship strategy across a
  portfolio, is someone other product managers bring a stuck senior
  relationship to, and has a track record of resolving genuinely
  contentious stakeholder disagreements.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific example of negotiating with a senior stakeholder to
  unblock a stalled decision, and what the resolution was.
- If you can, ask a manager, mentor or colleague to describe this person's reputation with senior
  stakeholders across the portfolio.
- Look back at a stakeholder map or engagement plan you authored.

### Skill: Strategic ownership

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead product manager supports and coaches others in creating and
implementing a successful long-term strategy and tactical approach that
others agree with, influences and persuades stakeholders to support
delivery of the strategy, supports strategic decision making, and ensures
strategic alignment across the organisation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The wording at this level is about supporting and
coaching others toward strategy, and influencing stakeholders to back it —
strong, independent contribution to strategy rather than sole ownership of
it, which keeps this one shade below the level's most senior-facing skills
even though the wording is identical at head of product.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not contributed to or supported a long-term product
  strategy beyond their own team's immediate plan.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe the organisation's strategy but has not
  helped shape or defend it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Supports a strategy someone else set, occasionally
  helping persuade a stakeholder to back a specific tactic.
- 3 — Advanced: Coaches another product manager through building a
  long-term strategy, has personally persuaded a resistant stakeholder to
  support it, and checks alignment between their portfolio's strategy and
  the wider organisation's.
- 4 — Expert: Is someone others bring a stalled or contested strategy
  to, and has visibly shaped strategic alignment beyond their own
  portfolio.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of coaching someone else through building a strategy,
  and what changed in the final version because of that coaching.
- Ask about a time a stakeholder resisted a strategic direction and how it
  was resolved.
- Look back at a strategy document or roadmap and ask how it was checked against
  the wider organisation's direction.
