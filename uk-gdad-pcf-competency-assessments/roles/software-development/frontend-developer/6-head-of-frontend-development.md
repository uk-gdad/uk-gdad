# Frontend developer (Head of frontend development) - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel, or a reviewer —
judging whether a person meets the baseline abilities the UK Government
Digital and Data (GDAD) Profession Capability Framework sets for a **head of
frontend development**, the top of the frontend developer ladder. It measures
one person against the framework's own description of this role level: what a
head of frontend development is accountable for, and what the framework's
named skills mean at this level of seniority.

Use it when hiring for a head of frontend development post, when running a
promotion board into the role, when checking a serving head of frontend
development's capability against the current framework, or as one part of
onboarding. It is one input among several into that process, never the whole
of it — see `## Advisory` below for why, and read it before you use this
document for anything that affects a real person's employment.

## Advisory

> This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
> educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
> not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
> form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
> disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
> resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
> colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so the accountabilities
of a head of frontend development are fresh in mind before you look at any
evidence. Then gather whatever evidence exists before you score anything: a
CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task, direct
observation of the person leading or influencing others, and references —
particularly references that speak to organisational or cross-government
work, which is hard to observe directly inside a single interview.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess — a guessed
rating at this level is more damaging than a gap, because a head of frontend
development post carries organisation-wide influence.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for the same post the same way, and
where you can, have more than one assessor score independently before
comparing notes and discussing any disagreement — this matters most for a
hiring or promotion decision, and it is the single most effective thing an
assessor can do to reduce the risk described next.

**Stay fair.** This is an AI-drafted, unvalidated instrument. Used
inconsistently, or by a single assessor relying on impression rather than
recorded evidence, an instrument like this can produce unlawful adverse
impact — treating people differently in a way that tracks a protected
characteristic rather than job performance, even unintentionally. Consistent,
evidence-based administration by more than one assessor materially reduces
that risk. See [Fair and lawful use](#fair-and-lawful-use) in the project
specification for what this means in practice: rate only demonstrated,
job-related behaviour, never compare one candidate against another, and never
let anything about a protected characteristic, health, or personal
circumstance enter a rating or a note.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same five-point
scale. It is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale — it rates how
well the person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they
do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using the skill *Community collaboration*, which this role
level names:

- **Intermediate (2)** evidence would look like a person who has helped
  resolve a disagreement between two developers on their own team, with a
  manager's support, but has not yet been asked to intervene between teams.
- **Advanced (3)** evidence would look like a person who, unprompted,
  identified and resolved a recurring source of friction between two teams
  sharing a codebase, and whose intervention held once they stepped back.
- **Expert (4)** evidence would look like a person a director calls in
  specifically to unblock a standoff between departments, who is then asked to
  run a short session teaching other senior leaders how they read and
  defused the situation, so the organisation's capacity to do this grows
  beyond the one intervention.

## About this role level

A **frontend developer** designs, builds and improves website software that
meets user needs. In this role, a frontend developer writes clean, accessible
code following a progressive enhancement approach; creates code that is open
by default and easy for others to reuse; designs software which meets user
needs and creates meaningful interactions and relationships with users; and
understands the three fundamental frontend technologies — HTML, CSS, and
JavaScript.

The frontend developer role has six levels, run in order from least to most
senior:

1. Apprentice frontend developer
2. Junior frontend developer
3. Frontend developer
4. Senior frontend developer
5. Lead frontend developer
6. **Head of frontend development** — the top of the ladder, assessed here

A **head of frontend development** is an expert practitioner with broad
industry experience — expert in many technical areas, or a specialist with
very deep knowledge in a particular technical area. At this role level, a head
of frontend development defines and assures best practice while influencing,
leading, and mentoring others; influences and develops organisational
strategy and priorities, collaborating with counterpart colleagues across
government; ensures the right conditions and environment for frontend
developers to work effectively; and develops departmental capability by
facilitating internal mobility, shaping career paths, and recruiting talent.
This is the top of the role's ladder: there is no level above it to defer to,
so a head of frontend development is expected to set the standard rather than
follow one set by someone more senior in the role.

The framework names the following skills for this role level, in this order:

1. Accessibility
2. Communicating information
3. Community collaboration
4. Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)
5. Modern development standards
6. Programming and build (frontend developer)
7. Prototyping
8. Strategic thinking
9. Systems integration (frontend developer)
10. User focus (frontend developer)
11. Web performance optimisation

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Accessibility

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development shows a strong understanding of accessibility
and is an advocate for users with accessibility needs. They build user
interfaces that meet a predefined set of standards, champion accessibility to
internal and external stakeholders, offer design solutions to mitigate the
risk of failing accessibility testing, and recommend the best tools and
methods to use when accessibility testing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This is the top of the ladder, and the skill's own
wording moves past building accessible interfaces into championing
accessibility to stakeholders and recommending tools and methods — advocacy
and organisational influence that only make sense once a person has already
demonstrated full mastery of the underlying practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how their organisation's accessibility standard
  applies to frontend work, and has no record of raising an accessibility
  issue.
- 1 — Beginner: Can name common accessibility issues in an interface when
  shown one, but needs someone else to identify the fix and needs close
  support to apply it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Runs standard accessibility checks against a predefined
  standard on their own work, and fixes what they find, but has not yet
  advised anyone else's team on accessibility.
- 3 — Advanced: Reviews another team's interface against the organisation's
  accessibility standard unprompted, proposes a design fix rather than only
  flagging the failure, and the fix is adopted.
- 4 — Expert: Is the person internal and external stakeholders are brought in
  to speak to when accessibility risk threatens a programme's timeline or
  reputation, and has changed which tools or methods the organisation uses for
  accessibility testing based on their recommendation.
- 5 — Expert-plus: N/A — this scale has five points, 0 through 4.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific example where the candidate changed an organisation's
  accessibility testing tooling or method, and what evidence they used to make
  that case.
- Present a scenario where a delivery deadline is at risk because of an
  accessibility failure discovered late, and observe how they balance the risk
  against the deadline in their answer.
- Ask a referee whether external stakeholders — a supplier, another
  department, an assurance body — have sought this person out specifically on
  accessibility.

- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Communicating information

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development mediates between people and mends
relationships, communicating with stakeholders at all levels. They manage
stakeholder expectations and host or moderate discussions about high risk and
complexity, even within constrained timescales, and they speak on behalf of
and represent the community to large audiences inside and outside government.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Hosting or moderating a high-risk, high-complexity
discussion under time pressure, and speaking for a community to a large
audience beyond the organisation, both require full command of the skill
under conditions where a lesser grasp would be visible immediately.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Struggles to summarise a technical position clearly even to their
  own team, and has no record of communicating outside it.
- 1 — Beginner: Can present prepared material to a small, familiar audience,
  but needs support to handle questions or disagreement in the room.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains a technical decision to a non-technical
  stakeholder clearly, one-to-one or in a small meeting, with some coaching
  beforehand.
- 3 — Advanced: Runs a stakeholder meeting on a contested topic unaided,
  manages the disagreement in the room, and the meeting ends with a shared
  understanding of next steps.
- 4 — Expert: Is asked to moderate a discussion between senior stakeholders in
  active disagreement, under a tight deadline, and has represented the
  frontend developer community to an audience outside their own department or
  outside government.
- 5 — Expert-plus: N/A — this scale has five points, 0 through 4.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of mending a relationship between stakeholders that had
  broken down, and what the person specifically did to repair it.
- Ask to see, or ask a referee about, a talk, panel or written piece where the
  candidate represented the frontend developer community to an external
  audience.
- Present a short scenario describing two senior stakeholders in disagreement
  under time pressure and ask the candidate to talk through how they would
  moderate it.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development solves and unblocks issues between teams or
departments at the highest level. They coach the organisation on team
dynamics and conflict resolution, while also building and growing the
community.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Solve and unblock issues... at the highest level" and
"coach the organisation" are both explicit descriptions of organisation-wide
mastery and of teaching others, which is the definition this scale gives for
Expert.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not been involved in resolving a disagreement beyond their
  immediate team, and cannot describe what a healthy frontend developer
  community looks like in their organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Takes part in a team-level conflict resolution conversation
  when asked, but does not lead it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Helps resolve friction within their own team without
  escalation, with some support from their manager.
- 3 — Advanced: Resolves a standing disagreement between two teams sharing
  work, unprompted, and it stays resolved once they step back.
- 4 — Expert: Is brought in specifically to unblock a standoff between
  departments, and has run sessions coaching other leaders on team dynamics or
  conflict resolution, growing the organisation's own capability rather than
  just fixing the one problem.
- 5 — Expert-plus: N/A — this scale has five points, 0 through 4.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific example of an issue between two teams or two departments
  that the candidate resolved, and what made it stick.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has coached other leaders — not just
  their own reports — on team dynamics or conflict resolution.
- Observe the candidate in, or ask them to describe, a community-building
  activity they started or grew, such as a cross-department frontend guild or
  community of practice.

### Skill: Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development changes organisational structures to fixable
and sustainable designs, and leads on strategy for the whole organisation,
marrying business needs with innovative analysis. They make and justify
decisions characterised by high levels of risk, impact and complexity, build
consensus between organisations — private or public — or highly independent
stakeholders, and solve and unblock issues between teams or departments at the
highest level. They understand the psychology of a team, have strong
mediation skills, and coach the organisation on team dynamics and conflict
resolution.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This is the most explicitly organisation-wide skill the
framework names for the role, covering strategy for the whole organisation,
consensus-building between organisations, and coaching — there is no reading
of this wording that sits below full mastery.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never led a change to how a team or organisation is
  structured, and cannot describe a time they influenced strategy beyond their
  own work.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes ideas to a strategy discussion when invited, but
  does not yet own a strategic recommendation.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads a small, contained change — restructuring one team's
  working practices, for example — with guidance from a more senior leader.
- 3 — Advanced: Proposes and delivers a structural change affecting more than
  one team, working independently, and can justify a high-risk decision they
  made with clear reasoning.
- 4 — Expert: Has led strategy that reshaped how frontend development works
  across a whole organisation, built consensus between two organisations or
  highly independent stakeholders who started from different positions, and
  can point to a specific instance of coaching the organisation on team
  dynamics or conflict.
- 5 — Expert-plus: N/A — this scale has five points, 0 through 4.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a structural change the candidate led that affected
  more than one team, including what did not work the first time.
- Ask the candidate to describe the highest-risk decision they have made in
  this area, and how they justified it to people who disagreed.
- Ask a referee about a time the candidate built consensus between two
  organisations, or between stakeholders who started a long way apart.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development identifies and applies modern development
standards to support complex projects and programmes, and leads others in
applying modern development standards.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The skill's own wording is bounded to applying
standards to programmes and directing others in applying them, which is
narrower and more delivery-focused than the organisation-wide strategic remit
that anchors most of this role level's other skills. A head of frontend
development is expected to have a strong, independent grasp of this and to
direct others in it, but the wording stops short of the explicit
organisation-wide teaching or strategy-setting language that justifies an
Expert baseline elsewhere in this matrix — Advanced is the more defensible
baseline, even though many people at this level will in practice operate
above it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name the modern development standards their organisation
  currently expects frontend work to follow.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows modern development standards on their own work when
  told which ones apply, but does not yet judge which standard fits a new
  situation.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses the right standard for a piece of work themselves,
  with occasional checking-in from a more senior colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Applies modern development standards independently across a
  complex project or programme, and has directed another developer or team in
  adopting a standard correctly.
- 4 — Expert: Has set which modern development standards a programme or the
  organisation adopts, and taught other senior developers to apply and lead
  the same standards themselves.
- 5 — Expert-plus: N/A — this scale has five points, 0 through 4.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a modern development standard the candidate
  introduced or changed on a complex programme, and what problem it solved.
- Present a scenario with two competing standards in tension — for example,
  speed of delivery against a stricter standard — and ask how they would
  decide, and who they would involve.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has led other developers, not just
  their own work, in adopting a new standard.

### Skill: Programming and build (frontend developer)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development advises on the right way to apply standards
and methods to ensure compliance, provides technical advice to stakeholders,
and sets team-based standards for programming tools and techniques. They
collaborate with others when required to review specifications, and use
specifications to build, test, and document user interfaces of high
complexity, using the appropriate standards and tools.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Setting team-based standards for tools and techniques,
and providing technical advice to stakeholders, are both descriptions of
directing and teaching others — the framework still expects hands-on building
of high-complexity interfaces at this level, but frames it alongside advising
and standard-setting, which together sit at full mastery.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot build a working user interface from a specification
  without significant help.
- 1 — Beginner: Builds a simple user interface from a clear specification
  with close supervision, and needs help interpreting anything ambiguous.
- 2 — Intermediate: Builds, tests, and documents a moderately complex
  interface from a specification independently, with review from a more
  senior colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Builds, tests, and documents a high-complexity user interface
  unaided, and reviews specifications with others before build starts.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the team-based standards for programming tools and
  techniques that other developers are expected to follow, and is the person
  stakeholders come to for technical advice on whether a proposed approach
  will actually work.
- 5 — Expert-plus: N/A — this scale has five points, 0 through 4.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a work sample of a high-complexity user interface the candidate
  built, and ask them to walk through a technical decision in it they would
  now make differently.
- Ask for an example of a team-based programming standard the candidate set,
  and how they got other developers to follow it.
- Ask a referee about a time stakeholders came to the candidate directly for
  technical advice on whether an approach was workable.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development uses a variety of prototyping methods, shares
best practice and coaches others, and looks at strategic service design end
to end.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Coaches others" is an explicit teaching descriptor, and
"strategic service design end to end" moves the skill past any one
prototype into organisation-wide practice — both are Expert-level framing.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not used a prototyping method to test a design idea before
  building it.
- 1 — Beginner: Builds a simple prototype using one method, with guidance on
  which method to choose.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses and builds a prototype independently for a
  contained piece of work, using more than one method as the situation needs.
- 3 — Advanced: Uses prototyping to test a service end to end, not just one
  screen or interaction, and the findings change what gets built.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches other developers or designers in prototyping method and
  practice, and has set how prototyping is used strategically across a whole
  service or programme rather than one team's slice of it.
- 5 — Expert-plus: N/A — this scale has five points, 0 through 4.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a prototype that changed the direction of a whole
  service, not just one component.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has coached others in prototyping
  practice, and for a specific example.
- Ask the candidate to describe how they chose between prototyping methods on
  a recent piece of strategic service design.

### Skill: Systems integration (frontend developer)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development defines the static assets build, co-ordinates
all aspects of the integration and takes responsibility for the tests around
the user interface, and configures the part of the system that uses the
network and tests that it works as expected.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This skill's wording stays bounded to a specific
technical deliverable — defining a build, coordinating an integration,
configuring and testing a network-facing component — without the explicit
teaching, coaching, or organisation-wide strategy language that justifies an
Expert baseline elsewhere in this matrix. A head of frontend development is
expected to be able to do this independently and to take full responsibility
for the outcome, which is Advanced; nothing in the wording itself demands
that they also teach it to others.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how the frontend build integrates with the rest
  of the system.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an existing integration setup, with support, without
  yet being able to change it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Makes a contained change to the static assets build or
  network configuration, with review from a more senior colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Defines the static assets build for a piece of work
  unaided, coordinates the integration end to end, and takes responsibility
  when the tests around it fail.
- 4 — Expert: Has set how systems integration is done across more than one
  team or programme, and other developers are taught the approach the
  candidate designed.
- 5 — Expert-plus: N/A — this scale has five points, 0 through 4.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a work sample or a description of a static assets build the
  candidate defined, including a decision that was hard to get right.
- Present a scenario where an integration test fails intermittently and ask
  the candidate to talk through how they would take responsibility for
  finding and fixing it.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has coordinated an integration across
  more than one team, and what their role in it was.

### Skill: User focus (frontend developer)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development collaborates with user researchers and
interaction designers to represent user needs internally, and explains the
difference between user needs and the desires of the user. They champion user
research to focus on all users, effectively prioritise and define approaches
to understand the user story, guiding others to do so, and offer
recommendations on the best tools and methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Champion" and "guiding others" describe advocacy and
teaching exercised across the organisation, not just on the candidate's own
work, and that is what this scale defines as Expert.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain the difference between what a user asked for and
  what a user needs.
- 1 — Beginner: Can repeat back user needs described by a researcher, but
  does not yet apply the distinction between need and desire unaided.
- 2 — Intermediate: Represents user needs in their own team's decisions,
  drawing on research a user researcher has already done.
- 3 — Advanced: Prioritises and defines an approach to understand a user
  story independently, including for users the team has not previously
  considered.
- 4 — Expert: Champions user research across the organisation so that it
  covers all users rather than the easiest to reach, guides other developers
  in how to prioritise and approach the user story, and their recommendations
  have changed which tools or methods the organisation uses.
- 5 — Expert-plus: N/A — this scale has five points, 0 through 4.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a time the candidate championed research into users
  who were being overlooked, and what changed as a result.
- Ask the candidate to explain, using a real example, the difference between
  a user's stated desire and their actual need.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has guided other developers on how to
  approach understanding a user story, beyond their own project.

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

### Skill: Web performance optimisation

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of frontend development advocates for web performance optimisation
best practices, coaches and guides others, and actively collects data from
multiple tools to monitor and resolve web performance issues.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Coaches and guides others" is explicit teaching

hands-on, data-led monitoring element.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 4 — Expert: Coaches and guides other developers in web performance

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a web performance issue the candidate diagnosed and

- Ask for an example of an organisational practice — a performance budget, a
  build gate, a monitoring standard — that the candidate introduced or
  changed.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has coached other developers
  specifically on web performance, and what changed as a result.

The baseline for this role level is normally met when every skill above is
rated at or above its baseline target. Given how high most of these targets
sit, a rating a point or two below target on one or two skills is not
automatically a fail — it is common even in a strong candidate for a post at
the very top of the ladder. A reasonable assessor weighs how central the
skill is to the head of frontend development's core accountabilities —
organisational strategy, departmental capability, and cross-government
influence sit at the heart of the role, so a shortfall there is more
