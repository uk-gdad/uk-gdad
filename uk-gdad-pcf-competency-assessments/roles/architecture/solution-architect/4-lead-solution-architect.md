# Lead Solution Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the assessing — a line manager, a
hiring panel member, or a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or employee
possesses the baseline abilities the lead solution architect role level
requires. A lead solution architect is responsible for a group of solution
architecture projects, or a single more complex area, and may lead teams of
more junior solution architects.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then complete
the overall determination. This assessment is one input among several into a
hiring, promotion or development decision, never the whole of it — see
`## Advisory`.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before scoring, so you are
assessing against this level's own accountabilities — a group of projects, or
a single complex area, with responsibility for junior architects — rather
than senior solution architect below it or principal solution architect
above it. Gather whatever evidence exists first: a CV or portfolio, a
structured interview, a work sample or task, direct observation, or
references, including from someone this person has led or coached.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, and
where possible have a second assessor score independently before you compare
notes — this matters most for a hiring or promotion decision, and materially
reduces the risk of an unlawful adverse impact from an unvalidated,
AI-drafted instrument used inconsistently.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence — see
[Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

A worked example, using **Problem definition and shaping**:

- A candidate rated **3 — Advanced** independently frames a high-complexity
  problem for their own group of projects, but has not yet coached another
  architect through doing the same.
- A candidate rated **4 — Expert** coaches other architects in defining
  problems and describing solution options, and has helped someone else
  challenge a requirement or assumption they would otherwise have accepted —
  the framework's own bar for this level.

## About this role level

**The role: solution architect.** A solution architect designs solutions for
problems that affect the organisation. In this role, a person will:

- ensure a problem and the desired outcomes are properly defined
- ensure the scope of a solution meets the organisation's requirements
- stay up to date on technology trends and approaches
- understand organisational objectives and external drivers, for example,
  legislation or financial constraints
- work with others to develop business and technical strategies
- work within business and technical constraints
- design and document solutions so they can be implemented by the
  organisation
- comply with standards and governance
- communicate and work effectively with stakeholders
- manage risks and decisions in a transparent way

**This level: lead solution architect.** A lead solution architect is
responsible for a group of solution architecture projects, or a single more
complex area. They may lead teams of more junior solution architects.

**Where this level sits.** The solution architect role has five levels:

1. Associate solution architect
2. Solution architect
3. Senior solution architect
4. **Lead solution architect** — this level
5. Principal solution architect

Two skills — Community collaboration and Making architectural decisions —
carry framework wording identical to senior solution architect below; an
assessor should use this level's own accountabilities (a group of projects,
or a complex area, with junior architects to lead) to judge whether the
evidence reflects genuinely broader scope. Architecture communication,
Problem definition and shaping, and Technical design throughout the life
cycle explicitly name coaching others at this level. Strategy design moves
from "supporting" a strategy to "defining" one across teams.

**Accountabilities at this level.** The framework expects a lead solution
architect to:

- build relationships with senior stakeholders across multiple business or
  technical areas in the organisation
- be proactive in identifying opportunities to improve the organisation
- support multiple architecture projects or programmes
- find and use emerging technologies and approaches
- develop best practice for solution architecture

**The skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Making architectural decisions
6. Problem definition and shaping
7. Strategy design
8. Technical design throughout the life cycle

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What the framework expects at this level**

Work to support wider organisational objectives beyond your immediate goals.
Track emerging internal and external issues over time that could affect the
work of teams across the organisation. Take action to solve or mitigate
problems by influencing colleagues across the organisation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Taking action across the organisation by influencing
colleagues, unsupported, is independent Advanced practice; the framework
reserves explicit coaching of others in this skill for principal solution
architect.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe an organisational objective beyond their own
  group of projects.
- 1 — Beginner: Aware of wider organisational objectives, needs support
  acting on them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Tracks internal and external issues for their own area,
  with support influencing colleagues elsewhere.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently tracks issues that could affect teams across
  the organisation, and takes action to solve or mitigate problems by
  influencing colleagues outside their own reporting line.
- 4 — Expert: Anticipates policy changes and coaches others in identifying
  important trends — the bar for principal solution architect, above this
  level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe influencing a colleague outside
  your own team or reporting line to solve or mitigate a problem. What was
  the problem, and how did you get their agreement?"
- A reference question: "Has this person's awareness of organisation-wide
  issues changed how another team's work was planned?"

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

Communicate with technical and non-technical stakeholders at all levels, and
across organisations, using architecture communication techniques. Mediate
between people in difficult architectural discussions. Gain support from
business and technical stakeholders for architectural topics with high
levels of risk, impact and complexity. Coach and support others in
architecture communication.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework names coaching others in this skill
explicitly at this level — the definition of Expert on this scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not mediated a difficult architectural discussion.
- 1 — Beginner: Communicates well one-to-one, but has not mediated between
  disagreeing stakeholders.
- 2 — Intermediate: Mediates straightforward disagreements with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently mediates difficult discussions and gains
  support for high-risk, high-complexity topics, but has not yet coached
  another architect in doing the same.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches and supports other architects in architecture
  communication, and their coaching has visibly improved someone else's
  practice.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe mediating a difficult
  architectural discussion between people who disagreed. How did you get to
  a resolution?"
- A reference question, ideally from someone coached: "What did this person
  teach you about communicating architecture, and did it change how you
  work?"

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What the framework expects at this level**

Identify appropriate contractual frameworks and approaches. Identify,
evaluate and select appropriate suppliers.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Selecting suppliers and contractual frameworks is
ownership of a commercial outcome, unsupported — Advanced, though the
framework does not describe this level as coaching others in commercial
judgement.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not identified a contractual framework or evaluated a
  supplier.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to supplier evaluation with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies contractual frameworks for straightforward
  cases with occasional support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies, evaluates and selects appropriate
  suppliers and contractual frameworks for real work.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe evaluating and selecting a
  supplier or contractual framework. What criteria mattered most, and why?"
- A document review: a supplier evaluation or business case the candidate
  led.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level**

Work collaboratively in a group, actively networking with others. Adapt
feedback to ensure it's effective and lasting. Use your topic to identify
problems or issues in the team dynamic and rectify them. Identify issues
through Agile 'health checks' with the team, and help to stimulate the right
responses.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This wording is identical to senior solution
architect's — record the repeat rather than assuming a higher bar. This
level's accountability to lead teams of junior architects means the evidence
should span more than one team, even though the skill's own text has not
changed.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not rectified a team-dynamic issue.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to a team environment with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Adapts feedback for short-term effect, with support
  making it last.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies and rectifies team-dynamic issues
  and runs Agile health checks, across more than one team where the role
  requires it.
- 4 — Expert: Solves and unblocks issues between teams or departments at the
  highest level, and coaches the organisation on team dynamics — the bar for
  principal solution architect, above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe a team-dynamic issue you
  rectified across more than one team you were responsible for."
- A reference question, from a junior architect this person has led: "Did
  they help you deal with a team issue, or only work around it?"

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

Make and guide architectural design decisions characterised by medium risk
and complexity. Identify and address architectural risks that affect
multiple teams or domains. Use architectural governance and assurance to
make design decisions and manage technical risks at the appropriate level.
Contribute to the development of architectural governance and assurance.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This wording is identical to senior solution
architect's — record the repeat. The framework reserves "lead and evolve"
architectural governance, the Expert marker, for principal solution
architect.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not made an architectural decision affecting multiple teams.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to decisions with regular support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Makes decisions of managed risk with occasional support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently makes and guides decisions of medium risk and
  complexity across multiple teams or domains, and contributes to how
  governance itself develops.
- 4 — Expert: Leads and evolves architectural governance and represents it
  within wider governance — the bar for principal solution architect, above
  this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A document review: a decision record spanning more than one team or
  domain that the candidate made or guided.
- A structured interview question: "Describe contributing to how your
  organisation's architectural governance works, not just following it."

- [ ] 0 — None

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What the framework expects at this level**

Lead the framing of a problem characterised by high complexity, complication,
or risk so that a solution can be created. Coach others in defining problems
and describing appropriate options for solutions. Help others challenge
requirements and assumptions, and identify opportunities when defining
problems and solution options.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework names coaching others in this skill
explicitly — leading the framing of high-complexity problems and coaching
other architects through the same is the definition of Expert.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not led the framing of a high-complexity problem.
- 1 — Beginner: Frames problems for their own work but has not led others
  through it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads framing for medium-complexity problems with
  support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently leads framing of high-complexity, high-risk
  problems, but has not yet coached another architect through the process.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches other architects in defining problems and options, and
  has helped someone else challenge a requirement or assumption they would
  otherwise have accepted.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe coaching another architect
  through framing a difficult problem. What did they get right on their
  own, and where did they need your input?"
- A portfolio item: a problem definition for a high-complexity, high-risk
  piece of work the candidate led.

### Skill: Strategy design

**What the framework expects at this level**

Define strategies or visions across teams that align with organisational
objectives. Direct the implementation of a strategy or vision, for example,
by creating roadmaps or plans. Define architectural principles and patterns.
Develop or maintain strategy in response to feedback and findings.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Define" and "direct" strategy across teams is a
clear step up from senior solution architect's "support" — independent
ownership, though the framework does not yet describe organisation-wide
scope, which it reserves for principal solution architect.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not defined a strategy or set of principles for their own
  area.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to defining strategy with regular support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Defines strategy elements with occasional support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently defines strategies or visions across the teams
  they are responsible for, directs implementation, and maintains the
  strategy in response to feedback.
- 4 — Expert: Defines and connects strategies across the organisation or
  wider government — the bar for principal solution architect, above this
  level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: a strategy, vision, or roadmap document the candidate
  defined and directed the implementation of, across more than one team.
- A structured interview question: "Describe maintaining a strategy in
  response to feedback that showed the original approach was not working."

### Skill: Technical design throughout the life cycle

**What the framework expects at this level**

Create technical designs characterised by high risk, impact, and complexity.
Lead and guide others in creating technical designs that achieve
organisational objectives. Use feedback to optimise and refine standards for
technical designs throughout the life cycle.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework names leading and guiding others in this
skill explicitly at this level — the definition of Expert.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not created a technical design of high risk and complexity.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to high-risk designs with regular support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Creates designs of medium risk and complexity with
  occasional support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently creates technical designs of high risk, impact
  and complexity, but has not yet led another architect through the process.
- 4 — Expert: Leads and guides other architects in creating technical
  designs that achieve organisational objectives, and refines the standards
  the designs are built against.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: a technical design of high risk, impact, or complexity
  the candidate led, with evidence others followed their guidance.
- A structured interview question: "Describe guiding another architect
  through a technical design. What standard did you refine as a result?"

**Scoring guidance.** The baseline is normally met when every skill is rated
at or above its baseline target. The three skills at Expert — Architecture
communication, Problem definition and shaping, and Technical design
throughout the life cycle — are the ones where the framework explicitly names
coaching or leading others; a shortfall in any of them is a significant
finding for a role that is meant to develop junior architects. Community
collaboration and Making architectural decisions carry the same baseline as
senior solution architect because their framework wording has not changed;
do not treat that repeat as a shortfall on its own. Weigh how central each
skill is to this level's accountabilities, consider whether a shortfall is
coachable quickly after appointment, and record the reasoning in the evidence
log rather than only the number.
