# Associate Network Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, an interview panel member,
or anyone else evaluating a candidate or an employee — who needs to judge
whether a person has the baseline abilities the Architecture profession's
associate network architect role level requires. It measures demonstrated
skill against the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of
this level in the network architect role, for a hiring decision, a promotion
or progression board, onboarding a new starter, or a periodic capability
check. It is one input into that decision, never the whole of it — see
`## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what the
framework actually expects before you look at any evidence. Then gather what
exists for this person: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work
sample or task, direct observation of them at work, or references from people
who have worked with them. An associate network architect works under
guidance, so expect early-career evidence — coursework, a supported project, a
first months-long placement — rather than a long independent track record.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess — an honest gap
teaches you more than a guess dressed up as a score.

**Stay consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
using the same evidence sources wherever you can. For a hiring or promotion
decision, have more than one assessor score independently, then compare notes,
rather than settling on a single view first.

**Fairness.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact even when nobody intends
it. Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

**Handling sensitive material.** Network architecture touches live
infrastructure and security controls even at associate level. When you record
evidence in `## Assessor notes and evidence log`, describe what the person did
and how they reasoned about it — not classified material, live vulnerability
detail, or specific security configuration.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale — how well the person does the thing, on the evidence
available, not how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example.** Take Skill: IT infrastructure. At 1 — Beginner, a
candidate can describe how to configure a switch port or a firewall rule with
someone checking their work step by step, but has not done it unsupervised. At
2 — Intermediate, the baseline for this level, they have built, configured or
supported a piece of infrastructure themselves — a test-lab network segment, a
supported production change — with a senior colleague available to check in
on but not directing every step. At 3 — Advanced, they have administered and
supported infrastructure independently across a change window without needing
a colleague to intervene. Calibrate against evidence like this before you
start scoring.

## About this role level

Architecture role: Network architect. A network architect is responsible for
network designs and specifications, including cloud networks.

The role has three levels, in order: associate network architect, network
architect, and lead network architect. This assessment covers the first of
those.

Role level: Associate network architect. An associate network architect works
with network architects on designs and specifications, with guidance.

At this role level, the framework expects someone to:

- be aware and knowledgeable of new technology solutions and produce network
  design policy
- draft recommendations for network support, creating and maintaining network
  plans and planning infrastructure runs
- ensure designs meet SLAs (service level agreements) and develop an
  understanding of common tools
- have an understanding of one or two of the following network technologies:
  LAN (local area network), WAN (wide area network), wifi, data centre LAN,
  SD (software-defined) networks, telephony, mobile solutions infrastructure,
  unified comms, network management solutions, network-specific security and
  remote access technologies and approaches
- collaborate with engineers to ensure systems are practically designed

The framework names five skills for this level: Governance and assurance, IT
infrastructure, Systems design, Systems integration, and Troubleshooting and
problem resolution.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate network architect can explain why governance and assurance
matter, and can follow governance and assurance processes when someone more
senior is supervising them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording caps this skill at
explaining and following-under-supervision; nothing here asks the person to
run a process unassisted, so a baseline above Beginner would be asking for
more than the level requires.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot say why a design needs sign-off or what a governance gate
  is for.
- 1 — Beginner: can explain in their own words why a change goes through a
  design review or an assurance gate, and follows the steps of a governance
  process when someone more senior checks their work.
- 2 — Intermediate: follows a governance or assurance process correctly with
  only occasional prompting, and can flag when a step has been missed.
- 3 — Advanced: runs a governance or assurance step unsupervised and spots
  where the process itself does not fit the change in front of them.
- 4 — Expert: designs or improves a governance process and coaches others
  through it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a recent design review or change approval
  they took part in, and explain what each governance step was checking for.
- Ask a referee whether the person has needed prompting to follow governance
  steps, or has done so reliably once shown.
- Review a document the person produced — a change request, a design note —
  for evidence they followed a required review or sign-off step.

### Skill: IT infrastructure

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate network architect can build, configure, administer and support
infrastructure solutions and services — for example networking and physical
infrastructure, commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) software, open source
packages and solutions, or virtual and cloud computing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is a hands-on operational skill the
framework expects the person to already be doing, not merely learning about,
so the baseline sits above Beginner; but it is one skill among several they
are still developing, so it stops short of Advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not built, configured or supported any infrastructure
  component.
- 1 — Beginner: has configured a component — a switch port, a virtual
  machine, a cloud resource — with someone checking each step.
- 2 — Intermediate: has built, configured or supported infrastructure
  themselves, with a senior colleague available but not directing every step.
- 3 — Advanced: administers and supports infrastructure independently across
  a full change window without needing a colleague to intervene.
- 4 — Expert: designs infrastructure standards others build to, and is the
  person colleagues escalate a hard infrastructure problem to.

**Evidence to gather**

- Set a small, realistic task: configure a network segment or a cloud
  resource against a written specification, and observe how much guidance
  they need.
- Ask for a work sample — a configuration file, a build script, a network
  diagram — they produced themselves, and ask them to explain the choices
  in it.
- Ask a referee what level of supervision the person needed on their last
  infrastructure change.

- [ ] 1 — Beginner

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate network architect translates logical designs into physical
designs, produces detailed designs, documents all work using required
standards, methods and tools including prototyping tools where appropriate,
and designs systems with managed levels of risk, manageable complexity and
meaningful impact, working with well understood technology and recognised
patterns.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Producing detailed designs and documenting them
to a standard is real design work, not an introductory task, but it is
explicitly scoped to well understood technology and managed risk — the
guard-rails that keep this below the independent, higher-risk design work
expected one level up.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot translate a logical design into a workable physical one.
- 1 — Beginner: can produce a physical design from a logical one with close
  review of every stage.
- 2 — Intermediate: independently turns a logical design into a documented
  physical design for a well understood, managed-risk system, using the
  required standards and templates.
- 3 — Advanced: produces designs for systems of medium complexity or risk
  without close supervision, and identifies which established pattern fits a
  new problem.
- 4 — Expert: sets the design standards other network architects work to, and
  is trusted to design for high-risk, high-complexity systems.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a detailed design document the person has produced, and check it
  against the organisation's required standards, methods and templates.
- Ask the candidate to talk through how they turned a logical design into a
  physical one, including what pattern they chose and why.
- Ask a referee whether the person's designs have needed significant rework
  after review, and if so, of what kind.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate network architect can explain the process and principles of
integrating systems, and describe the challenges of designing, building and
testing interfaces between systems.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own verbs here — explain, describe —
ask for understanding, not for the person to run an integration themselves;
that hands-on ownership arrives at the next level up, where the framework
asks the network architect to define and coordinate integration builds.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe what "integrating two systems" involves.
- 1 — Beginner: explains, when asked, the basic process of integrating
  systems and can name one or two challenges of building and testing an
  interface between them.
- 2 — Intermediate: explains integration principles unprompted and links them
  to a real example from their own work or study.
- 3 — Advanced: has taken part in planning or testing a real system
  integration and can describe what went wrong and why.
- 4 — Expert: has led the definition and coordination of an integration build
  across systems.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe the process of integrating two systems they
  are familiar with, and the challenges they would expect at the interface.
- Ask about any integration testing they have taken part in, even as an
  observer, and what they learned from it.
- Ask a referee whether the person has shown understanding of integration
  challenges when discussing a design.

### Skill: Troubleshooting and problem resolution

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate network architect troubleshoots and identifies problems across
different technology capabilities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Troubleshooting is a practical skill this level
is expected to already exercise day to day, across more than one technology
area, so the baseline sits above Beginner even though the person is still
working under guidance overall.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe an approach to diagnosing a fault.
- 1 — Beginner: follows a runbook or a colleague's direction to diagnose a
  known type of fault.
- 2 — Intermediate: diagnoses a fault in a familiar technology area
  unassisted, and asks the right questions when the fault spans an area they
  know less well.
- 3 — Advanced: diagnoses faults confidently across several different
  technology capabilities without needing a colleague's direction.
- 4 — Expert: is the person others escalate to when a fault has resisted
  diagnosis, and teaches a structured troubleshooting approach.

**Evidence to gather**

- Walk through a real or simulated fault scenario and observe how the
  candidate structures their diagnosis.
- Ask for an example of a problem they diagnosed and fixed, including what
  they ruled out along the way.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate diagnosing a fault outside
  their strongest technology area.
