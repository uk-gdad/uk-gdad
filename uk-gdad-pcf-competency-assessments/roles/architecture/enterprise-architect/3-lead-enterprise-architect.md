# Lead Enterprise Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an existing employee has the
baseline abilities required for the lead enterprise architect role level in
the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's architecture profession. Use it
for a hiring decision, a promotion or transfer into the role, onboarding a new
starter, or a periodic capability check against the framework. It measures one
person against the framework's own description of this role level; it is one
input among several into a decision, never the whole of it — see the Advisory
section below for why.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

Because this instrument is unvalidated, using it inconsistently between
candidates — different questions, different evidence standards, a single
assessor working alone on a hiring or promotion decision — increases the risk
of unlawful adverse impact against candidates who share a protected
characteristic. Consistent, evidence-based administration, ideally by more
than one assessor, materially reduces that risk.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` in full before you score
anything, so you know what this specific level expects rather than a general
impression of "architect". Gather whatever evidence exists ahead of time: a
CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task, direct
observation of the person's day-to-day work, or references — for a role at
this level, references that speak to influence over time matter more than
usual, because much of the evidence is a track record rather than a single
task.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating in the competency matrix
must trace to something the person did, said, produced or was observed doing.
If you cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess — a guess dressed up as a score is worse than an honest gap.

**Consistency.** Use the same method and the same evidence standard for every
candidate assessed against this role level. Where possible, especially for a
hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence. See the Advisory section above for why an
unvalidated instrument used inconsistently carries real legal risk, and treat
that risk as a reason to slow down and document your reasoning, not as small
print.

Enterprise architecture work regularly touches commercially sensitive
supplier information and, in some organisations, security-classified
material. Record evidence in general, job-relevant terms in the notes below —
never transcribe classified detail, live vulnerability information or
commercially confidential figures into this document.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale. It measures how well the person does the thing, on the
evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Architect for the whole context.** A person rated 2
(Intermediate) notices an emerging trend and raises it, but relies on others
to decide what to do with it. A person rated 3 (Advanced) independently
assesses how a trend in society or industry practice might affect the
organisation, and acts on it. A person rated 4 (Expert) — the baseline for
this role level — does all of that, works with people outside the
organisation to inform policy, strategy and standards, anticipates changes to
policy and builds resilience through their own architectural work, and coaches
others in identifying important trends, exactly as the framework describes
this level's coaching accountability.

## About this role level

Enterprise architects are leaders working across different levels within an
organisation to translate business strategy into business change and
technical delivery. In the role generally, an enterprise architect identifies
priorities for change, owns the enterprise architecture vision, strategy and
roadmaps across business, technology and data, takes a strategic view across
architectural domains, portfolios and programmes, establishes architectural
principles, policies and standards, and develops the wider architecture
community.

This is the third of the role's four levels: enterprise architect, senior
enterprise architect, **lead enterprise architect** (this level), and
principal enterprise architect.

At this level, a lead enterprise architect plays a high-level role in ensuring
the strategy is developed, agreed and followed. The framework describes this
level as networking and communicating with senior stakeholders across
enterprises and seeking out opportunities for improvement, supporting at
least one community or team (or a combination of both), finding and using
best practice and emerging technologies and approaches, leading teams that
include other enterprise architects and helping them understand how to
deliver the team's objectives to meet organisational goals, horizon scanning
for influences or risks, supporting successful delivery of the long-term
strategy, and taking a leading role in the overall direction of business and
digital capabilities.

This level names eight skills: Architect for the whole context, Architecture
communication, Commercial perspective, Community collaboration, Enterprise
architecture, Making architectural decisions, Problem definition and shaping,
and Strategy design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What the framework expects at this level**

Assess how trends in society and industry practices might affect the
organisation, work with people outside the organisation to inform policies,
strategies and standards, anticipate changes to policy and build resilience
through your architectural work, and coach others in identifying important
trends.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's own wording asks this level to coach
others in the skill, which is the definition of Expert on this scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not considered how a societal or industry trend might affect
  the organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe a trend when asked, but has not connected it to
  organisational impact or policy.
- 2 — Intermediate: Notices an emerging trend and raises it with others, but
  relies on someone else to act on it.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently assesses how a trend might affect the
  organisation and anticipates a coming change to policy.
- 4 — Expert: Works with people outside the organisation to inform policy,
  strategy or standards, builds resilience into their own architectural work
  ahead of a policy change, and coaches other architects in spotting
  important trends.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of the candidate working with an external body — a
  cross-government forum, a standards group, a supplier — to inform a policy
  or standard.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has coached another architect in
  spotting an important trend, and what came of it.
- Review a document showing the candidate anticipating a policy change and
  building resilience into a design ahead of it.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

Communicate with technical and non-technical stakeholders at all levels and
across organisations, using architecture communication techniques, mediate
between people in difficult architectural discussions, gain support from
business and technical stakeholders for architectural topics with high levels
of risk, impact or complexity, and coach and support others in architecture
communication.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. As with the previous skill, the framework names
coaching explicitly at this level, which is the definition of Expert here.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Communicates only within their immediate team and has not led a
  cross-organisational conversation.
- 1 — Beginner: Communicates a straightforward architecture topic to a single
  audience, with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Adapts a message across audiences unaided for topics of
  moderate risk or complexity.
- 3 — Advanced: Gains support from stakeholders for a topic of high risk,
  impact or complexity, and mediates a difficult architectural discussion.
- 4 — Expert: Does all of the above routinely across organisations, and
  actively coaches and supports other architects in architecture
  communication.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of the candidate mediating a difficult architectural
  disagreement between stakeholders, and how they resolved it.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate coaching a more junior
  architect on how to communicate a risky topic.
- Observe the candidate in a structured interview task presenting a
  high-complexity architecture topic to a mixed panel and handling challenge.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner

- [ ] 3 — Advanced

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What the framework expects at this level**

Identify appropriate contractual frameworks and approaches, and identify,
evaluate and select appropriate suppliers.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework has moved this skill from
understanding to acting — identifying and selecting suppliers directly —
without describing it as a coaching or teaching capability, so Advanced fits
better than Expert.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has no working knowledge of contractual frameworks or supplier
  selection.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe a contractual framework in general terms but has
  not applied one.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies an appropriate contractual approach with
  guidance from a commercial colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies an appropriate contractual framework
  and evaluates and selects a suitable supplier for a piece of work.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes the organisation's contractual and supplier strategy,
  and is asked to advise other teams on their own commercial approach.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a contractual framework or supplier the candidate
  selected, and the evaluation behind that choice.
- Ask a referee from a commercial function whether they have worked with the
  candidate on selecting a supplier, and how that went.

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level**

Work collaboratively in a group, actively networking with others, adapt
feedback so it is effective and lasting, use your own subject to identify
problems or issues in the team dynamic and rectify them, and identify issues
through Agile "health checks" with the team, helping to stimulate the right
response.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The skill's wording is unchanged from the level
below, but a lead enterprise architect leading teams that include other
enterprise architects is expected to exercise it across a wider group of
people, so the baseline holds at Advanced rather than stepping down.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Works alone and has not contributed to another team's output.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to another team's work when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: Networks actively and names a team dynamic issue when it
  arises, relying on someone else to act.
- 3 — Advanced: Adapts feedback so it lands well across a team that includes
  other enterprise architects, identifies an issue through a health check or
  similar signal, and helps rectify it.
- 4 — Expert: Is sought out to advise on team composition and dynamics across
  more than one community at once.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of the candidate rectifying a dynamic issue in a team of
  peer architects, not just more junior staff.
- Ask a referee for an example of feedback the candidate gave that visibly
  changed how a team of experienced architects worked afterwards.

### Skill: Enterprise architecture

**What the framework expects at this level**

Design an architecture that defines the technology, people, process and other
elements needed to achieve organisational objectives, and work with others
across the organisation to ensure that design is used to achieve those
objectives.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The wording is unchanged from the level below, but at
this level the design work sits inside a "high-level role... ensuring the
strategy is developed, agreed and followed" — the baseline holds at Advanced
because the framework has not asked explicitly for coaching or organisation-
wide ownership on this specific skill until the level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot design an architecture that connects technology, people
  and process to an organisational objective.
- 1 — Beginner: Designs a narrow part of an architecture with direction from
  a more senior architect.
- 2 — Intermediate: Designs a straightforward architecture element with some
  guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs an architecture defining technology,
  people and process elements, in support of a strategy being agreed across
  the organisation.
- 4 — Expert: Leads other architects in designing enterprise architecture and
  influences structures beyond the organisation's own boundary.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a work sample: an enterprise architecture the candidate designed
  that fed into an agreed organisational strategy.
- Ask the candidate to describe how their design was adopted across more
  than one part of the organisation.

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

Make and guide architectural design decisions characterised by medium risk
and complexity, identify and address architectural risks that affect
multiple teams or domains, use architectural governance and assurance to make
design decisions and manage technical risks at the appropriate level, and
contribute to the development of that governance and assurance.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The wording is unchanged from the level below; the
step up at this level shows in the level's own accountability to lead teams
of architects, not in this skill's text, so the numeric baseline holds.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not taken part in an architectural decision-making process.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to a decision made mainly by others.
- 2 — Intermediate: Makes a decision of managed risk affecting their own team
  or domain, with governance support.
- 3 — Advanced: Makes and guides architectural decisions of medium risk and
  complexity affecting multiple teams or domains, and contributes to
  developing the governance and assurance used to manage them.
- 4 — Expert: Leads and evolves architectural governance and assurance
  itself, and makes decisions of high risk and complexity across the
  organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a decision the candidate guided — not just made
  alone — involving another architect or team.
- Ask how the candidate has contributed to developing a governance or
  assurance process, and what changed as a result.

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What the framework expects at this level**

Lead the framing of a problem characterised by high complexity, complication
or risk so that a solution can be created, coach others in defining problems
and describing appropriate options for solutions, and help others challenge
requirements and assumptions and identify opportunities when defining
problems and solution options.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework names coaching explicitly and raises the
complexity from "medium" to "high" — both point to Expert as the baseline.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Takes a problem statement as given and does not question or
  reframe it.
- 1 — Beginner: Frames a simple problem with close support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Frames a problem of managed complexity with some
  guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently leads the framing of a problem of high
  complexity, complication or risk.
- 4 — Expert: Leads the framing of high-complexity problems and coaches other
  architects in defining problems and describing options, and helps them
  challenge requirements and assumptions in doing so.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of the candidate leading the framing of a genuinely
  high-risk or high-complexity problem, and the outcome.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate coaching another architect
  through defining a problem, rather than defining it for them.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Strategy design

**What the framework expects at this level**

Define and connect strategies or visions across the organisation or wider
government, and enable the implementation of those strategies or visions
across the organisation or wider government — for example, by advocating for
resources and removing blockers.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The scope has widened from "across teams" at the level
below to "across the organisation or wider government", and the verb has
shifted from directing implementation to enabling it — advocating for
resources, removing blockers — which is a mastery-level act of influence
rather than a managed task.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not contributed to defining a strategy or vision.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes a defined piece to someone else's strategy.
- 2 — Intermediate: Challenges an assumption in a strategy discussion and
  supports its implementation.
- 3 — Advanced: Defines a strategy or vision across teams and directs its
  implementation through a roadmap or plan.
- 4 — Expert: Defines and connects strategies or visions across the
  organisation, or wider government, and enables their implementation by
  advocating for resources and removing blockers that are outside any one
  team's control.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a strategy or vision document the candidate authored that spans more
  than one part of the organisation.
- Ask the candidate to describe a time they removed a blocker or secured a
  resource that no single team could have obtained alone.
- Ask a referee about the candidate's influence on strategy beyond their own
  immediate remit.
