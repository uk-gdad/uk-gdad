# Product Manager - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the measuring — a line manager, an
interview panel, or a reviewer — who needs to judge whether a candidate or
employee has the baseline abilities the **product manager** role requires at
the **product manager** role level. It can support a hiring decision, a
promotion board, onboarding a new joiner, or a periodic capability check
against the framework.

It is one input among several, never the whole of a decision. Read
`## Advisory` before you use it, and keep it in mind throughout.

## Advisory

> This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
> educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
> not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
> form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
> disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
> resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
> colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you know what this level is accountable for and where it sits among the
product manager role's other levels. Gather whatever evidence exists before
you start: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task
(for example, a roadmap the candidate has actually prioritised), direct
observation, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess — a confident
guess is worse than an honest gap.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way.
Where possible, use more than one assessor scoring independently, then compare
notes — this matters most for a hiring or promotion decision, where the
consequences for the person are highest.

**Stay fair.** This is not a validated instrument, and used inconsistently by
a single assessor working from impression rather than evidence, it can produce
unlawful adverse impact — a pattern of ratings that disadvantages people who
share a protected characteristic, even without anyone intending it. Consistent,
evidence-based scoring, and more than one assessor wherever the decision
matters, are the two things that materially reduce that risk. Never let a
rating be influenced by anything other than demonstrated, job-related
evidence — see the fairness constraints in the source specification for what
that rules out.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
**proficiency** scale — how well the person does the thing, on the evidence
available, not how often they do it or how they feel about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

Take **Creating value for money**, one of the skills this role level names.
Someone at Beginner (1) can list what is on a roadmap but cannot yet say why
one item outranks another in value. Someone at Intermediate (2) produces a
rough value-versus-effort comparison with a colleague's help, and can explain
an individual estimate when asked, but would not be trusted to rank a whole
roadmap unaided. Someone at Advanced (3) independently ranks a roadmap by
value against effort, makes and defends a reuse-versus-build decision, and
knows when a risk to value needs escalating rather than quietly absorbing. The
gap between 2 and 3 here is not about knowing the concepts — it is whether the
person does this unsupervised and can defend the reasoning under challenge.

## About this role level

A **product manager** makes sure that their products provide value and
achieve the right outcomes by balancing user and business needs. In this
role, whatever the level, a product manager works with multidisciplinary
teams, bringing together their views, user needs and business needs to frame
the problem; works with multidisciplinary teams to deliver the right
outcomes for users and the organisation; makes decisions on priorities for
their teams; and ensures teams comply with appropriate standards.

The role has five levels, in order: 1. Associate product manager;
**2. Product manager — you are assessing this level**; 3. Senior product
manager; 4. Lead product manager; 5. Head of product.

At the **product manager** level, the person is responsible for product
quality and value, working with the team to identify, implement and improve
strategies and tactics for their products. They might recruit and manage
people in the product profession, ensuring people with the right skills are
hired, supported and developed. The framework notes this can be an
entry-level role for people moving in from other digital roles.

At this role level, a person is expected to lead a product through the
different phases of the product life cycle, from discovery through
development, delivery and continuous improvement, to retirement; to support
lead and senior product managers; and to prioritise work for their teams and
be able to measure outcomes.

The skills this level names, in the order the summary lists them, are:

- Agile and Lean practices
- Applying user-centred insights
- Creating value for money
- Life cycle management
- Managing product outcomes
- Product leadership
- Product management
- Stakeholder relationship management
- Strategic ownership

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile and Lean practices

**What the framework expects at this level**

A product manager compares and selects the most appropriate delivery methods
and processes for the work in front of them, recognises and adapts when
something is not working while encouraging experimentation, blends approaches
depending on context rather than following one method rigidly, helps the team
measure, evaluate and visualise outcomes, and encourages reflective practice
so ways of working keep improving.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This level works within an approach the team
largely already has in place and adapts it with guidance, rather than
choosing and blending methods across teams unsupported.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not worked within an agile, lean or other iterative delivery
  approach, and cannot describe how their team plans or reviews its work.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows the ceremonies and artefacts the team already uses —
  standups, retrospectives, a backlog — but needs a more senior colleague to
  explain why a particular method suits the work at hand.
- 2 — Intermediate: Compares two or three delivery approaches for a piece of
  work and recommends one with reasons, and runs a retrospective that
  surfaces at least one genuine change to ways of working.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently blends elements of different methods to fit an
  unusual piece of work, and coaches the team through an experiment when
  something is not working, without a senior product manager designing the
  change for them.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the delivery approach for more than one team or a whole
  product area, is sought out by other product managers for advice on method
  choice, and visibly improves how outcomes are measured and visualised
  beyond their own team.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a real sprint or iteration they ran,
  including one thing that did not work and what they changed as a result.
- Review a retrospective output or action log they produced or contributed
  to.
- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you chose one
  delivery approach over another for a piece of work. What made you choose
  it?"

### Skill: Applying user-centred insights

**What the framework expects at this level**

A product manager uses user insights to represent users in internal
discussions, advocates for research to be completed with all types of user of
the product or service, uses user needs to agree the work that needs to be
done and to determine priorities with the team, and works with others to
understand and recommend tools for getting the right user insight.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. At this level a person should be able to use
research that already exists to inform priorities and to speak for users in
the room, without yet being expected to design a wider research programme
unaided.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot cite user research when discussing priorities, and treats
  user needs as an assumption rather than as evidence.
- 1 — Beginner: Repeats findings from a research report when asked, but needs
  support to connect them to a prioritisation decision.
- 2 — Intermediate: Brings user insight into a planning or prioritisation
  discussion unprompted, and asks a researcher to include a type of user who
  has been missed.
- 3 — Advanced: Spots a gap in the evidence base for a product decision and
  organises or commissions research to fill it, and visibly changes a
  roadmap decision on the strength of new insight.
- 4 — Expert: Builds a standing practice for how insight reaches
  decision-making across a product area, and is the person other product
  managers ask how to get insight from a hard-to-reach user group.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a decision they changed because of user research,
  and what the research showed.
- Review notes from, or observe, a prioritisation session where user insight
  was used to argue for or against an item.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate pushed for research to cover
  under-represented users.

### Skill: Creating value for money

**What the framework expects at this level**

A product manager estimates the value of each item on their roadmap, compares
effort to value when deciding priorities, decides when it is appropriate to
reuse something and when it is not, decides how and when to escalate risk to
achieving value, discusses estimates with stakeholders to validate
understanding, and analyses the benefit of investment against anticipated
outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This skill's own wording already asks for independent
judgement — estimating value, deciding on reuse, escalating risk — so a
product manager should be able to do this unsupervised for their own roadmap,
even while newer to the role than a senior product manager working across a
group of products.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain why one roadmap item was prioritised over another
  in terms of value or effort.
- 1 — Beginner: Can list the items on a roadmap but needs a more senior
  colleague to estimate value or effort for them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Produces a rough value-versus-effort comparison for
  roadmap items with guidance, and can explain an individual estimate when
  asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently estimates value across a roadmap, makes and
  defends a reuse-versus-build call, and knows when a risk to value needs
  escalating rather than quietly absorbing.
- 4 — Expert: Builds the value-estimation approach a wider team or area uses,
  and is trusted to make investment trade-off calls that other product
  managers adopt as the pattern.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through how they prioritised their current or
  most recent roadmap, including one item they chose not to build.
- Work sample: ask them to value-rank a short, anonymised list of roadmap
  items and explain the reasoning.
- Ask a referee about a time the candidate escalated a risk to delivering
  value, and how they did it.

### Skill: Life cycle management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A product manager manages the delivery of products and services that add
value through different phases of the life cycle, with some support; helps
identify if a product or service should move to the next phase and, if so,
how to do this; ensures the product or service is developed in line with the
appropriate service standards for a phase; and uses new information to adapt
approaches to life cycle management.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's own wording says "with some
support" at this level, so the baseline is that a person can run a product
through its phases with a senior colleague available, rather than entirely
alone.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe the phases a product or service moves through,
  or what changes between them.
- 1 — Beginner: Knows the names of the phases but needs close guidance to
  judge whether a product is ready to move between them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Manages a product through at least one phase transition
  with some support, and can point to the service standard evidence used to
  justify the move.
- 3 — Advanced: Runs a product through multiple phase transitions
  independently, adapting the approach when new information changes the
  picture, without a senior product manager making the call for them.
- 4 — Expert: Guides other product managers and stakeholders through life
  cycle decisions on complex or higher-risk products, and is the person
  consulted when a phase decision is contested.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a product they moved from one phase to the next: what evidence
  supported the move, and who signed it off.
- Review a phase-gate or assessment document they contributed to.
- Structured interview question: "What would make you recommend a product
  should not move to the next phase yet?"

### Skill: Managing product outcomes

**What the framework expects at this level**

A product manager works with others to define key performance indicators that
assess performance, value and outcomes; analyses and uses trusted data to
make decisions and enable continuous improvement; is open about their
decision making; and identifies and explains success metrics to stakeholders,
along with how the product can contribute to outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This level is expected to define and use outcome
metrics for their own product with the team, and to be open about how
decisions get made, rather than leading outcome measurement across other
teams.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a metric used to judge whether their product is
  succeeding.
- 1 — Beginner: Reports metrics someone else defined, without being able to
  explain why they were chosen.
- 2 — Intermediate: Works with the team to define a small set of KPIs, uses
  trusted data to inform a decision, and explains that decision openly when
  asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads the definition of outcome metrics for a product
  independently, uses data to drive a specific improvement, and proactively
  explains success metrics to stakeholders rather than waiting to be asked.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the standard for how outcomes are measured across a
  product area, and coaches others to make their decision making more
  transparent and evidence-led.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe the KPIs for their current product, how they
  were chosen, and one decision the data drove.
- Review a dashboard, report or stakeholder update they produced.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's decisions were explained openly,
  including ones that did not go as planned.

### Skill: Product leadership

**What the framework expects at this level**

A product manager advocates for their product and a product-led approach,
seeks opportunities for reuse and learning in their area, protects the team
from unnecessary distractions or requests to change direction, consults and
mediates with stakeholders, and participates in product management community
activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The wording is about advocating for a product and
protecting a single team, so the baseline is doing that unprompted, not yet
representing product management more widely.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Does not speak up for the product or the team's focus in
  meetings; direction changes without pushback even when unjustified.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain why the product matters when asked, but needs
  support to say no to a distracting request.
- 2 — Intermediate: Advocates for the product unprompted, shields the team
  from at least one unnecessary request or distraction, and takes part in a
  community of practice.
- 3 — Advanced: Mediates a genuine disagreement between stakeholders without
  it needing to be escalated, and is sought out to explain the product's
  direction to people outside the immediate team.
- 4 — Expert: Represents product-led thinking at a senior level across the
  organisation, and actively runs or leads community activity that other
  product managers benefit from.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time they said no to a stakeholder request and how they
  handled it.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate protected the team from unnecessary
  distraction, with an example.
- Ask about their involvement in any product management community, guild or
  community of practice.

### Skill: Product management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A product manager works with their team to investigate and validate what adds
value now and what could add value in future, creates a prioritised roadmap
and uses it to discuss product strategy with stakeholders, addresses
uncertainty while managing scope so that critical deadlines are met and value
is delivered, recommends a range of product management methods, and works in
the open, sharing their work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is the core discipline the role is named for,
and the level's own accountabilities already ask for owning a roadmap and
managing scope against critical deadlines, so a product manager should be
able to do this independently rather than with close supervision.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has no roadmap or prioritisation artefact they can point to, and
  cannot describe how scope or deadlines are managed on their product.
- 1 — Beginner: Maintains a roadmap someone else largely shaped, and needs
  support to manage scope when a deadline is at risk.
- 2 — Intermediate: Builds and updates a prioritised roadmap with guidance,
  and can explain trade-offs made under uncertainty when prompted.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently creates and defends a prioritised roadmap,
  manages scope to protect a critical deadline without losing sight of
  value, and shares plans and progress in the open without being asked.
- 4 — Expert: Recommends and adapts product management methods across
  multiple products or teams, and is the person others come to when scope
  and deadline pressure are in genuine conflict.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through their current roadmap and explain one
  trade-off they made under a deadline.
- Review a roadmap, product strategy document or public working-in-the-open
  update they produced.
- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a time scope had to change
  to hit a critical deadline. How did you decide what to cut?"

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A product manager works with the team to develop and maintain an
understanding of stakeholders, works with the team to develop and implement
stakeholder communications strategies, identifies and resolves issues while
influencing stakeholders and managing relationships effectively, and builds
long-term strategic relationships, communicating clearly and regularly with
stakeholders.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The wording asks for working with the team on
understanding and communicating with stakeholders, so the baseline is doing
that reliably with the team, rather than independently owning the hardest
relationships alone.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name the product's key stakeholders or describe what each
  of them needs from the product.
- 1 — Beginner: Knows who the stakeholders are but relies on others to
  manage the relationship or resolve issues.
- 2 — Intermediate: Maintains a stakeholder map with the team, communicates
  regularly, and resolves a straightforward disagreement without it needing
  to be escalated.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently builds and sustains a long-term relationship
  with a difficult or senior stakeholder, and resolves a genuine conflict of
  interest between two stakeholders.
- 4 — Expert: Is trusted with the organisation's most sensitive or senior
  stakeholder relationships on the product, and coaches others in how to
  manage theirs.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a stakeholder relationship that started difficult
  and how it improved.
- Review a stakeholder communications plan or update they authored.
- Ask a referee about an issue the candidate resolved with a stakeholder
  without it being escalated.

### Skill: Strategic ownership

**What the framework expects at this level**

A product manager engages and works with users, stakeholders and others to
develop a strategy and vision, ensures the strategy sets out the value,
benefits and outcomes needed to justify investment in the feature, product or
service, and regularly reviews and iterates the strategy.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This level contributes to and reviews the
strategy alongside others, so the baseline is bringing user and stakeholder
input into it and explaining its value case, rather than independently
setting or defending it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe the strategy or vision for their product beyond
  the current sprint's work.
- 1 — Beginner: Can repeat the strategy as written but had little part in
  shaping it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes user and stakeholder input into the
  strategy, and can explain the value case it sets out for investment.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads the development of a strategy or vision for a feature
  or product with others, and reviews and iterates it on a regular cycle
  rather than leaving it static.
- 4 — Expert: Owns strategy across a group of related products, and their
  strategic case is what secures investment decisions at a senior level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe the strategy for their current product and
  their part in writing it.
- Review a strategy or vision document they contributed to.
- Ask about the last time the strategy was reviewed and what changed as a
  result.
