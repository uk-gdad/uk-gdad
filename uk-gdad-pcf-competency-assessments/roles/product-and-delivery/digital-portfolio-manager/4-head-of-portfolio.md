# Digital Portfolio Manager (Head of Portfolio) - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a
reviewer — who needs to judge whether a candidate or an employee meets the
baseline abilities expected of a **Head of portfolio** in the **Digital
portfolio manager** role. Use it for a hiring decision, a promotion board, an
onboarding check, or a periodic capability review against the framework.

A head of portfolio leads a team of digital portfolio managers and is
accountable for the outcomes of the whole portfolio of digital projects they
govern. This assessment measures demonstrated skill against the framework's
own description of that level — it does not measure potential, effort, or how
well someone interviews.

This is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of it. Read
`## Advisory` before you use any part of this document.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you are holding the same picture of the job as the matrix. Gather whatever
evidence exists ahead of the session: a CV or portfolio of portfolio-level
work, a structured interview, a work sample or task (for example, reviewing a
sample portfolio dashboard or governance paper), direct observation of the
person leading a meeting or a review, and references from people who have seen
them lead a team or negotiate with senior stakeholders.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating you give must trace to
something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot
point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess at it —
a gap in evidence is not the same as a gap in skill, and treating it as one is
how bias creeps in.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this same role level the same
way, using the same evidence types wherever you can. Where possible, use more
than one assessor and score independently before comparing notes — this
matters most for hiring and promotion decisions, where the stakes for the
person being assessed are highest.

**Stay fair.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence — see `## Fair and lawful use` in this
project's specification. This instrument is unvalidated and AI-drafted: used
inconsistently, by a single assessor, without a shared evidence standard, it
can produce unlawful adverse impact against particular groups of candidates,
even unintentionally. Consistent, evidence-based administration, and having
more than one assessor score independently, materially reduces that risk. If
you are not confident you can administer it consistently, ask your
organisation's HR or occupational psychology colleagues for support before you
use it for a hiring or promotion decision.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
**proficiency** scale — it measures how well the person does the thing, on the
evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel about doing it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Governance and assurance.** At 1 (Beginner), a person can
describe what a governance process is for but has only followed one someone
else designed, with close supervision. At 2 (Intermediate), they have run an
existing governance process for a single project with some guidance, and can
explain why each control exists. At 3 (Advanced), they have designed a
governance or assurance process for a portfolio unsupervised, adjusted it when
it was not working, and can defend the design to a sceptical stakeholder. At 4
(Expert), they have done all of that and have also coached another portfolio
manager through designing their own process, and other people go to them when
their own governance approach is not working.

## About this role level

A **digital portfolio manager** co-ordinates and manages a group, or
"portfolio", of digital projects, using an iterative and flexible approach to
help define the governance processes for that portfolio.

The role has three levels, in order:

1. Digital portfolio manager
2. Senior digital portfolio manager
3. Head of portfolio — **you are assessing this level**

A **head of portfolio** leads a team of digital portfolio managers and is
accountable for ensuring the portfolio of digital projects delivers the best
possible outcomes. It is the most senior level in this role's ladder, and it
carries line management and team leadership responsibility that the levels
below it do not.

At this role level, a head of portfolio will:

- lead the tracking and governing of digital projects
- ensure the right controls are used
- address risks and deal with, or escalate, issues
- provide leaders with insights into the portfolio's future so they can make
  informed decisions
- build and lead a diverse, skilled and motivated team of digital portfolio
  managers
- ensure the portfolio team is meeting the needs of the organisation
- support the professional development of the portfolio community, including
  line management and coaching

This level names six skills, assessed in the competency matrix below:

- Agile working
- Communicating analysis and insight
- Governance and assurance
- Planning
- Stakeholder relationship management
- Strategic thinking

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of portfolio should show an awareness of Agile methodology and the ways
to apply its principles in practice, take an open-minded approach, explain why
iteration matters, and iterate quickly.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Leading a team that governs a whole portfolio of
digital projects means applying Agile principles to portfolio-level decisions
without needing another person to interpret them first, so a strong,
independent grasp is the minimum, even though the framework's own wording
stops short of asking this level to teach Agile working as a subject in its
own right.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain why an iterative approach might suit a portfolio of
  digital projects, or defaults to a fixed, plan-everything-up-front approach
  without reflecting on it.
- 1 — Beginner: Can recite Agile terms but applies them mechanically — for
  example, insists on a standard sprint cadence for every project in the
  portfolio regardless of what each one actually needs.
- 2 — Intermediate: Adapts an Agile approach to an individual project with
  guidance from someone more experienced, and can explain the reasoning after
  the fact.
- 3 — Advanced: Chooses and adjusts an iterative approach for the portfolio
  unsupervised, revisits a plan quickly when new information arrives, and
  stays open-minded when a project team wants to work differently from the
  rest of the portfolio.
- 4 — Expert: Does all of the above and is the person other portfolio managers
  ask when their own iterative approach has stalled, and has changed how the
  wider portfolio team works based on what they have learned from iterating.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a real portfolio decision they revisited
  after new information came in, and what changed as a result.
- In a structured interview, ask how they would respond to a project team in
  the portfolio that wants to work in a way that does not match the
  portfolio's standard cadence.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate changing direction quickly in
  response to evidence, rather than sticking to an original plan.

### Skill: Communicating analysis and insight

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of portfolio should communicate relevant, compelling stories using the
most appropriate medium, and make complex recommendations at both a tactical
and a strategic level.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This level exists to give organisational leaders
insight into the portfolio's future so they can make informed decisions, which
means translating complex portfolio analysis into something a senior audience
can act on, independently and without a communications specialist rewriting
it first.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot turn portfolio data into a narrative a non-specialist
  audience could follow.
- 1 — Beginner: Presents portfolio status as raw figures or a status report,
  with support needed to draw out what it means for decision-makers.
- 2 — Intermediate: Builds a straightforward update for a known audience with
  some guidance on what to include and what to leave out.
- 3 — Advanced: Chooses the medium and the level of detail to suit a senior
  audience unprompted, and turns a complex, multi-project analysis into a
  recommendation a leader can act on at a glance.
- 4 — Expert: Does all of the above, and coaches other portfolio managers on
  how to turn their own project analysis into a compelling story for leaders.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a real portfolio report, dashboard, or briefing paper the candidate
  has produced for a senior audience.
- Ask a structured interview question: how would you explain to an executive,
  in five minutes, why three projects in a portfolio are at risk for three
  different reasons?
- Observe the candidate presenting portfolio insight in a real or simulated
  leadership forum, and note what they chose to include and leave out.

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of portfolio should develop appropriate governance and assurance
processes, identify and recommend improvements to those processes, and support
others in following or applying them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Leading tracking and governing of digital projects and
ensuring the right controls are used means designing and refining governance
independently across a portfolio, not merely operating a process someone else
built.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has no experience designing or operating a governance or
  assurance process for digital projects.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an existing governance process step by step, with
  supervision, and cannot yet explain why a particular control exists.
- 2 — Intermediate: Operates an established governance process for a single
  project with some guidance, and flags where a control feels wrong for the
  situation.
- 3 — Advanced: Designs or substantially adapts a governance or assurance
  process for a portfolio, recommends specific improvements based on what is
  and is not working, and does this unsupervised.
- 4 — Expert: Does all of the above, and actively supports other portfolio
  managers in applying governance and assurance processes correctly,
  including resolving disagreements about how a control should be applied.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a governance or assurance process they
  designed or changed, and what evidence told them the change was needed.
- Review a governance framework, RAID log template, or assurance checklist
  the candidate has authored or substantially revised.
- Ask a referee whether other portfolio managers came to the candidate for
  help applying a governance process, and for an example.

### Skill: Planning

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of portfolio should understand the environment and prioritise the
highest-value tasks, use data to inform planning, manage complex internal and
external dependencies, provide delivery confidence, remove blockers or develop
a plan for difficult situations, and ensure teams plan appropriately for their
capacity.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This level is accountable for delivery confidence
across a whole portfolio and for removing blockers that a single project
manager could not remove alone, which requires planning independently at
portfolio scale rather than executing a plan someone else set.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how work is currently prioritised across the
  projects they are responsible for.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a plan set by someone else, and needs help
  identifying which dependencies matter.
- 2 — Intermediate: Builds a plan for a single project with guidance, and can
  point to the data behind a prioritisation decision when asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Manages complex dependencies across multiple projects in the
  portfolio unsupervised, gives leaders a credible view of delivery
  confidence, and removes blockers that individual project managers could not
  clear themselves.
- 4 — Expert: Does all of the above, and sets the standard other portfolio
  managers use for how their own teams plan for capacity, stepping in only
  where a plan has broken down badly enough to need portfolio-level authority.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a real dependency they managed across two
  or more projects, and how they resolved a conflict between them.
- In a structured interview, ask how they would give a leadership team an
  honest delivery-confidence view when two projects in the portfolio disagree
  about a shared deadline.
- Review a portfolio plan, roadmap, or dependency map the candidate has
  produced or maintained.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of portfolio should direct the stakeholder relationship strategy for
their teams, ensure stakeholders' objectives are set and support teams to meet
them, and influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to resolve issues
and enable progress.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's own wording is directive rather than
participative at this level — "direct the stakeholder relationship strategy",
"negotiate with senior stakeholders" — which describes someone who sets the
approach for others and is trusted to resolve the hardest relationships
personally, the definition of full mastery on this scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has no experience managing a relationship with a stakeholder
  outside their immediate team.
- 1 — Beginner: Attends stakeholder meetings but relies on someone more senior
  to lead the conversation and decide what to say.
- 2 — Intermediate: Manages a single stakeholder relationship with guidance,
  and can explain what that stakeholder needs from the portfolio.
- 3 — Advanced: Manages several stakeholder relationships independently, sets
  clear objectives with them, and resolves routine disagreements without
  escalation.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the stakeholder relationship strategy for a team of
  portfolio managers, personally negotiates with senior stakeholders to
  unblock progress, and coaches others in the team on how to handle a
  difficult stakeholder relationship.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a negotiation with a senior stakeholder that
  was going badly, and how they turned it around.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate setting the stakeholder
  approach for a team, rather than managing one relationship themselves.
- In a structured interview, present a scenario where two senior stakeholders
  want conflicting things from the portfolio, and ask how they would handle
  it.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of portfolio should define strategies and policies, providing guidance
to others on working in the strategic context, and evaluate current strategies
to ensure business requirements are being met and exceeded where possible.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Providing guidance to others on working in the
strategic context" is explicitly about developing other people's strategic
thinking, not only exercising it personally, which is the marker of full
mastery on this scale rather than independent, unsupported practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot connect a portfolio decision to the organisation's wider
  strategy.
- 1 — Beginner: Repeats the stated strategy when asked, but cannot yet apply
  it to a real portfolio decision without help.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies an existing strategy to a portfolio decision with
  guidance from someone more senior.
- 3 — Advanced: Defines strategy or policy for the portfolio independently,
  and evaluates whether the current strategy is meeting business requirements.
- 4 — Expert: Does all of the above, and actively guides other portfolio
  managers on how to think and act strategically, including when a
  well-established strategy needs to be challenged.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a strategy or policy they defined for a
  portfolio, and the evidence that led them to define it that way.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has coached someone else on connecting
  their day-to-day work to organisational strategy, and for an example.
- In a structured interview, ask how they would evaluate whether the
  portfolio's current strategy is still fit for purpose, and what they would
  do if it were not.

appointment, and record the reasoning behind the overall determination, not
