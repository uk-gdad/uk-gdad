# Test Manager - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is a competency assessment for the quality assurance testing (QAT) role of
test manager, as defined by the UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD)
Profession Capability Framework. It is written for the person doing the
assessing — a line manager, a hiring panel, or a reviewer — judging whether a
candidate or employee possesses the baseline abilities this role level
requires.

Test manager is the first of two levels in this role's ladder: it already
carries ownership of the QAT strategy and its delivery, ahead of head of test
above it. Use this assessment to judge readiness for that level of
responsibility — for a hiring decision, a promotion board, onboarding into the
role, or a periodic capability check against the framework.

This assessment is one input among several into that judgement, never the
whole of it. Read `## Advisory` below before you use it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you are assessing
against what this role level actually requires, not a general impression of
seniority. Then gather whatever evidence exists before you start scoring: a
CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task, direct
observation, or references. Trying to score from memory after the fact
produces worse ratings than scoring while the evidence is in front of you.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating you give must trace to
something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess
— an honest gap is more useful to the next stage of the process than a
confident guess dressed up as a score.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, using
the same evidence types where you can. Where possible, have more than one
assessor score independently and compare notes afterwards, especially for a
hiring or promotion decision — independent scoring catches where one
assessor's impression has drifted from the evidence.

**Fairness.** This is an unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument. Used
inconsistently, or scored on anything other than demonstrated, job-related
evidence, it can produce unlawful adverse impact against people with
protected characteristics. Consistent, evidence-based administration by more
than one assessor materially reduces that risk. See
[Fair and lawful use](#fair-and-lawful-use) in the project specification for
what this means in practice, and never let a rating be influenced by
anything other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same five-point
scale. This is a **proficiency** scale — how well the person does the thing,
on the evidence available — not a frequency scale.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Test and quality planning.** A person rated 1 (Beginner)
can produce a test plan from a template with someone checking it before it is
used. A person rated 3 (Advanced) creates and manages several quality testing
plans and strategies at once, unsupported, and can explain the trade-offs
behind each one. A person rated 4 (Expert) does that across teams they do not
manage directly, using data to show why one team's approach should change,
and other test managers ask them to review a strategy before it goes to a
delivery board. The difference between 3 and 4 is not effort — it is whether
the person's influence and judgement are being sought and used beyond their
own immediate team.

## About this role level

A test manager takes ownership of the quality assurance and testing (QAT)
strategy for their area, as part of a broader risk-based approach to
delivery. This role level takes ownership of delivery, creates the strategy,
and leads its implementation — it is not a hands-on testing role so much as
the role that decides how testing is done and makes sure that decision holds.

This role has two levels, in order: **test manager** (this level) and **head
of test** above it, which owns policy and strategy for the whole test team
across an organisation. Test manager is the level at which someone first
carries full ownership of a QAT strategy and its delivery; head of test is
where that ownership becomes organisation-wide.

At this role level, you will:

- be responsible for test improvement and optimisation
- potentially promote and advocate test capabilities internally and outside
  government
- take responsibility for talent, succession planning and supplier
  management
- make important decisions relating to test within the context of the
  delivery environment

The framework names six skills at this level:

- Communicating between the technical and non-technical
- Designing and executing tests
- Managing, reporting and resolving defects
- Test analysis
- Test and quality planning
- Test engineering

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What the framework expects at this level**

Mediate between people and strengthen relationships, choosing the right way
to communicate for stakeholders at every level. Manage stakeholder
expectations and moderate difficult discussions about high-risk, complex
topics, including when timescales are tight. Speak on behalf of, and
represent, the testing community to large audiences inside and outside the
organisation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Test manager owns delivery-level stakeholder
relationships and is expected to lead difficult, high-risk conversations
under time pressure without a more senior colleague stepping in; full
external representation of the community to large audiences matures further
at head of test.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot translate a testing issue into terms a non-technical
  stakeholder understands, and avoids or defers conversations about risk.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains straightforward test results to a non-technical
  colleague when asked, but needs a more senior colleague to lead the
  conversation once risk or timescales become contested.
- 2 — Intermediate: Runs routine stakeholder updates unsupported and
  explains test status and defect trends in plain language, but hands off a
  discussion once it becomes a contested disagreement about risk or a
  delivery date.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads difficult conversations about high risk or complex
  trade-offs with delivery stakeholders under constrained timescales,
  adapts the message to the audience without being asked, and de-escalates
  disagreements about scope or risk appetite without escalating further.
- 4 — Expert: Regularly represents the testing community to large audiences
  inside and outside the organisation, and is sought out by other teams to
  mediate contested disagreements about risk between technical and
  non-technical colleagues.

**Evidence to gather**

- Structured interview: ask the candidate to describe a time they had to
  tell a delivery lead that a release was not ready to ship, and how the
  conversation went.
- Work sample: a stakeholder-facing status report or risk summary they have
  written, reviewed for whether a non-technical reader could act on it.
- Observed scenario: give the candidate a set of test results and defect
  trends for a fictional go/no-go decision, and ask them to explain what it
  means in plain language.
- Referee question: ask a referee to describe an occasion the candidate
  mediated a disagreement between technical and non-technical colleagues
  about risk.

### Skill: Designing and executing tests

**What the framework expects at this level**

Set up suitable test environments. Influence and guide which test types and
techniques are used to mitigate risk early. Lead others in designing,
building, maintaining and executing tests that align to user needs and
requirements. Contribute to developing and implementing standards for
designing and executing tests. Improve test types and techniques through a
structured process.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The level owns delivery of the test strategy and
leads others in test design and execution, which requires independent,
standard-shaping practice; setting standards across the whole organisation
is head of test's ceiling, not test manager's.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot set up a test environment or choose a test type without
  step-by-step direction, and test designs miss basic scenarios.
- 1 — Beginner: Sets up straightforward test environments and designs tests
  for well-understood functionality with close guidance, and needs a senior
  colleague to choose which test types fit a given risk.
- 2 — Intermediate: Selects and applies suitable test types and techniques
  for most features with some guidance, and can build and execute a test
  suite that someone else reviewed first.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads a team through designing, building and executing
  tests that mitigate risk early without needing sign-off first, and
  contributes real changes to the team's test standards based on what
  worked or failed.
- 4 — Expert: Runs a structured process for improving test types and
  techniques over time, and is the person other teams ask for guidance on
  which technique fits a novel risk.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: a test strategy or approach document they authored or led,
  showing the reasoning behind technique choices.
- Structured interview: ask them to describe a test environment or
  technique they introduced and what problem it solved.
- Document review: review meeting notes or standards documents they
  contributed to.
- Observed scenario: give a short feature description and ask which test
  types and techniques they would use, and why.

### Skill: Managing, reporting and resolving defects

**What the framework expects at this level**

Lead and coach others in improving test and defect management processes.
Support others in assessing complex and challenging defects across the
organisation. Lead and coach others in using defect patterns and trends to
make tactical and strategic recommendations. Influence improvements to
quality processes, informed by defect patterns and trends.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The level explicitly leads and coaches others on
defect management and turns defect trends into recommendations that reach
beyond a single team, which is independent, organisation-facing practice
rather than supervised practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Logs defects inconsistently and cannot explain why a defect was
  prioritised the way it was.
- 1 — Beginner: Logs and triages straightforward defects with support, and
  needs help interpreting a defect trend or handling a contested severity
  rating.
- 2 — Intermediate: Manages a team's defect backlog independently and
  triages routine defects correctly, but asks a more senior colleague for
  help with an unusually complex or cross-team defect.
- 3 — Advanced: Coaches others in improving defect management practice,
  supports colleagues elsewhere in the organisation in assessing complex or
  contested defects, and turns defect trends into a tactical recommendation
  a delivery team acts on.
- 4 — Expert: Uses defect patterns and trends across multiple teams to make
  strategic recommendations that change organisational quality processes.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: a defect trend report or root-cause analysis they produced.
- Structured interview: ask about a time defect data changed a decision
  beyond their own team.
- Referee question: ask a referee whether the candidate has improved
  another team's defect management practice, and how.
- Document review: review a defect triage or severity policy they wrote or
  amended.

### Skill: Test analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

Lead work with stakeholders across teams to determine which functional and
non-functional quality characteristics add value. Decide if an approach
needs to change based on effort and risk. Make sure test needs are dealt
with early. Use multiple techniques to analyse complex information and
identify risks. Coach others in test analysis.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The level leads cross-team work to decide which
quality characteristics matter and coaches others in analysis, which needs
independent, cross-team judgement rather than analysis confined to a single
team's features.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say which functional or non-functional characteristics
  matter most for a given feature without being told.
- 1 — Beginner: Identifies obvious functional risks with guidance, and
  misses non-functional characteristics such as performance, accessibility
  or security unless prompted.
- 2 — Intermediate: Analyses risk for a single team's features
  independently, using one or two techniques, and adjusts the approach when
  asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Works with stakeholders across teams to decide which
  quality characteristics matter, uses multiple techniques to find risks
  others missed, and coaches a colleague through their first risk-based
  analysis.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the organisation's approach to identifying which
  quality characteristics matter for a given delivery, and is the person
  other test analysts are coached by.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: a risk-based test analysis or quality characteristics
  assessment they produced.
- Structured interview: ask them to describe a time they changed a test
  approach based on effort and risk, and what evidence drove the change.
- Observed scenario: give a short set of requirements and ask which quality
  characteristics they would test for and why, then ask what they would
  drop under time pressure.
- Referee question: ask about an occasion the candidate coached someone
  else in test analysis.

### Skill: Test and quality planning

**What the framework expects at this level**

Create and manage multiple quality testing plans, approaches and
strategies. Lead and guide multiple teams in adopting quality testing
strategy. Advocate for early quality testing involvement in organisational
delivery processes. Guide teams across an organisation in optimising
quality testing approaches, plans and strategies, using appropriate data.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This skill's own wording already describes managing
several plans at once and guiding teams across an organisation, so
independent, multi-team practice is the baseline expected of a test manager;
head of test sets the organisation's policy behind that practice rather than
doing markedly more of the activity itself.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot produce a test plan without a template and step-by-step
  help.
- 1 — Beginner: Produces a test plan for a single, well-understood piece of
  work with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Creates and manages a quality testing plan or approach
  for their own team independently, and adapts it when circumstances
  change.
- 3 — Advanced: Creates and manages multiple quality testing plans,
  approaches and strategies at once, and guides more than one team in
  adopting a shared quality testing strategy.
- 4 — Expert: Guides teams across the organisation in optimising their
  quality testing approaches using data, and is the person other test
  managers ask to review a strategy before it goes to a delivery board.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: a test strategy or quality plan covering more than one team
  or workstream.
- Structured interview: ask about a time they used data to change how more
  than one team approached quality testing.
- Document review: review evidence of advocacy for early quality
  involvement, such as a delivery process document they influenced.
- Referee question: ask a referee whether the candidate has guided teams
  other than their own on quality testing strategy.

### Skill: Test engineering

**What the framework expects at this level**

Explain why testing processes, environments and tools are important. Follow
test engineering practices and standards, with support. Support the
maintenance of automated tests and tools required for testing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's own wording for this skill at
test manager is explaining, following and supporting rather than leading or
setting direction — this is a supporting skill for the management track, so
its baseline sits a point below the level's other skills.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain why test environments, tools or engineering
  processes matter, and cannot follow an established process without being
  walked through it each time.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains, in general terms, why test engineering practices
  matter, but needs support to follow them correctly.
- 2 — Intermediate: Follows established test engineering practices and
  standards with some support, and can explain their purpose to others.
- 3 — Advanced: Follows test engineering practices independently, spots
  when a process is not fit for purpose, and contributes to maintaining
  automated tests and tooling without being asked.
- 4 — Expert: Sets or substantially revises the test engineering standards
  the team follows, and maintains automated tests and tooling as a matter
  of course.

**Evidence to gather**

- Structured interview: ask them to explain why a particular test
  environment or tool matters for their current testing approach.
- Work sample: evidence of maintaining or supporting automated tests or
  tooling, such as a change to a test pipeline.
- Observed scenario: describe a flaky automated test and ask how they would
  approach it and who they would involve.
