# Junior Accessibility Specialist - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a
reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities the UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) Profession Capability
Framework expects of a **junior accessibility specialist**. Use it for a
hiring decision, a probation or onboarding check, or a general capability
review against the framework.

The framework describes this level as someone who learns on the job by
assisting with accessibility testing, under supervision, and is explicitly
not yet responsible for assistive technology testing. The matrix below is
built to that description, not to a more senior one — do not mark someone
down for needing supervision; the framework says they should.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole
of one. Read `## Advisory` before you use it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you know what this level actually expects — and, just as importantly, what it
does not. Then gather whatever evidence you can before you start: a CV or
portfolio, a structured interview, a short work sample or task, direct
observation of the person working, or references from someone who has
supervised them.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating you give must trace back
to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess
— an honest "not enough evidence yet" is more useful to the process than a
number you cannot defend.

**Be consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
using the same evidence types where you can. For a hiring or promotion
decision, have more than one assessor score independently and then compare
notes, rather than scoring together from the start.

**Stay fair.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact between candidates.
Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. See [Fair and lawful use](#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification, and never let a rating be influenced by
anything other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This rates **how well** the person does the thing, on the evidence available
— not how often they do it, and not how they feel about it.

**Worked example**, using Technical understanding (accessibility), a skill
this level names:

- A candidate who knows accessibility legislation exists but cannot say where
  to find it, and cannot name a common accessibility issue in HTML without
  prompting, sits at **1 — Beginner**.
- A candidate who runs the standard automated and manual checks unsupported —
  headings, alt text, contrast, keyboard navigation — and writes up findings
  another person could act on, sits at **2 — Intermediate**. This is the
  baseline this assessment sets for this skill at this level.
- A candidate who does all of that and also spots issues the automated tools
  miss, and proposes a fix rather than just naming the problem, is already
  working above this level's baseline, at **3 — Advanced**.

## About this role level

**The role: accessibility specialist.** An accessibility specialist provides
support, advice and guidance to other roles in the GDAD profession about how
to create accessible digital services. The role uses knowledge of
accessibility guidelines, provides technical expertise, and brings the voice
of disabled users to ensure that product teams make digital services that
can be used by everyone.

**This level: junior accessibility specialist.** The framework describes this
level as someone who learns on the job by assisting with accessibility
testing. At this level, the framework expects the person to:

- develop skills while working under supervision to provide accessibility
  testing
- understand and use different types of testing
- be aware of, but not responsible for, assistive technology testing
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government accessibility community

**Where this level sits.** Accessibility specialist has four levels:

1. **Junior accessibility specialist** — assessed here
2. Accessibility specialist
3. Senior accessibility specialist
4. Head of accessibility

**What is deliberately absent.** The framework is explicit that this level is
"aware of but not responsible for" assistive technology testing. That is not
a gap to penalise — it is the framework drawing a line around what a person
at this level should be trusted with unsupervised. Do not score someone down
for limited independent assistive-technology experience; do check, in the
evidence log, whether they show promise for the level above.

**The five skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating information
2. Consultancy
3. Technical understanding (accessibility)
4. Testing
5. User focus

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Communicating information

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a junior accessibility specialist to listen to the
needs of design and business stakeholders and interpret what they are told,
and to take part in discussions within a multidisciplinary team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Listening and contributing when part of a
supervised, structured discussion is realistic for someone new to the role;
independently managing stakeholder communication is not expected until
accessibility specialist, where the framework adds advocacy and shaping
communications for the audience.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot accurately restate a stakeholder's need after hearing it,
  and does not contribute in team discussions even when directly invited.
- 1 — Beginner: Listens to a stakeholder and can restate their need
  accurately when asked; contributes to multidisciplinary discussions when
  prompted.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently interprets what a stakeholder is asking for
  without needing it re-explained; volunteers relevant points in team
  discussions without being asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Notices and raises a stakeholder need or a gap in the
  discussion that others have missed; is sought out for input.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes how the team runs accessibility discussions and coaches
  others in reading stakeholder needs accurately — well above this level's
  expectation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give a short written brief describing a stakeholder request and ask the
  candidate to identify, in their own words, what the stakeholder actually
  needs.
- Observe the candidate in a stand-up, design crit or similar
  multidisciplinary discussion and note whether they contribute unprompted.
- Ask a referee, ideally a supervisor, how the candidate behaves in team
  discussions.
- Structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you had to explain an
  accessibility finding to someone who was not a specialist. What did you
  say, and how did you know it had landed?"

### Skill: Consultancy

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to show an understanding of the need for
consulting in the role and its purpose; to work under guidance to identify
what advice, guidance or recommendations might be appropriate, usually by
referring to previous work by others; and to show an understanding of how to
frame problems that need to be solved.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording — "under guidance",
"usually by referring to previous work by others" — describes someone who
recognises good precedent rather than someone who originates advice
unsupported. Originating advice from specialist knowledge is the expectation
from accessibility specialist upwards.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain why giving advice and guidance is part of this
  role, and does not know where existing guidance is kept.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain the purpose of consultancy in the role; when
  asked for advice, looks for established guidance or asks a more senior
  colleague rather than guessing.
- 2 — Intermediate: Finds and adapts relevant prior guidance to a new but
  similar situation unsupported, and can frame a straightforward accessibility
  problem clearly enough for someone else to act on.
- 3 — Advanced: Gives sound, defensible advice on a problem with no obvious
  precedent, and can explain the reasoning — already working towards the
  level above.
- 4 — Expert: A source of consultancy advice others draw on, and writes new
  guidance rather than only following it — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give a written accessibility question that is not covered by an existing,
  documented pattern, and see whether the candidate looks for established
  guidance, asks for help, or guesses.
- Ask the candidate to describe a real occasion when they gave advice under
  supervision, and what precedent or guidance they used.
- Review any written recommendation or note the candidate has produced, if
  available.

### Skill: Technical understanding (accessibility)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects demonstrated knowledge of some details of accessibility
standards and legislation, and knowledge of where to find more; demonstrated
knowledge of basic HTML, CSS and JavaScript and the accessibility issues
commonly associated with them; the ability to perform basic automated and
manual checks on websites and documents and communicate findings and fixes;
advocacy for the people affected by accessibility barriers within the context
of the work; and demonstrated knowledge of using some assistive technology.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is the core technical skill of the role, and
the framework's wording for it goes beyond awareness — "perform basic
automated and manual checks" and "communicate findings and fixes" describe
actual, unsupported doing, not just an understanding of concepts. That sets
this skill's baseline a point higher than the more supervised skills in this
matrix, even though the person is at the most junior level of the role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot run even a basic automated accessibility check, and cannot
  identify an obvious HTML accessibility issue when shown one.
- 1 — Beginner: Knows accessibility standards and legislation exist but needs
  help finding them; can name a common accessibility issue but does not
  consistently spot one unaided.
- 2 — Intermediate: Runs the standard automated and manual checks unsupported
  — headings, alternative text, colour contrast, keyboard navigation — and
  writes up findings clearly enough for someone else to act on; knows enough
  HTML, CSS and JavaScript to explain why an issue occurs; has used at least
  one assistive technology.
- 3 — Advanced: Performs detailed manual checks beyond the basic set, spots
  issues automated tools miss, and proposes a fix a developer could implement
  without further guidance; comfortable with more than one assistive
  technology.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the standard other people's audits are checked against, and
  trains colleagues in technical accessibility testing and assistive
  technology use.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: give a short webpage or document and ask the candidate to
  identify and write up its accessibility issues.
- Observe the candidate working through a checklist using a screen reader or
  other assistive technology.
- Structured interview question: ask them to explain a Web Content
  Accessibility Guidelines success criterion in their own words, and why it
  matters to a real user.
- Review a real audit, checklist result or finding the candidate has
  produced, if one exists.

### Skill: Testing

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to correctly execute test scripts under
supervision, and to understand the role of testing and how it works.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording is "under supervision" —
executing a defined script accurately, with support available, is what this
level asks for. Reviewing specifications and defining test conditions
independently is the expectation from accessibility specialist upwards.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot follow a written test script accurately even with
  guidance.
- 1 — Beginner: Executes a given test script accurately under supervision,
  and can explain in general terms what testing is for.
- 2 — Intermediate: Executes test scripts unsupported, and flags an ambiguous
  result for review rather than guessing at it.
- 3 — Advanced: Adapts a test script sensibly when it does not quite fit the
  thing being tested, and can explain why the adaptation was reasonable.
- 4 — Expert: Designs test scripts and coaches others to execute them
  reliably — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Observe the candidate running a defined accessibility test script on a
  sample page or document.
- Ask what they do when a test result is unclear or borderline.
- Ask a referee how consistently the candidate follows agreed test scripts.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate
- [ ] 3 — Advanced
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects the person to show an awareness or understanding of
user experience analysis and its principles, and to explain the purpose of
user stories and the focus on user needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's wording is "show an awareness" and
"explain the purpose" — understanding and explaining, not yet independently
identifying user needs from evidence, which arrives at accessibility
specialist.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain what a user story is or why user needs matter to
  accessibility work.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain, in general terms, the purpose of user stories
  and why user experience analysis matters.
- 2 — Intermediate: Connects a specific accessibility finding to the user
  need it affects, with some support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently reasons from a stated user need to a testing
  priority or a fix recommendation — already working towards the level above.
- 4 — Expert: Brings first-hand or directly observed evidence of disabled
  users' experience into the team's decisions, and coaches others to do the
  same.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to take a real or example accessibility finding and
  explain which user it affects and how.
- Ask whether they have observed user research with disabled participants,
  and what they took from it.
- Structured interview question: ask about a user story they have read
  recently and what user need it was written to address.

every skill above is rated at or above its baseline target. One skill below
target is not automatically a fail at this junior level, where close
supervision is the expectation rather than the exception — weigh how central
the skill is to the level's accountabilities (Technical understanding
(accessibility) is the most central here), consider whether the shortfall is
coachable quickly after appointment, and record your reasoning in the
evidence log rather than relying on the number alone.
