# Head of Service Design - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the
baseline a UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) head of service design needs. It is
written for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a reviewer — not for the
person being assessed.

A service designer designs the end-to-end journey of a service, so that a user can
complete their goal and government can deliver its policy intent. A head of service
design is an expert practitioner with broad industry experience, who can define and
assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others. This assessment
measures demonstrated capability against the framework's own description of that
level, across the seven skills the framework names for it.

It is one input among several into a hiring, promotion or development decision, never
the whole of one — see `## Advisory` for why.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for educational
purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed
or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this form must never be the sole basis
for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision. Before using it,
consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational
psychology or legal compliance colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything. Gather
whatever evidence exists: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or
task, direct observation, or references. This is the most senior of the six levels the
framework publishes for this role — expect evidence about influencing organisational
and design strategy, defining and assuring best practice across an organisation, and
building the conditions and talent pipeline for designers to work well, rather than
evidence about a single service or team.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something the
person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to evidence for a
rating, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, using the same
kinds of evidence where possible. For a hiring or promotion decision, have more than one
assessor score independently before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than demonstrated,
job-related evidence. See [Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification for what this rules out.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This rates how well the person does the thing, on the evidence available — not how often
they do it. A worked example, using this level's skill of Leading design:

- **3 — Advanced**: they set direction for design work within their own team and
  mentored the designers in it, without yet setting a vision beyond that team.
- **4 — Expert**: they set a vision for design across multiple teams or the whole
  organisation, worked effectively with senior leaders across the organisation or the
  wider public sector, and measurably improved design maturity in the organisation and
  its partners as a result.

A head of service design's baseline for this skill, below, is 4.

## About this role level

**The role: service designer.** A service designer designs the end-to-end journey of a
service. This helps a user complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent.
The work may involve creating, or changing, transactions, products and content across
both digital and offline channels provided by different parts of government.

**The level: head of service design.** The framework describes this level as an expert
practitioner with broad industry experience, who can define and assure best practice
while influencing, leading and mentoring others. It is the most senior of six levels
the framework publishes for this role — the full ladder, in order, is associate
service designer, junior service designer, service designer, senior service designer,
lead service designer, and head of service design. At this level, you influence both
design and organisational strategy and priorities; collaborate with counterpart
colleagues across government; and focus on ensuring the right conditions and
environment for designers to work effectively.

**The skills this level names**, in the summary's order: Design communication;
Designing for everyone; Designing strategically; Designing together; Evidence-based
design; Iterative design; and Leading design. Five of these — Design communication,
Designing for everyone, Designing together, Evidence-based design, and Iterative
design — carry text identical to the lead service designer level below; only
Designing strategically and Leading design have wording distinct to this level. Where
the text repeats, this level's real difference shows in its own accountability bullets
above — influencing organisational strategy, collaborating across government, and
shaping the conditions designers work in — not in a claim the repeated skill text does
not make.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a head of service design to gain support for design decisions and
design strategy from senior leaders; communicate design strategy across organisational
boundaries; coach others in how to effectively communicate design; and develop or
introduce more effective methods of communicating design and working in the open. This
wording is identical to the lead service designer level below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The text repeats the lead service designer level's wording word
for word, and that level's own baseline for this skill was already 4 — Expert — so the
honest baseline here stays the same; this level's real step up shows in its own
accountability to influence organisational strategy and collaborate across government,
not in this specific skill's text.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not gained support for a design decision from a senior leader.
- 1 — Beginner: has presented a design decision to a senior leader with someone else
  leading the conversation.
- 2 — Intermediate: has gained a senior leader's support for one design decision, with
  guidance preparing the case.
- 3 — Advanced: independently gains senior leader support for design decisions and
  communicates design strategy across an organisational boundary.
- 4 — Expert: coaches other designers in how to communicate design effectively across
  an organisation, and has introduced a new method of communicating design or working
  in the open that spread beyond one team.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about gaining organisation-wide support
  for a piece of design strategy, not just one design decision."
- Ask for an example of coaching designers in more than one team on how to
  communicate design.
- Ask for a method of communicating design, or working in the open, that they
  introduced at an organisational scale.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a head of service design to measurably improve the practice of
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable design in multiple teams or
across the organisation; and help others understand and mitigate the influence of
privilege and power on the design and delivery of content and services. This wording
is identical to the lead service designer level below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. As with Design communication, the text repeats the level below's
wording, so the honest baseline stays at 4 rather than implying a higher figure the
text does not claim — though this level's own remit for the "right conditions and
environment for designers to work effectively" is where the real seniority in this
area shows.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not improved inclusive design practice beyond their own work.
- 1 — Beginner: has raised an accessibility issue in their own team.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped one team improve one aspect of inclusive design
  practice, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently advises multiple teams on inclusive, accessible and
  sustainable design.
- 4 — Expert: has measurably improved inclusive, accessible or sustainable design
  practice across the organisation, and has shaped the conditions that make that
  practice possible for designers organisation-wide.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a measurable, organisation-wide
  improvement to inclusive or accessible design practice you were responsible for."
- Ask for an example of shaping the conditions or environment that let designers
  practise inclusive design more consistently.
- Ask a referee about the candidate's influence on accessibility or inclusion
  practice across the organisation, not just one team.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a head of service design to work with others to ensure design
becomes part of the development and implementation of the organisation's strategy;
work with leaders to ensure design is included in policy, technology and delivery
processes early enough to inform effective decision making; and enable the use of
patterns and components across the organisation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This is the one place in the matrix where the framework's own
text visibly changes from the lead service designer level below: instead of helping a
team align to organisational goals, this level embeds design into how the
organisation's strategy itself is made, and enables patterns and components across the
whole organisation — organisation-facing, mastery-level ownership.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not connected design to organisational strategy formation.
- 1 — Beginner: has contributed design input to a strategy document someone else led.
- 2 — Intermediate: has worked with leaders to include design considerations in one
  policy, technology or delivery decision, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently works with leaders to ensure design is included early
  in policy, technology or delivery processes.
- 4 — Expert: ensures design becomes part of how the organisation's strategy itself is
  developed and implemented, and enables the use of design patterns and components
  across the organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about getting design included in how
  your organisation's strategy was actually made, not just responding to it after the
  fact."
- Ask for an example of enabling patterns or components to be reused across the
  organisation, and what changed as a result.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate is involved by leaders early in policy,
  technology or delivery decisions.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a head of service design to effectively plan and run design
sessions that include senior leaders or stakeholders; help teams connect and work
across organisational boundaries; coach other designers in how to involve stakeholders
of any level in the design process; and develop or introduce more effective ways to
design together. This wording is identical to the lead service designer level below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The text repeats the level below's wording word for word, so the
baseline stays the same as that level; the real difference at this level is scale —
across counterpart colleagues across government, not one organisation's teams.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not run a design session that included a senior leader.
- 1 — Beginner: has attended a design session with senior leaders present, without
  running any part of it.
- 2 — Intermediate: has co-run a design session with senior leaders present, with
  support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently plans and runs design sessions that include senior
  leaders or stakeholders, and helps teams connect across organisational boundaries.
- 4 — Expert: coaches designers across the organisation in how to involve stakeholders
  of any level, and has introduced a way of designing together that other
  organisations or counterpart teams across government adopted.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about running a design session with
  counterparts from another government organisation. What made it work?"
- Ask for an example of coaching a designer in a different team on stakeholder
  involvement.
- Ask for a new way of designing together that spread beyond the candidate's own
  organisation.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a head of service design to embed a practice of hypothesis-driven
design in a team; guide the organisation in how to use evidence to improve services;
analyse, synthesise and use evidence to improve the way the organisation works; and
ensure user insights are shared with and used by the wider organisation and across
government. This wording is identical to the lead service designer level below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The text repeats the level below's wording word for word, so the
baseline stays the same; at this level the accountability is to make this true across
government, working with counterpart colleagues in other organisations, rather than
within one organisation alone.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not embedded a hypothesis-driven design practice in a team.
- 1 — Beginner: has used a design hypothesis in their own work.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped one team adopt hypothesis-driven design, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently embeds hypothesis-driven design in a team, and uses
  evidence to improve how a team works.
- 4 — Expert: guides the organisation in how to use evidence to improve services, and
  ensures user insights are shared and used across government, not just within one
  organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about ensuring a user insight was shared
  and used beyond your own organisation."
- Ask for an example of guiding organisational leaders on how to use evidence to
  improve a service.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has influenced how another government
  organisation uses evidence.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a head of service design to embed the practice of iterative
design and agile working in teams or the wider organisation; lead on prototyping
complex ideas at any fidelity; help teams adapt quickly to changes in requirements,
priorities or user needs; and iterate delivery processes or team structures to help
teams work effectively. This wording is identical to the lead service designer level
below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The text repeats the level below's wording word for word, so the
baseline stays the same; the difference at this level is the scope in which the
practice is embedded — the whole organisation and its conditions for designers, not
one team.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not embedded an iterative design practice beyond their own work.
- 1 — Beginner: has followed an iterative design practice someone else set up.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped one team adopt iterative practice, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently leads on prototyping complex ideas at any fidelity, and
  helps teams adapt quickly to changing requirements.
- 4 — Expert: embeds iterative design and agile working across the organisation, and
  has iterated the delivery processes or team structures that shape how designers
  across the organisation work.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about changing a delivery process or team
  structure at an organisational level to help design teams work more effectively."
- Ask for an example of leading on a prototype at an unusually high or low fidelity
  for a strategic decision.
- Ask a referee about the candidate's influence on how iterative design is practised
  across the organisation.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a head of service design to set a vision for and lead design across
multiple teams or a whole organisation; work effectively with senior leaders across the
organisation or wider public sector; support a culture of learning and experimentation
in the organisation; and iterate and measurably improve design maturity within the
organisation and its partners.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Setting a vision across a whole organisation, working with
senior leaders across the wider public sector, and measurably improving design
maturity across an organisation and its partners is the clearest step up from the lead
service designer level below, where leading design is scoped to "a team".

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not set a design vision beyond their own team.
- 1 — Beginner: has contributed to a design vision that someone else set for a team.
- 2 — Intermediate: has led design work across more than one team, with support
  setting direction.
- 3 — Advanced: independently sets direction for design work within their own team,
  and mentors the designers in it, without yet setting a vision beyond that team.
- 4 — Expert: sets a vision for and leads design across multiple teams or the whole
  organisation, works effectively with senior leaders across the organisation or
  wider public sector, supports a culture of learning and experimentation, and
  measurably improves design maturity within the organisation and its partners.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about setting a vision for design across
  more than one team, or the whole organisation. How did you measure whether it
  worked?"
- Ask for an example of working effectively with a senior leader across the
  organisation or the wider public sector.
- Ask a referee about a measurable improvement in design maturity the candidate was
  responsible for, in the organisation or with a partner organisation.

The baseline is normally met when every skill is rated at or above its baseline target.
Every skill in this matrix targets Expert at this, the most senior level the framework
publishes for this role: five skills repeat the lead service designer level's text word
for word and were already targeted at Expert there, and the two skills that do change —
Designing strategically and Leading design — describe organisation-wide, or wider
public sector, scope that is itself Expert-level. A high, uniform target here is an
honest reading of the framework's own wording at the top of the ladder, not a
mechanical default — do not read it as evidence this level is easy to reach.

One or two skills below target is not automatically a fail. Weigh how central the skill
is to this level's core purpose — influencing design and organisational strategy,
collaborating with counterparts across government, and shaping the conditions
designers work in — consider whether the shortfall is coachable quickly, and record
the reasoning behind the determination, not only the number.
