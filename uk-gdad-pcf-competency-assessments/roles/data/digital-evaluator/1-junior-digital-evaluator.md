# Junior Digital Evaluator - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the
baseline a UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) junior digital evaluator needs. It is
written for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a reviewer — not for the
person being assessed.

A junior digital evaluator is the entry-level step into the digital evaluator role: an
understanding of the role and evidence of potential, working under guidance while
building skill through training. This assessment measures demonstrated capability
against the framework's own description of that level, across the seven skills the
framework names for it. It is one input among several into a hiring, onboarding or
development decision, never the whole of one — see `## Advisory` for why.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for educational
purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed
or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this form must never be the sole basis
for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision. Before using it,
consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational
psychology or legal compliance colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so the
framework's own words are fresh in mind. Gather whatever evidence exists for this
person: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task, direct
observation, or references. At this level, expect training records and supervised work
to matter as much as independent output — a junior digital evaluator is not expected to
have worked unsupervised.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something the person
did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to evidence for a
rating, record it as unknown rather than guess — an unknown is honest information; a
guess is not.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, using the same
kinds of evidence where possible. For a hiring or promotion decision, have more than one
assessor score independently before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than demonstrated,
job-related evidence. See [Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification for what this rules out.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This rates how well the person does the thing, on the evidence available — not how often
they do it. A worked example, using this level's skill of Evaluation delivery:

- **1 — Beginner**: with close direction from a colleague, they collected survey
  responses for one evaluation and could say, when asked, which technique was being
  used and why.
- **2 — Intermediate**: given a template and a steer on which technique applied, they
  collected and did a first pass of analysis on both quantitative and qualitative data
  for a small evaluation, checking in at each stage.
- **3 — Advanced**: they planned and ran the data collection and analysis for an
  evaluation with only occasional review, and could explain why they chose one
  analytical approach over another.

A junior digital evaluator is not expected to reach the third example unsupported —
their baseline for this skill, below, is 1.

## About this role level

**The role: digital evaluator.** A digital evaluator assesses the design, implementation
and outcomes of a digital product or service, and whether it delivers value for the
public and society. Some are also members of a government analytical profession where
evaluation is a core skill. In this role, you work closely with delivery and policy
teams; develop theories of change for products and services that explain the links
between activities, outputs, intended outcomes and unintended effects; follow
established monitoring and evaluation methods and government guidance; produce
evaluation plans; design quantitative and qualitative research; collect and analyse
data; and communicate findings, including by publishing reports.

**The level: junior digital evaluator.** This is the entry-level step of the role — the
framework describes it as someone who has an understanding of the role and can show
potential. It is the first of five levels in this role: junior digital evaluator,
digital evaluator, senior digital evaluator, lead digital evaluator, and head of digital
evaluation. At this level, you support the work of more experienced digital evaluators,
develop your skills through training, and receive guidance from other digital
evaluators on how to produce good work.

**The skills this level names**, in the summary's order: Communicating analysis and
insight; Data ethics and privacy; Evaluation delivery; Evaluation planning and strategy;
Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle; Product and service monitoring;
and Quality assurance of data and analysis.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Communicating analysis and insight

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a junior digital evaluator to explain why clear communication of
analysis findings matters, and to explain some methods for doing it effectively. Both
bullets are about understanding, not yet about doing the communicating unsupervised.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The published wording is entirely explanatory ("explain why",
"explain some methods"), which matches close-supervision understanding rather than
independent practice — the expected starting point for the entry level of this role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot say why clear communication of findings matters, or name any method
  for doing it.
- 1 — Beginner: can explain, in their own words, why a stakeholder needs findings put
  clearly, and can name at least one method (a chart, a plain-language summary, a short
  briefing) for doing so.
- 2 — Intermediate: has drafted part of a findings summary under guidance and can say
  why they chose the format they used.
- 3 — Advanced: has independently drafted a findings summary for a real stakeholder and
  adjusted it after feedback.
- 4 — Expert: coaches a colleague on how to communicate a finding clearly.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to talk through a chart or short report they have seen or produced,
  and explain what made it clear or unclear.
- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you had to explain something
  technical to someone who was not a specialist. What did you do?"
- If a portfolio exists, look for any written summary, however short, and ask them to
  talk through their choices.

### Skill: Data ethics and privacy

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a junior digital evaluator to explain the importance of using data
ethics and privacy in their work, and to identify appropriate channels to discuss
ethical issues, with support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Both bullets are about awareness and identifying where to raise
a concern, with support explicitly named — this is a starting point, not independent
ethical judgement.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: shows no awareness that data ethics or privacy is relevant to evaluation
  work.
- 1 — Beginner: can explain, in general terms, why data ethics and privacy matter to
  evaluation, and knows there is a route (a manager, a data protection officer) to raise
  a concern, even if they have not used it unsupported.
- 2 — Intermediate: has raised, or role-played raising, a specific ethical or privacy
  concern through the right channel, with some prompting on who to tell.
- 3 — Advanced: has identified and escalated a real ethics or privacy issue in their
  work without being prompted.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted to advise colleagues on which channel a concern should go
  through.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "What would you do if you thought some data you were
  asked to work with should not have been collected, or should not be used the way it
  is being used?"
- Ask them to describe the channels available in a previous team or a training
  placement for raising a data ethics concern.
- Look for any completed data protection or ethics training as supporting evidence,
  alongside — not instead of — a real or role-played example.

### Skill: Evaluation delivery

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a junior digital evaluator to evaluate interventions with support,
using techniques including process evaluation, impact evaluation, theory of change,
experimentation and economic evaluation; to collect quantitative and qualitative data
with support; to analyse and interpret data they have collected and data from other
sources with support; and to use some analytical approaches, including descriptive and
inferential analysis. "With support" appears three times in four bullets.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The repeated "with support" language is a close match for the
Beginner definition — just starting to learn, requiring close supervision — and this is
the core delivery skill of the role, so it is worth naming precisely rather than
rounding it up.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not collected or analysed evaluation data, even under close direction.
- 1 — Beginner: with a colleague setting the task, has collected some quantitative or
  qualitative data for an evaluation and produced a simple, guided first pass of
  analysis.
- 2 — Intermediate: given a template and a named technique, collects and analyses data
  for a small evaluation with periodic check-ins rather than step-by-step direction.
- 3 — Advanced: independently runs data collection and analysis for a straightforward
  evaluation, choosing an appropriate technique and explaining the choice.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted to design and run evaluation delivery for a complex
  intervention and to teach the techniques to others.

**Evidence to gather**

- A work sample: give them a small, realistic dataset and ask them to run a guided
  first-pass analysis, then talk through what they did.
- A structured interview question: "Talk me through a piece of data collection or
  analysis you've done, even as part of training or study. What support did you need?"
- Ask a referee or training supervisor what level of direction the candidate typically
  needed on a data collection or analysis task.

### Skill: Evaluation planning and strategy

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a junior digital evaluator to describe how they might work with a
stakeholder to understand what they need to evaluate and why, to describe a process for
translating stakeholder needs into potential research questions and evaluation
approaches, and to describe some methods for monitoring and evaluating a product or
service. All three bullets are framed as description, not delivery.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Purely explanatory wording again places this at the starting
point of the scale — the person is expected to be able to talk through the process, not
yet run it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe how stakeholder needs turn into an evaluation approach.
- 1 — Beginner: can describe, in outline, how they would find out what a stakeholder
  needs to evaluate and why, and can name a method for monitoring or evaluating a
  product.
- 2 — Intermediate: has taken part in a real stakeholder conversation about evaluation
  needs, alongside a more senior colleague, and helped draft a research question.
- 3 — Advanced: has independently turned a stakeholder's need into a research question
  and a proposed evaluation approach.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted to agree an evaluation approach directly with a stakeholder.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "If a policy team asked you to help them work out
  whether their service was working, what would you ask them first, and why?"
- A short scenario: describe a fictional service and ask them to sketch what they would
  want to find out and how.
- Ask a training supervisor or referee whether the candidate has observed or taken part
  in a stakeholder needs conversation.

### Skill: Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework's only bullet for a junior digital evaluator is to describe how
evaluation needs and activities vary at different phases of the product life cycle.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. A single, explanatory bullet, with nothing about doing the work
across phases — a description-level understanding is the whole of what this level asks
for.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot say why evaluation needs would differ between an early discovery
  phase and a live service.
- 1 — Beginner: can describe, at a general level, how evaluation needs change across the
  product life cycle — for example, that early-stage evaluation looks different from
  live-service evaluation.
- 2 — Intermediate: can describe what evaluation activity would suit a specific
  named phase, with some prompting.
- 3 — Advanced: has contributed evaluation input at more than one life cycle phase on a
  real product or service.
- 4 — Expert: advises a team on what evaluation activity fits their current phase.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "What's different about evaluating a brand-new
  service compared with one that's been live for two years?"
- Ask them to place a short list of evaluation activities (a survey, a live analytics
  review, an early user testing exercise) against the life cycle phase each best suits.
- Review any training material or coursework that covered the service standard's
  phases.

### Skill: Product and service monitoring

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a junior digital evaluator to describe what a theory of change is,
identify some appropriate stakeholders to involve in developing one, describe common
issues when measuring performance indicators, and explain the difference between
analysis of digital performance and broader monitoring of a product, for example
against strategic objectives.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Four bullets, all "describe", "identify" or "explain" — concept-
level understanding, matching the close-supervision definition rather than independent
practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe what a theory of change is.
- 1 — Beginner: can describe what a theory of change is in their own words, name a
  stakeholder who might be involved in one, and name a common issue with measuring
  performance indicators.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped populate part of a theory of change or a set of
  indicators under guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: has drafted a section of a theory of change, or a set of indicators, for
  review by a more senior colleague.
- 4 — Expert: leads stakeholders through developing a theory of change unsupported.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "What is a theory of change, and what would it
  usually include?"
- Ask them to explain the difference between watching how many people use a service
  (digital performance) and asking whether the service met its strategic aim (broader
  monitoring).
- If any training exercise produced a theory of change diagram or performance indicator
  list, ask them to talk through it.

### Skill: Quality assurance of data and analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a junior digital evaluator to describe some basic data issues and
how to check that data and analysis look right, explain the concept of data being fit
for purpose and the context of the data, describe some methods for preparing data for
analysis, and describe quality assurance processes. Again, all four bullets are
description rather than doing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. Consistently explanatory wording — the person is expected to
understand what quality assurance involves, not yet to run it independently.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot name a basic data quality issue or describe a quality check.
- 1 — Beginner: can describe a basic data issue (missing values, duplicate rows,
  inconsistent formats) and explain, in outline, what "fit for purpose" means for data.
- 2 — Intermediate: has checked a small dataset for an obvious quality issue under
  guidance and flagged what they found.
- 3 — Advanced: independently spots and documents data quality issues in a real
  dataset before analysis begins.
- 4 — Expert: designs a quality assurance process for others to follow.

**Evidence to gather**

- A work sample: give them a small, deliberately flawed dataset and ask them to
  describe what looks wrong with it.
- A structured interview question: "What would make you trust, or not trust, a set of
  numbers someone handed you?"
- Ask a referee or training supervisor whether the candidate has flagged a data issue
  before, and how.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 0 — None
- [ ] 1 — Beginner
- [ ] 2 — Intermediate

The baseline is normally met when every skill is rated at or above its baseline target.
Every target here is 1, reflecting the entry-level, close-supervision expectation the
framework sets out for this level in every skill.

One or two skills below target is not automatically a fail. Weigh how central the skill
is to the level's core purpose — Evaluation delivery is the accountability the whole
role exists for, so a 0 there matters more than a 0 in a skill this level only asks the
person to describe. Consider whether the shortfall is coachable quickly through the
training this level explicitly provides, and record the reasoning behind the
determination, not only the number.
