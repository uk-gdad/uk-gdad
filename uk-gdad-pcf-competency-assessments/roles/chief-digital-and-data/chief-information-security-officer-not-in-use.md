# Chief Information Security Officer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for you if you are judging whether a candidate or an
employee has the abilities needed to do the job of chief information security
officer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — for a hiring
panel, a promotion conversation, an onboarding check, or a periodic
capability review.

**One thing to know before you start.** The framework publishes this role and
describes what it involves, but it marks the role level itself as **not in
use**, and it publishes no expectation against any of the eight skills it
names for it. That is the framework's own position, not a gap in this
document. It means there is no published baseline to measure a person against
here. This assessment therefore does something different from every other
competency assessment in this set: instead of comparing a person's rating to
a target figure, it gives you a structured way to record what a person's
practice actually looks like against the skill names the framework publishes,
so that the record is useful even though there is nothing to score it
against.

This is one input to a much larger process. It is not the whole of a
decision, and it must never stand alone — see `## Advisory` below for why, and
read that section before you use anything else in this document.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
that the role's actual duties are fresh in your mind. Gather whatever evidence
exists ahead of time: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample
or task such as a security strategy or a risk paper, direct observation of the
person operating at this level, or references from people who have worked
with them.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every entry in this record must
trace to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If
you cannot point to evidence for an entry, record it as unknown rather than
guess — a chief information security officer's real work happens in board
rooms, incident response rooms and cross-government security forums that an
assessor rarely sees directly, so gaps in your evidence are normal and should
be recorded as gaps.

**Consistency.** Record practice the same way for every candidate you assess
for this role, and, especially for a hiring or promotion decision, have more
than one assessor complete this record independently before comparing notes.

**This is an unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument.** Used inconsistently, or by
a single assessor working from impression rather than evidence, it can
produce unlawful adverse impact under the Equality Act 2010. Consistent,
evidence-based administration, ideally by more than one assessor, materially
reduces that risk — see [Fair and lawful
use](../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
for the constraints that follow from this.

**This role sits at the centre of the organisation's sensitive material.**
Nearly every skill this role names — cyber incident management, cyber risk
management, cyber security governance and security architecture especially —
means a candidate's answers may touch classified information, unresolved
vulnerabilities, or live security incidents. Do not record classified
material, the detail of a live vulnerability, or anything that would itself
create a disclosure risk in the evidence log. Record that evidence exists and
what it showed — for example, "described a specific incident response they
led, evidence was credible and detailed" — never the sensitive content
itself.

## Rating scale

Every skill in this record is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.
Because no baseline is published for this role level, use the scale here to
describe the person's actual, observed practice rather than to compare that
practice against a target.

**A worked example**, using Cyber incident management, one of the eight
skills this role names. Evidence at 1 — Beginner might be a candidate who can
describe the phases of incident response in general terms, but has only ever
followed someone else's instructions during an actual incident. Evidence at 3
— Advanced might be a candidate who has led the response to a real cyber
incident for a mid-sized organisation, can describe the decisions they made
under pressure, and can explain what they changed afterwards so the
organisation would detect and respond faster next time. Evidence at 4 —
Expert might be a candidate who has led an organisation through a genuinely
severe incident at board level, whose incident response approach other
organisations or a cross-government forum have adopted, and who can describe
how their thinking about detection, response and recovery has changed over
several incidents rather than just one.

## About this role level

**The role: chief information security officer.** A chief information
security officer creates an environment and culture in the organisation that
ensures the security of its information and technology. They enable the
organisation to achieve its objectives and deliver services in a safe and
secure way. The role is part of both the Government Digital and Data
profession and the Government Security profession. The framework describes
the role's duties as:

- create a strategy for information and cyber security that supports both
  the organisation's strategy and wider government security strategy
- lead the organisation in implementing the information and cyber security
  strategy
- evaluate the current status and maturity of information and cyber security
  in the organisation
- determine how to get to the level of information and cyber security
  maturity the organisation needs
- understand risks across the organisation and advise the board and other
  leaders on how to mitigate risks in their areas and in future plans
- enable the organisation to be innovative in a safe and secure way
- ensure the organisation is prepared for cyber attacks and can detect,
  respond to and recover from an attack
- ensure that information and cyber security aspects of crisis management
  are effective
- encourage a culture of cyber security awareness and good security
  practices
- implement practices to increase the maturity of information and cyber
  security

**The role level is published as NOT IN USE.** The framework lists chief
information security officer in the chief digital and data profession, keeps
the role description above, and marks the level itself — and every skill
expectation beneath it — as not in use. There is no level above or below
within this role to compare a candidate's rating against, and no published
wording for what any of the eight named skills should look like at this
level.

**The eight skills the framework names for this role**, with no expectation
published against any of them:

1. Capability building for digital, data and technology
2. Changing security culture
3. Cyber incident management
4. Cyber risk management
5. Cyber security governance
6. Innovation in digital, data and technology
7. Security architecture
8. Strategic cyber security planning

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Capability building for digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has built security capability in
  others — no training programme, no coaching relationship, no example of
  raising a team's or organisation's security skill.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe why security capability building
  matters, but the evidence shows only informal, occasional help to
  individual colleagues rather than a deliberate effort.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a capability activity — a
  security training programme, a coaching relationship, a recruitment or
  upskilling plan for a security function — with support from a learning and
  development function.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently implemented a practice that
  increased the maturity of information and cyber security through building
  people's capability, and can describe how they measured whether it worked.
- 4 — Expert: The person has built security capability at a scale that
  changed how a whole organisation, or a wider community of security
  professionals, works with security over several years, and they are sought
  out by peers for that judgement.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a specific security capability initiative
  they led, including how they knew whether it worked.
- Ask a referee to name someone whose security capability grew under the
  candidate's leadership, and how.
- Review a capability plan or training strategy the candidate authored or
  co-authored.

### Skill: Changing security culture

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has changed how people in an
  organisation think about or practise security day to day.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe what a good security culture looks
  like, but has not led an effort to change one themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a security awareness
  campaign or culture initiative led by someone else, and can describe what
  made it land or fail.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently encouraged a culture of cyber
  security awareness and good security practices, and can describe a
  measurable change in behaviour — not just awareness — that resulted.
- 4 — Expert: The person's approach to security culture has demonstrably
  reshaped how a whole organisation behaves around security over several
  years, and other security leaders look to them as an example.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a specific behaviour they changed in the
  organisation, and how they know it changed rather than just improved on
  paper.
- Ask a referee whether the security culture felt different because of the
  candidate's leadership, and how.
- Review a culture change plan, communications strategy or engagement survey
  result the candidate can speak to.

### Skill: Cyber incident management

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has been part of, let alone led,
  the response to a real cyber incident.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe the phases of incident response in
  general terms, but has only followed instructions during an actual
  incident rather than led one.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has taken a defined role in incident response,
  with support from a more senior incident lead, and can describe what they
  personally were responsible for.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently ensured their organisation is
  prepared for cyber attacks and can detect, respond to and recover from one,
  and can describe a real incident they led, without disclosing sensitive
  detail, and what changed afterwards.
- 4 — Expert: The person has led an organisation through a genuinely severe
  incident at board level, their incident management approach has been
  adopted elsewhere, and they can describe how their thinking about
  detection, response and recovery has evolved across several incidents.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a real incident they led the response to, at
  a level of detail that shows judgement without disclosing sensitive or
  classified information.
- Ask a referee who worked alongside the candidate during an incident how
  they performed under pressure.
- Review a redacted post-incident review or lessons-learned document the
  candidate authored, checking it is appropriately sanitised before it goes
  anywhere near this log.

### Skill: Cyber risk management

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has assessed or advised on cyber
  risk for an organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe cyber risk concepts in general terms,
  but has not personally advised the board or a senior leader on a real risk.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a risk assessment or advice
  paper, with support from a risk function, and can describe the reasoning
  behind a specific risk judgement.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently understood risks across an
  organisation and advised the board and other leaders on how to mitigate
  them, in their areas and in future plans, and can describe a risk they
  judged should be accepted rather than mitigated, and why.
- 4 — Expert: The person's cyber risk judgement is sought at board level as a
  matter of course, they have shaped how an organisation's risk appetite for
  cyber is set, and they can describe a genuinely difficult risk trade-off
  they navigated.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a cyber risk they judged should be accepted
  rather than mitigated, and how they made that case to the board.
- Review a risk register entry or board risk paper the candidate authored,
  without recording live risk detail in this log.
- Ask a board member or senior stakeholder how the candidate's risk advice
  has shaped decisions.

### Skill: Innovation in digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has enabled an organisation to
  innovate safely and securely, or that they have balanced innovation
  against security risk.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe examples of safe innovation
  elsewhere, but has no example of enabling one themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has supported an innovative approach or
  technology introduced by someone else, and can describe how security was
  built in alongside it.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently enabled the organisation to be
  innovative in a safe and secure way, and can describe a specific
  innovation they made possible by finding a proportionate security approach
  rather than blocking it.
- 4 — Expert: The person's approach to enabling safe innovation has
  demonstrably changed what an organisation is able to do, and they can
  describe how they built an environment — not just a single project — where
  security is seen as an enabler of innovation rather than a blocker.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an innovation they enabled by finding a
  proportionate security approach, rather than refusing it outright.
- Ask a referee from a delivery or product team whether security felt like an
  enabler or a blocker under the candidate's leadership.
- Review a risk-based approval or security assurance case the candidate
  authored or sponsored.

### Skill: Security architecture

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

- Review an architecture decision record or security architecture strategy

### Skill: Strategic cyber security planning

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has written or led a cyber
  security strategy.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can explain what a cyber security strategy is and
  why an organisation needs one, but has never written or led one themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a cyber security strategy,
  with support from a more senior sponsor, and can describe how it aligned
  with the organisation's and wider government security strategy.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently created a strategy for
  information and cyber security that supports both the organisation's
  strategy and wider government security strategy, led its implementation,
  and can describe how it raised the organisation's maturity.
- 4 — Expert: The person's cyber security strategy has demonstrably shaped a

  years, has been cited by peers or a cross-government forum as a strong
  example, and they can show how they adapted it as the threat landscape
  changed.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe their cyber security strategy in two
  sentences, and name a senior leader outside the security function who
  could repeat it.
- Review a cyber security strategy document the candidate authored or led.
- Ask a senior stakeholder how the candidate secured their support for the
  strategy, and what changed as a result of it.

**There is no published baseline for this role level, so this table is not a
pass or fail scorecard.** It records what you observed, skill by skill,
against the framework's own names for those skills, and nothing more. Do not
write "meets baseline" or "does not meet baseline" anywhere in this document
— there is no baseline for a person to meet or fall short of, and treating the
ratings as if there were one would misrepresent what the framework actually
publishes for this role level.

What the record is useful for instead: comparing a person's practice against
the role's actual duties (in `## About this role level`), noticing where the
evidence is strong and where it is thin, and giving the organisation's own
process — which may set its own local expectations for this role — something
concrete to work from. Where ratings are low across several skills, that may
reflect genuinely limited experience, or it may reflect that the assessor
could not gather strong evidence in the time available, particularly given
how much of this role's real work is necessarily kept confidential; say
