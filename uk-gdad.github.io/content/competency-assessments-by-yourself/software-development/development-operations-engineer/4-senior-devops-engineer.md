# Senior DevOps Engineer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

Commonly referred to as ‘DevOps engineers’, development operations engineers support the development and operation of software through tools, environments and practices. A senior DevOps engineer delivers and integrates software to form a complete DevOps toolchain. They are responsible for planning and designing large groups of stories.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example — Systems design.** At Intermediate (2), someone could
describe producing a design for a component with someone checking their
choice of patterns and standards. At Advanced (3) — this level's baseline —
they independently design systems characterised by medium risk, impact and
complexity, select appropriate standards, methods and tools, and can point to
a time they reviewed a colleague's design and improved its choice of
technology or resource use. At Expert (4), above this level's baseline, they
would be someone others bring their hardest design decisions to.

## About this role level

Development operations (DevOps) engineers — commonly called "DevOps
engineers" — support the development and operation of software through tools,
environments and practices. The role is responsible for underpinning good
development processes, including managing tools and testing environments,
central code control, maintaining development standards, and writing software
that automates systems.

This assessment covers the **Senior DevOps engineer** level, technical track —
the fourth step of the role's ladder, where the role splits into a technical
and a management track. The full ladder, in order, is: Apprentice DevOps
engineer, Junior DevOps engineer, DevOps engineer, Senior DevOps engineer
(this level, technical track, alongside a separate management track), Lead
DevOps engineer (technical and management), and Principal DevOps engineer
(technical and management).

A senior DevOps engineer delivers and integrates software to form a complete
DevOps toolchain, and is responsible for planning and designing large groups
of stories. At this level, a person is expected to:

- transform technical requirements into an effective DevOps toolchain to
  enable product delivery
- provide coaching and mentoring to more junior colleagues
- ensure that deployment strategies for products are repeatable, scalable and
  highly available
- have deep technical knowledge, providing support to delivery teams and
  solving complex problems

This level names ten skills: Availability and capacity management,
Development process optimisation, Information security, Modern development
standards, Programming and build (software engineering), Prototyping, Service
support, Systems design, Systems integration, and User focus.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior DevOps engineer is expected to manage service components to ensure
they meet business needs and key performance indicators (KPIs).

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This wording is unchanged from the level below, but
this level's deep technical knowledge and support-to-delivery-teams
accountability means a senior engineer should manage this independently and
be able to support others doing the same.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe what KPIs a service is managed against.
- 1 — Beginner: can explain the KPIs but has not managed a service against
  them.
- 2 — Intermediate: manages a service component's availability and capacity
  with occasional guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: manages multiple service components against business needs
  and KPIs independently, and proactively adjusts before a KPI is at risk.
- 4 — Expert: sets the KPIs, coaches junior colleagues on managing to them,
  and is consulted when a service repeatedly misses targets.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a time a service you managed was at risk of
  missing a KPI, and what you did.
- Ask about how you have coached a more junior colleague on availability or
  capacity management.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior DevOps engineer is expected to work under guidance to identify
process optimisation opportunities, and to contribute to the implementation
of proposed solutions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is worth noting plainly rather than smoothing
over: the framework's own wording for this skill at senior level ("work under
guidance") reads no more independently than at the level below, so the
baseline stays at Intermediate rather than rising to match the level's other,
more senior accountabilities.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot identify a process optimisation opportunity, even with
  guidance.

- 3 — Advanced: identifies opportunities with little guidance and takes a
  visible lead on implementing them.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior DevOps engineer is expected to collaborate with others when
necessary to review specifications, and to use the agreed specifications to
design, code, test and document programs or scripts of medium-to-high
complexity, using the right standards and tools.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This wording matches the level below's, and a
strong, independent grasp of medium-to-high complexity work is consistent
with this level's deep technical knowledge and problem-solving
accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot independently build from an agreed specification.
- 1 — Beginner: needs significant support even on medium-complexity work.
- 2 — Intermediate: builds medium-complexity programs or scripts with
  occasional guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: designs, codes, tests, corrects and documents programs or
  scripts of medium-to-high complexity independently, and collaborates
  effectively on specification review.
- 4 — Expert: does this at the highest complexity the team sees, and is
  sought out to unblock others' hardest technical problems.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask to see a medium-to-high complexity program or script you
  built recently, and discuss the trade-offs you made.
- Ask about a time you solved a complex technical problem another delivery
  team was stuck on.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior DevOps engineer is expected to design systems characterised by
medium levels of risk, impact, and business or technical complexity, to
select appropriate design standards, methods and tools and ensure they are
applied effectively, and to review the systems designs of others to ensure
the selection of appropriate technology, efficient use of resources and
integration of multiple systems and technology.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Reviewing others' designs, in addition to producing
their own, is a real step up from the level below and matches this level's
coaching and mentoring accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot design a system of medium risk or complexity
  independently.
- 1 — Beginner: designs simple systems with heavy support.
- 2 — Intermediate: designs medium-complexity systems with some guidance, and
  does not yet review others' work.
- 3 — Advanced: independently designs systems of medium risk, impact and
  complexity, selects appropriate standards, methods and tools, and reviews
  others' designs for good technology choice and resource use.
- 4 — Expert: designs the most complex systems the team sees, and their
  review is sought out for the hardest design decisions.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask to see a design document you produced, and one you reviewed
  for someone else.
- Ask about a time reviewing a colleague's design led to a better choice of
  technology or resource use.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

A senior DevOps engineer is expected to collaborate with user researchers and
represent users internally, to explain the difference between user needs and
the desires of the user, to champion user research to focus on all users, to
prioritise and define approaches to understand the user story while guiding
others in doing so, and to offer recommendations on the best tools and
methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Championing user research and guiding others is a
clear step up from independently gathering and using user evidence, matching
this level's coaching and mentoring accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: does not represent user needs in their work or decisions.
- 1 — Beginner: uses user evidence when given it, but does not yet champion
  research.
- 2 — Intermediate: engages with user researchers and uses evidence
  independently, with some guidance on prioritising approaches.
- 3 — Advanced: represents users internally, distinguishes user needs from
  user desires in a real decision, champions research across the team, and
  guides others on how to approach understanding a user story.
- 4 — Expert: not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a time you had to explain the difference
  between what a user asked for and what you actually needed.
- Ask about a time you guided a colleague on the right approach to
  understanding a user story.
