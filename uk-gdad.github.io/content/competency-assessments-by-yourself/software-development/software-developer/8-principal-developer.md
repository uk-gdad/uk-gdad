# Software developer (Principal developer) - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A software developer designs, runs and improves software that meets user needs. A principal developer leads and plans development across large or multiple teams.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale — it rates how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example — Systems design.** Imagine three people for this role
level, all of whom have worked on system design in some capacity.

- A rating of **2 — Intermediate** might be supported by evidence that someone designed a moderately complex system component with guidance from
  a more senior architect, and can explain the component's main risks when
  asked directly, but has not yet been trusted to make a design call alone on
  something with high business impact.
- A rating of **3 — Advanced** might be supported by evidence that someone independently designed a system carrying real risk or complexity —
  for example, a component whose failure would affect several other teams —
  and that the design demonstrably adhered to the organisation's technical
  strategies and standards without needing correction.
- A rating of **4 — Expert** might be supported by evidence that someone
  has controlled system design practice across an enterprise or an industry
  architecture: for example, they set the design standards other architects
  now design against, shaped a procurement or implementation strategy to match
  a business need, or their thinking has visibly influenced how a new
  technology gets applied beyond their own team.

## About this role level

**The role.** A software developer designs, runs and improves software that
meets user needs. In this role, a person is responsible for writing clean,
secure code following a test-driven approach, and for creating code that is
open by default and easy for others to reuse.

**This level.** A principal developer leads and plans development across
large or multiple teams. At this role level, a person will:

- be an expert in many technical areas, or a specialist with very deep
  knowledge in a particular technical area
- use this knowledge, and work with related disciplines, to keep multiple
  teams working effectively
- develop capability by facilitating internal mobility, shaping career paths
  and recruiting talent, ensuring they collaborate
- identify, test and champion the adoption of emerging technologies

There are two different roles at this level — a technical role and a
management role. This assessment covers the **technical** role. The
management role, Principal developer (management), sits at the same
seniority and is covered by its own assessment; do not use this document to
assess it.

**Where this sits in the ladder.** The software developer role has nine
published role levels, run in order:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. Senior developer
5. Senior developer (management)
6. Lead developer
7. Lead developer (management)
8. **Principal developer — this level**
9. Principal developer (management)

Principal developer is near the top of the ladder — the last purely technical
step before the framework has nothing above it in this role, alongside its
management-track counterpart at the same seniority.

**The skills this level names**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

One of these is worth flagging before you score it. The framework's wording
for **Availability and capacity management** at this level — "manage service
components to ensure they meet business needs and key performance indicators
(KPIs)" — is the same, thinner wording used at less senior levels of this
role. It has not grown the way the other nine skills' wording visibly has, so
do not assume this skill's baseline should sit as high as the others simply
because the role level around it is senior. Score what the framework's own
words for this skill actually support; see the baseline given for it below.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer manages service components so that they meet business
needs and agreed key performance indicators (KPIs).

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's wording for this skill at this level
is unchanged from less senior levels of this role — it does not describe
leading others, setting policy, or working across multiple teams the way the
level's other nine skills do. A principal developer should still be able to
manage a service component's availability and capacity independently and
well, but nothing in the framework's own text supports expecting the
teaching-and-mastery behaviour that a 4 implies for this specific skill. Do
not inflate this baseline to match the rest of the level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never been responsible for monitoring or reporting on a
  service component's availability or capacity, and cannot describe how KPIs
  for a service are set or tracked.
- 1 — Beginner: Can read an existing availability or capacity dashboard and
  describe what a metric means, but needs close support to identify when a
  component is at risk of breaching a KPI.
- 2 — Intermediate: With guidance, adjusts capacity settings or escalates an
  emerging availability risk for a single service component, and can explain
  the KPI it is measured against.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently manages the availability and capacity of one or
  more service components against agreed KPIs, spotting emerging risk before
  it breaches target and adjusting plans without being told to.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the KPIs a service component is measured against,
  negotiates capacity trade-offs directly with business stakeholders, and is
  someone other engineers ask when a component's availability strategy
  needs rethinking.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd walk through a service component you managed: what
  the KPIs were, how you tracked you, and one occasion where you adjusted
  capacity ahead of a problem rather than after one.
- Look back at a capacity plan, dashboard, or incident postmortem you produced or
  contributed to.
- Ask a referee whether you have ever been the person accountable
  for a component's availability during a period of real business pressure —
  a launch, a peak season, a migration.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer analyses current development processes, identifies and
implements opportunities to optimise them, leads and develops a team of
experts to deliver service improvements, and helps evaluate and establish
requirements for changes by setting policy and standards.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This skill's wording at this level explicitly names
leading a team of experts and setting policy and standards — behaviour that
sits well above independent process improvement, and matches the level's
overall accountability for keeping multiple teams working effectively.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not analysed a development process for improvement
  opportunities, and has no experience proposing or implementing a process
  change.
- 1 — Beginner: Can point to an inefficiency in a process they follow day to
  day, but needs support to turn that observation into a concrete proposal.
- 2 — Intermediate: Analyses one team's process, identifies a specific
  improvement, and implements it with guidance from a more senior colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently analyses and improves development processes for
  their own team, and can explain the trade-offs of the change to
  stakeholders outside the team.
- 4 — Expert: Leads a team of experts to deliver process improvements across
  large or multiple teams, sets the policy and standards that changes are
  evaluated against, and is accountable for whether an optimisation actually
  lands in practice.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific example of a process you changed across more
  than one team, including how you measured whether it worked.
- Look back at a piece of policy or standards documentation you wrote or
  owns.
- Ask a referee who reported into, or was led by, you on a process
  change, what your role in leading that change actually was.

### Skill: Information security

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer designs, reviews and quality assures solutions and
services, engineering them specifically to mitigate security threats, and
leads other people in using appropriate information security practices.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework explicitly names leading others in
security practice, not just applying it personally, which matches this
level's expert-across-many-areas accountability and its influence over
multiple teams.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not designed or reviewed a solution with security threats
  explicitly in mind.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a documented secure-coding checklist when writing
  code, but needs a more experienced colleague to review for threats the
  checklist misses.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies common security threats in a design under
  review and proposes mitigations, usually validated by someone more senior
  before being adopted.
- 3 — Advanced: Designs and quality assures solutions engineered against a
  broad set of threats without oversight, and can explain the reasoning
  behind the mitigations chosen.
- 4 — Expert: Leads other engineers, across more than one team, in adopting
  appropriate information security practices, and is the escalation point
  when a design's security posture is in genuine doubt.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd review a sample design or architecture decision record
  and identify the security threats it should be engineered against.
- Ask for an example where you changed how a team approached
  security, rather than just fixing one issue yourself.
- Look back at any security standard, threat model, or review checklist you authored or led the adoption of.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer identifies and applies modern development standards to
support complex projects and programmes, and leads other people in applying
them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. As with information security, the framework explicitly
adds a leadership dimension at this level — leading others in applying
standards, not only applying them personally — which is consistent with this
level's remit across large or multiple teams.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name the development standards their team is expected to
  follow, or why.
- 1 — Beginner: Applies modern development standards when told which ones to
  use, but does not yet know how to select them for a new situation.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies and applies an appropriate modern development
  standard for a project with some guidance from a more experienced
  colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies and applies the right modern
  development standards for a complex project, adapting them as the
  project's needs change.
- 4 — Expert: Leads other developers, across multiple teams or a programme,
  in applying modern development standards consistently, and is someone
  who decides when an existing standard no longer serves the work.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a standard you introduced or changed across
  more than one team, and how you got other developers to adopt it.
- Look back at a technical standards document, style guide, or architecture
  guideline you authored.
- Ask a structured interview question: "Tell me about a modern development
  standard you decided to move away from, and why."

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer advises on the right way to apply standards and
methods to ensure compliance, maintains technical responsibility for all the
stages and iterations of a software development project, and provides
technical advice to stakeholders while setting the team-based standards for
programming tools and techniques.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Maintaining technical responsibility for an entire
project's stages and iterations, and setting the standards a team programmes
against, is expert-level ownership consistent with the level's description of
someone who keeps multiple teams working effectively.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not held technical responsibility for a software development
  project beyond their own individual contribution.
- 1 — Beginner: Writes code to specifications set by others, and needs
  supervision to apply the team's standards and methods correctly.
- 2 — Intermediate: Takes technical responsibility for a discrete piece of a
  project, applying agreed standards and methods with occasional guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Maintains technical responsibility for all stages and
  iterations of a software development project, advising others on the right
  way to apply standards to stay compliant.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the team-based standards for programming tools and
  techniques, and is sought out by stakeholders outside their own team for
  technical advice on a project's direction.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a project where you held technical
  responsibility end to end, including a decision you had to revisit
  between iterations.
- Review code, architecture decisions, or a build pipeline you set
  the standards for.
- Ask a referee who was a stakeholder on a project you led
  technically what kind of advice you went to you for.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer uses a variety of prototyping methods, shares best
practice and coaches others in prototyping, and looks at strategic service
design end to end.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework explicitly names coaching others and
strategic, end-to-end service design — both expert-level behaviours that go
beyond using prototyping methods for a single piece of work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not built a prototype to test an idea before committing to
  full development.
- 1 — Beginner: Builds a simple prototype using one method, under close
  direction on what to test and how.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses an appropriate prototyping method for a given
  problem and builds it with some guidance, interpreting the results with
  support.
- 3 — Advanced: Uses a variety of prototyping methods independently,
  selecting the right one for the question being answered, and connects the
  results to service design decisions.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches other developers in prototyping best practice, and uses
  prototyping as part of thinking about a service's strategic design end to
  end, not just a single feature.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a prototype that changed the direction of a
  service, not just a feature.
- Ask for an example of coaching another developer through choosing a
  prototyping method for a problem you were stuck on.
- Look back at a prototype, spike, or proof of concept you built or
  directed, and the decision it led to.

### Skill: Service support

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer identifies, locates and fixes service faults.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Although the framework's wording for this skill is
brief, a person operating as an expert across many technical areas is
expected to be the point other engineers escalate the hardest, least
understood faults to, and to be able to teach others how to find them —
consistent with the level's overall description, even though this particular
bullet does not spell that out the way some of the other nine skills do.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not diagnosed or fixed a live service fault without
  step-by-step direction from someone else.
- 1 — Beginner: Investigates a straightforward, well-understood service fault
  with close supervision, and needs help interpreting logs or monitoring
  data.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently identifies and fixes common service
  faults, escalating unfamiliar or ambiguous faults to a more experienced
  colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Locates and fixes unfamiliar or complex service faults across
  a system independently, and writes up what was found so others can learn
  from it.
- 4 — Expert: Is someone other engineers, across more than one team, bring
  the hardest faults to, and turns a repeated fault into a case for a
  structural fix rather than another one-off patch.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd walk through the hardest service fault you have
  diagnosed, including what made it hard and how you found it.
- Ask for an example of a fault you turned into a structural fix, rather
  than a repeated patch.
- Ask a referee whether other engineers, including from other teams, bring
  your hardest faults to you.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer designs systems characterised by high levels of risk,
impact, and business or technical complexity, controls system design
practice within an enterprise or industry architecture, influences
industry-based models for the development of new technology applications,
develops effective implementation and procurement strategies consistent with
business needs, and ensures adherence to relevant technical strategies,
policies, standards and practices.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This is the most explicitly senior wording in the
level's whole skill set — controlling design practice at enterprise or
industry level and influencing models beyond the organisation is unambiguous
expert-level accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not designed a system where a design mistake would have
  significant business or technical consequences.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to the design of a straightforward system
  component under close direction from a more senior designer.
- 2 — Intermediate: Designs a moderately complex system component with some
  guidance, and can explain the design's main risks when asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Designs systems with high levels of risk, impact or technical
  complexity independently, and ensures the design adheres to the
  organisation's technical strategies and standards.
- 4 — Expert: Controls system design practice across an enterprise or
  industry architecture, shapes implementation and procurement strategy to
  match business needs, and has visibly influenced industry-facing models for
  how new technology gets applied.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd present a design you own for a system with genuine
  business or technical risk, and to defend the decisions in it.
- Ask for evidence of influence beyond your own organisation — a published
  design, a conference talk, a working group, a shared standard adopted
  elsewhere.
- Review an implementation or procurement strategy document you
  authored or led.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer defines the integration build, co-ordinates build
activities across systems, and understands how to undertake and support
integration testing activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Defining and coordinating an integration build across
systems, at this level, means doing so across systems owned by more than one
team — consistent with the level's overall remit of keeping multiple teams
working effectively together.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not defined or coordinated an integration build across more
  than one system.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an integration build plan someone else has defined,
  and needs support to coordinate activities across systems.
- 2 — Intermediate: Defines a straightforward integration build with
  guidance, and coordinates build activities across two or three systems.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently defines the integration build for complex,
  multi-system work, coordinating build activities and supporting
  integration testing across every system involved.
- 4 — Expert: Is trusted to define and coordinate integration builds spanning
  systems owned by multiple teams, anticipating where the boundaries between
  systems will cause problems before build begins.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe an integration build spanning systems owned
  by different teams, and a boundary problem you anticipated before it
  happened.
- Review an integration plan or test strategy you defined.
- Ask a referee from another team whether you coordinated

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal developer collaborates with user researchers and can represent
users internally, explains the difference between user needs and the desires
of the user, champions user research to focus on all users, prioritises and
defines approaches to understand the user story while guiding others in
doing so, and offers recommendations on the best tools and methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Guiding others in defining an approach to
understanding a user story, and prioritising where research effort goes, are
expert-level, cross-team accountabilities consistent with the rest of this
level's skill set.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not represented user needs in a technical decision, and
  cannot describe the difference between a user's stated desire and their
  underlying need.
- 1 — Beginner: Refers to user research findings that someone else has
  gathered when making a technical decision, with support to interpret them
  correctly.
- 2 — Intermediate: Collaborates with user researchers on a specific piece of
  work, and can explain the difference between a user need and a user
  desire when asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently represents users internally in technical
  discussions, champions research that covers the full range of users rather
  than the easiest to reach, and recommends appropriate tools and methods
  for understanding a user story.
- 4 — Expert: Guides other developers, across multiple teams, in how to
  define an approach to understanding a user story, and is trusted to
  prioritise where user research effort goes across a programme of work.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a decision where you pushed back on a
  stated user desire in favour of an underlying user need, and how you made
  that case.
- Ask for an example of guiding another developer, or another team, on how
  to approach understanding a user story.
- Ask a referee — ideally a user researcher — how you collaborates
  with research and whether you champion research into users who are harder
  to reach.
