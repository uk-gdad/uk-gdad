# Principal Enterprise Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

Enterprise architects are leaders working across different levels within an organisation to translate the business strategy into business change and technical delivery. A principal enterprise architect leads at the highest level and is responsible for ensuring the strategy is developed, agreed and followed.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale. It measures how well someone does the thing, on the
evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Enterprise architecture.** A person rated 3 (Advanced)
independently designs an architecture defining technology, people and process
elements, and works with others across the organisation to see it used. A
person rated 4 (Expert) — the baseline for this role level — leads other
architects in designing architectures that achieve organisational objectives,
works with people beyond the organisation to influence structures, technology
and process elsewhere, and ensures that organisation-wide change stays
aligned with the architectural strategy, exactly as the framework describes
this level's remit.

## About this role level

Enterprise architects are leaders working across different levels within an
organisation to translate business strategy into business change and
technical delivery. In the role generally, an enterprise architect identifies
priorities for change, owns the enterprise architecture vision, strategy and
roadmaps across business, technology and data, takes a strategic view across
architectural domains, portfolios and programmes, establishes architectural
principles, policies and standards, and develops the wider architecture
community.

This is the fourth and most senior of the role's four levels: enterprise
architect, senior enterprise architect, lead enterprise architect, and
**principal enterprise architect** (this level).

At this level, a principal enterprise architect leads at the highest level
and is responsible for ensuring the strategy is developed, agreed and
followed. The framework describes this level as networking and communicating
with senior stakeholders across enterprises while actively seeking out
opportunities for improvement, supporting multiple communities and teams at
once, finding and using best practice and emerging technologies and
approaches, inspiring other enterprise architects and helping them understand
how to meet organisational goals, horizon scanning for external influences or
risks, supporting successful delivery of the long-term strategy, being
responsible for the overall direction of business and digital capabilities,
and being responsible for the creation, maintenance and consumption of a
Digital Twin.

This level names eight skills: Architect for the whole context, Architecture
communication, Commercial perspective, Community collaboration, Enterprise
architecture, Making architectural decisions, Problem definition and shaping,
and Strategy design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What the framework expects at this level**

Assess how trends in society and industry practices might affect the
organisation, work with people outside the organisation to inform policies,
strategies and standards, anticipate changes to policy and build resilience
through your architectural work, and coach others in identifying important
trends.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This wording is unchanged from the level below, and at
this level it is exercised across the organisation's whole future direction,
so the baseline holds at the ceiling of the scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not considered how a societal or industry trend might affect
  the organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe a trend when asked, but has not connected it to
  organisational impact or policy.
- 2 — Intermediate: Notices an emerging trend and raises it, relying on
  someone else to act.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently assesses how a trend might affect the
  organisation and anticipates a coming policy change.
- 4 — Expert: Works with people outside the organisation to inform policy or
  standards at a national or cross-government level, anticipates policy
  change well ahead of it becoming public, and coaches other enterprise
  architects across the organisation in spotting important trends.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of you influencing a national or
  cross-government policy, strategy or standard, and what your contribution
  was.
- Ask a referee whether you have built a habit, across the
  organisation, of other architects coming to you for a read on emerging
  trends.
- Look back at a document showing you anticipating a significant policy
  shift well before it was public, and what you did as a result.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

Communicate with technical and non-technical stakeholders at all levels and
across organisations, using architecture communication techniques, mediate
between people in difficult architectural discussions, gain support from
business and technical stakeholders for architectural topics with high levels
of risk, impact or complexity, and coach and support others in architecture
communication.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Unchanged wording from the level below; at principal
level it is exercised on the organisation's highest-stakes topics and across
its whole architecture community.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Communicates only within their immediate team.
- 1 — Beginner: Communicates a straightforward topic to a single audience,
  with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Adapts a message across audiences unaided for topics of
  moderate risk or complexity.
- 3 — Advanced: Gains support from stakeholders for a topic of high risk,
  impact or complexity, and mediates a difficult architectural discussion.
- 4 — Expert: Is someone the organisation turns to for its most contested,
  highest-stakes architecture conversations, and has built the architecture
  community's overall capability in this skill through coaching.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of you mediating a dispute between senior
  stakeholders on a topic with organisation-wide consequences.
- Ask a referee for evidence that you have raised the communication
  capability of the wider architecture community, not just individuals.

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What the framework expects at this level**

Identify appropriate contractual frameworks and approaches, and identify,
evaluate and select appropriate suppliers.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's wording is identical to the level
below and, unlike several other skills in this matrix, is not framed as a
coaching or organisation-wide capability at this level — it remains a
supporting skill a principal enterprise architect applies directly, so the
baseline holds at Advanced rather than rising to Expert.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has no working knowledge of contractual frameworks or supplier
  selection.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe a contractual framework in general terms but has
  not applied one.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies an appropriate contractual approach with
  guidance from a commercial colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies an appropriate contractual framework
  and evaluates and selects a suitable supplier for significant, high-value
  work.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes the organisation's overall contractual and supplier
  strategy and is asked to advise other principals on their own commercial
  approach.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a high-value or high-risk supplier decision you shaped, and the evaluation behind it.
- Ask a referee from a commercial function about your judgement on
  contractual approach for significant programmes.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level**

Solve and unblock issues between teams or departments at the highest level,
and coach the organisation on team dynamics and conflict resolution, while
also building and growing the community.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework explicitly names coaching the whole
organisation, which is a step beyond the lead level's work within a single
team or community.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not been involved in resolving an issue between teams or
  departments.
- 1 — Beginner: Helps resolve a dynamic issue within a single team, with
  support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Resolves a dynamic issue within a single team or
  community independently.
- 3 — Advanced: Solves and unblocks an issue between two teams or
  departments.
- 4 — Expert: Solves and unblocks issues between teams or departments at the
  highest level, coaches the organisation on team dynamics and conflict
  resolution as a matter of course, and grows the architecture community over
  time.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of you resolving a conflict between two
  departments, not just two individuals.
- Ask a referee for evidence that you have grown the size or
  capability of an architecture community over a sustained period.
- Think about how you'd describe how you coach the organisation, rather than
  one person, on team dynamics.

### Skill: Enterprise architecture

**What the framework expects at this level**

Lead others designing architectures that define the technology, people,
process and other elements needed to achieve organisational objectives, work
with others beyond the organisation to influence organisational structures,
technology, people and processes to achieve those objectives, and ensure
organisation-wide changes align with the architectural strategy.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework explicitly asks this level to lead
others' design work and to ensure organisation-wide alignment, both squarely
mastery-level acts.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot design an architecture that connects technology, people
  and process to an organisational objective.
- 1 — Beginner: Designs a narrow part of an architecture with direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: Designs a straightforward architecture element with some
  guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs an architecture and works with others
  across the organisation to see it used.
- 4 — Expert: Leads other architects' design work, works with people beyond
  the organisation to influence structures, technology and process
  elsewhere, and ensures organisation-wide change stays aligned with the
  architectural strategy.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of you leading a team of architects through a
  significant design, and what your leadership specifically contributed.
- Think about how you'd describe how you have influenced structures,
  technology or process outside the organisation's own boundary.
- Ask a referee whether an organisation-wide change stayed aligned with the
  architectural strategy under your watch, or drifted.

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

Make and guide architectural design decisions characterised by high levels of
risk and complexity, identify and address architectural risks across the
organisation or wider government, lead and evolve architectural governance
and assurance, and represent architectural governance as part of wider
governance — for example, legal or commercial.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Risk and complexity have risen from "medium" to "high",
the scope from "multiple teams or domains" to the whole organisation or wider
government, and the accountability from contributing to governance to leading
and evolving it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not taken part in an architectural decision-making process.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to a decision made mainly by others.
- 2 — Intermediate: Makes a decision of managed risk affecting a single team
  or domain.
- 3 — Advanced: Makes and guides decisions of medium risk and complexity
  affecting multiple teams or domains.
- 4 — Expert: Makes and guides decisions of high risk and complexity across
  the organisation or wider government, leads and evolves the governance and
  assurance processes themselves, and represents architectural governance
  within wider governance forums such as legal or commercial.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a high-risk, organisation-wide decision you
  made or guided, and its consequences.
- Think about how you'd describe how you have evolved a governance or
  assurance process, and what changed.
- Ask a referee whether you have represented architectural
  governance in a wider forum such as a legal or commercial governance board.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What the framework expects at this level**

Lead the framing of a problem characterised by high complexity, complication
or risk so that a solution can be created, coach others in defining problems
and describing appropriate options for solutions, and help others challenge
requirements and assumptions and identify opportunities when defining
problems and solution options.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Unchanged wording from the level below; at principal
level it is exercised on the organisation's hardest problems and its coaching
reaches the whole architecture community.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Takes a problem statement as given and does not question or
  reframe it.
- 1 — Beginner: Frames a simple problem with close support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Frames a problem of managed complexity with some
  guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently leads the framing of a problem of high
  complexity, complication or risk.
- 4 — Expert: Leads the framing of the organisation's hardest problems, and
  has built a habit across the architecture community of coaching others in
  defining problems and describing solution options well.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of the single hardest problem you have framed
  in your career, and how you approached it.
- Ask a referee for evidence that your coaching has visibly
  improved how other architects define problems, not just solved one problem
  for you.

### Skill: Strategy design

**What the framework expects at this level**

Define and connect strategies or visions across the organisation or wider
government, and enable the implementation of those strategies or visions
across the organisation or wider government — for example, by advocating for
resources and removing blockers.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Unchanged wording from the level below; a principal
enterprise architect is responsible for the overall direction of business and
digital capabilities, so this level's evidence should show strategy work at
that same organisation-wide or cross-government scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not contributed to defining a strategy or vision.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes a defined piece to someone else's strategy.
- 2 — Intermediate: Challenges an assumption in a strategy discussion and
  supports its implementation.
- 3 — Advanced: Defines a strategy or vision across teams and directs its
  implementation.
- 4 — Expert: Defines and connects strategies or visions across the
  organisation or wider government, and personally removes blockers or
  secures resources that no team below principal level could obtain alone.

**Evidence to gather**

- Look back at a strategy or vision document you authored that spans the
  whole organisation or reaches into wider government.
- Think about how you'd describe the largest blocker you have personally
  removed, or the largest resource you have secured, to enable a strategy's
  implementation.
- Ask a referee about your influence on strategy at
  organisation-wide or cross-government level.
