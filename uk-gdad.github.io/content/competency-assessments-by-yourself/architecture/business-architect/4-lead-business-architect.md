# Lead Business Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A business architect develops an integrated view of an enterprise, such as a business area or organisation. They use this view to help their organisation put its strategy in place. A lead business architect uses their expert knowledge and experience to direct the work addressing high level and complex business scenarios. They support and lead other business architects.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example — Stakeholder relationship management.** Someone rated 3
would build and maintain good relationships with stakeholders but would
still lean on others to negotiate the hardest cases. Someone rated 4
(the baseline expected at this level) directs the stakeholder relationship
strategy for their team, makes sure stakeholders' objectives are set and
supported, and personally influences and negotiates with senior stakeholders
to resolve issues and enable progress — without needing another business
architect to step in. Someone falling short of this — for example,
someone who manages individual relationships well but has not directed a
team-wide strategy or negotiated at senior level — would be rated 3, and the
gap is worth naming plainly rather than rounding up.

## About this role level

**Architecture role: Business architect.** A business architect develops an
integrated view of an enterprise, such as a business area or organisation,
and uses that view to help their organisation put its strategy in place.

In this role, a business architect will:

- understand and apply business architectural principles, techniques, and
  tools to business challenges
- ensure design work from projects and programmes aligns with strategy
- ensure business change is implemented effectively to meet strategy
- aggregate and disaggregate complex information from across the organisation
- communicate complex information about the organisation to stakeholders

**Role level: Lead business architect.** This is the fourth and most senior
of four levels in the business architect role, in order: trainee business
architect, associate business architect, business architect, and lead
business architect. A lead business architect uses their expert knowledge
and experience to direct the work addressing high level and complex business
scenarios. They support and lead other business architects.

At this role level, a lead business architect will:

- be accountable for outputs produced by the business architecture team
- lead and direct the application of architectural principles, methods, and
  tools to business scenarios
- use architectural methods to form a view of the whole enterprise in a way
  that is easily understood by stakeholders
- work with stakeholders at executive levels
- provide overall direction, guidance, and definition of business
  architecture to support strategy
- develop and maintain a strong business architecture capability through
  coaching, mentoring, and training

The framework names eight skills for this role level: Business architecture,
Communicating information, Consultancy, Making and guiding decisions, Methods
and tools, Stakeholder relationship management, Strategic design and
business change, and Strategic thinking.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Business architecture

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead business architect simplifies complex business architecture
frameworks into clear actions, develops business architecture maturity in
their organisation — such as through a community of practice — leads or
guides others in using best practice, such as industry standards, and
identifies strategic misalignment within the organisation, making
recommendations for improvement.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's wording at this level is explicitly
about developing organisational maturity and leading others' use of best
practice — mastery applied on behalf of the whole practice, not just someone's own work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot identify a case of strategic misalignment within the
  organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: identifies misalignment when it is pointed out, but needs
  support to simplify or act on it.
- 2 — Intermediate: simplifies a moderately complex framework for a
  colleague, with guidance on developing organisational maturity.
- 3 — Advanced: simplifies complex frameworks into clear actions and guides
  individual colleagues in best practice.
- 4 — Expert: develops business architecture maturity across the
  organisation — for example through a community of practice — leads
  others in adopting industry best practice, and identifies and acts on
  strategic misalignment unsupported.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about how you have developed
  business architecture maturity in an organisation — through a community of
  practice, standards work, or something similar."
- Think about how you'd review a short scenario describing organisational
  activity and identify a case of strategic misalignment, with a
  recommendation.
- If already in post, review evidence of a community of practice, standards
  initiative or maturity assessment the person has led.

### Skill: Communicating information

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead business architect mediates between people and mends relationships,
communicating with stakeholders at all levels, manages stakeholder
expectations and hosts or moderates discussions about high risk and
complexity — even within constrained timescales — and speaks on behalf of
and represents the community to large audiences inside and outside
government.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework describes this skill in identical
words to the business architect level below — an anomaly worth naming rather
than smoothing over. The framework's own text does not by itself raise the
bar for this specific skill at lead level, so the baseline stays where the
level below leaves it; what genuinely changes at lead level is the scale and
stakes of the audience, which the indicators below reflect, and you should look for that breadth of evidence even though the numeric baseline is
unchanged.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot mediate between people in conflict or manage stakeholder
  expectations under pressure.
- 1 — Beginner: manages stakeholder expectations in routine cases but needs
  support to mediate a serious disagreement.
- 2 — Intermediate: mediates straightforward disagreements and manages
  expectations for moderately complex stakeholder situations.
- 3 — Advanced: mediates and mends relationships involving high risk or
  complexity, including under time pressure, and represents the community
  competently to audiences beyond the immediate organisation, across more
  than one significant occasion.
- 4 — Expert: is habitually someone the organisation calls on for its
  hardest cross-government stakeholder situations, and its most
  consequential external representation.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about the highest-stakes
  stakeholder situation you have mediated — what made it high stakes, and
  what did you do?"
- Ask a referee: "Have you seen this person represent the business
  architecture community outside government, or to a very senior internal
  audience? How did it go?"
- If already in post, review two or more cases, across different occasions,
  where the person managed a high-risk or high-complexity stakeholder
  situation.

### Skill: Consultancy

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead business architect provides advice and recommendations to
stakeholders based on their significant specialist knowledge and experience,
leads the definition of guidance, and informs how the organisation
approaches delivery or uses digital and data skills more effectively.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. As with Communicating information, the framework
repeats this skill's wording from the business architect level below without
change. The baseline stays at 3 for that reason; what you should look
for at lead level is the same advice and guidance work exercised across the
team's whole remit, which the indicators reflect, rather than a higher
number the framework's own text does not support.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: gives advice with no clear grounding in specialist experience.
- 1 — Beginner: gives advice on straightforward matters but needs support for
  anything organisation-wide.
- 2 — Intermediate: gives sound advice on moderately complex matters and
  contributes to guidance documents with oversight.
- 3 — Advanced: gives advice and recommendations grounded in significant
  specialist knowledge, and leads the definition of guidance across the
  team's whole area of work, changing how the organisation approaches
  delivery.
- 4 — Expert: is the acknowledged specialist whose advice shapes strategy
  across the organisation, sought out beyond their own team.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe a piece of guidance you led the
  definition of that changed practice across more than one team."
- Think about how you'd advise, in a short scenario, on how an organisation
  should approach a specific delivery problem involving digital and data
  skills, and explain your reasoning.
- If already in post, review a guidance document the person led that was
  adopted beyond your own immediate team.

### Skill: Making and guiding decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead business architect makes decisions characterised by medium levels of
risk and complexity, recommends decisions as risk and complexity increase
further, and builds consensus between services or independent stakeholders.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework repeats this skill's wording exactly
from the business architect level below, with no increase in the stated
level of risk or complexity — another anomaly worth naming rather than
inflating. The baseline stays at 3; what should genuinely differ at lead
level is the breadth of what is being decided, which the indicators below
describe, given this level's accountability for the whole team's outputs.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: avoids owning decisions of any real risk or complexity.
- 1 — Beginner: makes decisions with support once risk or complexity rises
  above the routine.
- 2 — Intermediate: makes decisions of moderate risk and complexity with
  occasional guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: makes decisions characterised by medium risk and complexity
  unsupported, recommending rather than deciding as it rises further, and
  builds consensus between services or independent stakeholders across the
  team's full remit.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted with the organisation's highest-risk, most complex
  decisions in this area, well beyond medium risk and complexity.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about a decision you made that
  affected more than one service or stakeholder group. How did you build
  consensus?"
- Ask a referee: "Can you describe a case where this person's decision-making
  was tested by disagreement between services?"
- If already in post, review a documented decision that shows the breadth of
  services or stakeholders involved and the reasoning behind it.

### Skill: Methods and tools

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead business architect sets direction and gives others the means and
authority to advise on the best methodologies and tools for the work,
ensuring they are adopted consistently, and assesses the effectiveness of
different approaches — such as Agile or plan-driven — recommending tools to
support organisational collaboration and build capability.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's wording moves from building capability,
as at business architect level, to setting direction and delegating
authority to advise — genuine mastery exercised through others, which is
the framework's own definition of the top of this scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has no involvement in setting the organisation's direction on
  methods and tools.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes an opinion on methods and tools when asked, but
  does not set direction or delegate authority.
- 2 — Intermediate: recommends a method or tool for a specific piece of work
  with support.
- 3 — Advanced: assesses the effectiveness of different approaches and
  recommends tools for a team, without yet setting direction more widely.
- 4 — Expert: sets direction on methods and tools for the practice, gives
  others the means and authority to advise on them, and ensures consistent
  adoption.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about a time you set direction
  on which methods or tools a practice should use, and gave others authority
  to advise on you."
- Think about how you'd compare two approaches — such as Agile and
  plan-driven — for a described piece of work, and recommend one with
  reasoning.
- If already in post, review evidence of a methods-and-tools direction the
  person set, and how consistently it was adopted.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead business architect directs the stakeholder relationship strategy for
their teams, ensures stakeholders' objectives are set and supports teams to
meet them, and influences and negotiates with senior stakeholders to resolve
issues and enable progress.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's wording moves from a business
architect building strategic relationships themselves, to a lead directing
the relationship strategy for their teams and personally negotiating with
senior stakeholders — ownership and influence at the top of the scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has no involvement in directing how the team manages
  stakeholders.
- 1 — Beginner: manages their own stakeholder relationships but does not
  direct a strategy for others.
- 2 — Intermediate: contributes to a team's stakeholder strategy with
  support, and negotiates with senior stakeholders on straightforward
  matters.
- 3 — Advanced: directs part of a team's stakeholder relationship strategy
  and negotiates with senior stakeholders on most issues.
- 4 — Expert: directs the stakeholder relationship strategy for their teams,
  ensures stakeholders' objectives are set and met, and personally
  influences and negotiates with senior stakeholders to resolve issues and
  enable progress.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about a time you negotiated with
  a senior stakeholder to resolve an issue that was blocking progress. What
  was at stake, and what did you do?"
- Ask a referee: "How has this person set or directed a stakeholder
  relationship strategy for a team?"
- If already in post, review a stakeholder relationship strategy document the
  person directed, and evidence of a senior-level negotiation you led.

### Skill: Strategic design and business change

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead business architect demonstrates a strong understanding of business
issues, events and activities and their short to long term impact, defines
principles, patterns, standards, policies, roadmaps and vision statements,
effectively focuses on outcomes rather than solutions and activities, and
develops, maintains or updates strategy in response to feedback and
findings.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's wording drops the "support" and "help"
qualifiers used at business architect level: this level defines principles,
patterns, standards, policies, roadmaps and vision statements outright,
rather than supporting someone else who does — full ownership of the work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe the short to long term impact of a business
  issue with any confidence.
- 1 — Beginner: describes impact when prompted but needs significant support
  to define a principle or standard.
- 2 — Intermediate: contributes to defining principles, patterns, standards
  or roadmaps with oversight.
- 3 — Advanced: defines principles, patterns, standards, policies, roadmaps
  or vision statements for a defined area with little support.
- 4 — Expert: defines principles, patterns, standards, policies, roadmaps and
  vision statements for the organisation outright, focuses consistently on
  outcomes, and develops or updates strategy in response to feedback and
  findings without needing another business architect to own the work.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about a roadmap, set of
  principles, or vision statement you defined. How did you decide what went
  in it?"
- Think about how you'd review a short scenario with conflicting feedback and
  describe how you would update a strategy in response.
- If already in post, review a roadmap, vision statement or set of principles
  the person authored, and evidence of how it was updated over time.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

A lead business architect defines strategies and policies, providing
guidance to others on working in the strategic context, and evaluates
current strategies to ensure business requirements are being met and
exceeded where possible.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework repeats this skill's wording exactly
from the business architect level below — a third instance of the same
anomaly seen in Communicating information, Consultancy and Making and
guiding decisions at this level. The baseline stays at 3 for consistency with
how the framework's own text is treated elsewhere in this document; what
should differ at lead level is the breadth of strategy being defined, which
the indicators below describe.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot evaluate whether a current strategy is meeting business
  requirements.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes an opinion on a strategy's fit when asked, but
  needs support to define strategy or guide others.
- 2 — Intermediate: evaluates a strategy against business requirements with
  guidance, and drafts strategy or policy content for others to review.
- 3 — Advanced: defines strategies and policies across the team's full
  remit, guides others in working strategically, and evaluates current
  strategies unsupported, identifying where they fall short and where they
  exceed requirements.
- 4 — Expert: sets the organisation's overall strategic direction, well
  beyond a single team's remit, and develops others' capability to think and
  act strategically at scale.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about a strategy or policy you
  defined that guided more than one team's work. How did you evaluate
  whether it was working?"
- Think about how you'd review a short strategy summary and identify where it
  meets, falls short of, or exceeds business requirements.
- If already in post, review a strategy or policy document the person
  authored that guided work beyond your immediate team.
