# Chief Information Security Officer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to record your own current practice against the
skills the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework names for this role,
using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A chief information security officer creates an environment and culture in the organisation that ensures the security of its information and technology. They enable the organisation to achieve its objectives and deliver services in a safe and secure way.

Work through the record below, one skill at a time, then fill in the
overall determination. There's no published baseline for this role level to
compare yourself against — the framework marks it **not in use** — so treat
this as a mirror, not a test: it's a record of your own practice, not a
pass or fail. Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in this record is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.
Because no baseline is published for this role level, use the scale here to
describe someone's actual, observed practice rather than to compare that
practice against a target.

**A worked example**, using Cyber incident management, one of the eight
skills this role names. Evidence at 1 — Beginner might be someone who can
describe the phases of incident response in general terms, but has only ever
followed someone else's instructions during an actual incident. Evidence at 3
— Advanced might be someone who has led the response to a real cyber
incident for a mid-sized organisation, can describe the decisions they made
under pressure, and can explain what they changed afterwards so the
organisation would detect and respond faster next time. Evidence at 4 —
Expert might be someone who has led an organisation through a genuinely
severe incident at board level, whose incident response approach other
organisations or a cross-government forum have adopted, and who can describe
how their thinking about detection, response and recovery has changed over
several incidents rather than just one.

## About this role level

**The role: chief information security officer.** A chief information
security officer creates an environment and culture in the organisation that
ensures the security of its information and technology. They enable the
organisation to achieve its objectives and deliver services in a safe and
secure way. The role is part of both the Government Digital and Data
profession and the Government Security profession. The framework describes
the role's duties as:

- create a strategy for information and cyber security that supports both
  the organisation's strategy and wider government security strategy
- lead the organisation in implementing the information and cyber security
  strategy
- evaluate the current status and maturity of information and cyber security
  in the organisation
- determine how to get to the level of information and cyber security
  maturity the organisation needs
- understand risks across the organisation and advise the board and other
  leaders on how to mitigate risks in their areas and in future plans
- enable the organisation to be innovative in a safe and secure way
- ensure the organisation is prepared for cyber attacks and can detect,
  respond to and recover from an attack
- ensure that information and cyber security aspects of crisis management
  are effective
- encourage a culture of cyber security awareness and good security
  practices
- implement practices to increase the maturity of information and cyber
  security

**The role level is published as NOT IN USE.** The framework lists chief
information security officer in the chief digital and data profession, keeps
the role description above, and marks the level itself — and every skill
expectation beneath it — as not in use. There is no level above or below
within this role to compare someone's rating against, and no published
wording for what any of the eight named skills should look like at this
level.

**The eight skills the framework names for this role**, with no expectation
published against any of them:

1. Capability building for digital, data and technology
2. Changing security culture
3. Cyber incident management
4. Cyber risk management
5. Cyber security governance
6. Innovation in digital, data and technology
7. Security architecture
8. Strategic cyber security planning

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Capability building for digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence someone has built security capability in
  others — no training programme, no coaching relationship, no example of
  raising a team's or organisation's security skill.
- 1 — Beginner: Someone can describe why security capability building
  matters, but the evidence shows only informal, occasional help to
  individual colleagues rather than a deliberate effort.
- 2 — Intermediate: Someone has contributed to a capability activity — a
  security training programme, a coaching relationship, a recruitment or
  upskilling plan for a security function — with support from a learning and
  development function.
- 3 — Advanced: Someone has independently implemented a practice that
  increased the maturity of information and cyber security through building
  people's capability, and can describe how they measured whether it worked.
- 4 — Expert: Someone has built security capability at a scale that
  changed how a whole organisation, or a wider community of security
  professionals, works with security over several years, and they are sought
  out by peers for that judgement.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a specific security capability initiative
  you led, including how you knew whether it worked.
- If you can, ask a manager, mentor or colleague to name someone whose security capability grew under your leadership, and how.
- Look back at a capability plan or training strategy you authored or
  co-authored.

### Skill: Changing security culture

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

### Skill: Cyber incident management

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence someone has been part of, let alone led,
  the response to a real cyber incident.
- 1 — Beginner: Someone can describe the phases of incident response in
  general terms, but has only followed instructions during an actual
  incident rather than led one.
- 2 — Intermediate: Someone has taken a defined role in incident response,
  with support from a more senior incident lead, and can describe what they
  personally were responsible for.
- 3 — Advanced: Someone has independently ensured their organisation is
  prepared for cyber attacks and can detect, respond to and recover from one,
  and can describe a real incident they led, without disclosing sensitive
  detail, and what changed afterwards.
- 4 — Expert: Someone has led an organisation through a genuinely severe
  incident at board level, their incident management approach has been
  adopted elsewhere, and they can describe how their thinking about
  detection, response and recovery has evolved across several incidents.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a real incident you led the response to, at
  a level of detail that shows judgement without disclosing sensitive or
  classified information.
- Ask a referee who worked alongside you during an incident how
  you performed under pressure.
- Look back at a redacted post-incident review or lessons-learned document you authored, checking it is appropriately sanitised before it goes
  anywhere near this log.

### Skill: Cyber risk management

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence someone has assessed or advised on cyber
  risk for an organisation.
- 1 — Beginner: Someone can describe cyber risk concepts in general terms,
  but has not personally advised the board or a senior leader on a real risk.
- 2 — Intermediate: Someone has contributed to a risk assessment or advice
  paper, with support from a risk function, and can describe the reasoning
  behind a specific risk judgement.
- 3 — Advanced: Someone has independently understood risks across an
  organisation and advised the board and other leaders on how to mitigate
  them, in their areas and in future plans, and can describe a risk they
  judged should be accepted rather than mitigated, and why.
- 4 — Expert: Someone's cyber risk judgement is sought at board level as a
  matter of course, they have shaped how an organisation's risk appetite for
  cyber is set, and they can describe a genuinely difficult risk trade-off
  they navigated.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a cyber risk you judged should be accepted
  rather than mitigated, and how you made that case to the board.
- Look back at a risk register entry or board risk paper you authored,
  without recording live risk detail in this log.
- Ask a board member or senior stakeholder how your risk advice
  has shaped decisions.

### Skill: Innovation in digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

  innovate safely and securely, or that they have balanced innovation
