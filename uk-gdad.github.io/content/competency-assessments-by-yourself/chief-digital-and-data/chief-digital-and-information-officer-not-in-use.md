# Chief Digital and Information Officer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to record your own current practice against the
skills the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework names for this role,
using the framework's own words for what it covers.

The chief digital and information officer shapes and enables the delivery of the organisation’s strategy through digital, data and technology. They develop partnerships across the organisation to design and deliver better outcomes that make lives easier.

Work through the record below, one skill at a time, then fill in the
overall determination. There's no published baseline for this role level to
compare yourself against — the framework marks it **not in use** — so treat
this as a mirror, not a test: it's a record of your own practice, not a
pass or fail. Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in this record is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.
Because no baseline is published for this role level, use the scale here to
describe someone's actual, observed practice rather than to compare that
practice against a target.

**A worked example**, using Leading digital products and services, one of the
eight skills this role names. Evidence at 1 — Beginner might be someone
who can describe what oversight of digital solutions involves, but has only
ever contributed to a single product team rather than overseen a portfolio.
Evidence at 3 — Advanced might be someone who has overseen the delivery,
management and maintenance of a set of digital services for a mid-sized
organisation, and can describe how they balanced strategic goals against user
needs when the two pulled in different directions. Evidence at 4 — Expert
might be someone whose oversight of an organisation's digital products and
services demonstrably changed how reliably and sustainably they were run over
several years, and who other chief digital and information officers or a
cross-government forum have cited as an example.

## About this role level

**The role: chief digital and information officer.** The chief digital and
information officer shapes and enables the delivery of the organisation's
strategy through digital, data and technology. They develop partnerships
across the organisation to design and deliver better outcomes that make lives
easier. The framework describes the role's duties as:

- lead innovative strategies that deliver the organisation's objectives
- identify and act on the changing landscape of digital, data and technology
- enable the use of innovative approaches and emerging technologies whilst
  ensuring inclusivity, security and ethical practices
- oversee the delivery, management and maintenance of sustainable digital
  solutions that meet strategic goals and user needs
- ensure data and technology architecture decisions support scalability,
  reusability and interoperability across government
- ensure the organisation remains safe, secure and resilient to cyber threats
  and attacks
- negotiate and secure investment for digital, data and technology
- advise on investment priorities across competing demands to maximise
  benefits and outcomes
- ensure the organisation anticipates and adapts to changes in capability
  needs
- be a trusted advisor to the board on all matters of digital, data and
  technology

**The role level is published as NOT IN USE.** The framework lists chief
digital and information officer in the chief digital and data profession,
keeps the role description above, and marks the level itself — and every
skill expectation beneath it — as not in use. There is no level above or
below within this role to compare someone's rating against, and no
published wording for what any of the eight named skills should look like at
this level.

**The eight skills the framework names for this role**, with no expectation
published against any of them:

1. Capability building for digital, data and technology
2. Ensuring resilience of digital, data and technology
3. Financial management for digital, data and technology
4. Innovation in digital, data and technology
5. Leading digital products and services
6. Making strategic performance-led change
7. Optimising technology and data architecture
8. Shaping the direction of organisational strategy through digital, data and
   technology

### Skill: Capability building for digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence someone has planned for or built digital,
  data or technology capability — no workforce plan, no capability review, no
  example of anticipating a future skills gap.
- 1 — Beginner: Someone can describe why capability planning matters, but
  the evidence shows only reactive responses to skills gaps as they appeared,
  rather than anticipation.
- 2 — Intermediate: Someone has contributed to a capability plan or
  workforce review, with support from a learning and development or HR
  function, and can describe a specific capability gap it addressed.
- 3 — Advanced: Someone has independently ensured their organisation
  anticipates and adapts to changes in its digital, data and technology
  capability needs, and can point to a specific future gap they identified
  before it became a problem.
- 4 — Expert: Someone's capability planning has demonstrably shaped how an
  organisation's digital, data and technology workforce evolved over several
  years, and other senior leaders look to them for judgement on where
  capability needs are heading next.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a capability gap you anticipated before it
  became urgent, and what you did about it.
- Look back at a workforce plan or capability strategy you authored or
  sponsored.
- Ask a referee how the organisation's digital, data and technology
  capability changed under your leadership.

### Skill: Ensuring resilience of digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Someone cannot describe how their organisation protects itself
  against cyber threats or disruption, and shows no evidence of having been
  accountable for resilience.
- 1 — Beginner: Someone can describe what resilience involves in general
  terms, but has not personally been accountable for a resilience decision.
- 2 — Intermediate: Someone has contributed to a resilience or security
  improvement, working with a security function, and can describe a specific
  threat or disruption it addressed.
- 3 — Advanced: Someone has independently ensured their organisation
  remains safe, secure and resilient to cyber threats and attacks, and can
  describe a real incident or near-miss and how the organisation's resilience
  held up or was strengthened as a result.
- 4 — Expert: Someone's approach to resilience has demonstrably reduced an
  organisation's exposure to cyber threats over time, they have led the
  organisation through a genuine security incident at board level, and other
  senior leaders look to them for judgement on resilience investment.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a resilience or security investment decision
  you made, and what trade-off it required against other priorities.
- Ask the organisation's chief information security officer, if available,
  how you have engaged with resilience in practice.
- Look back at a resilience strategy or post-incident review you authored
  or sponsored, without recording live vulnerability detail in this log.

### Skill: Optimising technology and data architecture
