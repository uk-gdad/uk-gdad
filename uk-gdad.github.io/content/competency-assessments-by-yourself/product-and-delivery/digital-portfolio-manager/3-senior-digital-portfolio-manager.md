# Senior Digital Portfolio Manager - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A digital portfolio manager co-ordinates and manages a group , or ‘portfolio’, of digital projects. In this role, you will use an iterative and flexible approach to help define governance processes for this portfolio. A senior digital portfolio manager is responsible for co-ordinating and managing a portfolio of digital projects.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale.
This is a **proficiency** scale: it rates how well someone does the thing,
on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Planning.** At 1, a person can explain why a portfolio
plan matters and has helped build part of one under close supervision, but
has not managed dependencies between projects themselves. At 2, they can
build a plan for part of a portfolio, using data to inform it, though a more
senior colleague still checks it before it is shared widely. At 3, they
manage complex dependencies across several projects unaided, spot and remove
a blocker before it derails a delivery date, and can develop a workable plan
for a genuinely difficult situation — for example, two projects competing for
the same specialist team. At 4, beyond what this level expects, they would be
setting the planning approach for a whole portfolio function and coaching
other senior portfolio managers on it.

## About this role level

A digital portfolio manager co-ordinates and manages a group, or "portfolio",
of digital projects, using an iterative and flexible approach to help define
the governance processes for that portfolio.

This role has three levels: **2. Digital portfolio manager**, **3. Senior
digital portfolio manager** — you are assessing this level — and **4. Head
of portfolio**.

At this level, a senior digital portfolio manager is responsible for
co-ordinating and managing a portfolio of digital projects. In this role, you
will:

- track, analyse and help deliver outcomes in your portfolio
- give guidance and deal with issues to help teams to deliver faster
- manage governance and assurance processes, and ensure standards are met
- provide information to stakeholders on work performance and related risks

This level names the same six skills as the level below it: Agile working,
Communicating analysis and insight, Governance and assurance, Planning,
Stakeholder relationship management, and Strategic thinking. What the
framework expects of each one, and to what depth, is set out skill by skill
in the competency matrix below.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an awareness of Agile methodology and of the ways to
apply its principles in practice. It expects an open-minded approach, the
ability to explain why iteration matters, and the ability to iterate
quickly.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework repeats this skill's wording
unchanged from the entry level — it is still "show an awareness" and
"explain why" rather than a description that grows with seniority. The
baseline still moves up one point here, not because this skill's own text
demands it, but because the surrounding accountabilities at this level —
tracking and delivering outcomes, giving guidance to teams — assume someone
who applies Agile principles unprompted, in day-to-day work, rather than
someone still learning what they are.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what an iterative or Agile approach is, or has no
  experience working in a team that used one.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain why working iteratively helps a portfolio, and
  has taken part in Agile ceremonies as a team member under someone else's
  facilitation.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies Agile principles to their own work without
  prompting, and adjusts a team's working pattern based on what a
  retrospective surfaced.
- 3 — Advanced: Adapts an Agile approach to a portfolio's specific
  constraints, and helps more than one team move from a rigid plan to an
  iterative one when circumstances change.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches other portfolio staff on applying Agile principles, and
  is sought out when a team is stuck on how to work more iteratively.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a portfolio-level decision you changed after
  an early iteration showed it was not working.
- In a structured interview, ask how you have helped a team that was
  resistant to working iteratively.
- Ask a referee for an example of you adjusting a plan mid-flight
  because an iteration surfaced new information.

### Skill: Communicating analysis and insight

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to turn complex data into compelling, clear
and actionable stories, to share data communication skills with the team and
across government, to present analysis and visualisations clearly to
communicate complex messages, to communicate both negative and positive
information to stakeholders, and to give tactical and strategic
recommendations.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework has dropped "with support" entirely and
added "share... skills with the team and across government" and "strategic
recommendations" — independent, complex communication work that goes beyond
this level's own portfolio, which matches an advanced rating.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not produced or presented any analysis of portfolio data to
  another person.
- 1 — Beginner: Has put together a status update with a more senior
  colleague checking it before it goes to a stakeholder.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses the right format and level of detail for the
  audience unprompted, but sticks to reporting what has happened rather than
  recommending what to do about it.
- 3 — Advanced: Turns complex, messy portfolio data into a clear story
  unaided, delivers difficult news to a stakeholder without it landing
  badly, and gives recommendations that shape a decision.
- 4 — Expert: Is asked by colleagues beyond their own department to help
  them communicate analysis more clearly, raising the standard across
  government rather than only within their own portfolio.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a work sample: a short briefing that delivers bad news about a
  portfolio's performance to a senior stakeholder, alongside a
  recommendation.
- In a structured interview, ask you to describe a time you helped a
  colleague present analysis more clearly, and what changed as a result.
- Ask a referee whether your recommendations, not just your
  reporting, have changed a decision.

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to develop appropriate governance and
assurance processes, to identify and recommend improvements to governance and
assurance processes, and to support others in following or applying them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Develop... processes" and "support others" are a
real step up from the level below's "explain" and "follow", but the framework
does not yet describe owning governance across more than one portfolio or
setting the wider standard — that sits with the level above — so an
intermediate baseline fits.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what a governance process such as a stage gate
  or a project board is for.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an existing governance process correctly when told
  to, but has not designed or changed one.
- 2 — Intermediate: Develops a governance process fitted to a specific
  portfolio's size and risk, and helps a colleague understand how to apply
  it.
- 3 — Advanced: Spots a systemic weakness in how governance is being applied
  across a portfolio, and gets a recommended improvement adopted.
- 4 — Expert: Sets or reviews governance standards across more than one
  portfolio, and other senior portfolio managers seek their advice.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a governance process you designed or
  significantly changed, and why the existing one was not fit for purpose.
- In a work sample, give a portfolio scenario with a governance gap and ask
  how you would close it.
- Ask a referee for an example of you helping a colleague apply a
  governance process correctly, rather than doing it for you.

### Skill: Planning

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to understand the environment and
prioritise the most important or high-value tasks, to use data to inform
planning, to manage complex internal and external dependencies, to provide
delivery confidence, to remove blockers or impediments affecting plans (and
to develop a plan for difficult situations), and to ensure that teams plan
appropriately for their capacity.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Six substantive bullets describing independent,
complex work — managing dependencies, removing blockers, developing plans for
difficult situations — none of which need close guidance, which matches an
advanced rating.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe having planned or prioritised work across more
  than a single task.
- 1 — Beginner: Has helped build part of a plan for a small piece of work,
  with someone more senior checking the result.
- 2 — Intermediate: Prioritises tasks within a project using data, with some
  guidance on how the pieces fit together across the wider portfolio.
- 3 — Advanced: Manages dependencies across several projects unaided,
  removes a blocker before it derails a delivery date, and can give
  stakeholders confidence in a plan even when the situation is genuinely
  difficult.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the planning approach for a whole portfolio function, and
  is brought in to rescue planning on portfolios other than their own.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a time you managed a dependency between two
  projects that were competing for the same people or budget.
- In a work sample, give a scenario where a delivery date is at risk because
  of a blocker outside your direct control, and ask how you
  would remove it.
- Ask a referee whether stakeholders trusted your delivery
  confidence — did your plans hold up, and if not, did you say so early?

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to work with the team to develop and
maintain an understanding of stakeholders, to work with the team to develop
and implement stakeholder communications strategies, to identify and resolve
issues, influence stakeholders and manage relationships effectively, and to
build long-term strategic relationships, communicating clearly and regularly.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Identify and resolve issues, influence
stakeholders" and "build long-term strategic relationships" describe
independent, effective relationship management that goes beyond the entry
level's "build and reach consensus", so the baseline moves up to advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name the stakeholders on a portfolio they have been part
  of.
- 1 — Beginner: Communicates with stakeholders when told to, but has not
  led a stakeholder communications approach.
- 2 — Intermediate: Works with the team to build a stakeholder
  communications strategy, and tailors messages to different audiences
  without prompting.
- 3 — Advanced: Resolves a stakeholder issue by influencing them directly,
  rather than escalating it, and sustains a long-term relationship with a
  senior stakeholder across more than one piece of work.
- 4 — Expert: Is asked to lead or rescue a relationship with a difficult
  stakeholder on behalf of colleagues, and mentors others in doing the same.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a stakeholder relationship that was
  difficult when you inherited it, and what you did to improve it.
- In a work sample, give a scenario where a senior stakeholder disagrees
  with a portfolio decision, and ask how you would influence the outcome.
- Ask a referee whether stakeholders have specifically asked for this person
  to be involved in future work.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to work within a strategic context and
communicate how activities meet strategic goals, and to contribute to the
development of strategy and policies.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Contribute to the development of strategy and
policies" is a genuine step beyond the entry level's administrative support,
but it stops short of setting strategic direction, which is why the baseline
sits at intermediate rather than advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain how the portfolio they work on connects to any
  wider organisational goal.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain why their work matters to the wider strategy,
  but has only supported strategic planning administratively.
- 2 — Intermediate: Communicates how portfolio activities meet strategic
  goals to others, and contributes original input to a strategy or policy
  document.
- 3 — Advanced: Shapes part of a strategy or policy, and makes the case for
  a portfolio-level change that supports a wider organisational goal.
- 4 — Expert: Sets strategic direction for a portfolio function and
  represents that thinking to senior leadership.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a piece of strategy or policy work you
  contributed to, and what your specific contribution was.
- In a structured interview, ask how you have explained a strategic goal to
  a team that did not initially see the connection to your work.
- Ask a referee whether you have been invited to strategy
  discussions, and what you brought to you.
