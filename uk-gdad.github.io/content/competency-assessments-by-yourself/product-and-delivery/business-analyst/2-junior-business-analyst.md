# Junior Business Analyst - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

At this level, you will have limited skills and will work with others or under supervision. You will support stakeholder relationship management.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or
how they feel about it.

**Worked example — Business modelling.** At 0, someone cannot describe
what a business model or process diagram is for. At 2 — the baseline for this
role level — someone can explain a basic business modelling technique and
has supported the representation of a business situation within a task someone
else scoped, such as producing a simple process diagram for one part of a
larger piece of work. At 4, by contrast, someone leads the modelling of
complex business processes across an entire programme and manages the impact
of proposed changes — evidence of that scope would be well beyond a junior
business analyst's accountabilities and would suggest someone is ready to
be assessed against a more senior role level instead.

## About this role level

The product and delivery profession's business analyst role exists to help
teams analyse and understand a business problem or opportunity: to research
how a business or business area works — its people, organisation, processes,
information, data and technology — identify areas for improvement, explore
feasible options, analyse the effects of change and define success measures.
Business analysts identify and elaborate user and business needs so that
services and business change can be effectively designed, developed and
tested; they make prioritisation and minimum viable product decisions using
analysis-led insights; they ensure new products and services meet business and
user needs and align with organisational goals; and they understand the
business and policy constraints a piece of work sits within, and assess the
implications.

The role has six levels: trainee business analyst, junior business analyst,
business analyst, senior business analyst, lead business analyst, and head of
business analysis. This assessment is for the second of those.

A **junior business analyst** receives direction from more senior business
analysts, but is responsible for the output of specific tasks. At this level,
the framework says someone will have limited skills and will work with
others or under supervision, and will support stakeholder relationship
management. This marks a step up from a trainee: the framework now expects
ownership of a defined task's output, not just contribution to someone else's
work, though the scope of that ownership stays narrow and supervision stays
close.

The framework names nine skills for this level: Adapting to delivery
methodologies, Business modelling, Business process improvement, Context,
problem and option analysis, Defining and managing business needs, user needs
and requirements, Stakeholder relationship management, Digital and data
systems analysis, Testing (business analysis), and User experience analysis.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Adapting to delivery methodologies

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior business analyst is expected to explain the differences between
delivery methodologies such as scrum or kanban, to explain how their approach
would change based on the delivery methodology in use, to explain why
iteration is important, and to explain how roles in a multidisciplinary team
work together to support delivery.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's wording for this skill is identical to
the trainee level — knowledge and explanation, not independent adaptation of
working practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a delivery methodology or say what scrum or kanban are
  for.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe, in general terms, the difference between an
  Agile and a waterfall approach, and can say why teams work in iterations.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has worked within a scrum or kanban team's rhythm on real
  task-based work, and can describe how their own output fitted the team's
  cadence.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level. Explaining how roles interact
  across a whole multidisciplinary team, with independent judgement about
  approach, belongs to a later level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a real piece of task-based work and how the
  team's delivery rhythm shaped when and how you delivered it.
- Give a short scenario about a missed sprint goal or a blocked kanban column
  and try to reason through what might be going on.
- Ask a supervisor or referee how actively you takes part in
  ceremonies such as stand-ups or retrospectives.

### Skill: Business modelling

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior business analyst is expected to explain basic business modelling
techniques, to support the representation of business situations within a
defined task, and to visualise business processes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is a new skill at this level and the
framework already asks for a produced output — supporting representation
within a defined task — not just description, so the baseline sits a point
above pure explanation.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what a business model or process diagram is for.
- 1 — Beginner: Can name a basic business modelling technique, such as a
  process flow diagram, but has not produced one.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has produced a simple visualisation of a business process
  or situation within a task someone else scoped, and can walk through what it
  shows.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level. Modelling with limited direction,
  and reasoning about how changes ripple through a process, belongs to the
  business analyst level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask to see a process diagram, flowchart, or similar visualisation you have produced, even from training.
- A short task: describe a simple, multi-step business scenario and try to sketch it as a basic diagram.
- Ask a supervisor whether your diagrams and models have needed
  significant rework, or have been usable with light review.

### Skill: Business process improvement

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior business analyst is expected to contribute to the analysis and
evaluation of business processes to identify problems and opportunities, to
contribute to the design and implementation of business process improvements,
and to contribute to business process testing and usability evaluation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The wording is unchanged from the trainee level —
"contribute to" throughout — so the baseline stays the same even though someone now owns discrete task outputs elsewhere.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never taken part in mapping or improving a business process.
- 1 — Beginner: Has documented an existing process under direction and can
  explain what it was for.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has contributed a specific, useful observation or
  suggestion to a process improvement exercise.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level. Designing and implementing a
  process improvement independently belongs to a later level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a process map or "as-is" description you have
  helped produce.
- Ask what you noticed while documenting a process that you thought could be
  better, and why.
- A short task: give a deliberately clunky process and try to
  describe it back and flag anything odd.

### Skill: Context, problem and option analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior business analyst is expected to describe the analysis techniques
they could use to help understand the context, problem and options to meet
business and user needs, to explain the importance of understanding that
internal and external factors influence the organisation, and to contribute to
identifying and assessing multiple options and formulating recommendations,
under supervision.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The wording is unchanged from the trainee level —
description, explanation, and supervised contribution.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name an analysis technique or explain why context matters
  to a business problem.
- 1 — Beginner: Can name one or two analysis techniques and explain, in
  general terms, why internal and external factors matter to a problem.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has contributed, under supervision, to identifying options
  for a real piece of work, and can explain the reasoning behind at least one
  option considered.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level. Independently formulating and
  defending a recommendation belongs to a later level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a problem you have analysed and what
  technique you used to understand it.
- Present a short business scenario and ask which of two or three named
  techniques you would reach for first, and why.
- Ask a supervisor whether you have contributed usefully to
  identifying options in a supervised setting.

### Skill: Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior business analyst is expected to support identifying, analysing,
capturing and validating business and user requirements, to work under
supervision to manage requirements and support their prioritisation within a
defined scope, and to provide distinct task-based outputs for parts of a
project as directed, using a predetermined requirements management life
cycle.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework repeats the trainee wording, but at
this level "distinct task-based outputs" are someone's own responsibility
rather than assistance to someone else's — a small but real step up in what
you should expect to see delivered.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never captured or documented a requirement.
- 1 — Beginner: Has captured a small number of requirements under close
  direction, using a template someone else set.
- 2 — Intermediate: Owns the requirements output for a small, clearly bounded
  part of a project, using a predetermined life cycle, and produces work that
  needs only light review.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level. Co-ordinating and reviewing
  prioritisation with limited supervision belongs to the business analyst
  level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask to see a requirement or user story you have owned end to end
  for a defined task.
- A short task: give a brief business need and try to turn it
  into two or three clear requirements, then explain how you would validate
  you.
- Ask a supervisor how much rework your requirements outputs
  typically need.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior business analyst is expected to describe who their stakeholders are
and the importance of managing relationships with them, and to explain what
their stakeholders find important and why.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The skill's own wording is unchanged from
trainee, but the level description explicitly says a junior business analyst
"will support stakeholder relationship management" — real, if supported,
practice rather than description alone — so the baseline moves up a point.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot identify who a stakeholder is on a piece of work, or why
  their views matter.
- 1 — Beginner: Can name the stakeholders on a piece of work and give a
  plausible reason why each one cares about the outcome, but has had no direct
  contact with them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has had direct, supported contact with a stakeholder — for
  example fielding a question or providing an update — and can describe what
  they learned about that stakeholder's priorities from it.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level. Independently identifying and
  building relationships with stakeholders, and tailoring communication to
  their needs, belongs to a later level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd map the stakeholders on a piece of work and explain
  each one's interest and how you interacted with you.
- Ask about a time a stakeholder's priorities were unclear or conflicting, and
  what you did.
- Ask a supervisor whether you handles direct stakeholder contact
  professionally and knows when to escalate.

### Skill: User experience analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

A junior business analyst is expected to understand the importance of making
data-informed decisions based on user research findings, and to support the
analysis and prioritisation of user experience needs, understanding how needs
tie to system, product or service requirements.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. This is a new skill at this level, and the wording —
"understand" and "support" — describes supervised, supporting work rather than
independent analysis.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain why user research findings should inform a
  decision.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain, in general terms, why decisions should be
  informed by user research, and can describe an example of user research
  findings they have seen used.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has supported the analysis of user experience needs on a
  real or simulated piece of work, and can describe how a need connected to a
  requirement.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level. Advising on the approach to
  analysing and prioritising user experience needs belongs to a later level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a piece of user research you have read or
  used, and what decision it informed.
- A short task: give a short user need statement and try to say
  which system or service requirement it might connect to.
- Ask a supervisor whether you have supported user experience
  analysis work usefully when directed.
