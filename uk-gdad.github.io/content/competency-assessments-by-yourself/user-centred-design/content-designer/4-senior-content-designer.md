# Senior Content Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

Content designers make things easier for people to understand and use. This can involve working on a single piece of content or on the end-to-end journey of a service to help users complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. In this role your work may involve the creation of, or change to, a transaction, product or single piece of content that stretches across digital and offline channels. A senior content designer is an expert practitioner who develops content strategy and solutions for large-scale problems and high-profile events.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using User-centred content design, a skill this level
names:

- Someone who designs and publishes their own content well but has not
  yet taken responsibility for anyone else's sits at **3 — Advanced**, below
  this assessment's baseline.
- Someone who takes responsibility for assuring the quality of more
  junior colleagues' content, coaches and guides them, and delivers through
  others by directing and critiquing their work sits at **4 — Expert** — the
  baseline this assessment sets for this skill.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for
people to understand and use. Their work can involve a single piece of
content or the end-to-end journey of a service, helping users complete their
goal and government deliver a policy intent. The role makes sure appropriate
content is shown to a user in the right place and the best format, starting
from discovery and working closely with user researchers, service designers
and interaction designers.

**This level: senior content designer.** The framework describes this level
as an expert practitioner who develops content strategy and solutions for
large-scale problems and high-profile events. At this level, the framework
expects someone to:

- take responsibility for content quality, manage small teams and mentor
  content colleagues
- write and map user stories
- review the work of others to assure quality
- lead on cross-government content projects
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government content community

**Where this level sits.** Content designer has six levels:

1. Associate content designer
2. Junior content designer
3. Content designer
4. **Senior content designer** — assessed here
5. Lead content designer
6. Head of content design

**A note on Agile working (content design).** The framework's published
skill text for this skill repeats, word for word, the text published for
content designer (level 3) — coaching and leading teams in Agile and Lean
practice is not published until lead content designer (level 5). This
assessment sets that skill's baseline from the framework's actual published
text rather than assuming this level's general seniority carries into every
skill equally, and says so here rather than smoothing the anomaly away.

**The six skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to understand and demonstrate awareness of
Agile methodology and apply an Agile mindset to their work; work in a
fast-paced, evolving environment using an iterative and flexible approach;
appreciate the importance of Agile project delivery to digital projects in
government; be unafraid to take risks and willing to learn from mistakes;
ensure the team has situational awareness of one another's work; and use a
range of tools and platforms to plan and manage their work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. As noted above, this skill's published text
repeats content designer's exactly, and leading a team in Agile and Lean
practice is not published for this skill until lead content designer — this
assessment reflects what is actually written rather than the level's general
seniority.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Follows an Agile process without understanding why the team
  works that way.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains Agile methodology but needs support to apply it
  independently.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies an Agile mindset independently, takes reasonable
  risks, and uses the team's tools to plan and manage their own work.
- 3 — Advanced: Proactively keeps the team's situational awareness current
  across a piece of work with several moving parts — already working
  towards lead content designer's coaching accountability.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches teams in Agile and Lean practice and decides the right
  approach for a project — the lead content designer expectation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a time you took a reasonable risk in a
  fast-paced delivery and what you learned from the outcome.
- Ask how you keep a team aware of each other's work when several people
  are contributing to the same content at once.
- Observe you in a sprint planning or delivery-tracking session, if
  practical.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to build and create a variety of different
prototypes, from paper sketches to coded prototypes; choose the most
appropriate option to visualise content in context for different audiences;
effectively combine prototypes with other content and design outputs to test
with users and show to stakeholders; and establish content patterns, and
test and iterate them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Build and create a variety" and "establish content
patterns" describe independent, self-directed prototyping practice across a
whole range of techniques, a real step up from content designer's choosing
between existing techniques.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot build a prototype without significant support.
- 1 — Beginner: Builds one type of prototype competently but is not
  confident moving between paper sketches and coded prototypes.
- 2 — Intermediate: Builds a range of prototype types with some support
  choosing which suits the audience.
- 3 — Advanced: Builds and creates prototypes across the full range
  independently, chooses the right one for the audience, combines them with
  other outputs to test with users, and establishes and iterates content
  patterns.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches others in prototyping technique and sets the standard
  a wider team works to — above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: ask you to produce two different prototypes of the
  same content idea, at different fidelities, and explain when each would be
  used.
- Think about how you'd describe a content pattern you established and how you
  tested and iterated it.
- Look back at a range of prototypes you have produced for different audiences.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to negotiate with and influence
stakeholders, and manage relationships effectively; influence decisions,
deal with challenging situations, and remove blockers; and build long-term
strategic relationships and communicate clearly and regularly with
stakeholders, supporting mutual needs and commitments while focusing on user
needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Negotiating, influencing decisions and removing
blockers describes managing relationships as an active lever on the work's
outcome, not just maintaining them — a genuine step from content designer's
consensus-building.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe a blocker they removed through a stakeholder
  relationship.
- 1 — Beginner: Manages straightforward stakeholder relationships but
  escalates a challenging situation rather than working through it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Negotiates on a lower-stakes issue with some support.
- 3 — Advanced: Negotiates with and influences a stakeholder to remove a
  genuine blocker, manages a challenging situation without escalating it,
  and builds a long-term strategic relationship that supports both parties'
  needs.
- 4 — Expert: Directs the organisation's whole strategic approach to
  stakeholder relationships — above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a blocker you removed by negotiating with a
  stakeholder, and what the negotiation involved.
- Think about how you'd describe a challenging stakeholder situation and how you
  resolved it without it escalating further.
- Ask a referee, ideally a stakeholder you have worked with over time, how
  the relationship has developed.

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to help lead the design and implementation
of strategies, evaluating their impact and progress to ensure business
objectives and the needs of users are being met, and to lead and direct
strategic content improvement projects, focusing effort in the areas of
greatest priority and ensuring goals and objectives are met.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Help lead" and "lead and direct" describe active
leadership of strategic work, not contribution to someone else's — matching
this level's own accountability to lead on cross-government content
projects.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Contributes to strategy only when told exactly what to write.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes meaningfully to a strategy someone else leads.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads a small, contained strategic improvement with
  support from a more senior colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps lead the design and implementation of a strategy,
  evaluates its impact against business objectives and user needs, and
  directs a strategic content improvement project by prioritising where
  effort matters most.
- 4 — Expert: Leads content strategy for the whole organisation — the head
  of content design expectation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a strategy or strategic improvement project
  you helped lead, and how you evaluated whether it worked.
- Think about how you'd describe how you decided where to focus effort on a
  cross-government content project.
- Look back at a strategy document or evaluation you have authored or co-authored.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to collaborate with user researchers and
performance analysts to advocate effectively for users; understand the
different types of user research, evidence and data and how they are used
at different stages of product development (alpha, beta, live); formulate
hypotheses, gain insights from research, and make decisions based on
findings, clearly explaining how decisions were made; evaluate quantitative
and qualitative data and prioritise and define approaches to best understand
users; champion user research to focus on all users; put accessibility
requirements at the heart of approaches to designing content, and encourage
others to do the same; and guide others and make recommendations on the best
tools and methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This block explicitly guides and champions on others'
behalf — "encourage others to do the same", "guide others and make
recommendations" — which is this assessment's Expert point by the rating
scale's own definition of teaching or guiding others in a skill.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain how user research differs across alpha, beta and
  live.
- 1 — Beginner: Uses research appropriate to a project stage with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Formulates a hypothesis and makes a decision from
  findings, but has not yet guided a colleague through the same process.
- 3 — Advanced: Champions user research for underrepresented users on their
  own work, and puts accessibility at the heart of their own approach — one
  step below this level's baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Guides colleagues on which research tools and methods to use,
  and actively encourages others to put accessibility at the heart of their
  own content design.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a time you guided a colleague's choice of
  research method or tool, and what changed as a result.
- Think about how you'd describe how you have championed accessibility in another
  person's content design work.
- Look back at a written explanation of a decision you made from research
  findings, aimed at a wider audience than your immediate team.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to show a deep understanding of end-to-end
journeys and how content is affected within these journeys; identify where
journey fixes or content improvements need to be made; take responsibility
for assuring the quality of content from more junior colleagues, coaching
and guiding them to improve; ensure that content is regularly reviewed and
evaluated, contributing to continuous improvements and iteration; encourage
a continuous improvement mindset in teams and more junior content
colleagues; and deliver through others, directing and critiquing their work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Assuring the quality of content from more junior
colleagues, coaching and guiding them" and "deliver through others" describe
exactly what the rating scale's Expert point means: demonstrating mastery
and teaching or guiding others in the skill, and it matches this level's own
accountability to review the work of others to assure quality.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never reviewed another content designer's work for quality.
- 1 — Beginner: Reviews a colleague's content when specifically asked, but
  does not yet coach them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Gives feedback on a colleague's content that improves it.
- 3 — Advanced: Identifies where a journey fix or content improvement is
  needed across a whole service, not just one piece of content — one step
  below this level's baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Takes responsibility for assuring the quality of junior
  colleagues' content, coaches and guides them to improve, and delivers a
  significant piece of work through others by directing and critiquing
  their output.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a time you coached a junior colleague to
  improve a specific piece of content, and what changed.
- Think about how you'd describe a journey-level content improvement you identified
  and led.
- Review feedback or critique you have given on another person's published
  work, with names removed if needed.
- Ask a referee, ideally someone you have mentored, what the coaching
  relationship was like.
