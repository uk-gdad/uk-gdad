# Content Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

Content designers make things easier for people to understand and use. This can involve working on a single piece of content or on the end-to-end journey of a service to help users complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. In this role your work may involve the creation of, or change to, a transaction, product or single piece of content that stretches across digital and offline channels. A content designer is responsible for creating, evaluating, updating and reviewing content at all stages of the end-to-end user journey.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using User-centred content design, a skill this level
names:

- Someone who applies style standards consistently but still needs
  review before publishing sits at **2 — Intermediate**, below this
  assessment's baseline.
- Someone who designs content to meet complex user needs, writes,
  edits and publishes it using a content management system, understands
  government accessibility requirements, and works autonomously sits at
  **3 — Advanced** — the baseline this assessment sets for this skill.
- Someone who additionally takes responsibility for the quality of
  other people's content and coaches them is already working towards senior
  content designer, at **4 — Expert**.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for
people to understand and use. Their work can involve a single piece of
content or the end-to-end journey of a service, helping users complete their
goal and government deliver a policy intent. The role makes sure appropriate
content is shown to a user in the right place and the best format, starting
from discovery and working closely with user researchers, service designers
and interaction designers.

**This level: content designer.** The framework describes this level as
responsible for creating, evaluating, updating and reviewing content at all
stages of the end-to-end user journey. At this level, the framework expects
someone to:

- be comfortable using evidence, data and research to make content decisions
- build relationships across government to focus on the needs of the user
  and to influence stakeholders
- contribute to and use the style guides and content patterns
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government content community

**Where this level sits.** Content designer has six levels:

1. Associate content designer
2. Junior content designer
3. **Content designer** — assessed here
4. Senior content designer
5. Lead content designer
6. Head of content design

Every skill's published wording expands substantially at this level compared
with junior content designer, and User-centred content design explicitly
states "work autonomously" for the first time in the role's ladder — the
clearest marker that this is the level where full, independent practice is
expected.

**The six skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to understand and demonstrate awareness of
Agile methodology and apply an Agile mindset to their work; work in a
fast-paced, evolving environment using an iterative and flexible approach to
enable rapid delivery; appreciate the importance of Agile project delivery
to digital projects in government; be unafraid to take risks and willing to
learn from mistakes; ensure the team has situational awareness of one
another's work and how it relates to objectives and user needs; and use a
range of tools and platforms to plan and manage their work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Applying an Agile mindset and managing work with
a range of tools describes solid, independent day-to-day practice; leading
or coaching a team in Agile and Lean practice is reserved for senior content
designer upwards.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Follows an Agile process without understanding why the team
  works that way.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains Agile methodology but needs support to apply it to
  their own work in a fast-paced environment.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies an Agile mindset independently, takes reasonable
  risks and learns visibly from mistakes, and uses the team's tools to plan
  and manage their own work.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps the team maintain situational awareness of each
  other's work and proactively flags where objectives and user needs have
  drifted apart — already working towards senior content designer.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches teams in Agile and Lean practice and decides the right
  approach for a project — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a time you took a reasonable risk in a
  fast-paced delivery and what you learned from the outcome.
- Ask which tools and platforms you use to plan and manage your own work,
  and why.
- Observe you in a sprint planning or delivery-tracking session, if
  practical.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to understand different prototyping
techniques, from paper sketches to coded prototypes, and use them to
visualise content in context; choose the best option to make content
understandable to different audiences and demonstrate a proposed approach to
content changes or improvements; and show the value of prototyping to the
team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Choosing between techniques and demonstrating a
proposed approach unsupported is genuine independent practice; building and
iterating a range of prototypes for multiple audiences at scale is reserved
for senior content designer.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain the difference between a low- and high-fidelity
  prototype.
- 1 — Beginner: Builds a simple prototype with support from a colleague.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses a prototyping technique appropriate to an
  audience unsupported, and uses it to demonstrate a proposed content
  change.
- 3 — Advanced: Combines prototyping with other content and design outputs
  to test with users and show to stakeholders — already working towards
  senior content designer.
- 4 — Expert: Establishes content patterns and tests and iterates them at
  scale — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: ask you to sketch or build a quick prototype for a
  given piece of content, and explain the technique you chose and why.
- Think about how you'd describe a time you used a prototype to demonstrate a
  proposed change to a stakeholder.
- Look back at a prototype or content mock-up you have produced.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to identify important stakeholders and
communicate with them clearly and regularly; tailor communication to
stakeholders' needs and work with them to build relationships while also
meeting user needs; build and reach consensus; and work to improve
stakeholder relationships, using evidence to explain decisions made.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Identifying, communicating with and building
consensus with stakeholders directly is real independent practice, matching
this level's own accountability to build relationships across government;
negotiating and removing blockers for stakeholders is reserved for senior
content designer.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name the stakeholders relevant to a piece of work they
  are responsible for.
- 1 — Beginner: Communicates with an identified stakeholder when told to,
  but does not yet initiate the relationship.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies important stakeholders unprompted,
  communicates with them clearly and regularly, and uses evidence to explain
  a content decision when a stakeholder disagrees.
- 3 — Advanced: Negotiates with a stakeholder to remove a blocker, and
  manages a challenging situation without escalating it — already working
  towards senior content designer.
- 4 — Expert: Builds long-term strategic relationships across the
  organisation — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a stakeholder relationship you built and
  maintained, and how you kept communication regular.
- Think about how you'd describe a time you used evidence to explain a content
  decision a stakeholder disagreed with.
- Ask a referee how you are regarded by the stakeholders you work
  with regularly.

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to contribute to content strategies and
policies, create content patterns or standards, provide support for content
improvement projects, and effectively focus on outcomes rather than
solutions and activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Creating content patterns or standards, not just
using them, and focusing on outcomes rather than activity is a real step
from junior content designer's supporting role, matching this level's
accountability to contribute to and use style guides and content patterns.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Describes their work in terms of tasks completed rather than
  outcomes achieved.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to a content strategy or pattern when
  specifically asked, but does not yet focus on outcomes unprompted.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes independently to a content strategy or
  policy, creates or improves a content pattern or standard, and can explain
  their work in terms of the outcome it achieved.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps lead the design and implementation of a content
  strategy, evaluating its impact — already working towards senior content
  designer.
- 4 — Expert: Leads content strategy for the whole organisation — well
  above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a content pattern or standard you created
  or improved, and how you knew it worked.
- Think about how you'd describe a piece of work in terms of the outcome it achieved,
  not just what you did.
- Look back at a content strategy or policy document you contributed to.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to identify tasks that will provide
insights into a problem; formulate hypotheses, gain insights from data and
user research, and make decisions on findings; understand the range of
different users who might access content and services and identify their
needs based on evidence; translate user stories into content that meets
users' needs and propose suitable design approaches; and use quantitative
and qualitative data about users to turn user insights into outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is one of the two skills that carries this
level's central accountability — being comfortable using evidence, data and
research to make content decisions — and the framework's own wording
describes independently forming hypotheses and making decisions from
findings, not just applying someone else's research.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe a content decision they made based on user
  evidence.
- 1 — Beginner: Uses user research someone else gathered to inform a
  decision, with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies a task that would give insight into a
  problem, and uses quantitative or qualitative data with some support to
  reach a decision.
- 3 — Advanced: Formulates a hypothesis, gathers or draws on user research
  to test it, makes a content decision from the findings, and translates a
  user story into content that meets the need it describes.
- 4 — Expert: Champions user research to focus on all users and evaluates
  data to prioritise across a whole programme of work — the senior content
  designer expectation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: give a short user story and try to translate it
  into content, explaining your reasoning.
- Think about how you'd describe a hypothesis you formed, how you tested it, and
  what you decided as a result.
- Look back at a piece of analysis where you turned quantitative or qualitative
  user data into a content outcome.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to design content to meet user needs and
make complex language and processes easy to understand; present the right
information in the right format for what users need; show robust experience
in writing, editing and publishing digital content, and experience using
content management systems and content production processes; write in plain
language in a way that users understand, making information accessible to
all; understand government accessibility requirements and design content
that works with common assistive technologies; use data, research and
evidence to review and evaluate content to make improvements; consistently
and effectively apply content standards and style guidelines; work to
continuously improve content and understand why content life cycle
management is important; and work autonomously.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This block's own text is explicit — "work
autonomously" — and every other bullet describes robust, independent,
end-to-end practice: designing, writing, publishing and evaluating content
without support. This is the clearest single statement in the whole role of
what full practitioner competence looks like.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot design a piece of content that meets a stated user need
  without significant rework.
- 1 — Beginner: Writes and edits content competently but needs regular
  review before publishing.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies content standards and style guidelines
  consistently, and writes in plain language, but still relies on others for
  the more complex accessibility or evaluation judgements.
- 3 — Advanced: Designs content that makes complex language and processes
  easy to understand, writes, edits and publishes it end to end using the
  team's content management system, understands government accessibility
  requirements, and uses evidence to evaluate and improve their own content
  — all without needing supervision.
- 4 — Expert: Takes responsibility for assuring the quality of other
  people's content and coaches them to improve it — the senior content
  designer expectation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: give a complex or jargon-heavy piece of source material and try to design accessible, plain-language content from it.
- Think about how you'd describe how you used data or research to evaluate and
  improve a real piece of content you had published.
- Think about how you'd explain how you design content to work with common assistive
  technologies.
- Look back at a portfolio of published content across more than one channel or
  format.
