# Lead Technical Writer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A technical writer takes a user-centred approach to making complicated technical concepts easier to understand for a specialist audience. In this role, you will focus on specialist content and software documentation, and write for technologists such as developers, technical architects, and technology leaders. A lead technical writer is an expert practitioner who directs a team of technical writers, and assures the quality of technical writing across teams and the alignment to strategy.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This rates how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available — not how often
they do it. A worked example, using this level's skill of Stakeholder relationship
management:

- **3 — Advanced**: they managed their own set of stakeholder relationships
  effectively, resolving one disagreement about scope without escalating it.
- **4 — Expert**: they directed the stakeholder relationship strategy for more than one
  team, made sure each team's stakeholder objectives were set and supported, and
  negotiated directly with a senior stakeholder to unblock a stalled piece of work.

A lead technical writer's baseline for this skill, below, is 4.

## About this role level

**The role: technical writer.** A technical writer takes a user-centred approach to
making complicated technical concepts easier to understand for a specialist audience.
The role focuses on specialist content and software documentation, writing for
technologists such as developers, technical architects and technology leaders.

**The level: lead technical writer.** The framework describes this level as an expert
practitioner who directs a team of technical writers, and assures the quality of
technical writing across teams and its alignment to strategy. It is the second and most
senior of the two levels the framework publishes for this role, above technical writer.
At this level, you standardise tools, ways of communicating and processes; work closely
with service managers, technology leaders and senior stakeholders to resource teams and
resolve problems; promote the discipline of technical writing; engage with the
cross-government community; and keep up to date with industry changes.

**The skills this level names**, in the summary's order: Agile working; Prototyping;
Stakeholder relationship management; Strategic thinking; Technical understanding
(user-centred design); User focus; and User-centred content design. Five of these —
Agile working, Prototyping, Strategic thinking, Technical understanding (user-centred
design), and User focus — carry text identical to the technical writer level below;
only Stakeholder relationship management and User-centred content design differ in
wording between the two levels, and that is where this level's real step up shows most
clearly in the text itself. Elsewhere, the step up shows in this level's own
accountability bullets above — directing a team, resourcing teams, and resolving
problems with senior stakeholders — rather than in a claim the repeated skill text does
not make.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a lead technical writer to identify and compare the best processes
or delivery methods to use, including measuring and evaluating outcomes; help the team
decide the best approach; and help teams manage and visualise outcomes, prioritise
work, and adhere to an agreed minimum viable product (MVP), priorities and scope. This
wording is identical to the technical writer level below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Because the text repeats the level below's wording word for
word, the honest baseline for this specific skill stays the same as that level — this
level's real step up shows in its own accountability to standardise tools, ways of
communicating and processes across teams, not in a claim this text does not make.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not compared delivery methods for a piece of technical writing work.
- 1 — Beginner: has followed a delivery process someone else chose.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped a team evaluate one delivery approach, with guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: independently identifies and compares delivery methods, measures and
  evaluates outcomes, and helps a team manage its outcomes, priorities and scope
  against an agreed MVP.
- 4 — Expert: standardises the delivery approach across more than one team of
  technical writers.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about standardising a delivery process or
  way of working across more than one team of writers."
- Ask for an example of helping more than one team keep to an agreed scope under
  pressure to expand it.
- Ask a referee whether you have shaped how multiple teams measured or
  visualised your delivery outcomes.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a lead technical writer to understand when to use a specific
prototyping technique or method, and to show the value of prototyping to the team.
This wording is identical to the technical writer level below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's own wording here — "understand" and "show
the value" — is lighter than the directing and standardising language used for this
level's other skills, and it is unchanged from the level below, so the honest baseline
for this one skill stays at Intermediate even at the top of this role's ladder.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not used a prototyping technique in documentation work.
- 1 — Beginner: has followed a prototyping technique someone else chose for a
  documentation deliverable.
- 2 — Intermediate: understands when to use a specific prototyping technique, such as a
  draft documentation structure or a sample API reference, and has shown a team the
  value of prototyping before committing to a full piece of content.
- 3 — Advanced: independently chooses between more than one prototyping technique for
  documentation and advises other writers on the choice.
- 4 — Expert: has standardised how more than one team of technical writers approaches
  prototyping documentation.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about showing a team of writers the value
  of prototyping a piece of documentation before committing to it."
- Ask for an example of advising another writer on which prototyping technique fitted
  your documentation problem.
- Ask for an example prototype document, if available.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a lead technical writer to direct the stakeholder relationship
strategy for their teams; ensure stakeholders' objectives are set and support teams to
meet them; and influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to resolve issues and
enable progress.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Directing a stakeholder relationship strategy across teams, and
negotiating with senior stakeholders to resolve issues, is a clear step up from the
technical writer level below, whose wording was scoped to working with a team and
building individual relationships. This is one of the two skills whose text genuinely
changes at this level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not directed a stakeholder relationship strategy for a team.
- 1 — Beginner: has managed their own individual stakeholder relationships, without
  directing a strategy for others.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped set stakeholder objectives for one team, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently ensures a team's stakeholder objectives are set and
  supported, and has resolved a moderately difficult stakeholder issue alone.
- 4 — Expert: directs the stakeholder relationship strategy across more than one
  team, and has negotiated directly with a senior stakeholder to resolve an issue and
  enable progress on a stalled piece of work.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about negotiating with a senior
  stakeholder to unblock progress on a piece of work."
- Ask for an example of directing the stakeholder relationship strategy for more than
  one team.
- Ask a referee — ideally a service manager or technology leader — about your effectiveness resolving stakeholder issues.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a lead technical writer to lead the design and implementation of
strategy, directing the evaluation of strategies and policies to ensure business
requirements are being met. This wording is identical to the technical writer level
below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Lead the design and implementation" and "directing the
evaluation" are explicit leading-and-directing, mastery-level expectations, and this
text is unchanged from the level below — the framework holds both levels of this role
to the same high bar for this specific skill, so the honest baseline stays at 4 here
too.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not led the design of a strategy or directed its evaluation.
- 1 — Beginner: has contributed to a strategy that someone else led.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped evaluate one strategy or policy against business
  requirements, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: has led part of the design of a strategy independently, without yet
  directing its full evaluation.
- 4 — Expert: leads the design and implementation of a strategy for technical writing,
  and directs the evaluation of strategies or policies to check business requirements
  are being met.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about leading the design of a strategy for
  how technical writing should work across your organisation."
- Ask for an example of directing the evaluation of a strategy or policy against
  business requirements.
- Look back at a strategy document you led, if available.

### Skill: Technical understanding (user-centred design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a lead technical writer to demonstrate knowledge of the
technologies used to build and operate digital services, and to collaborate closely
with colleagues in different digital disciplines. This wording is identical to the
technical writer level below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This text is unchanged from the level below, and technical
understanding at this level underpins directing a whole team's documentation and
assuring its accuracy, so the honest baseline stays at the same high point.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot demonstrate knowledge of the technologies behind a digital service
  they are responsible for documenting.
- 1 — Beginner: has a surface-level understanding of one technology, gained secondhand.
- 2 — Intermediate: demonstrates knowledge of one technology used to build or operate a
  service, with some support from technical colleagues.
- 3 — Advanced: independently demonstrates knowledge of the technologies used to build
  and operate a digital service, and collaborates closely with colleagues across
  different digital disciplines.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted by technical leaders to assure the technical accuracy of a
  whole team's documentation, because their own technical understanding is not in
  doubt.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about assuring the technical accuracy of a
  piece of documentation you did not write yourself."
- Ask for an example of collaborating closely across digital disciplines to resolve a
  technical disagreement affecting documentation.
- Ask a referee — ideally a technology leader — whether you trusted your
  technical judgement.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a lead technical writer to give direction on which tools or
methods to use; demonstrate experience meeting the needs of users across a variety of
channels; bring insight and expertise in how user needs have changed over time to
ensure they are met by the business; and apply strategic thinking to provide the best
service for the end user. This wording is identical to the technical writer level
below.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Give direction on which tools or methods to use" is
teach-and-guide-others, mastery-level ownership, and this text is unchanged from the
level below — the baseline for this specific skill stays the same, with the real step
up showing in this level's accountability to standardise tools and ways of working
across teams.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not given direction on tools or methods to meet a user need.
- 1 — Beginner: has followed direction someone else gave on which tool or method to
  use.
- 2 — Intermediate: has met a user need across one channel, with support choosing the
  method.
- 3 — Advanced: independently meets user needs across a variety of channels, and
  brings insight into how those needs have changed over time.
- 4 — Expert: gives direction to a team of writers on which tools or methods to use,
  and applies strategic thinking to shape the best overall service for the end user
  across the whole team's output.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about directing a team of writers on which
  tool or method to use, and how you got you to adopt it."
- Ask for an example of how user needs for a technical audience changed over time, and
  how the team's approach adapted as a result.
- Ask a referee whether you have shaped a strategic decision about the
  overall service for a technical audience, not just one piece of content.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a lead technical writer to direct the approach to content life
cycle management, ensuring content is regularly reviewed and evaluated by teams;
oversee teams' work to ensure the right content is being produced to meet the needs of
users; demonstrate extensive experience creating, iterating, managing and overseeing
content across multiple channels; encourage teams to review and evaluate the
effectiveness of processes and systems and support them to iterate for improvements; be
accountable for the production of high quality, user-focused content; and identify gaps
in content design skills and capability, helping teams to grow and develop.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Directing content life cycle management across teams, being
accountable for the quality of content produced, and identifying capability gaps to
help teams grow, is explicit teach-and-guide-others, mastery-level ownership at
organisational rather than individual scale — the other skill, alongside Stakeholder
relationship management, whose text genuinely changes from the level below.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not directed a content life cycle process across more than one team.
- 1 — Beginner: has reviewed their own content on a cycle set by someone else.
- 2 — Intermediate: has overseen one team's content production, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently oversees a team's content work to ensure it meets user
  needs, and demonstrates extensive experience across multiple channels.
- 4 — Expert: directs the approach to content life cycle management across teams, is
  accountable for the quality of content produced, and identifies capability gaps in
  content design, helping teams grow and develop as a result.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about directing how content is reviewed
  and evaluated across more than one team. What changed as a result?"
- Ask for an example of identifying a gap in a team's content design capability and
  what you did to close it.
- Ask a referee — ideally someone in a team you oversaw — about your
  accountability for content quality.
