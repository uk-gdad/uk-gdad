# Junior Graphic Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A graphic designer creates graphic elements that underpin interaction and service design. You will use layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure that content is legible and readable and that users see and understand interactions. A junior graphic designer is a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject or an individual with some relevant work experience, or both.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using Iterative design, a skill this level names:

- Someone who produces one version of a design and does not revisit it
  in light of research or feedback sits at **0 — None**.
- Someone who only iterates when told exactly what to change sits at
  **1 — Beginner**.
- Someone who applies iterative and agile principles to their own work,
  prototypes ideas at more than one fidelity, and uses and iterates design
  patterns and components across successive rounds of research sits at
  **2 — Intermediate** — the baseline this assessment sets for this skill.

## About this role level

**The role: graphic designer.** A graphic designer creates graphic elements
that underpin interaction and service design, using layout, spacing,
colour, type and iconography to ensure that content is legible and
readable, and that users see and understand interactions.

**This level: junior graphic designer.** The framework describes this level
as a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject, or an individual with
some relevant work experience, or both. At this level, the framework's
accountabilities are: explain design decisions; work collaboratively; have
a responsibility as part of a service; work independently after being given
direction by more senior designers; and identify user issues and important
needs.

**Where this level sits.** This repository's summaries carry two levels for
graphic designer: associate graphic designer, the level below this one, and
junior graphic designer, assessed here. The framework publishes further
levels above junior graphic designer, but this repository's summaries do
not name them, so this note does not enumerate a ladder beyond the two
levels it can see. Three of this level's seven skills — Designing
strategically, Designing together and Leading design — repeat associate
graphic designer's wording exactly; the other four move from explaining a
concept to actually applying it, which is where this assessment's
baselines differ from the level below.

**The seven skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to clearly explain problems and design
ideas to others, and to clearly explain design decisions to others using
appropriate tools and methods.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Both bullets now describe actually explaining
problems and decisions to others using appropriate tools, not just
explaining why communication matters — a step up from associate graphic
designer's explain-the-importance wording, though still short of the
independent judgement about audience the framework asks for at higher
levels.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain a design problem or idea clearly enough for
  someone outside the immediate team to follow.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a design problem or idea when prompted, with
  support choosing the right tool or method.
- 2 — Intermediate: Clearly explains design problems, ideas and decisions
  to others, choosing an appropriate tool or method — such as an annotated
  mock-up or a short walkthrough — without being told which one to use.
- 3 — Advanced: Adapts how a design decision is explained to suit different
  audiences, including non-designers and senior stakeholders — already
  working above this level's own baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the standard for how a team communicates design
  decisions, and coaches others in it — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd explain a design problem and your proposed solution
  as you would to a non-designer colleague.
- Work sample: review a piece of design documentation or a walkthrough you
  have produced.
- Ask a referee whether you chooses communication methods
  appropriate to the audience without prompting.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to work with others to design and deliver
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services
that meet the needs of all users, and to ensure a design meets appropriate
standards, for example accessibility regulations, with support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Work with others to design and deliver" and
"ensure a design meets appropriate standards... with support" describe
actually producing inclusive, accessible work, not just explaining the
principles behind it — a genuine step up from associate graphic designer,
though the framework still expects support with standards rather than
independent assurance.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Produces design work that overlooks an obvious accessibility or
  inclusion need.
- 1 — Beginner: Applies an inclusive design principle when someone else
  points it out.
- 2 — Intermediate: Works with others to design and deliver content or a
  service that meets the needs of a range of users, and checks it against
  accessibility standards with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently checks a piece of design work against
  accessibility regulations and fixes what it finds — already working
  above this level's own baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Sets inclusive design standards for a team and reviews
  others' work against them — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: give you a short design and try to identify
  and fix an accessibility or inclusion problem in it.
- Think about how you'd describe a time you worked with others to make a piece of
  content or a service more inclusive.
- Ask a referee how confidently you applies accessibility
  standards, and how much support you still need.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to explain the importance of aligning to
the strategy of an organisation; describe how their design work aligns to
the wider goals and vision of their team; and explain the value of using
patterns and components in design.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework repeats associate graphic designer's
wording for this skill exactly — explain and describe throughout — so this
level asks nothing more of someone yet, and the baseline stays where it
sat at associate graphic designer.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say why a design should align with an organisation's
  strategy.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains why aligning to organisational strategy matters,
  describes how their own work connects to the team's goals, and explains
  the value of using existing patterns and components.
- 2 — Intermediate: Connects a specific piece of their own work to a stated
  team goal without being told to.
- 3 — Advanced: Chooses an existing pattern or component for a piece of
  work and explains why it fits the team's strategy — already working
  above this level's own baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes design strategy for a team or organisation — well
  above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd explain how a piece of design work you have done
  connects to a wider team or organisational goal.
- Think about how you'd explain the value of using existing patterns and components
  rather than designing from scratch each time.
- Ask a referee whether you shows awareness of the "why" behind
  design decisions, not just the "how".

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to explain different techniques for
bringing together perspectives from multiple people to inform a design, and
to explain the importance of constructive feedback in the design process.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework repeats associate graphic designer's

associate graphic designer.

**Behavioural indicators**

  perspectives into a design.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a technique for gathering multiple perspectives,
  such as a design crit or workshop, and explains why constructive feedback
  matters in the design process.
- 2 — Intermediate: Takes part constructively in a design crit or workshop
  and visibly applies feedback received.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps facilitate a small collaborative design session —
  already working above this level's own baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Runs collaborative design practice across a team — well above
  this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd explain a technique for bringing together different
  people's perspectives on a design.
- Think about how you'd describe a time you received constructive feedback on your
  design work and what you did with it.
- Ask a referee how you behaves in a group design or critique
  session.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to analyse, synthesise and clearly explain
evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics data, and
to work with researchers or analysts to use evidence to inform, develop and
test a design idea.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Both bullets describe actually analysing and
using evidence with others, not just explaining where evidence comes from —
a genuine step up from associate graphic designer's explain-and-identify
wording.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot connect a piece of evidence, such as analytics data, to
  a design decision.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains what a piece of evidence shows, with support
  working out what it means for a design.
- 2 — Intermediate: Analyses and synthesises evidence relevant to users or
  a service, and works with a researcher or analyst to use it to inform,
  develop or test a design idea.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently designs a small piece of evidence-gathering,
  such as a short test, to answer a specific design question — already
  working above this level's own baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Leads evidence-based design practice for a team — well above
  this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd interpret a short piece of evidence, such as a
  simple analytics summary, and explain what it means for a design.
- Ask about a time you worked with a researcher or analyst to test or
  develop a design idea.
- Work sample: review a piece of design work alongside the evidence that
  shaped it.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to apply iterative design principles and
agile methodologies to their work; iterate and improve a design based on
successive rounds of research; prototype their ideas at different
fidelities to explore and test designs; use a range of design tools and
techniques; and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Every bullet is now a doing verb — apply,
iterate, prototype, use — a clear step up from associate graphic designer's
describe-and-explain wording, though still short of running iteration
independently at scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Produces one version of a design and does not revisit it in
  light of research or feedback.
- 1 — Beginner: Makes a change to a design when told the research findings
  that require it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies iterative and agile principles to their own
  work, prototypes ideas at more than one fidelity, and uses and iterates
  design patterns and components across successive rounds of research.
- 3 — Advanced: Plans and runs a full iteration cycle — prototype, test,
  learn, revise — with minimal support — already working above this
  level's own baseline.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches others in iterative practice and improves how a team
  iterates — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: review two or more versions of the same piece of design
  work and try to explain what changed and why.
- Think about how you'd describe the different prototype fidelities you have used
  and when you chose each one.
- Ask a referee how independently you runs a round of iteration
  once given direction.

**Behavioural indicators**
