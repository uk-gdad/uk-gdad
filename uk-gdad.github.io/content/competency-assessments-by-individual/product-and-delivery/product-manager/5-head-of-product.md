# Head of Product - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A product manager makes sure that their products provide value and achieve the right outcomes by balancing user and business needs. A head of product provides support to all product managers in the organisation and to the product community across government. They lead on product strategy, working closely with lead product managers and their business units. They are responsible for setting and raising standards for product management in their organisation.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill below is rated on the same five-point scale. This is a
**proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale — it measures how well someone does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example, using Product leadership.** Someone rated
**2 — Intermediate** represents their own product area to senior
stakeholders when asked, but has not yet been asked to speak for product
management more broadly and does not yet coordinate other product managers'
work. Someone rated **3 — Advanced** independently represents a
business unit to senior stakeholders, mentors several product managers, and
has led a specific piece of community activity, such as a guild session or
a hiring panel. Someone rated **4 — Expert** co-ordinates the whole
product management community across the organisation, is someone other
business units ask to represent product management in a cross-government
forum, and has visibly raised the standard other product managers are held
to. Look for evidence at organisation and community scale, not just within
one product area.

## About this role level

A **product manager** makes sure that their products provide value and
achieve the right outcomes by balancing user and business needs. In this
role, a product manager works with multidisciplinary teams, bringing
together their views, user needs and business needs, to frame the problem;
works with multidisciplinary teams to provide the right outcomes for users
and the organisation; makes decisions on priorities for their teams; and
ensures teams comply with appropriate standards.

The role has five levels, run in order of seniority:

1. Associate product manager
2. Product manager
3. Senior product manager
4. Lead product manager
5. **Head of product — you are assessing this level**

A **head of product** provides support to all product managers in the
organisation and to the product community across government. They lead on
product strategy, working closely with lead product managers and their
business units, and are responsible for setting and raising standards for
product management in their organisation. At this role level, a head of
product will:

- coach others in product management
- work with organisation management and other communities to represent
  product community interests
- oversee every member of the community, including their role,
  assignments, development, pay and performance
- manage recruitment, development, moves and exits from the community,
  covering civil servants and contractors
- line manage senior and lead product managers

A head of product is assessed against nine skills, in this order: Agile and
Lean practices, Applying user-centred insights, Creating value for money,
Life cycle management, Managing product outcomes, Product leadership,
Product management, Stakeholder relationship management, and Strategic
ownership.

Six of these nine skills — Agile and Lean practices, Applying user-centred
insights, Life cycle management, Product management, Stakeholder
relationship management, and Strategic ownership — carry the same published
wording as lead product manager, the level below. The identical wording
does not mean the same expected proficiency: a head of product exercises
each of these across the whole product community and represents them
externally, where a lead product manager exercises them across a single
portfolio. The remaining three skills — Creating value for money, Managing
product outcomes, and Product leadership — are worded distinctly at this
level, and each explicitly names organisation-wide or cross-government
scope: owning a business case throughout its life cycle, influencing senior
stakeholders' priorities with data, and co-ordinating the product
management community.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile and Lean practices

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of product coaches and leads teams in Agile and Lean good practice,
creates and tailors the right working approach for a team, and challenges,
evaluates and iterates that approach through the product's life cycle. They
experiment with new and innovative ways of working to improve delivery
across the organisation, and act as a recognised expert and advocate for
Agile and Lean approaches.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. As someone setting and raising standards for
product management across the whole organisation, a head of product is
expected to be the recognised, sought-after authority the framework's own
wording describes, not simply a strong independent practitioner within a
portfolio.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not shaped how a team or the organisation works, beyond
  following an approach designed elsewhere.
- 1 — Beginner: Understands Agile and Lean concepts, but has not
  introduced or changed a working approach at scale.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has adapted a working approach for one team or
  portfolio, with support from more experienced colleagues.
- 3 — Advanced: Has independently introduced or reshaped a working
  approach across several teams and can defend the reasoning.
- 4 — Expert: Is the organisation's recognised authority on Agile and Lean
  practice, has run experiments that changed how delivery works
  organisation-wide, and is asked to advise other organisations or the
  wider cross-government product community.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of an organisation-wide change to working practice
  you introduced or championed, and what evidence justified it.
- Ask a referee whether this person is recognised beyond your own
  organisation as an authority on Agile or Lean practice, and for a
  specific instance.
- Look back at any written guidance, playbook, or standard on ways of working
  that you authored or owned.

### Skill: Applying user-centred insights

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of product advocates for a range of effective research approaches
across the organisation, coaches others in making decisions that meet user
needs across a range of channels, advocates for the continuous use of user
insights in teams, and uses user insights to make strategic decisions that
provide the best user experience.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Advocacy "across the organisation" is this level's own
wording, and a head of product is accountable for making that advocacy
stick community-wide — full mastery, not the strong independent practice
within a portfolio that the same wording implies at lead product manager.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not championed the use of user insight beyond their own
  immediate decisions.
- 1 — Beginner: Uses research findings when handed them but does not seek
  them out proactively or push others to.
- 2 — Intermediate: Encourages research use within their own area, with
  mixed success getting other teams to follow.
- 3 — Advanced: Has visibly changed how more than one business area uses
  user insight in decision making.
- 4 — Expert: Has built or embedded an organisation-wide standard for using
  user insight in product decisions, and senior leaders outside product
  management cite this person's advocacy as the reason it changed.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of embedding user-insight practice into how more than
  one business area makes decisions.
- Ask a referee outside the immediate product team whether this person's
  advocacy for user insight is visible organisation-wide.
- Look back at a strategic decision paper and try to point to where user
  insight changed the direction, and how that became standard practice.

### Skill: Creating value for money

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of product develops and owns a business case, iterating it
throughout the life cycle, coaches others on identifying benefits while
balancing user and business needs, communicates the delivery of incremental
value, builds the case for an organisational approach to value through
product strategy, and coaches and empowers teams to take a value-for-money
approach.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This level's wording is explicit about owning a
business case end to end and building an organisation-wide approach to
value — a materially larger and more senior accountability than lead
product manager's wording, which speaks to contributing to and persuading
others about a business case rather than owning one.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not owned a business case across its life cycle, and
  cannot describe how value is tracked once a product goes live.
- 1 — Beginner: Has contributed sections to a business case but has not
  owned or iterated one themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: Owns a business case for one product, with support
  when it needs to be revised.
- 3 — Advanced: Owns and iterates a business case independently across a
  product's life cycle, and coaches one or two other product managers on
  value-for-money thinking.
- 4 — Expert: Has built the organisation's approach to demonstrating
  value-for-money through product strategy, owns and defends a business
  case at the most senior level, and has empowered multiple teams to make
  their own value-based trade-offs confidently.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a business case this person owned end to end, including a point
  where you had to revise it and why.
- Think about how you'd describe how you built an organisation-wide approach to
  articulating value through product strategy.
- Ask a referee for an example of you coaching a team to take a
  value-for-money approach without needing to be told what to cut.

### Skill: Life cycle management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of product leads and coaches teams through different phases of the
product life cycle, ensures effective support for the product or service
and its users, predicts, prevents, mitigates and manages potential risks at
different phases of that life cycle, and contributes to the assessment of
other teams, providing guidance and support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The published wording describes coaching teams and
contributing to assessments — the same accountability, at the same
independent, strong-practice level, as lead product manager. Unlike
Creating value for money or Product leadership, nothing in this level's own
text raises life cycle management specifically to an organisation-wide
standard-setting role, so the baseline stays where the wording actually
supports it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not led a product through a full life cycle transition or
  contributed to another team's service assessment.
- 1 — Beginner: Understands the life cycle phases but has followed rather
  than led decisions about moving between them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Manages life cycle risk for one product with support,
  and has sat on but not led an assessment.
- 3 — Advanced: Coaches teams through life cycle transitions, anticipates
  and mitigates risk before it lands, and formally contributes guidance to
  other teams' assessments.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the organisation's expectations for how a life cycle
  transition or assessment is run, and is the escalation point when a
  transition carries serious risk.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a life cycle transition you coached a team through, and a risk
  you caught early.
- Review notes or an outcome from a service assessment you contributed
  guidance to.
- Ask a referee whether this person is the escalation point when a
  transition looks risky, and for a specific example.

### Skill: Managing product outcomes

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of product uses data to make prioritisation decisions for multiple
or complex projects, products or services, uses data to influence the
priorities of senior stakeholders, guides and challenges teams to make
better use of data, guides and challenges teams to demonstrate achievement
of outcomes, and identifies opportunities to share and connect product data
across government.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This level's wording names influencing senior
stakeholders' priorities with data and connecting product data across
government — an organisation- and government-facing accountability that
goes well beyond lead product manager's distinct wording about leading
teams within a portfolio to build measurement methods.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not used data to influence a decision above their own
  product's scope.
- 1 — Beginner: Reports outcome data but has not used it to change a
  senior stakeholder's priorities.
- 2 — Intermediate: Uses data to influence prioritisation within their own
  area, with mixed success at a senior level.
- 3 — Advanced: Has changed a senior stakeholder's priorities with data on
  more than one occasion, and challenges teams to demonstrate outcomes
  properly.
- 4 — Expert: Routinely uses data to shape senior, cross-organisation
  prioritisation decisions, and has identified and acted on an opportunity
  to share or connect product data across government.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific example of using data to change a senior stakeholder's
  priorities, and what the data showed.
- Ask about an opportunity you identified to share or connect product data
  beyond your own organisation.
- Ask a referee for an example of you challenging a team, constructively,
  to better demonstrate an outcome.

### Skill: Product leadership

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of product seeks opportunities for reuse and learning across
government, co-ordinates the product management community, leads on
coaching and mentoring for product management in their organisation, leads
some activities in cross-government communities and establishes and
maintains wider relationships across the profession, and represents product
management in their organisation in conversations with senior stakeholders.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Co-ordinating the whole product management community
and leading cross-government relationships is the role's defining
accountability at this level, and the framework's own wording asks for
exactly the reach and mastery that an Expert rating describes — a clear
step up from lead product manager's distinct wording, which is scoped to a
local area and its community.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not co-ordinated a product community or represented
  product management to a senior stakeholder beyond their own team.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to community activities organised by someone
  else, without leading any of them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads occasional community activities within their own
  organisation, and represents one product area to senior stakeholders.
- 3 — Advanced: Regularly leads coaching and mentoring for product
  management across the organisation, and takes part in cross-government
  community work.
- 4 — Expert: Co-ordinates the entire product management community in
  their organisation, leads cross-government community activities, and is
  someone senior stakeholders and other organisations turn to for the
  product management view.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific example of co-ordinating product management activity
  across the whole organisation, not just your own team.
- Ask about a cross-government community relationship you built or
  maintained, and what came of it.
- Ask a referee — someone outside your immediate reporting line — how
  visible this person's leadership of the product community actually is.

### Skill: Product management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of product sets the strategy and vision for their products in
collaboration with others, creates a prioritised roadmap and uses it to
discuss product strategy at all levels, plans prioritised work across
multiple teams while minimising dependencies and risk, supports multiple
teams in delivering quality outcomes the right way, and coaches and mentors
others in product management.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The published wording is identical to lead product
manager's, but a head of product exercises it across the whole product
community rather than a single portfolio, discussing strategy "at all
levels" up to the most senior stakeholders in the organisation — full
mastery is the expectation at that scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not set a product strategy that shaped work across more
  than one portfolio.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a roadmap set at portfolio level without shaping
  organisation-wide strategy.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes to strategy discussions above portfolio
  level, with support from more senior colleagues.
- 3 — Advanced: Sets strategy independently for more than one portfolio,
  and discusses it credibly with senior stakeholders.
- 4 — Expert: Sets and defends product strategy at organisation level,
  routinely discusses it with the most senior stakeholders, and coaches
  and mentors product managers across the whole community as a matter of
  course.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe an organisation-level strategy discussion
  you led, and how you defended a difficult prioritisation call to senior
  leaders.
- Think about how you'd walk through how you coach and mentor product managers
  across the community, not just your direct reports.
- Ask a referee for a concrete example of strategic advice from this
  person changing another portfolio's direction.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of product directs the stakeholder relationship strategy for their
teams, ensures stakeholders' objectives are set and supports teams in
meeting them, and influences and negotiates with senior stakeholders to
resolve issues and enable progress.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The wording is identical to lead product manager's,
but a head of product directs stakeholder strategy for the whole
organisation's relationship with the product community, and is someone
other product managers escalate a stuck senior relationship to — full
mastery at that scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not personally negotiated with the most senior
  stakeholders in the organisation to resolve a contentious issue.
- 1 — Beginner: Manages stakeholder relationships for a single product,
  escalating anything senior to someone else.
- 2 — Intermediate: Negotiates with senior stakeholders on their own
  product's behalf, with occasional support.
- 3 — Advanced: Directs stakeholder strategy for a portfolio, and has
  independently resolved a contentious senior disagreement.
- 4 — Expert: Directs stakeholder strategy at organisation level, and is
  someone other product managers bring their most difficult senior
  stakeholder relationships to for resolution.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for the most contentious senior stakeholder disagreement you have
  personally resolved, and how you approached it.
- Ask a referee for an example of another product manager escalating a
  stuck relationship to this person.
- Look back at a stakeholder engagement strategy document you authored or
  directed.

### Skill: Strategic ownership

**What the framework expects at this level**

A head of product supports and coaches others in creating and implementing
a successful long-term strategy and tactical approach that others agree
with, influences and persuades stakeholders to support delivery of the
strategy, supports strategic decision making, and ensures strategic
alignment across the organisation.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's own wording at this level, as at
lead product manager, describes supporting and coaching others toward
strategy rather than personally owning or setting it — strong, independent
contribution and organisation-wide alignment-checking, but the text stops
short of naming this skill as the seat of the role's authority the way
Product leadership and Managing product outcomes explicitly do.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not checked or influenced strategic alignment beyond their
  own immediate area.
- 1 — Beginner: Can describe organisation strategy but has not supported
  anyone else in shaping or delivering it.
- 2 — Intermediate: Supports one other product manager's strategic work,
  with guidance from someone more senior.
- 3 — Advanced: Coaches multiple product managers through building and
  delivering strategy, has persuaded a resistant senior stakeholder to back
  one, and actively checks alignment across the organisation.
- 4 — Expert: Is the final point of escalation for strategic alignment
  questions across the whole organisation, and has personally reshaped
  organisation-wide strategy when misalignment was found.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of coaching more than one product manager through
  building a long-term strategy, and how your approaches differed.
- Ask about a time you found and corrected a strategic misalignment
  between two parts of the organisation.
- Ask a referee for an example of this person persuading a resistant senior
  stakeholder to back a strategic direction.
