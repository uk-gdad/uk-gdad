# Quality Assurance Test Analyst - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A quality assurance test analyst interprets testing needs, creating and executing appropriate tests to ensure quality and alignment with requirements. A quality assurance test analyst designs and executes appropriate functional and non-functional tests. They apply multiple testing techniques to inform the approach to mitigate risk.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale.
This is a **proficiency** scale — how well someone does the thing, on the
evidence available — not a frequency scale of how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Test analysis.** At 1 (Beginner), someone can describe
what a quality risk is but needs a colleague to help them spot one in a real
user story. At 2 (Intermediate), they work with stakeholders to determine
which functional and non-functional quality characteristics matter for a
piece of work, and analyse the artefacts themselves to identify risks,
following an approach the team has already agreed. At 3 (Advanced), they lead
that conversation with stakeholders across more than one team, decide when an
approach needs to change based on effort and risk, and coach someone more
junior through the same analysis. The words "I do test analysis" cover all
three; only the evidence of what they actually did tells you which point
they are at.

## About this role level

A quality assurance test analyst interprets testing needs, creating and
executing appropriate tests to ensure quality and alignment with
requirements. Across the role, this means planning, designing and executing
tests; applying testing techniques and standards to learn about systems and
mitigate risk; analysing artefacts and prototypes; testing outcomes and
recording defects; seeking to improve testing practices to enhance quality
and reliability; and recommending testing approaches.

The role has four levels, in order: associate quality assurance test
analyst, **quality assurance test analyst** (this level), senior quality
assurance test analyst, and lead quality assurance test analyst. This is the
second rung: a quality assurance test analyst designs and executes
appropriate functional and non-functional tests, applying multiple testing
techniques to inform the approach and mitigate risk. Where the associate
level works under supervision, this level is expected to operate largely
independently on their own area of work.

At this role level, a person will:

- apply the most appropriate test approaches and plans based on risk
- analyse artefacts, such as user stories, prototypes, processes and designs
- determine what to test following an agreed approach
- ensure test coverage aligns with business risk
- follow appropriate standards to mitigate risk early
- execute tests, develop reports, record outcomes and support the resolution
  of defects

The framework names six skills at this level:

- Communicating between the technical and non-technical
- Designing and executing tests
- Managing, reporting and resolving defects
- Test analysis
- Test and quality planning
- Test engineering

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What the framework expects at this level**

At this level, a person is expected to communicate effectively with technical
and non-technical stakeholders, support and host discussions within a
multidisciplinary team that may have difficult dynamics, act as an advocate
for the team externally, and manage differing stakeholder perspectives.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The bullets describe real, independent
communication accountability with no "support" qualifier, but leading
communication across senior stakeholders or multiple teams belongs to the
level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Struggles to explain a test result clearly to anyone outside the
  test team.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain a test finding one-to-one, but needs help
  preparing for a group discussion with mixed technical and non-technical
  attendees.
- 2 — Intermediate: Runs a stand-up or a show-and-tell that includes both
  technical and non-technical colleagues, adjusting language on the fly, and
  represents the team's testing position in a cross-team meeting.
- 3 — Advanced: Hosts a discussion with genuinely competing stakeholder
  priorities and steers it to a workable outcome without escalating.
- 4 — Expert: Is regularly asked to mediate a tense conversation between
  technical and non-technical parties because colleagues trust their
  judgement in the room.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask a structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you had to
  explain a testing decision to a stakeholder who disagreed with it. What did
  you do?"
- Ask for a work sample: minutes or an outcome summary from a discussion you hosted or led.
- Ask a referee whether the person has represented the test team externally,
  and how that went.

### Skill: Designing and executing tests

**What the framework expects at this level**

At this level, a person is expected to set up suitable environments with some
support, select appropriate test types and techniques with some support,
design, build, maintain and execute tests that align to user needs and
requirements, conduct exploratory testing, and research and try new test
types and techniques.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Building, maintaining and executing tests, and
exploratory testing, are unqualified accountabilities; two supporting
activities (environment set-up, technique selection) still carry "with some
support", which keeps this short of full independence.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot design a test without a template or a colleague's close
  direction.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an existing test plan closely and needs help choosing
  which technique fits a given piece of work.
- 2 — Intermediate: Designs, builds and executes a test suite for a familiar
  feature unsupervised, and runs exploratory testing sessions that surface
  real issues.
- 3 — Advanced: Selects and combines test types confidently for unfamiliar,
  ambiguous functionality, and sets up their own test environment without
  help.
- 4 — Expert: Trials a new test technique the team has not used before,
  evaluates whether it worked, and recommends whether to adopt it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give a work sample task: a short, real (or realistic) requirement, and try to design and briefly execute a small test suite against it.
- Ask a structured interview question: "Tell me about an exploratory testing
  session that found something useful. What made you look there?"
- Ask a referee how much support the person needs setting up an unfamiliar
  test environment.

### Skill: Managing, reporting and resolving defects

**What the framework expects at this level**

At this level, a person is expected to collaborate with others to create a
defect management process, with support, critically assess dependencies,
defects and risks with support, contribute to mitigation and contingency
plans, and clearly communicate risks and the impact of defects to
stakeholders.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The higher-order activities — process creation,
critical assessment of risk — are still qualified "with support"; independent
communication of risk and impact to stakeholders, on its own, is a genuine
step up from the associate level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Logs defects but does not connect them to wider risk or impact.
- 1 — Beginner: Logs and tracks defects reliably but needs a colleague to help
  weigh which ones matter most.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains the business impact of a set of open defects to
  a stakeholder clearly and accurately, without help.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads the creation or update of part of the team's defect
  management process, and manages defect-related risk across more than one
  workstream.
- 4 — Expert: Spots and articulates a systemic defect trend before it becomes
  a visible problem, and changes team practice as a result.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a work sample: a stakeholder-facing summary of open defects and
  your impact that you wrote.
- Ask a structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you had to
  explain to a non-technical stakeholder why a defect mattered, or didn't."
- Ask a referee whether the person contributes to how the team manages
  defects, or only follows the existing process.

### Skill: Test analysis

**What the framework expects at this level**

At this level, a person is expected to work with stakeholders to determine
which functional and non-functional quality characteristics add value,
determine what to test following an agreed approach, identify and advocate
for test needs such as data, access and environments with support, and
analyse information to identify risks.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Working with stakeholders and analysing
information for risk are unqualified accountabilities; advocacy for test
needs still carries "with support", consistent with Intermediate rather than
Advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Analyses only what they are directly told to test.
- 1 — Beginner: Identifies an obvious quality risk in a user story with
  prompting.
- 2 — Intermediate: Works with a product owner or developer to agree which
  quality characteristics matter for a piece of work, and independently
  analyses the artefacts to find risks.
- 3 — Advanced: Determines, on their own judgement, that the agreed test
  approach needs to change given new information about effort or risk.
- 4 — Expert: Uses more than one analysis technique to find risks in complex,
  ambiguous information that a single technique would have missed.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a document review: give you a short, real (anonymised)
  set of requirements and ask which quality characteristics you would
  prioritise and why.
- Ask a structured interview question: "Tell me about a time your test
  approach changed partway through. What made you change it?"
- Ask a referee whether the person needs support advocating for test data,
  access or environments, or does this independently.

### Skill: Test and quality planning

**What the framework expects at this level**

At this level, a person is expected to create or adapt quality testing
approaches based on risk, with some support, follow a quality testing
strategy and contribute to its development, and contribute to continuous
improvement of quality testing approaches, plans and strategies.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Creating or adapting an approach still carries
"with some support"; contributing to a strategy and to its improvement are
real but junior-to-strategy activities, together placing this at Intermediate.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never adapted a test plan, only followed one exactly as
  written.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an existing quality testing strategy without
  deviation, even where it clearly does not fit the work.
- 2 — Intermediate: Adapts part of a test plan to fit a change in risk, with a
  colleague reviewing the change.
- 3 — Advanced: Creates a risk-based test approach for a new piece of work
  independently, and proposes an improvement to the team's wider testing
  strategy.
- 4 — Expert: Leads a retrospective look at how well the team's quality
  testing strategy worked, and drives an agreed change to it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a work sample: a test approach or plan you wrote or
  adapted, and what risk-based reasoning sat behind it.
- Ask a structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you changed a
  test plan because the risk profile of the work changed."
- Ask a referee whether the person has contributed ideas to the team's
  broader testing strategy, or only executed against it.

### Skill: Test engineering

**What the framework expects at this level**

At this level, the framework repeats the wording used at the associate level:
a person is expected to explain why testing processes, environments and
tools are important, follow test engineering practices and standards with
support, and support the maintenance of automated tests and tools required
for testing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording for this specific skill is
unchanged from the associate level, and every action bullet still carries
"with support" — nothing in the text supports moving the baseline up, even
though the level's other accountabilities have grown.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never touched a test environment or an automated test.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains clearly why consistent test environments and tools
  matter, and can point to an example from their own experience.
- 2 — Intermediate: Updates an automated test's data or configuration under
  light guidance, and flags when a test environment looks wrong.
- 3 — Advanced: Sets up or resets a test environment from documentation
  without help, and helps troubleshoot a failing automated test.
- 4 — Expert: Diagnoses the root cause of a flaky automated test and proposes
  a fix, unprompted.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask a structured interview question: "Why does the reliability of a test
  environment matter to the results you report?"
- Ask for observation or a work sample: how you helped maintain an
  automated test or test tool in a previous role.
- Ask a referee how much guidance the person still needs for test engineering
  tasks.
