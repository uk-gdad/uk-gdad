# Interaction Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

An interaction designer works out the best way to let users interact with services, in terms of both overall flow and at the level of individual design elements. An interaction designer is a confident and competent designer who can develop designs based on evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same five-point
scale. This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well someone does the
thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel
about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example — Iterative design.** The framework expects an interaction
designer to "apply iterative design principles and agile methodologies to
your work," "iterate and improve a design based on successive rounds of
research," and "prototype your ideas at different fidelities to explore and
test designs." Someone who can only make a small change to a design when
told exactly what to change is showing at most **1 — Beginner**. Someone
who independently iterates a design across rounds of research, prototypes at
more than one fidelity, and reuses and adjusts existing patterns is showing
**2 — Intermediate** — the baseline this level expects. Someone who does
this for complex designs and adapts quickly when requirements shift is
showing evidence closer to **3 — Advanced**, above this level's own baseline.

## About this role level

The User-centred design profession's Interaction designer role works out the
best way to let users interact with services, in terms of both overall flow
and the level of individual design elements.

The role has six levels, in order: Associate interaction designer, Junior
interaction designer, Interaction designer, Senior interaction designer, Lead
interaction designer, and Head of interaction design. This assessment is for
the third of them.

An **interaction designer** at this level is a confident and competent
designer who can develop designs based on evidence of user needs and
organisational outcomes. At this role level, they will be trusted to make
good decisions, recognise when to ask for further guidance and support,
contribute to the development of design concepts, and interpret
evidence-based research and incorporate it into their work.

The framework names seven skills for this role level: Design communication,
Designing for everyone, Designing strategically, Designing together,
Evidence-based design, Iterative design, and Leading design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to clearly explain problems and design
ideas to others, and clearly explain design decisions to others using
appropriate tools and methods.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Clearly explain" describes independent,
competent communication, a step beyond the guided description expected of a
junior designer, but short of the cross-boundary, consensus-building
communication a senior designer is expected to manage.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain a design decision, even when asked directly what
  problem it solves.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains a design decision when prompted, but the
  explanation is vague or needs a colleague to fill gaps.
- 2 — Intermediate: Clearly explains a design problem and the reasoning
  behind an interaction decision, choosing an appropriate way to show it,
  such as an annotated wireframe or a short walkthrough.
- 3 — Advanced: Explains complex design problems to stakeholders outside
  design, documents decisions and unresolved issues, and builds consensus by
  asking searching questions.
- 4 — Expert: Gains support for design strategy from senior leaders and
  coaches others in communicating design effectively.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to walk through a real interaction flow or prototype and
  explain the decisions behind it, as if to a stakeholder unfamiliar with
  design.
- Look back at a design rationale document or presentation you have produced.
- Ask about a time a design decision was challenged, and how you explained
  or defended it.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to work with others to design and
deliver inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or
services that meet the needs of all users, and to ensure a design meets
appropriate standards, for example accessibility regulations, with support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Work with others to design and deliver" is
applied, collaborative practice, while "with support" on the standards
bullet keeps full, independent assurance out of reach.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what makes an interaction accessible, for example
  keyboard navigation or a clear focus state.
- 1 — Beginner: With guidance, checks a design against a basic accessibility
  checklist.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently applies accessible and inclusive design
  practice to an interaction flow, and asks for support to confirm the
  design meets formal standards.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises other designers on inclusive design and ensures a
  design meets accessibility regulations without prompting.
- 4 — Expert: Measurably improves inclusive design practice across multiple
  teams.

**Evidence to gather**

- Look back at a prototype or flow the designer produced and ask how you checked
  it works for users with different access needs, including keyboard-only
  or screen-reader users.
- Ask about a specific accessibility standard and how you check for it.
- Ask about a design choice you changed because it excluded some users.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to align their design work to both the
goals and vision of their team and the strategic objectives of their
organisation, identify potential risks and use design to mitigate them,
identify opportunities and use design to take advantage of them, and
contribute to the development of design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This moves beyond explaining why alignment
matters — a more junior framing — into actually aligning work and spotting
risk and opportunity, short of the senior designer's coaching role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot connect a piece of interaction design work to the team's
  goals.
- 1 — Beginner: Says, when asked, how a specific piece of work supports the
  team's goals.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently aligns interaction design choices to team
  goals, and contributes new pattern or component ideas to a shared library.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps a team understand how design contributes to its
  goals, and shapes design using risk, opportunity and constraints in
  technology, systems and policy.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds design in organisational strategy.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to describe a project where your interaction choices
  were driven by a team goal, not just personal preference.
- Look back at a contribution you made to a shared pattern library or component
  set.
- Ask about a risk you spotted in a design and what you did about it.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to effectively plan and run a design
session with their team, users or stakeholders, identify and engage the
right people throughout the design process, and give and receive
constructive design feedback.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Planning and running a session independently,
and handling feedback both ways, is competent practice — a step up from
taking part in a session someone else runs, and short of advising others on
how to do it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what a design session or crit is for.
- 1 — Beginner: Takes part in a design session and gives feedback when
  prompted.
- 2 — Intermediate: Plans and runs a design session, identifies who should
  be in the room, and both gives and receives constructive feedback.
- 3 — Advanced: Advises others on running sessions, adapts a session that is
  not working, and works across team or profession boundaries.
- 4 — Expert: Runs sessions that include senior leaders and coaches others
  in collaborative design practice.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to describe a design session or crit you planned and
  ran, including who you invited and why.
- Observe you giving feedback on a colleague's work, if possible.
- Ask about a time you received difficult feedback on your design and what
  you did with it.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to analyse, synthesise and clearly
explain evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics
data, and to work with researchers or analysts to use evidence to inform,
develop and test a design idea.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Analysing and explaining evidence, and working
with a researcher or analyst to test an idea, is applied, collaborative
practice — a step beyond explaining what evidence shows with support, and
short of handling complex evidence or framing hypotheses for a team.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what evidence, such as analytics or user research,
  might tell them about a design.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains what a piece of evidence shows, with support.
- 2 — Intermediate: Analyses and explains evidence relevant to a design, and
  works with a researcher or analyst to test a design idea.
- 3 — Advanced: Analyses and explains complex evidence, for example large
  data sets, and helps a team frame and use design hypotheses.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds hypothesis-driven design practice and guides the
  organisation on using evidence.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to talk through a piece of user research or analytics
  data that shaped an interaction design decision.
- Ask how you worked with a user researcher or analyst on a project.
- Look back at a design that changed as a result of evidence, and ask what the
  evidence showed.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to apply iterative design principles and
agile methodologies to their work, iterate and improve a design based on
successive rounds of research, prototype their ideas at different
fidelities to explore and test designs, use a range of design tools and
techniques, and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Iterating across rounds of research, working at
more than one fidelity, and adjusting existing patterns is competent,
independent practice — short of iterating complex designs or adapting
quickly to fast-changing requirements.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what changed between two versions of a design
  and why.
- 1 — Beginner: Makes a small change to a design following feedback, with
  guidance on what "iteration" means in practice.
- 2 — Intermediate: Iterates a design across successive rounds of research,
  prototypes at more than one fidelity, and uses and adjusts existing
  patterns and components.
- 3 — Advanced: Iterates complex designs, prototypes independently at the
  right fidelity for the question being asked, and adapts quickly when
  requirements change.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds iterative practice across teams and leads prototyping
  of complex ideas at any fidelity.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to show two or three versions of an interaction flow and
  explain what changed and why, referencing research or feedback.
- Ask about the range of tools you use for different fidelities, for
  example a paper sketch versus a clickable prototype.
- Look back at a pattern or component you reused or adapted from a library.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to lead and coordinate design work in
their team, with support, communicate the value of user-centred design to
their team, support other designers, and work with digital and data leaders
in their organisation when needed.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "With support" caps full independence, but
leading or coordinating a piece of work, even with support, is a step beyond
simply describing leadership responsibilities in the abstract, so
Intermediate is the right baseline rather than Beginner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe why user-centred design matters to their team.
- 1 — Beginner: Describes the value of user-centred design when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: With support, leads or coordinates a piece of design
  work in the team, communicates the value of user-centred design, and
  supports another designer.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads and coordinates design work without needing support,
  advocates for user-centred design outside the design team, and mentors
  other designers.
- 4 — Expert: Sets a vision for design leadership across multiple teams or
  the organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the designer led or coordinated a piece of work, even a
  small one, and what support you needed.
- Ask how you have supported a more junior designer.
- Ask about a time you explained the value of user-centred design to
  someone outside the design team.
