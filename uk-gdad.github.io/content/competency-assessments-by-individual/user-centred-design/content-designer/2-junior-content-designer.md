# Junior Content Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

Content designers make things easier for people to understand and use. This can involve working on a single piece of content or on the end-to-end journey of a service to help users complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. In this role your work may involve the creation of, or change to, a transaction, product or single piece of content that stretches across digital and offline channels. A junior content designer is a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject or an individual with some relevant work experience in content creation.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using Strategic thinking (content design), a skill this
level names:

- Someone who can explain why their work matters but has never
  contributed to a strategy or process document sits at **1 — Beginner**,
  below this assessment's baseline.
- Someone who supports the creation and development of strategies and
  policies, contributes to iterating and improving processes and
  guidelines, and understands how their day-to-day activities meet
  strategic goals sits at **2 — Intermediate** — the baseline this
  assessment sets for this skill.
- Someone who additionally helps lead a strategic content improvement
  project is already working towards content designer, at
  **3 — Advanced**.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for
people to understand and use. Their work can involve a single piece of
content or the end-to-end journey of a service, helping users complete their
goal and government deliver a policy intent. The role makes sure appropriate
content is shown to a user in the right place and the best format, starting
from discovery and working closely with user researchers, service designers
and interaction designers.

**This level: junior content designer.** The framework describes this level
as a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject, or an individual with
some relevant work experience in content creation. At this level, the
framework expects someone to:

- explain content decisions
- work collaboratively
- work independently after being given direction by more senior content
  designers
- independently identify user issues and needs
- support content team administration and publishing processes
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government content community

**Where this level sits.** Content designer has six levels:

1. Associate content designer
2. **Junior content designer** — assessed here
3. Content designer
4. Senior content designer
5. Lead content designer
6. Head of content design

**A note on skill wording.** The framework's published skill text for Agile
working (content design), Content concepts and prototyping, Stakeholder
relationship management (content design), User focus (content design) and
User-centred content design repeats word for word what is published for
associate content designer — only Strategic thinking (content design) has
distinct wording at this level. This assessment sets baselines from each
skill's own published wording rather than assuming a higher bar the text
does not state, and says so plainly rather than smoothing the repetition
away. This level's broader accountabilities — working independently after
direction, identifying user issues independently — are real and worth
probing in the evidence log even where a specific skill's baseline has not
moved from the level below.

**The six skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to demonstrate experience of working in
Agile teams, including an awareness of Agile tools and how to use them;
advise colleagues on how and why Agile methods are used and provide a clear,
open and transparent framework for delivery; adapt, reflect and be
responsive to feedback; and see outside of the process.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. As noted above, this skill's published wording
repeats associate content designer's exactly, so this assessment keeps the
same baseline rather than inventing a higher one the text does not support.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a basic Agile tool or ceremony the team uses.
- 1 — Beginner: Has worked in an Agile team, knows the common tools by name,
  and responds constructively when given feedback.
- 2 — Intermediate: Explains to a colleague why the team uses a particular
  Agile method, not just what it is.
- 3 — Advanced: Notices when the team's process is getting in the way of the
  outcome and says so.
- 4 — Expert: Coaches a team in Agile practice and decides the right
  approach for a project — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe an Agile ceremony you took part in and what
  it was for.
- Observe you in a stand-up or sprint planning session, if practical.
- Ask a referee how you respond to feedback given in an Agile team setting.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to show an awareness of prototyping and
explain why and when to use it, and understand how to work in an open and
collaborative environment, for example by pair working.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. This skill's published wording repeats associate
content designer's exactly, so this assessment keeps the same baseline.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain what a content prototype is or why a team might
  make one.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains what prototyping is and gives a plausible reason to
  use it, and has taken part in pair working.
- 2 — Intermediate: Has helped build a simple prototype as part of a team.
- 3 — Advanced: Chooses between prototyping techniques for a given problem
  and explains the choice.
- 4 — Expert: Builds a range of prototypes independently and shows their
  value to a team — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd explain, in your own words, why a team might
  prototype a piece of content before finalising it.
- Ask about a time you worked in a pair or small group on a design problem.
- Look back at any prototype or sketch you contributed to, if one exists.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to understand who their stakeholders are,
what evidence is relevant to them, and the importance of managing
relationships with them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. This skill's published wording repeats associate
content designer's exactly, so this assessment keeps the same baseline.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name who the stakeholders are for a piece of content
  work they have done.
- 1 — Beginner: Names the stakeholders for a piece of work and explains, in
  general terms, why the relationship with them matters.
- 2 — Intermediate: Identifies what evidence a specific stakeholder would
  find relevant, with some support.
- 3 — Advanced: Communicates with a stakeholder directly and clearly on a
  straightforward matter.
- 4 — Expert: Manages complex, long-term stakeholder relationships
  independently — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a piece of work and name who the
  stakeholders were and why.
- Ask what kind of evidence you think would matter to a named stakeholder
  in a hypothetical scenario.
- Ask a referee whether you understands who needs to be kept
  informed on a piece of work.

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to support the creation and development of
strategies and policies, contribute to iterating and improving processes and
guidelines, and understand how activities meet strategic goals.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is the one skill whose published wording
genuinely advances beyond associate content designer — "support the
creation" and "contribute to iterating" describe active participation in
strategy work, not administrative support for it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never contributed to a strategy, policy or process document.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to a strategy or process document when
  specifically asked to add something.
- 2 — Intermediate: Proactively supports the creation of a strategy or
  policy, and contributes ideas to improving an existing process or
  guideline.
- 3 — Advanced: Helps lead a strategic content improvement project, focusing
  effort where it matters most — already working towards content designer.
- 4 — Expert: Leads the design and implementation of strategy for the whole
  discipline — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a strategy, policy or process document you
  contributed to, and what your contribution was.
- Think about how you'd describe a process or guideline you helped improve, and how
  you knew it needed improving.
- Look back at any written contribution to a strategy or process document, if one
  exists.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to show an awareness or understanding of
user experience analysis and its principles, and to see the purpose of user
stories and the focus on user needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. This skill's published wording repeats associate
content designer's exactly, so this assessment keeps the same baseline —
even though this level's broader accountability of "independently identify
user issues and needs" suggests real independent practice, which is worth
probing directly in the evidence log.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain what a user story is or why it matters to
  content.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains, in general terms, what user experience analysis is
  for and can describe the purpose of a user story.
- 2 — Intermediate: Connects a specific user story to the content decision
  it should influence, with some support.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies a user issue or need from a piece
  of evidence, matching this level's own stated accountability.
- 4 — Expert: Gathers and synthesises user evidence to shape content
  decisions independently — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd read a short user story and explain what content
  decision it should influence.
- Think about how you'd describe a user issue or need you identified independently,
  without being told where to look.
- Ask a referee whether you notices user problems unprompted.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects someone to demonstrate basic but decent editorial
standards, show an awareness of style standards, and work well under
supervision.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. This skill's published wording repeats associate
content designer's exactly, including "work well under supervision" — so
this assessment keeps the same baseline rather than assuming the
independence this level's overview describes elsewhere applies to editorial
standards specifically.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Misses obvious spelling, grammar or plain-language problems in a
  short piece of copy.
- 1 — Beginner: Performs a basic copy edit competently, and applies a named
  style rule when it is pointed out; works well when supervised.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies the organisation's style standards consistently
  without being reminded, and catches issues beyond spelling and grammar.
- 3 — Advanced: Writes original short-form content that meets style
  standards with only light review.
- 4 — Expert: Designs content to meet complex user needs independently,
  publishes it, and reviews others' work — well above this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- Work sample: give a short piece of copy with deliberate errors and try to edit it.
- Think about how you'd support content team administration or publishing on a real
  or example piece of work, and observe how independently you manage it.
- Ask a referee how you responds to editorial feedback and whether
  you apply it consistently afterwards.
