# Data Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is for you: a chance to rate your own current strengths against the
baseline the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework sets for this role
level, using the framework's own words for what it covers.

A data architect sets the vision for the organisation’s use of data, through data design, to ensure that data is managed properly and meets the organisation’s needs. A data architect designs and builds data models to fulfil the strategic data needs of the organisation, as defined by chief data architects.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then fill in
the overall determination at the end. Treat this as a mirror, not a test —
there's no assessor watching, so being honest with yourself only costs you a
slightly less flattering picture today in exchange for a far more useful one.
Read `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating you
give yourself here must never be treated as equivalent to a formal
assessment for a hiring, promotion, pay or performance decision. Use it for
your own reflection, and talk to your manager, a mentor, or your
organisation's people development team about what you find.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you're rating
yourself against what this level actually expects rather than the level
above or below it. Then think back over recent, real examples of your work —
not how you'd like to have handled something, but what you actually did.

**Rate on evidence, not impression.** For every skill, try to recall a
specific moment that supports the rating you give yourself. If you can't
think of one, that's useful information too — it might mean the rating
should be lower, or just that you haven't had the chance to show this skill
yet, which is worth noting separately.

**Be consistent with yourself.** Try to apply the same standard across every
skill, rather than being generous where you feel confident and harsh where
you feel unsure. Coming back to this after a few days, with fresh examples in
mind, often sharpens a rating more than sitting with it in one go.

**Be kind, and be honest.** This only works if you can look at the result
without flinching. A low rating here is not a verdict on you — it's a
starting point. Everyone doing this role, at every level, has skills they are
still building.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale — how well someone does the thing, on the evidence
available, not how often they do it or how they feel about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example**, using the skill Data modelling, which this role level
names: a person who can describe what an entity-relationship diagram is but
has never built one is a 1. A person who has produced and maintained a data
model for a live system, with some review from a colleague, is a 2 or 3
depending on how much of that work was independent. A person who can also
reverse-engineer a data model from a live system with no supporting
documentation, unaided, is a 3 or 4 — the framework names this specific
ability at this level, so it is strong evidence for the higher end of the
scale.

## About this role level

A **data architect** sets the vision for the organisation's use of data,
through data design, to make sure that data is managed properly and meets the
organisation's needs.

This is the entry level of the data architect role's ladder, which runs: data
architect (this level) → senior data architect → chief data architect. A data
architect designs and builds data models to fulfil the strategic data needs of
the organisation, as defined by chief data architects — the strategic
direction comes from above; this level does the hands-on design work within
it.

At this role level, you will:

- design, support and provide guidance for the upgrade, management,
  decommissioning and archiving of data, in compliance with data policy
- provide input into data dictionaries
- define and maintain the data technology architecture, including metadata,
  integration and business intelligence or data warehouse architecture

The skills this level names are: Communicating between the technical and
non-technical, Communicating data, Data analysis and synthesis, Data
governance (data architect), Data innovation, Data modelling, Data standards,
Metadata management, Problem management, and Strategic thinking.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What the framework expects at this level**

Communicate effectively with technical and non-technical stakeholders. Support
and host discussions within a multidisciplinary team, including where the
dynamics are difficult. Act as an advocate for the team externally, and manage
differing stakeholder perspectives.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The role already asks for hosting difficult
discussions and managing differing views, which is more than a beginner can
do unaided, but this level works within direction set by chief data
architects, so full independent management of senior, conflicting stakeholders
is not yet the expectation.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain a data concept to a non-technical colleague without
  a technical specialist translating for them.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain a straightforward data concept one-to-one, with
  preparation, but struggles in a live multidisciplinary discussion.
- 2 — Intermediate: Holds their own in a multidisciplinary meeting, adjusts
  language for the audience, and can summarise a technical decision for a
  non-technical stakeholder with some support from a colleague.
- 3 — Advanced: Hosts a discussion between technical and non-technical
  stakeholders unaided, including one with some tension in the room, and
  represents the team's position to an external audience.
- 4 — Expert: Is sought out to mediate the hardest stakeholder conversations
  across the organisation, and coaches others in how to do the same.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a time you explained a data model or data
  quality issue to a non-technical stakeholder, and what changed in how you
  explained it.
- Observe, or ask a referee about, how the person behaves in a meeting where a
  technical team and a business team disagree about a data change.
- Look back at a document, such as a data dictionary or a design note, the person
  wrote for a mixed technical and non-technical audience.

### Skill: Communicating data

**What the framework expects at this level**

Show an awareness that data needs to be aligned to the needs of the end user.
Create basic visuals and presentations.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording is "show an awareness" and
"create basic visuals" — this is a starting point, not independent mastery, at
this level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Presents raw data or a query result with no framing for the
  audience.
- 1 — Beginner: Produces a basic chart or table and can explain what it shows,
  when asked, with some prompting.
- 2 — Intermediate: Chooses a chart type that fits the data and the point
  being made, and builds a short presentation around it with some guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently turns a data set into a clear presentation for a
  named audience, checking the framing lands before presenting it.
- 4 — Expert: Is asked by colleagues to review or improve how they are
  presenting data, and sets a visible standard for it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example chart, dashboard or presentation you built, and
  what you decided to leave out.
- Give a small, anonymised data set as a task and try to produce
  one visual and one sentence of explanation for a non-technical audience.

### Skill: Data analysis and synthesis

**What the framework expects at this level**

Undertake data profiling and source system analysis. Present clear insights to
colleagues to support the end use of the data.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is core, hands-on work for a data architect,
so a person should be able to do it with some guidance from day one, even
though full independence is not yet expected.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not profiled a data source or identified data quality issues
  in one.
- 1 — Beginner: Can run a profiling task with a template or checklist and
  needs help interpreting the results.
- 2 — Intermediate: Profiles a source system, identifies obvious quality or
  structural issues, and writes up findings with some review.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently profiles unfamiliar source systems and turns the
  findings into insight colleagues can act on, without prompting.
- 4 — Expert: Designs the profiling approach for a new or unusual source, and
  is someone others check their findings against.

**Evidence to gather**

- Walk through a past data profiling exercise you led: what you
  found, and how you decided what mattered.
- Give a sample source system schema or extract and try to
  identify likely quality issues and what you would check next.
- Ask a referee what happened after this person's analysis reached the team —
  did it change a decision.

### Skill: Data governance (data architect)

**What the framework expects at this level**

Understand what data governance is required. Take responsibility for the
assurance of data solutions and make recommendations to ensure compliance.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework asks this level to take
responsibility for assurance and recommend action, which needs more than
awareness, but the wider governance framework itself is set above this level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what data governance requirements apply to a solution
  they have worked on.
- 1 — Beginner: Can name the governance requirements that apply, with
  prompting, but does not yet check a solution against them unaided.
- 2 — Intermediate: Checks a data solution against known governance
  requirements and raises a compliance concern, with some support in deciding
  what to recommend.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently assures a data solution against governance
  requirements and makes a clear, actionable recommendation when it falls
  short.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes what the governance requirements should be for a new kind
  of data solution, and is trusted to sign off assurance for others.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a data solution you assured for governance
  compliance, and what recommendation you made.
- Present a short scenario — a proposed data solution with a plausible
  governance gap — and ask what you would check and recommend.

### Skill: Data innovation

- 3 — Advanced: Brings a genuinely useful new tool or technique to the team's

  innovation and to judge when it is worth adopting.

**Evidence to gather**

### Skill: Data modelling

**What the framework expects at this level**

Explain the concepts and principles of data modelling. Produce, maintain and
update relevant data models for an organisation's specific needs.
Reverse-engineer data models from a live system.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is the core technical work of the role at this
level, and the framework specifically names reverse-engineering a model from a
live system unaided — that is independent, applied work, not guided practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot produce a data model from a set of requirements.
- 1 — Beginner: Can build a simple data model from clear requirements with
  close supervision.
- 2 — Intermediate: Produces and maintains a data model for a defined need
  with some review, and can explain basic modelling concepts correctly.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently produces, maintains and updates data models for
  real organisational needs, and can reverse-engineer a model from a live
  system with no supporting documentation.
- 4 — Expert: Is someone others bring their hardest modelling problems to,
  and their models are used as the reference standard by colleagues.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a data model you produced or maintained, and what
  trade-offs you made in its design.
- Give a small live system, or a description of one, and try to
  sketch the data model you would reverse-engineer from it, and how.
- Ask a referee how independently this person handles data modelling work.

### Skill: Data standards

**What the framework expects at this level**

Use data policies, processes and standards effectively. Work with subject
matter experts to develop standards, policies and guidance to protect data.
Monitor compliance with policies and standards in a team, and take action if
needed. Analyse the impact if a standard is breached.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The level applies existing standards and
contributes to developing them alongside subject matter experts, rather than
setting organisation-wide standards, which sits higher up the role's ladder.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Is not aware of the data standards that apply to their work.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows a named standard when told which one applies, with
  supervision.
- 2 — Intermediate: Applies the right standards to their own work
  unprompted, and monitors a team's compliance with some guidance on what
  action to take when it slips.
- 3 — Advanced: Works with subject matter experts to develop a new standard
  or refine an existing one, and independently analyses the impact of a
  breach.
- 4 — Expert: Is someone the team turns to when a standard is ambiguous or
  contested, and their analysis of a breach's impact is trusted without
  further review.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a time you found and reported a breach of a
  data standard, and what you did next.
- Ask for an example of a standard, policy or piece of guidance you helped
  develop with subject matter experts.

### Skill: Metadata management

**What the framework expects at this level**

Use metadata repositories to complete complex tasks, such as data and systems
integration impact analysis. Maintain a metadata repository to ensure
information remains accurate and up to date.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Using a repository for complex analysis, and
keeping it accurate, is applied day-to-day work at this level rather than the
design of a repository, which belongs to the level above.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not used a metadata repository as part of their work.
- 1 — Beginner: Can look up an entry in a metadata repository but needs help
  using it for anything more than a simple lookup.
- 2 — Intermediate: Uses a metadata repository to support an integration
  impact analysis, and keeps entries they are responsible for accurate, with
  some review.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently uses metadata repositories for complex,
  multi-system impact analysis, and proactively corrects stale entries.
- 4 — Expert: Is relied on to judge whether a metadata repository is fit for
  purpose, and trains others in using it well.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd describe a systems integration impact analysis you
  did using a metadata repository, and what it changed about the approach
  taken.
- Ask how you check that a metadata repository you maintain stays accurate
  over time.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at this level**

Initiate and monitor actions to investigate patterns and trends to resolve
problems. Effectively consult specialists where required. Determine the
appropriate resolution and assist with its implementation. Determine
preventative measures.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The level owns investigating and resolving
problems it encounters, and knows when to bring in a specialist, but does not
yet co-ordinate a wider team's problem-solving, which is a senior data
architect accountability.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Does not investigate a recurring data problem beyond fixing the
  immediate symptom.
- 1 — Beginner: Investigates a problem when directed to, and needs help
  identifying the root cause.
- 2 — Intermediate: Spots a pattern behind a set of related issues, consults a
  specialist appropriately, and helps implement a resolution with some
  oversight.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently drives a problem from pattern-spotting through
  resolution to a preventative measure, without needing to be told to.
- 4 — Expert: Is brought in on the hardest, longest-running data problems, and
  their preventative measures reliably stop the problem recurring.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd walk through a data problem you investigated: what
  pattern you spotted, who you consulted, and what you did to stop it
  recurring.
- Ask a referee for an example of a problem this person resolved
  independently, and how it stayed fixed.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

Explain the strategic context of your work and why it is important. Support
strategic planning in an administrative capacity.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own wording — "support... in an
administrative capacity" — sets a supporting, not a leading, expectation at
this level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot connect their day-to-day work to the organisation's wider
  data strategy.
- 1 — Beginner: Can explain, when asked, how their work fits the strategic

  instructions.
- 2 — Intermediate: Proactively frames their own work in terms of the
  strategy, and contributes useful input to a strategic planning exercise
  without being asked.
- 3 — Advanced: Identifies where their work should shift to better serve the
  strategy, and raises it before being asked.
- 4 — Expert: Shapes strategic planning discussions directly and is trusted
  to represent the strategic context to others.

**Evidence to gather**

- Think about how you'd explain how a piece of work you did served the
  organisation's data strategy.
- Ask for an example of supporting a strategic planning activity — a
  workshop, a briefing paper, a roadmap input — and what your contribution
