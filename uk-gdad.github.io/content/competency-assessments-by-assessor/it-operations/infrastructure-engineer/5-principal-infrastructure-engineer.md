# Principal Infrastructure Engineer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, an interview panel
member, or anyone else evaluating a candidate or an employee — who needs to
judge whether a person has the baseline abilities the IT operations
profession's principal infrastructure engineer role level requires. It
measures demonstrated skill against the UK GDAD Profession Capability
Framework's own description of this level, for a hiring decision, a
promotion or progression board, onboarding a new starter, or a periodic
capability check. It is one input into that decision, never the whole of it
— see `## Advisory` before you begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what the
framework actually expects before you look at any evidence. Then gather what
exists for this person: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work
sample or task, direct observation of them at work, or references from people
who have worked with them. At this level the framework expects an accomplished
technical leader who develops strategic and tactical engineering roadmaps and
may own operational supplier relationships, so look for a track record over
time — a roadmap that played out, a supplier relationship managed through a
difficult period — rather than a single task.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess — an honest gap
teaches you more than a guess dressed up as a score.

**Stay consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
using the same evidence sources wherever you can. For a hiring or promotion
decision, have more than one assessor score independently, then compare notes,
rather than settling on a single view first.

**Fairness.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact even when nobody intends
it. Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

**Handling sensitive material.** Infrastructure engineering at this level
routinely touches live infrastructure, supplier contracts and security
controls. When you record evidence in `## Assessor notes and evidence log`,
describe what the person did and how they reasoned about it — not classified
material, live vulnerability detail, or commercially sensitive contract
terms.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale — how well the person does the thing, on the evidence
available, not how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example.** Take Skill: Systems design. At 3 — Advanced, a candidate
independently designs systems of medium risk, impact and complexity and
reviews colleagues' designs. At 4 — Expert, the baseline for this level, they
design systems of high risk, impact and complexity, control system design
practice within an enterprise or industry architecture, influence
industry-based models for new technology applications, and develop
implementation and procurement strategies consistent with business needs —
the framework's own wording for this level, and evidence of influence well
beyond a single team. Calibrate against evidence like this before you start
scoring.

## About this role level

IT operations role: Infrastructure engineer. An infrastructure engineer
designs, builds, manages and supports the infrastructure services that
underpin all internal user services and services to the public.

The role has five levels, in order: associate infrastructure engineer,
infrastructure engineer, senior infrastructure engineer, lead infrastructure
engineer, and principal infrastructure engineer. This assessment covers
principal infrastructure engineer, the highest of those.

Role level: Principal infrastructure engineer. A principal infrastructure
engineer is an accomplished technical leader.

At this role level, the framework expects someone to:

- develop strategic and tactical engineering roadmaps for technologies and
  services in their area of responsibility, ensuring they are future-proofed
  and that the organisation derives maximum value from investment in
  technologies
- potentially own the operational relationships with suppliers, ensuring
  services and products are delivered and aligned to industry best practice
  and regulatory and contractual requirements
- work with technical architects to translate the architectural designs into
  operations, and support technical architects in operationalising the
  designs
- lead and direct infrastructure specialist teams in building, managing,
  supporting and maintaining solutions according to departmental policy, if
  taking a managerial path

The framework names eleven skills for this level: Coding and scripting, IT
infrastructure, Information security, Modern development standards, Ownership
and initiative, Problem management, Service focus, Systems design, Systems
integration, Testing, and Troubleshooting and problem resolution.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Coding and scripting

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal infrastructure engineer interacts with, reads and writes code,
and carries out baseline repair activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's wording is unchanged from the lead
level, so the baseline holds — hands-on coding and baseline repair remain
Advanced at this level, with the level's added weight sitting in strategic
and roadmap work instead.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot read or interpret a piece of code or a script.
- 1 — Beginner: reads code with help to understand what it does.
- 2 — Intermediate: reads and makes small, guided edits to existing code or
  scripts.
- 3 — Advanced: reads and writes code independently, and carries out baseline
  repair activities when something breaks.
- 4 — Expert: is the person colleagues bring the hardest code or scripting
  problems to.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a piece of code or a script they wrote or
  repaired.
- Observe or ask them to describe a baseline repair activity they carried
  out.
- Ask a referee about the quality and reliability of the candidate's code or
  scripts.

### Skill: IT infrastructure

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal infrastructure engineer designs and implements infrastructure
solutions and services — for example, networking and physical infrastructure,
commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) software, open source packages and solutions,
or virtual and cloud computing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Notably, the framework's own wording for this skill
at principal level matches the senior level's independent design-and-implement
description, and sits below the lead level's team-and-department leadership
wording — an anomaly in the framework's own text, not a judgement made here.
A principal is expected to remain a strong, independent hands-on designer, so
the baseline is set at Advanced to match the framework's actual words rather
than assumed to be Expert because of the level's seniority.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not designed an infrastructure solution.
- 1 — Beginner: has helped implement an infrastructure design someone else
  produced.
- 2 — Intermediate: designs straightforward infrastructure components with a
  colleague checking the approach.
- 3 — Advanced: independently designs and implements infrastructure solutions
  and services, and takes responsibility for how well they perform once live.
- 4 — Expert: sets infrastructure design standards for others to follow.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review an infrastructure design the candidate produced end to end, and ask
  them to justify the key decisions in it.
- Ask about a piece of infrastructure they designed and implemented, and what
  happened once it went live.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's infrastructure designs have needed
  significant intervention after go-live.

### Skill: Ownership and initiative

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal infrastructure engineer takes accountability for issues that
occur and is proactive in searching for potential problems, and achieves
excellent user outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's wording is unchanged from the lead
level, so the baseline holds; at principal level this proactive accountability
is expected to extend across strategic roadmap decisions too.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: only acts once an issue has already been raised.
- 1 — Beginner: owns an existing issue through to resolution when reminded to.
- 2 — Intermediate: owns issues through to resolution unprompted, but has not
  yet proactively surfaced a potential problem before it occurred.
- 3 — Advanced: takes accountability for issues that occur, proactively looks
  for potential problems, and consistently achieves a good outcome for users.
- 4 — Expert: builds a habit of proactive problem-searching into how the
  whole team works.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a potential problem the candidate spotted and
  addressed before it became an incident, at a strategic or roadmap level.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate takes accountability when something
  goes wrong, or shifts blame.
- Review evidence of a user outcome the candidate achieved that went beyond
  the minimum fix.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal infrastructure engineer ensures that the right actions are taken
to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems, and co-ordinates the team to
investigate problems, implement solutions and take preventive measures.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Ensuring the right actions are taken and co-ordinating
the whole team, rather than personally determining a resolution as at the
levels below, is directing others through problem management — full mastery
on this scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe how problem management should be co-ordinated
  across a team.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes to a problem investigation someone else
  co-ordinates.
- 2 — Intermediate: co-ordinates a straightforward problem investigation with
  a colleague available to check the approach.
- 3 — Advanced: ensures the right actions are taken on most problems and
  co-ordinates the team's response independently.
- 4 — Expert: ensures the team consistently investigates, resolves and
  anticipates problems, and is the escalation point when co-ordination breaks
  down.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe how they co-ordinated a team through a
  significant problem investigation.
- Review a problem management process or escalation record they own.
- Ask a referee whether the team's problem management has improved under the
  candidate's co-ordination.

### Skill: Service focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal infrastructure engineer sees the bigger picture by taking groups
of services and investigating how to get the best of underlying services.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Taking groups of services together and investigating
how to get the best out of what underlies them is the strategic, big-picture
view the level description names, a real step up from establishing
frameworks for a single area at the level below.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: only ever considers one service at a time.
- 1 — Beginner: helps analyse a group of services under a colleague's
  direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: analyses how two related services interact, with support.
- 3 — Advanced: improves how a single service's underlying components work
  together.
- 4 — Expert: takes a group of services together, investigates how the
  underlying services can best support them, and acts on what that reveals.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an improvement they made that spanned more
  than one service.
- Review a strategy document or roadmap where the candidate analysed a group
  of services together.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate spotting an opportunity
  across services that a narrower view would have missed.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal infrastructure engineer designs systems characterised by high
levels of risk, impact, and business or technical complexity, controls system
design practice within an enterprise or industry architecture, influences
industry-based models for the development of new technology applications,
develops effective implementation and procurement strategies consistent with
business needs, and ensures adherence to relevant technical strategies,
policies, standards and practices.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This is the biggest single jump in the whole matrix:
from medium-risk design and reviewing colleagues at the level below to
high-risk design, controlling enterprise-wide practice and influencing
industry models — unambiguous, full-mastery Expert work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot judge whether a high-risk systems design is fit for
  purpose.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes to a high-risk design under close direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: designs a system of medium risk unassisted, but has not
  yet taken on high-risk, enterprise-scale design.
- 3 — Advanced: designs high-risk, high-complexity systems independently,
  without yet controlling practice beyond their own designs.
- 4 — Expert: controls system design practice within an enterprise or
  industry architecture, influences industry-based models for new technology
  applications, and develops implementation and procurement strategies
  consistent with business needs.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a high-risk, high-complexity system they
  designed, and how it fit within enterprise or industry architecture.
- Review an implementation or procurement strategy the candidate developed.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate influencing an industry-based
  model or standard beyond their own organisation.

### Skill: Testing

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal infrastructure engineer manages testing activities within
development or integration activities, manages risks and takes preventative
action when risks become unacceptable, and manages customer relations.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. Managing risk to the point of preventative action, and
managing the customer relationship around that decision, is a wider,
judgement-heavy remit than the authoritative-advice role at lead level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe how testing risk connects to customer relations.
- 1 — Beginner: monitors a testing risk that someone else manages.
- 2 — Intermediate: manages a straightforward testing risk with a colleague

- 3 — Advanced: manages testing activities and risk within development or

- 4 — Expert: takes preventative action when testing risk becomes
  unacceptable, and manages the customer relationship around that decision.

**Evidence to gather**

  unacceptable and took preventative action.

### Skill: Troubleshooting and problem resolution

**What the framework expects at this level**

A principal infrastructure engineer uses lateral thinking to break a problem
down into its component parts to identify and diagnose root causes, and
troubleshoots and identifies problems across different technology
capabilities, including computing, storage, networking, physical
infrastructure, software, commercial-off-the-shelf software (COTS) and open
source software.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. The framework's wording is unchanged from the lead
level, so the baseline holds at Expert — lateral, cross-capability root-cause
diagnosis remains the expectation at this level too.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe a structured approach to diagnosing a fault.
- 1 — Beginner: diagnoses a familiar fault type when guided through it.
- 2 — Intermediate: breaks down a fault within one technology capability
  unassisted.
- 3 — Advanced: independently breaks a cross-capability problem into its
  component parts and diagnoses the root cause.
- 4 — Expert: uses lateral thinking to diagnose a root cause across
  computing, storage, networking, physical infrastructure, software, COTS
  and open source software that has resisted a straightforward breakdown, and
  is the escalation point for others.

**Evidence to gather**

- Walk through a real or simulated multi-capability fault scenario and observe
  how the candidate uses lateral thinking to decompose it.
- Ask for an example of a root cause they found only by thinking laterally
  about the problem, beyond the obvious suspects.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate being the last resort when a
  fault had resisted diagnosis by others.
