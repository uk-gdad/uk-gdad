# Lead Application Operations Engineer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the assessing — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or a reviewer — who needs to judge whether a candidate or employee has the baseline abilities the lead application operations engineer role level requires. It covers the IT operations role of application operations engineer at its lead level: someone who defines the application strategy and the strategy for security administration, liaises with leads across IT operations on how applications are performing and what changes need to be put in place, and may still manage and maintain aspects of an application directly where its complexity, size or likely business impact warrants it.

The competency matrix below turns the framework's own description of this role level into a rated, evidence-based instrument across the eleven skills it names. It is one input among several into a hiring, onboarding or development decision, never the whole of one — see `## Advisory` for why.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so the framework's own description of the level is fresh in your mind. Gather whatever evidence exists ahead of time: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task, direct observation of the person working, or references — and at this level, look for evidence that spans a period of time rather than a single incident, since a lead's accountabilities are largely about sustained judgement and influence rather than one-off tasks.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess. Seniority in job title is not evidence of skill; ask for the specific decisions, changes or investigations the candidate led.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, using the same evidence types where possible, and for a hiring or promotion decision have more than one assessor score independently before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one can produce unlawful adverse impact if it is used inconsistently or if ratings drift away from demonstrated, job-related evidence. Score against the behavioural indicators below, keep your evidence log, and use more than one assessor wherever you can — consistent, evidence-based administration is what materially reduces that risk. See [Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use) for the full constraints this assessment is written to.

## Rating scale

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a proficiency scale, not a frequency scale — it rates how well the person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example — Incident management.** A candidate who can log an incident accurately but has never run an investigation sits at 2. A candidate who leads the investigation and resolution of incidents independently, including moderately complex ones with more than one plausible cause, sits at 3 — this level's own baseline, since the framework describes this level as leading incident investigation and resolution. A candidate who, beyond that, is the person other leads call in for the organisation's most difficult incidents, and who has changed how their team runs a major incident because of what they learned, is showing more than this level's baseline and starts to look like level 4 evidence.

## About this role level

The application operations engineer supports, manages and maintains a single application or a suite of applications. The role runs across five levels: associate application operations engineer, application operations engineer, senior application operations engineer, lead application operations engineer, and principal application operations engineer.

The lead application operations engineer level is the fourth of the five, sitting above senior application operations engineer and below principal application operations engineer. The framework describes it as defining the application strategy and the strategy for security administration. At this level, you liaise with leads across IT operations on how applications are performing and what changes need to be put in place, and — depending on the complexity and size of a project, and the likely impact it will have on the business — you may still manage and maintain certain aspects of an application yourself rather than delegating everything.

The framework names eleven skills at this level: asset and configuration management (maintaining secure configuration and accurate information, controlling IT assets in one or more areas, verifying the location and state of IT assets), availability and capacity management (managing service components to meet business needs and KPIs), change management (managing high impact, complex change requests, and ensuring that release policies, procedures and processes are applied), incident management (leading the investigation and resolution of incidents), ownership and initiative (taking accountability for issues that occur, being proactive in searching for potential problems, and achieving excellent user outcomes), problem management (initiating and monitoring actions to investigate patterns and trends, consulting specialists where required, determining the appropriate resolution and assisting with its implementation, and determining preventative measures), service focus (taking inputs and establishing coherent frameworks that work), service management framework knowledge (holding a Level 3 service management framework qualification and demonstrating knowledge of the life cycle or capability elements of ITIL), technical specialism (drafting and maintaining procedures and documentation, and setting standards for the definition, security and integrity of objects, ensuring conformity to those standards), testing (managing the planning of system and acceptance tests, co-ordinating functional and non-functional specifications, providing authoritative advice and guidance on test planning, and identifying process improvements and contributing to the definition of best practice), and user focus (collaborating with user researchers and representing users internally, explaining the difference between user needs and user desires, championing user research, prioritising and defining approaches to understanding the user story while guiding others in doing so, and offering recommendations on the best tools and methods to use).

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Asset and configuration management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to maintain secure configuration and accurate information, to control IT assets in one or more areas, and to verify the location and state of IT assets.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework's wording for this skill does not change from the base level, but a lead is expected to hold this accurately and independently across one or more areas as a matter of course, without the checking-in that a more junior colleague would still receive, so the baseline moves to Advanced on the strength of the level's overall seniority and its liaison accountabilities.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe what a configuration record is for or has never checked one against reality.
- 1 — Beginner: Updates a configuration record correctly only when told exactly what to change.
- 2 — Intermediate: Maintains accurate configuration and asset records for one area unprompted, with occasional oversight.
- 3 — Advanced: Maintains configuration and asset accuracy across one or more areas without oversight, catches discrepancies before they cause an incident, and is the person other engineers check with when a record looks wrong.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the configuration and asset management standard for the wider service and is asked to review other teams' practice against it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe how they have kept configuration accurate across more than one application or area over an extended period, not just a single fix.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's areas have needed fewer configuration audit corrections over time, and why.
- Give a scenario where two configuration sources disagree across systems the candidate does not own outright, and ask how they would resolve it and who they would involve.

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to manage service components so that they meet business needs and key performance indicators (KPIs).

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Managing components to KPIs is stated plainly, but at this level it sits alongside strategy-setting and cross-team liaison, so an assessor should expect this to be done independently and proactively rather than reactively.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Does not know what availability or capacity KPIs apply to the applications they support.
- 1 — Beginner: Reads the relevant availability or capacity figures, with help interpreting them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Monitors service components against agreed KPIs and takes routine action to keep them within target.
- 3 — Advanced: Spots capacity and availability trends before they breach KPIs, adjusts service components proactively, and explains the trade-offs to the leads they liaise with.
- 4 — Expert: Sets availability and capacity targets for a suite of applications and is consulted by other leads on how to reach theirs.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a capacity or availability risk they identified before it became a breach, and what they changed as a result.
- Ask them to talk through how they have reported availability or capacity performance to a lead in another part of IT operations.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's applications have a track record of staying within agreed KPIs, and what the candidate did to keep it that way.

### Skill: Change management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to manage high impact, complex change requests, and to ensure that release policies, procedures and processes are applied.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Manage high impact, complex change requests" and "ensure … are applied" describe independent ownership of the hardest changes and oversight of process, which is a clear step above applying procedure under supervision, and matches Advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never managed a change request beyond following a documented procedure.
- 1 — Beginner: Manages a straightforward change request with close oversight.
- 2 — Intermediate: Manages routine change requests independently, following release policy without deviation.
- 3 — Advanced: Manages high impact, complex change requests independently, and checks that release policies and procedures are actually being applied by others, not just by themselves.
- 4 — Expert: Owns or shapes the release policy itself, and is brought in to manage the organisation's most business-critical changes.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a high impact or complex change they managed: what made it complex, who they had to coordinate with, and how they assured themselves the release process was followed.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has been asked to step in on a change others found too risky to manage alone.
- Ask the candidate to describe a time they identified that a release policy or procedure was not being followed properly, and what they did about it.

### Skill: Incident management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to lead the investigation and resolution of incidents.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. "Lead the investigation and resolution" is explicit leadership language, describing someone who runs an incident rather than works a step of it, which matches independent, Advanced practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has never run an incident investigation, only followed instructions during one.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to an investigation someone else is leading, with direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads the investigation of straightforward incidents, with a more senior colleague available if needed.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads the investigation and resolution of incidents, including moderately complex ones with more than one plausible cause, coordinating other engineers and communicating clearly with affected stakeholders throughout.
- 4 — Expert: Is called on to lead the organisation's most difficult or highest-impact incidents, and has changed how their team runs a major incident based on what they learned.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an incident they led from first alert to resolution, including how they decided who else needed to be involved.
- Ask a referee how the candidate performs under the pressure of a live, high-impact incident, and whether others look to them to take the lead.
- Ask the candidate what they changed about their own or their team's incident process after a past incident, and why.

### Skill: Ownership and initiative

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to take accountability for issues that occur, to be proactive in searching for potential problems, and to achieve excellent user outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Proactively searching for problems before they are reported, rather than only owning what lands on their desk, describes independent, self-directed practice, which matches Advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Only acts on issues once someone else has raised them.
- 1 — Beginner: Takes accountability for an issue once assigned it, but does not look for problems unprompted.
- 2 — Intermediate: Takes accountability for issues in their own area and occasionally spots a potential problem before it is reported.
- 3 — Advanced: Regularly searches out potential problems before they surface, takes accountability for the outcome even when the cause sits outside their own area, and can point to user outcomes that improved because of it.
- 4 — Expert: Is the person other leads expect to have already spotted a coming problem, and sets the standard for proactive ownership that others in the team are measured against.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a potential problem they found before anyone reported it, and what they did next.
- Ask a referee to describe how the candidate behaves when an issue's cause turns out to be outside their own area of responsibility.
- Ask the candidate for an example of a user outcome that improved because of something they proactively fixed rather than were asked to fix.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to initiate and monitor actions to investigate patterns and trends to resolve problems, to consult specialists effectively where required, to determine the appropriate resolution and assist with its implementation, and to determine preventative measures.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Initiating and monitoring investigations, choosing the resolution, and setting preventative measures describes independent judgement across the whole problem management cycle, which matches Advanced; "assist with" implementation, rather than own it outright, is why this stops short of Expert.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot distinguish an incident from a problem.
- 1 — Beginner: Investigates a pattern or trend when told to, with direction on what to look for.
- 2 — Intermediate: Investigates problems unprompted and proposes a workable remedy for straightforward cases.
- 3 — Advanced: Initiates and monitors investigation of patterns and trends across systems, consults the right specialists at the right time, determines the appropriate resolution, and defines preventative measures that stop the problem recurring.
- 4 — Expert: Runs problem management across a suite of applications, teaches others how to distinguish a symptom from a root cause, and their preventative measures are adopted beyond their own team.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a recurring problem they investigated, including which specialists they brought in and why, and what preventative measure they put in place.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's preventative measures have actually reduced repeat incidents, and over what period.
- Give a short scenario describing a pattern of related faults across systems and ask the candidate how they would decide where to start and who to involve.

### Skill: Service focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to take inputs and establish coherent frameworks that work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. At this level, "coherent frameworks that work" sit alongside cross-team liaison and strategy-setting duties, so an assessor should expect frameworks that other people actually adopt, not just ones that work for the author, which matches Advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how their work fits into the wider service.
- 1 — Beginner: Follows an existing framework without adapting it, even when it does not fit.
- 2 — Intermediate: Puts together a working approach from a set of inputs for their own area.
- 3 — Advanced: Builds coherent frameworks from varied inputs — requirements, constraints, existing tools — that other engineers and teams adopt, and revises them as circumstances change.
- 4 — Expert: Designs frameworks that span multiple services and are adopted as the reference approach across IT operations.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a framework or approach they built that other people ended up using without being told to.
- Ask them to critique a deliberately awkward cross-team process and propose a coherent alternative, including who they would need to bring on board.
- Ask a referee whether an approach the candidate put together has spread beyond their own immediate team.

### Skill: Service management framework knowledge

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to show they have a Level 3 service management framework qualification, and to demonstrate knowledge of the life cycle or capability elements of ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library).

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This level names the same Level 3 qualification as the base level but adds demonstrated knowledge of ITIL's life cycle or capability elements, a materially deeper expectation, which moves the baseline up to Advanced.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Holds no service management framework qualification and cannot explain basic service management terms.
- 1 — Beginner: Holds a Level 3 qualification but cannot explain how the ITIL life cycle or capability elements relate to their own work.
- 2 — Intermediate: Holds a Level 3 qualification and can describe the ITIL life cycle or capability elements in general terms.
- 3 — Advanced: Holds a Level 3 qualification and demonstrates working knowledge of the ITIL life cycle or capability elements by applying them to real decisions in their own applications.
- 4 — Expert: Holds an expert-level service management qualification and is asked to explain ITIL life cycle or capability concepts to other leads or teams.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to provide or describe their service management framework qualification and certificate level.
- Ask them to explain how a specific ITIL life cycle or capability element (for example, service transition or problem management as a capability) shows up in a decision they made recently.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has been asked to advise colleagues on service management framework practice.

### Skill: Technical specialism

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to draft and maintain procedures and documentation, and to set standards for the definition, security and integrity of objects, ensuring conformity to those standards.

### Skill: Testing

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to manage the planning of system and acceptance tests, co-ordinating both functional and non-functional specifications, to provide authoritative advice and guidance on test planning, and to identify process improvements and contribute to the definition of best practice.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Authoritative advice and guidance" and "contribute to the definition of best practice" are explicit statements that this level is expected to guide others' testing practice, not just perform their own, which is the definition of Expert on this scale.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot plan a test or explain what functional and non-functional specifications are.
- 1 — Beginner: Executes a test plan someone else wrote, with checking.
- 2 — Intermediate: Plans and runs system and acceptance tests for their own application unprompted.
- 3 — Advanced: Manages the planning of system and acceptance tests independently, co-ordinating functional and non-functional specifications across more than one component.
- 4 — Expert: Is sought out for authoritative advice on test planning beyond their own application, and has contributed a process improvement or a piece of best practice that other teams have adopted.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time another team or engineer came to them for advice on how to plan a set of tests, and what they advised.
- Ask them to describe a test planning process improvement or best practice they proposed, and whether it was adopted beyond their own work.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects a lead application operations engineer to collaborate with user researchers and represent users internally, to explain the difference between user needs and the desires of the user, to champion user research so that it focuses on all users, to prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story while guiding others in doing so, and to offer recommendations on the best tools and methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Guiding others in doing so" is an explicit statement that this level teaches other people how to understand user need, which is the definition of Expert on this scale, on top of representing users internally and championing research in the first place.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Does not engage with users or user research in their work.
- 1 — Beginner: Uses user research findings someone else produced, without contributing to how research is done.
- 2 — Intermediate: Collaborates with user researchers, represents users internally, and can explain the difference between a user need and a user desire.
- 3 — Advanced: Champions user research so that it covers all relevant users, not just the loudest or most convenient ones, and prioritises and defines an approach to understanding a user story independently.
- 4 — Expert: Guides other engineers in how to define an approach to understanding a user story, and is asked to recommend which tools and methods a team should use.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they guided a less experienced colleague through understanding a user story, including what they specifically taught them.
- Ask them to describe a case where they championed research into a group of users who were being overlooked, and what changed as a result.
- Ask a referee whether other engineers come to the candidate for advice on which user research tools or methods to use.
