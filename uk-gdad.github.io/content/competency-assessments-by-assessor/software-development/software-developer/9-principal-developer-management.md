# Principal developer (management) - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This is a competency assessment for the **principal developer (management)**
role level in the UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) software developer
role — the top of the whole software developer ladder. It is written for an
assessor — a line manager, a panel, or a reviewer — judging whether a candidate
or employee has the baseline abilities this level requires, against the UK
GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of the role and level.

Use it for a hiring decision, a promotion board, onboarding into the role, or a
capability check against the framework. It is one input among several, never
the whole of a decision — see `## Advisory` for why, and read it before you use
this document with a real person.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what this
level is actually accountable for before you look at any evidence. Then gather
what exists: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task,
direct observation, or references — at this level, references from client
functions or peers who have seen the candidate's programme-level work carry
particular weight.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating in the competency matrix
must trace to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing.
If you cannot point to evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than
guess from a general impression of the person.

**Consistency matters.** Score every candidate for this role level the same
way. Where you can, have more than one assessor score independently and compare
notes afterwards — this matters most for a hiring or promotion decision, where
the stakes for the person are highest.

**Fairness is not automatic.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument used
inconsistently, or scored from impression rather than evidence, can produce
unlawful adverse impact — rating some groups of candidates systematically lower
for reasons that have nothing to do with the job. Consistent, evidence-based
administration by more than one assessor materially reduces that risk. Never
let a rating be influenced by anything other than demonstrated, job-related
evidence — see [Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification for what that rules out.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale.
This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well the person does the thing,
on the evidence available — not a frequency scale, and it is not about how
often they do it or how they feel about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**A worked example**, using Development process optimisation, one of the skills
this level names:

- **3 — Advanced**: the candidate can describe a development process they
  improved for their own team, with a measurable result, but the change was
  scoped to one team and did not require negotiating resource or funding with
  a client function.
- **4 — Expert**: the candidate can describe setting the strategy and managing
  resource allocation for a whole solution development programme — working
  with a client function to establish business requirements, proposing the
  programme, and leading it — evidence at the scale this level's own wording
  claims.
- Anything below 3 at this level is worth pausing on: the framework's own text
  for this skill, at this level, is explicitly programme-and-strategy scale, so
  a rating below 3 usually means the evidence gathered so far has not reached
  that scale yet, not that the standard should be lowered.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs — writing clean, secure code following
a test-driven approach, and creating code that is open by default and easy for
others to reuse.

**This level: principal developer (management).** The framework describes a
principal developer as someone who leads and plans development across large or
multiple teams. At this role level, you will:

- be an expert in many technical areas or a specialist with very deep
  knowledge in a particular technical area
- use this knowledge, and work with related disciplines, to keep multiple
  teams working effectively
- develop capability by facilitating internal mobility, shaping career paths
  and recruiting talent, ensuring they collaborate
- identify, test and champion the adoption of emerging technologies

**This is the management track, and the top of the ladder.** The framework
defines two roles at this seniority — a technical one and a management one —
and this document assesses the management track. Its sibling, the technical
track, is [Principal developer](8-principal-developer.md), assessed in a
separate document at the same level in the ladder. The two share every
accountability above; where they differ is in three of the ten skills, set out
below. There is no level above this one on the software developer ladder, so
questions of judgement and trade-off dominate more here than anywhere else in
this project.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. Senior developer
5. Senior developer (management)
6. Lead developer
7. Lead developer (management)
8. Principal developer
9. **Principal developer (management)** — you are here, the top of the ladder

**How this level's skill wording differs from the technical track.** Two
skills read more senior — indeed programme- or strategy-scale — here than at
the technical Principal developer level: Availability and capacity management
moves from managing service components to ensuring correct implementation of
standards and initiating remedial action, and Development process optimisation
moves from leading a team of experts to setting the strategy and managing
resource allocation for whole solution development programmes, working with
client functions to establish business requirements. One skill reads *less*
senior here than at the technical track: Programming and build stays at the
"collaborate... use the agreed specifications... medium-to-high complexity"
wording seen at developer and senior developer level, rather than the
technical principal's "maintain technical responsibility for all stages... set
team-based standards". This is what the framework's own text says at this
level — it is not smoothed into a clean progression here, and the relevant
skill block below says so plainly.

**The skills this document assesses**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to ensure availability and capacity management
standards and procedures are correctly implemented, to identify capacity
issues, to implement whatever changes those issues require, and to initiate
remedial action rather than waiting for it to be requested.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. At the top of the ladder, owning and correctly
implementing standards across the organisation's services, and initiating
remedial action unprompted, is the expectation this wording sets — a
principal in the management track should be able to teach this to others, not
just do it themselves.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never been responsible for whether services meet availability
  or capacity targets.
- 1 — Beginner: can describe an availability or capacity standard, but has only
  followed one someone else wrote.
- 2 — Intermediate: has escalated a capacity problem to someone else to fix.
- 3 — Advanced: has independently identified and remediated a capacity issue
  for a service they own.
- 4 — Expert: owns the organisation's availability and capacity standard, has
  materially revised it, and coaches other leads and principals on
  implementing it correctly.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of an organisation-wide availability or capacity standard
  they own or have revised, and the impact of that change.
- Ask to see the standard or a document evidencing their ownership of it.
- In a structured interview, present a scenario where two teams' capacity
  needs conflict and ask how they would resolve it.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to set the strategy and manage resource allocation
for solution development programmes, and to work with client functions to
establish business requirements and to identify, propose, initiate and lead
these programmes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. This is programme- and strategy-scale wording — setting
strategy, managing resource allocation, and working with client functions to
establish business requirements are all things a principal in the management
track should be doing independently and should be able to lead others through.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never proposed or led a development programme spanning more
  than one team.
- 1 — Beginner: has contributed to a programme someone else set the strategy
  for.
- 2 — Intermediate: has led delivery of one workstream within a programme, with
  support setting its direction.
- 3 — Advanced: has independently proposed and led a programme scoped to their
  own area, with a measurable business result.
- 4 — Expert: has set the strategy and managed resource allocation for a
  solution development programme, working directly with a client function to
  establish the business requirements that shaped it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a specific programme they set the strategy for, including how
  resource allocation was decided and who the client function was.
- Ask to see a business case or programme proposal they authored.
- Ask a referee from outside their immediate team — ideally a client function
  or peer principal — how much of the programme's direction was genuinely set
  by this person.

### Skill: Information security

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to design, review and quality assure solutions and
services, ensuring they are specifically engineered to mitigate security
threats, and to lead others in using appropriate information security
practices.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Lead others in using appropriate information security
practices" is explicit teach-and-set-direction wording, matching the top of
the ladder.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never designed or reviewed a solution for security.
- 1 — Beginner: can name relevant threats but has not led a security review.
- 2 — Intermediate: reviews a colleague's solution for security with support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently reviews and quality assures solutions for
  security across their own team.
- 4 — Expert: sets the security practice other teams follow, and has changed
  how the organisation approaches a class of threat at scale.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a security practice or standard they set that other
  teams adopted.
- Ask to see a quality-assurance review of a solution's security design that
  they authored.
- In a structured interview, present a novel threat scenario and ask how they
  would lead the organisation's response.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to identify and apply modern development standards
to support complex projects and programmes, and to lead others in applying
them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. As at lead level, "lead others in applying" is explicit
teach-and-set-direction language; at principal, the expectation is that this
happens across programmes, not just a single project.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: does not know what "modern development standards" means for their
  technology area.
- 1 — Beginner: can name a standard but has only applied one someone else
  chose.
- 2 — Intermediate: applies standards reliably to their own work.
- 3 — Advanced: independently identifies and applies the right standards for a
  complex project.
- 4 — Expert: has introduced a standard adopted across multiple programmes, and
  can describe coaching several other leads or principals through it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a standard they introduced that spread beyond their
  own team.
- Ask to see a technical standard, RFC, or architecture guideline they
  authored that is now used organisation-wide.
- Ask a referee whether this person is who the organisation turns to for
  "what should our standard be" questions.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to collaborate with others when necessary to review
specifications, and to use agreed specifications to design, code, test and
document programs or scripts of medium-to-high complexity, using the right
standards and tools.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This is the anomaly flagged in `## About this role
level`: the wording here is the same as developer and senior developer
level — independent delivery against agreed specifications — not the
technical principal developer's "maintain technical responsibility for all
stages... set team-based standards". Do not inflate this to 4 just because the
level is the most senior on the ladder; the framework's own text for this
specific skill does not support it here.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot deliver a program or script from an agreed specification
  without significant support.
- 1 — Beginner: delivers small pieces of a specification under close
  direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: delivers medium-complexity work against a specification
  with some guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: independently designs, codes, tests and documents work of
  medium-to-high complexity from an agreed specification, using the right
  standards and tools without prompting.
- 4 — Expert: takes technical responsibility for a whole development project
  end to end and sets team-based standards for how others build — a step
  beyond what this level's own text claims, useful context if evidence shows
  it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for a work sample: a piece of medium-to-high complexity code they built
  from a specification, and walk through the design choices.
- Ask them to review a specification and flag gaps before building against it.
- Ask a referee whether this person still writes code personally, and to what
  standard.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to use a variety of prototyping methods, share best
practice and coach others, and look at strategic service design end to end.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Share best practice and coach others" and "strategic

### Skill: Service support

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to identify, locate and fix service faults.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This wording stays the same short, plain form used
across senior, lead and principal levels — the expectation is independent,
reliable fault resolution; the framework does not claim an expert or
teach-others dimension for this specific skill at this level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never diagnosed or fixed a service fault unsupervised.
- 1 — Beginner: fixes faults only when told exactly what to do.
- 2 — Intermediate: fixes straightforward faults with some support on
  diagnosis.
- 3 — Advanced: independently identifies, locates and fixes faults, including
  ones with an unclear root cause.
- 4 — Expert: is the person other teams escalate the hardest faults to, and has
  changed how the organisation diagnoses a class of fault — beyond what this
  level's own text claims, useful context if evidence shows it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a fault they diagnosed and fixed without being told
  what was wrong.
- Ask to see an incident record or postmortem they authored.
- Ask a referee how often this person is the one who ultimately resolves the
  hardest faults.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to design systems characterised by high levels of
risk, impact, and business or technical complexity; to control system design
practice within an enterprise or industry architecture; to influence
industry-based models for developing new technology applications; to develop
effective implementation and procurement strategies consistent with business
needs; and to ensure adherence to relevant technical strategies, policies,
standards and practices.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 4 — Expert. "Control system design practice within an enterprise or
industry architecture" and "influence industry-based models" are both
unambiguously expert, organisation- and industry-scale claims — the clearest
signal in this level's skill set of what "principal" means.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not designed a system beyond a component of managed
  complexity.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes to high-complexity system designs under direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: designs systems of managed complexity with support on
  standards.
- 3 — Advanced: independently designs systems of high risk, impact and
  complexity within an established architecture.
- 4 — Expert: controls system design practice across an enterprise
  architecture, has influenced how the wider industry approaches a class of
  system, and sets implementation and procurement strategy.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of enterprise architecture practice they set or
  controlled, and evidence of its adoption.
- Ask to see a procurement or implementation strategy document they
  authored.
- Ask a referee whether this person's design decisions have shaped practice
  beyond their own organisation — a conference talk, an open standard, an
  industry working group.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to define the integration build, co-ordinate build
activities across systems, and understand how to undertake and support
integration testing activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This wording is identical to lead and senior developer
level — co-ordinating across systems independently — and does not itself claim
a further teach-others or programme-wide dimension, so a baseline of 3 reflects
what the text actually supports rather than the level's general seniority.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not defined or co-ordinated an integration build.
- 1 — Beginner: has taken part in an integration build defined by someone
  else.
- 2 — Intermediate: co-ordinates a straightforward integration with support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently defines the integration build for work spanning
  multiple systems, and co-ordinates the activity across them.
- 4 — Expert: has set the integration approach for a programme spanning many
  systems and teams, adopted as the pattern others follow — beyond what this
  level's own text claims, useful context if evidence shows it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of an integration build they defined and co-ordinated
  across multiple systems or teams.
- Ask to see an integration test plan or build definition they wrote.
- Ask a referee how much of the co-ordination on a cross-system programme was
  actually driven by this person.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects you to collaborate with user researchers and represent
users internally, explain the difference between user needs and the desires of
the user, champion user research to focus on all users, prioritise and define
approaches to understanding the user story while guiding others in doing so,
and offer recommendations on the best tools and methods to use.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. This wording is unchanged from senior and lead level —
"guiding others" is present, but the organisation-wide championing seen in
other principal-level skills is not explicit here, so 3 reflects what the text
supports rather than the level's general seniority.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has never engaged with user research findings in their work.
- 1 — Beginner: reads user research someone else produced, without acting on
  it independently.
- 2 — Intermediate: uses user research to inform their own work, with support
  distinguishing needs from wants.
- 3 — Advanced: independently represents user needs in technical and
  programme-level decisions, and coaches others on distinguishing a user need
  from a stated desire.
- 4 — Expert: has championed user research at organisation scale, changing how
  multiple teams approach understanding the user story — beyond what this
  level's own text claims, useful context if evidence shows it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask for an example of a programme-level decision they changed because of
  user research.
- Ask to see a case where they distinguished a stated user desire from an
  underlying need, in their own words.
- Ask a referee whether this person's influence on user focus reaches beyond
  their immediate team.
