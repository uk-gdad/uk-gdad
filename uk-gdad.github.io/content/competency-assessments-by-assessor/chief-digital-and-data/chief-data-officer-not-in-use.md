# Chief Data Officer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for you if you are judging whether a candidate or an
employee has the abilities needed to do the job of chief data officer in the
UK Government Digital and Data profession — for a hiring panel, a promotion
conversation, an onboarding check, or a periodic capability review.

**One thing to know before you start.** The framework publishes this role and
describes what it involves, but it marks the role level itself as **not in
use**, and it publishes no expectation against any of the six skills it names
for it. That is the framework's own position, not a gap in this document. It
means there is no published baseline to measure a person against here. This
assessment therefore does something different from every other competency
assessment in this set: instead of comparing a person's rating to a target
figure, it gives you a structured way to record what a person's practice
actually looks like against the skill names the framework publishes, so that
the record is useful even though there is nothing to score it against.

This is one input to a much larger process. It is not the whole of a decision,
and it must never stand alone — see `## Advisory` below for why, and read that
section before you use anything else in this document.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
that the role's actual duties are fresh in your mind. Gather whatever evidence
exists ahead of time: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample
or task such as a strategy paper or a business case, direct observation of the
person operating at this level, or references from people who have worked with
them.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every entry in this record must trace
to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you
cannot point to evidence for an entry, record it as unknown rather than guess
— a chief data officer's real work happens in board rooms, one-to-one
conversations with senior leaders and cross-government forums that an assessor
rarely sees directly, so gaps in your evidence are normal and should be
recorded as gaps.

**Consistency.** Record practice the same way for every candidate you assess
for this role, and, especially for a hiring or promotion decision, have more
than one assessor complete this record independently before comparing notes.

**This is an unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument.** Used inconsistently, or by
a single assessor working from impression rather than evidence, it can produce
unlawful adverse impact under the Equality Act 2010. Consistent,
evidence-based administration, ideally by more than one assessor, materially
reduces that risk — see [Fair and lawful
use](../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
for the constraints that follow from this.

**A note on sensitivity.** Two of the six skills named for this role — Data
governance leadership and Data risk management — mean a candidate's answers
may touch live data risks, unresolved compliance issues or matters affecting
the organisation's own information assets. Do not record classified material,
the detail of a live risk, or anything that would itself create a disclosure
risk in the evidence log. Record that evidence exists and what it showed, not
the sensitive content itself.

## Rating scale

Every skill in this record is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.
Because no baseline is published for this role level, use the scale here to
describe the person's actual, observed practice rather than to compare that
practice against a target.

**A worked example**, using Strategic data planning, one of the six skills
this role names. Evidence at 1 — Beginner might be a candidate who can explain
what a data strategy is and why the organisation needs one, but has never
written or led one — a graduate trainee's placement report, say, rather than
anything they were accountable for. Evidence at 3 — Advanced might be a data
strategy document the candidate wrote and led the implementation of for a
mid-sized organisation, with senior leaders they can name as having secured
support from. Evidence at 4 — Expert might be a data strategy that
demonstrably shaped a whole organisation's direction over several years, that
other chief data officers or a cross-government forum have cited as an
example, and that the candidate can show they adapted as circumstances
changed rather than simply wrote once and left.

## About this role level

**The role: chief data officer.** A chief data officer ensures the
organisation can use data to make better decisions, policies and services.
They create an environment where data protection and privacy is balanced with
access, sharing and innovation. The framework describes the role's duties as:

- define a vision and strategy for data that enables the organisation to
  achieve its objectives and aligns with wider government data strategy
- lead the organisation in implementing the data strategy
- work with senior leaders to align priorities and secure their support
- determine the data and data technology that the organisation needs and the
  strategy for sourcing it
- ensure data can be easily used, reused and shared by those who need it
- improve the quality of data used across the organisation
- ensure the organisation uses data ethically and complies with data
  standards and regulations
- increase data literacy and ensure data is properly managed by coaching the
  whole organisation
- work with cyber security to ensure data is secure while staying accessible
  and controllable
- ensure the organisation has people with the level of data skills it needs

**The role level is published as NOT IN USE.** The framework lists chief data
officer in the chief digital and data profession, keeps the role description
above, and marks the level itself — and every skill expectation beneath it —
as not in use. There is no level above or below within this role to compare a
candidate's rating against, and no published wording for what any of the six
named skills should look like at this level.

**The six skills the framework names for this role**, with no expectation
published against any of them:

1. Capability building for digital, data and technology
2. Data architecture
3. Data governance leadership
4. Data risk management
5. Innovation in digital, data and technology
6. Strategic data planning

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Capability building for digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has built data or digital
  capability in others — no coaching relationship, no capability programme,
  no example of raising a team's or an organisation's data literacy.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe what building data capability
  involves, but the evidence shows only informal, occasional help to
  individual colleagues rather than a deliberate capability effort.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has run or contributed to a capability
  activity — a data literacy programme, a coaching relationship with a
  specific junior colleague, a recruitment or upskilling plan — with support
  from a learning and development function or a more senior sponsor.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently designed and delivered an
  organisation-wide effort to raise data literacy or close a data skills gap,
  can describe how they measured whether it worked, and can point to people
  whose capability grew because of it.
- 4 — Expert: The person has built data capability at a scale that changed how
  a whole organisation works with data over several years, is sought out by
  peers for that judgement, and can show a track record of developing people
  who have gone on to senior data roles themselves.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a specific data literacy or capability
  initiative they led, including how they knew whether it worked.
- Ask a referee to name someone whose data capability grew under the
  candidate's leadership, and how.
- Review a capability plan, business case or workforce plan the candidate
  authored or co-authored.

### Skill: Data architecture

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: The person cannot describe the organisation's data architecture,
  and has no evidence of having made or influenced a decision about what data
  technology an organisation needs or how it should be sourced.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can talk about data architecture in general terms
  — platforms, sourcing models, interoperability — but has not been
  accountable for a real architecture decision.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a decision about the data
  or data technology an organisation needs, working alongside specialist data
  architects, and can explain the reasoning behind at least one such decision.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently determined what data and data
  technology an organisation needs and set a sourcing strategy for it, and can
  explain how that architecture makes data easier to use, reuse and share
  across the organisation.
- 4 — Expert: The person sets the organisation's overall approach to data
  architecture, has shaped how a whole organisation's data platforms and
  sourcing decisions evolved over several years, and is sought out by peer
  organisations or specialist architects for that judgement.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a specific decision about what data
  technology to adopt or retire, and what alternatives they rejected and why.
- Review a business case, architecture decision record, or sourcing strategy
  document the candidate authored.
- Ask a specialist data architect who has worked with the candidate how much
  of the architecture direction came from them.

### Skill: Data governance leadership

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: The person shows no evidence of having led or shaped how an
  organisation governs its data — no policy, no board, no accountable
  ownership structure they were responsible for.
- 1 — Beginner: The person understands why data governance matters and can
  describe common governance structures, but has only followed governance set
  by others rather than led it.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has led a specific governance activity — for
  example, establishing accountable ownership for a data set, or chairing a
  data governance forum — with support from legal, compliance or a data
  protection officer.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently ensured, across a whole
  organisation, that data use is compliant with data standards and
  regulations and can point to a specific improvement in governance maturity
  they drove, from diagnosis to change.
- 4 — Expert: The person's data governance leadership has demonstrably shaped
  how a whole organisation, or several organisations, are held to account for
  their data, and other senior leaders look to them as the standard for how
  governance should be run.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a specific gap in data governance they found,
  what they did about it, and how they knew it had improved.
- Review a governance framework, terms of reference for a governance board, or
  an ethical use of data policy the candidate authored or sponsored.
- Ask the organisation's data protection officer or legal team how the
  candidate has engaged with data governance in practice.

### Skill: Data risk management

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: The person cannot name a data risk they have owned or mitigated,
  and shows no evidence of working with a cyber security or risk function.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe data risk concepts — accessibility
  versus control, for example — but has not personally been accountable for
  managing a data risk.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has worked with cyber security or a risk
  function to manage a specific data risk, with support, and can describe the
  trade-off between keeping data secure and keeping it accessible.
- 3 — Advanced: The person independently works with cyber security to ensure
  data stays secure while remaining accessible and controllable, can describe
  a risk they accepted, mitigated or escalated, and the reasoning behind that
  choice.
- 4 — Expert: The person sets the organisation's approach to balancing data
  security with data accessibility, has handled a genuinely difficult data
  risk decision at board level, and is sought out by peers for judgement on
  where that balance should sit.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a specific data risk they judged, including
  one they chose not to mitigate fully and why.
- Ask the organisation's cyber security lead or chief information security
  officer, if available, how the candidate has engaged with data risk in
  practice.
- Review a risk register entry or a risk-related paper the candidate authored,
  without recording the risk's live detail in this log.

### Skill: Innovation in digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: The person shows no evidence of having introduced a new way of
  working with data, and cannot describe an occasion where they balanced
  innovation against data protection and privacy.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe examples of data innovation elsewhere,
  but has no example of having enabled one themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has supported a data innovation — a new
  technique, tool or way of sharing data — introduced by someone else, and
  can describe what made it work or fail.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently enabled a specific data
  innovation in their organisation, and can describe how they balanced it
  against data protection, privacy and the organisation's appetite for risk.
- 4 — Expert: The person's approach to data innovation has demonstrably
  changed what an organisation is able to do, and they can describe how they
  built an environment — not just a single project — where safe innovation
  with data is the norm.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a data innovation they enabled, and what they
  changed about policy, process or culture to make it possible.
- Ask a referee whether the organisation's appetite for data innovation
  changed under the candidate's leadership.
- Review a business case or pilot evaluation for a data innovation the
  candidate sponsored.

### Skill: Strategic data planning

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: The person has no evidence of having written or led a data
  strategy, and cannot describe how a data strategy should align with an
  organisation's wider objectives.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can explain what a data strategy is and why an
  organisation needs one, but has never written or led one themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a data strategy, with
  support from a more senior sponsor, and can describe how it aligned with
  the organisation's objectives.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently defined a vision and strategy
  for data that aligns with wider government data strategy, led its
  implementation, and can name senior leaders whose support they secured for
  it.
- 4 — Expert: The person's data strategy has demonstrably shaped a whole
  organisation's direction over several years, has been cited by peers or a
  cross-government forum as a strong example, and the person can show how
  they adapted it as circumstances changed rather than leaving it static.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe their data strategy in two sentences, and
  name a senior leader outside the data function who could repeat it.
- Review a data strategy document the candidate authored or led.
- Ask a senior stakeholder how the candidate secured their support for the
  strategy, and what changed as a result of it.
