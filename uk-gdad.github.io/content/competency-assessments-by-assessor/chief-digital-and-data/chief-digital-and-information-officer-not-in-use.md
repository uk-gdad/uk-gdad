# Chief Digital and Information Officer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for you if you are judging whether a candidate or an
employee has the abilities needed to do the job of chief digital and
information officer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — for a
hiring panel, a promotion conversation, an onboarding check, or a periodic
capability review.

**One thing to know before you start.** The framework publishes this role and
describes what it involves, but it marks the role level itself as **not in
use**, and it publishes no expectation against any of the eight skills it
names for it. That is the framework's own position, not a gap in this
document. It means there is no published baseline to measure a person against
here. This assessment therefore does something different from every other
competency assessment in this set: instead of comparing a person's rating to
a target figure, it gives you a structured way to record what a person's
practice actually looks like against the skill names the framework publishes,
so that the record is useful even though there is nothing to score it
against.

This is one input to a much larger process. It is not the whole of a
decision, and it must never stand alone — see `## Advisory` below for why, and
read that section before you use anything else in this document.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
that the role's actual duties are fresh in your mind. Gather whatever evidence
exists ahead of time: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample
or task such as a strategy or investment paper, direct observation of the
person operating at this level, or references from people who have worked
with them.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every entry in this record must
trace to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If
you cannot point to evidence for an entry, record it as unknown rather than
guess — a chief digital and information officer's real work happens in board
rooms, investment negotiations and cross-government forums that an assessor
rarely sees directly, so gaps in your evidence are normal and should be
recorded as gaps.

**Consistency.** Record practice the same way for every candidate you assess
for this role, and, especially for a hiring or promotion decision, have more
than one assessor complete this record independently before comparing notes.

**This is an unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument.** Used inconsistently, or by
a single assessor working from impression rather than evidence, it can
produce unlawful adverse impact under the Equality Act 2010. Consistent,
evidence-based administration, ideally by more than one assessor, materially
reduces that risk — see [Fair and lawful
use](../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
for the constraints that follow from this.

**A note on sensitivity.** The skill Ensuring resilience of digital, data and
technology means a candidate's answers may touch how the organisation defends
itself against cyber threats and attacks. Do not record the detail of a live
vulnerability, an unresolved security gap, or anything that would itself
create a disclosure risk in the evidence log. Record that evidence exists and
what it showed, not the sensitive content itself.

## Rating scale

Every skill in this record is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.
Because no baseline is published for this role level, use the scale here to
describe the person's actual, observed practice rather than to compare that
practice against a target.

**A worked example**, using Leading digital products and services, one of the
eight skills this role names. Evidence at 1 — Beginner might be a candidate
who can describe what oversight of digital solutions involves, but has only
ever contributed to a single product team rather than overseen a portfolio.
Evidence at 3 — Advanced might be a candidate who has overseen the delivery,
management and maintenance of a set of digital services for a mid-sized
organisation, and can describe how they balanced strategic goals against user
needs when the two pulled in different directions. Evidence at 4 — Expert
might be a candidate whose oversight of an organisation's digital products and
services demonstrably changed how reliably and sustainably they were run over
several years, and who other chief digital and information officers or a
cross-government forum have cited as an example.

## About this role level

**The role: chief digital and information officer.** The chief digital and
information officer shapes and enables the delivery of the organisation's
strategy through digital, data and technology. They develop partnerships
across the organisation to design and deliver better outcomes that make lives
easier. The framework describes the role's duties as:

- lead innovative strategies that deliver the organisation's objectives
- identify and act on the changing landscape of digital, data and technology
- enable the use of innovative approaches and emerging technologies whilst
  ensuring inclusivity, security and ethical practices
- oversee the delivery, management and maintenance of sustainable digital
  solutions that meet strategic goals and user needs
- ensure data and technology architecture decisions support scalability,
  reusability and interoperability across government
- ensure the organisation remains safe, secure and resilient to cyber threats
  and attacks
- negotiate and secure investment for digital, data and technology
- advise on investment priorities across competing demands to maximise
  benefits and outcomes
- ensure the organisation anticipates and adapts to changes in capability
  needs
- be a trusted advisor to the board on all matters of digital, data and
  technology

**The role level is published as NOT IN USE.** The framework lists chief
digital and information officer in the chief digital and data profession,
keeps the role description above, and marks the level itself — and every
skill expectation beneath it — as not in use. There is no level above or
below within this role to compare a candidate's rating against, and no
published wording for what any of the eight named skills should look like at
this level.

**The eight skills the framework names for this role**, with no expectation
published against any of them:

1. Capability building for digital, data and technology
2. Ensuring resilience of digital, data and technology
3. Financial management for digital, data and technology
4. Innovation in digital, data and technology
5. Leading digital products and services
6. Making strategic performance-led change
7. Optimising technology and data architecture
8. Shaping the direction of organisational strategy through digital, data and
   technology

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Capability building for digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has planned for or built digital,
  data or technology capability — no workforce plan, no capability review, no
  example of anticipating a future skills gap.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe why capability planning matters, but
  the evidence shows only reactive responses to skills gaps as they appeared,
  rather than anticipation.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a capability plan or
  workforce review, with support from a learning and development or HR
  function, and can describe a specific capability gap it addressed.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently ensured their organisation
  anticipates and adapts to changes in its digital, data and technology
  capability needs, and can point to a specific future gap they identified
  before it became a problem.
- 4 — Expert: The person's capability planning has demonstrably shaped how an
  organisation's digital, data and technology workforce evolved over several
  years, and other senior leaders look to them for judgement on where
  capability needs are heading next.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a capability gap they anticipated before it
  became urgent, and what they did about it.
- Review a workforce plan or capability strategy the candidate authored or
  sponsored.
- Ask a referee how the organisation's digital, data and technology
  capability changed under the candidate's leadership.

### Skill: Ensuring resilience of digital, data and technology

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: The person cannot describe how their organisation protects itself
  against cyber threats or disruption, and shows no evidence of having been
  accountable for resilience.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe what resilience involves in general
  terms, but has not personally been accountable for a resilience decision.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a resilience or security
  improvement, working with a security function, and can describe a specific
  threat or disruption it addressed.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently ensured their organisation
  remains safe, secure and resilient to cyber threats and attacks, and can
  describe a real incident or near-miss and how the organisation's resilience
  held up or was strengthened as a result.
- 4 — Expert: The person's approach to resilience has demonstrably reduced an
  organisation's exposure to cyber threats over time, they have led the
  organisation through a genuine security incident at board level, and other
  senior leaders look to them for judgement on resilience investment.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a resilience or security investment decision
  they made, and what trade-off it required against other priorities.
- Ask the organisation's chief information security officer, if available,
  how the candidate has engaged with resilience in practice.
- Review a resilience strategy or post-incident review the candidate authored
  or sponsored, without recording live vulnerability detail in this log.

### Skill: Making strategic performance-led change

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: There is no evidence the person has led a strategy or a change
  effort tied to measurable organisational objectives.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can describe how strategy and change should be
  linked to performance, but has not led a strategy or a change effort
  themselves.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to a strategic change effort,
  with support from a more senior sponsor, and can describe how progress was
  measured.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently led an innovative strategy that
  delivered the organisation's objectives, and can describe the performance
  measures they used to know whether the change had worked.
- 4 — Expert: The person's strategic, performance-led change has
  demonstrably shifted a whole organisation's direction and measurable
  outcomes over several years, and they can show how they adapted the
  strategy as circumstances changed rather than leaving it static.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a strategy they led, the objective it was
  meant to deliver, and the evidence that it did.
- Review a strategy document or performance report the candidate authored or
  led.
- Ask a senior stakeholder what changed in the organisation as a result of
  the candidate's strategy.

### Skill: Optimising technology and data architecture

**No expectation is published for this skill at this level.**

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: The person cannot describe how their organisation's technology
  and data architecture decisions relate to scalability, reusability or
  interoperability, and shows no evidence of having shaped one.
- 1 — Beginner: The person can talk about architecture principles in general
  terms, but has not been accountable for a real architecture decision.
- 2 — Intermediate: The person has contributed to an architecture decision,
  working alongside specialist architects, and can explain how it supported
  scalability, reusability or interoperability.
- 3 — Advanced: The person has independently ensured that technology and data
  architecture decisions support scalability, reusability and
  interoperability across government, and can point to a specific decision

  architects or peer organisations look to them for judgement on this.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an architecture decision and the trade-off it
  required between scalability, reusability and interoperability.
- Review an architecture decision record, strategy or cross-government
  interoperability proposal the candidate authored or sponsored.
- Ask a specialist architect who has worked with the candidate how much of

pass or fail scorecard.** It records what you observed, skill by skill,
against the framework's own names for those skills, and nothing more. Do not
write "meets baseline" or "does not meet baseline" anywhere in this document

publishes for this role level.

the role's actual duties (in `## About this role level`), noticing where the
evidence is strong and where it is thin, and giving the organisation's own
process — which may set its own local expectations for this role — something
