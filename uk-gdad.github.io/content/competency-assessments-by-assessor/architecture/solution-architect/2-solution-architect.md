# Solution Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the assessing — a line manager, a
hiring panel member, or a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or employee
possesses the baseline abilities the solution architect role level requires.
A solution architect is responsible for a single solution, usually working
independently on solutions where risk is low, and often supports or
contributes to work led by more senior solution architects.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then complete
the overall determination. This assessment is one input among several into a
hiring, promotion or development decision, never the whole of it — see
`## Advisory`.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before scoring, so you are
assessing against this level's own accountabilities — independent work on a
single, low-risk solution — rather than associate solution architect below it
or senior solution architect above it. Gather whatever evidence exists first:
a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task, direct
observation, or references.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, and
where possible have a second assessor score independently before you compare
notes — this matters most for a hiring or promotion decision, and materially
reduces the risk of an unlawful adverse impact from an unvalidated,
AI-drafted instrument used inconsistently.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence — see
[Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

A worked example, using **Making architectural decisions**:

- A candidate rated **1 — Beginner** describes the reasoning behind decisions
  someone else made, but has not yet contributed to making one.
- A candidate rated **2 — Intermediate** works with others to make
  architectural decisions of managed risk and complexity for their solution,
  with support available for the harder judgement calls.
- A candidate rated **3 — Advanced** makes and guides architectural decisions
  of medium risk and complexity unsupported, and addresses risks affecting
  more than one team — the bar for senior solution architect, above this
  level.

## About this role level

**The role: solution architect.** A solution architect designs solutions for
problems that affect the organisation. In this role, a person will:

- ensure a problem and the desired outcomes are properly defined
- ensure the scope of a solution meets the organisation's requirements
- stay up to date on technology trends and approaches
- understand organisational objectives and external drivers, for example,
  legislation or financial constraints
- work with others to develop business and technical strategies
- work within business and technical constraints
- design and document solutions so they can be implemented by the
  organisation
- comply with standards and governance
- communicate and work effectively with stakeholders
- manage risks and decisions in a transparent way

**This level: solution architect.** A solution architect is responsible for a
single solution. They usually work independently on solutions where risk is
low, and often support or contribute to work led by more senior solution
architects.

**Where this level sits.** The solution architect role has five levels:

1. Associate solution architect
2. **Solution architect** — this level
3. Senior solution architect
4. Lead solution architect
5. Principal solution architect

Four skills carry framework wording identical between this level and senior
solution architect above it — Architect for the whole context, Architecture
communication, Problem definition and shaping, and Strategy design — an
assessor should note this and use the "at this role level" accountabilities
(single, low-risk solution here; more complex, moderate-risk solution above)
to judge whether evidence reflects this level's scope rather than
over-crediting text shared with the level above. Making architectural
decisions and Community collaboration change in wording between the two
levels; Commercial perspective and Technical design throughout the life cycle
change relative to associate solution architect below.

**Accountabilities at this level.** The framework expects a solution
architect to:

- build relationships with stakeholders across different business or
  technical areas in the organisation
- be proactive in identifying opportunities to improve the organisation
- follow best practice for solution design
- use emerging technologies and approaches
- help your team achieve its objective

**The skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Making architectural decisions
6. Problem definition and shaping
7. Strategy design
8. Technical design throughout the life cycle

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What the framework expects at this level**

Align your work with the work being done by other architects and technical
professionals. Track emerging issues, strategies, roadmaps, patterns and
technologies over time to assess opportunities and risks to your work.
Identify how other teams contribute to delivering outcomes through change.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. Aligning and tracking are active, ongoing
practices applied to a single solution — a step beyond associate's
"identify relevant information" — but this level does not yet act on
organisation-wide trends independently.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot describe how their solution relates to other teams'
  architectural work.
- 1 — Beginner: Identifies relevant strategies and roadmaps when pointed to
  them, but needs help aligning their own work to them.
- 2 — Intermediate: Actively aligns their solution's design with other
  architects' work and tracks relevant emerging issues, with support
  available for judging opportunity or risk from them.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently tracks and assesses emerging issues across
  more than one solution, and identifies risks to their work before they are
  pointed out — not yet expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe a time you aligned your
  solution's design with work another architect or team was doing. How did
  you find out about it?"
- A work sample: give a short description of a solution and ask what other
  teams' work the candidate would check before finalising the design.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

Lead the communication of complicated, complex or risky architecture topics
with technical and non-technical stakeholders. Communicate with senior
stakeholders across your organisation. Adapt your message and communication
techniques to your audience. Advocate on behalf of a team to other
stakeholders. Manage stakeholder expectations effectively.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This text is ambitious and is shared, unchanged,
with senior solution architect above; read against this level's own scope —
a single, low-risk solution — the realistic bar is leading communication for
that one solution with support, not yet across multiple complex programmes.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not led a stakeholder conversation about an architectural
  topic.
- 1 — Beginner: Communicates architectural topics to a familiar audience, but
  needs support with senior or unfamiliar stakeholders.
- 2 — Intermediate: Leads communication of their solution's architecture to
  technical and non-technical stakeholders, adapting the message to the
  audience, with support for the more contested conversations.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently manages stakeholder expectations for
  complicated or risky topics across more than one solution — the bar for
  senior solution architect, above this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe leading a conversation about
  your solution's architecture with a senior stakeholder. What did you
  adapt for that audience?"
- A portfolio item: an architecture representation or briefing the candidate
  produced for a mixed technical and non-technical audience.
- A reference question: "Has this person advocated for their solution to a
  stakeholder who was initially resistant?"

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What the framework expects at this level**

Understand commercial processes and the appropriate internal contacts within
a government department. Understand different sourcing strategies and when
to apply them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The move from associate's "awareness" to
"understand... and when to apply them" is a real step up, though this level
is not yet expected to select and evaluate suppliers itself.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot name a sourcing strategy or an internal commercial
  contact relevant to their work.
- 1 — Beginner: Aware that commercial processes and sourcing strategies
  exist, but needs help judging which applies.
- 2 — Intermediate: Knows the appropriate internal commercial contacts for
  their work and can say which sourcing strategy applies to a given
  situation, with support for the less common cases.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "For your last solution, which sourcing
  strategy applied, and who were the right internal commercial contacts?"

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level**

Contribute to the work of others. Motivate and empower teams. Create the
right environment for teams to work in, and can identify the best team
makeup depending on the situation. Recognise and deal with issues.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This is a step up from associate's
"understanding" of team dynamics to actively motivating teams and dealing
with issues, though on the scale of a single solution rather than across
multiple teams.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not contributed to motivating or shaping a team's working
  environment.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes constructively to a team but has not yet taken
  action to motivate others or resolve an issue.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes to others' work, helps create a positive
  working environment for their team, and recognises and deals with
  straightforward issues, with support for harder ones.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe an issue you noticed in how a
  team was working together. What did you do?"
- A reference question: "Has this person helped motivate a team, or improve
  how it works, beyond their own individual contribution?"

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

Work with others to make architectural design decisions characterised by
managed levels of risk and complexity. Identify and address architectural
risks relevant to your team or domain, for example, business, data, or
security. Engage with architectural governance and assurance to effectively
manage decisions and risks, with support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework's own words — "with support" — set
this level's ceiling explicitly at Intermediate for this skill.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not contributed to an architectural decision beyond
  describing one someone else made.
- 1 — Beginner: Contributes to decisions when directed, and needs help
  identifying risks relevant to their domain.
- 2 — Intermediate: Works with others to make decisions of managed risk and
  complexity, identifies risks relevant to their own solution, and engages
  with governance processes with support.
- 3 — Advanced: Makes and guides decisions unsupported and addresses risks
  affecting multiple teams — the bar for senior solution architect, above
  this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe an architectural decision you
  helped make for your solution. What risk did you identify, and what did
  you do about it?"
- A document review: a decision record or architecture governance submission
  the candidate contributed to.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What the framework expects at this level**

Frame a problem characterised by medium complexity, complication, or risk so
that a solution can be created. Produce architectural representations that
enable different teams to have a shared understanding of problems throughout
the life cycle. Describe options for solving problems so that appropriate
delivery methods can be decided.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This text is identical to senior solution
architect's; read against this level's single-solution scope, the realistic
bar is framing a medium-complexity problem for one solution with occasional
support, not yet leading problem-framing across a programme.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not independently framed a problem for a real solution.
- 1 — Beginner: Frames a problem with help, and needs support producing
  representations others can follow.
- 2 — Intermediate: Frames a medium-complexity problem for their own
  solution, produces representations that different teams can understand,
  and describes solution options, with occasional support.
- 3 — Advanced: Leads problem framing for high-complexity or high-risk
  problems and coaches others in it — the bar for lead solution architect,
  well above this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: a problem definition or option-appraisal document the
  candidate produced.
- A structured interview question: "Describe a problem you framed for your
  solution. What options did you describe, and how did you choose between
  them?"

### Skill: Strategy design

**What the framework expects at this level**

Support the development of a strategy or vision that aligns with
organisational objectives. Challenge requirements and assumptions, and
identify opportunities to develop strategy. Support the implementation of a
strategy or vision, for example, by using a roadmap or plan. Use
architectural principles, patterns, and constraints when appropriate.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. "Support" is the operative word — this level
contributes to strategy someone else owns, a step up from associate's purely
explanatory bar but short of independent strategy design.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not contributed to a strategy or vision document.
- 1 — Beginner: Explains how organisational objectives link to strategy, but
  has not yet contributed to developing one.
- 2 — Intermediate: Supports the development and implementation of a
  strategy or vision, challenges a requirement or assumption at least once,
  and uses architectural principles and patterns appropriately.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe a time you challenged a
  requirement or assumption while supporting a strategy or vision. What
  happened?"
- A document review: a roadmap, plan, or strategy document the candidate
  contributed to.

### Skill: Technical design throughout the life cycle

**What the framework expects at this level**

Create technical designs characterised by managed levels of risk, impact, and
complexity. Provide guidance and support to teams using technical designs
throughout the life cycle. Adapt a technical design if needed during
delivery. Work with well-understood technology and identify appropriate
patterns.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework repeats associate solution
architect's wording for this skill word for word — record that rather than
raising the number on assumption. This level's independence comes from
owning a whole solution rather than supporting one, which the assessor should
look for in the evidence even though the skill text itself has not changed.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not produced a technical design used in real delivery.
- 1 — Beginner: Produces a technical design with close supervision, and
  needs help adapting it when circumstances change.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently creates a technical design for
  well-understood technology and managed risk, owning the whole solution
  rather than a supporting part of it, and adapts it during delivery with
  occasional support.
- 3 — Advanced: Maintains quality and coherence of a technical design in
  response to change, unsupported, for medium risk and complexity — the bar
  for senior solution architect, above this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: a technical design the candidate owned for a real
  solution, from initial design through to delivery.
- A structured interview question: "Describe adapting your technical design
  during delivery. What forced the change, and what did you do?"
