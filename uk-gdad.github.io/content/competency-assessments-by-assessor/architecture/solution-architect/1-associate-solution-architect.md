# Associate Solution Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the assessing — a line manager, a
hiring panel member, or a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or employee
possesses the baseline abilities the associate solution architect role level
requires. An associate solution architect supports other solution architects
to design solutions, usually working under supervision, and helps their team
achieve its objectives. This is the entry level of a five-level ladder.

Work through the competency matrix below, one skill at a time, then complete
the overall determination. This assessment is one input among several into a
hiring, promotion or development decision, never the whole of it — see
`## Advisory`.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you are assessing against this level's own accountabilities — supported work
under supervision — and not against solution architect above it. Gather
whatever evidence exists first: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a
work sample or task, direct observation, or references. For this level, a
training exercise or a supervised real task is legitimate evidence; do not
expect the unsupervised track record a more senior level would show.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, and
where possible have a second assessor score independently before you compare
notes — this matters most for a hiring or promotion decision, and materially
reduces the risk of an unlawful adverse impact from an unvalidated,
AI-drafted instrument used inconsistently.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than
demonstrated, job-related evidence — see
[Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

A worked example, using **Technical design throughout the life cycle**:

- A candidate rated **1 — Beginner** has produced a technical design for a
  training exercise or a small, well-understood real task, with a supervisor
  checking the result before it was used.
- A candidate rated **2 — Intermediate** has created a technical design of
  managed risk and complexity for a real piece of work, and adapted it during
  delivery when circumstances changed, with support available.
- A candidate rated **3 — Advanced** independently produces technical designs
  for real work and supports other people in using them throughout delivery
  — a bar this level is not yet expected to meet.

## About this role level

**The role: solution architect.** A solution architect designs solutions for
problems that affect the organisation. In this role, a person will:

- ensure a problem and the desired outcomes are properly defined
- ensure the scope of a solution meets the organisation's requirements
- stay up to date on technology trends and approaches
- understand organisational objectives and external drivers, for example,
  legislation or financial constraints
- work with others to develop business and technical strategies
- work within business and technical constraints
- design and document solutions so they can be implemented by the
  organisation
- comply with standards and governance
- communicate and work effectively with stakeholders
- manage risks and decisions in a transparent way

**This level: associate solution architect.** An associate solution architect
supports other solution architects to design solutions, usually working under
supervision.

**Where this level sits.** The solution architect role has five levels:

1. **Associate solution architect** — this level
2. Solution architect
3. Senior solution architect
4. Lead solution architect
5. Principal solution architect

The step up to solution architect is independence: the framework moves this
level's supporting, help-framed language ("help to frame a problem", "help
your team achieve its objectives") to owning a single solution and building
stakeholder relationships without close supervision.

**Accountabilities at this level.** The framework expects an associate
solution architect to:

- support the design of solutions by working with stakeholders
- help your team achieve its objectives

**The skills this assessment covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Making architectural decisions
6. Problem definition and shaping
7. Strategy design
8. Technical design throughout the life cycle

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What the framework expects at this level**

Identify relevant information that can inform your architectural work, such
as strategies, roadmaps, policies and technical trends. Understand how your
work supports the team in enabling change.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework asks the person to identify relevant
information and understand their place in the team's wider change — awareness
and connection, not yet independent judgement about what matters.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot say what information — a strategy, a roadmap, a policy —
  would be relevant to a piece of architectural work.
- 1 — Beginner: Identifies relevant strategies, roadmaps or policies when
  pointed toward them, and can explain in general terms how their work
  supports the team's change.
- 2 — Intermediate: Finds relevant information independently for
  straightforward work, with some guidance on less obvious sources.
- 3 — Advanced: Independently identifies the full range of context that
  bears on a piece of work, including material that is not the obvious
  first place to look.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a piece of work you
  supported. What information — a strategy, a roadmap, a policy — did you
  look for, and where did you find it?"
- A work sample: give a short scenario and ask the candidate to list the
  kinds of information they would want before starting.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

Listen to the needs of technical and business stakeholders. Create and use
different architecture representations to communicate effectively, achieving
agreement with technical and non-technical stakeholders. Provide support in
discussions about architectural topics within a multidisciplinary team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. "Provide support in discussions" is a supporting,
close-supervision bar; the framework does not yet expect this level to lead
or own a stakeholder conversation.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not created or used an architecture diagram or similar
  representation.
- 1 — Beginner: Listens to stakeholder needs and can create a simple
  architecture representation with guidance, and contributes to team
  discussions when supported.
- 2 — Intermediate: Creates representations that achieve agreement with a
  stakeholder on a straightforward topic, with occasional support.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: a diagram or representation the candidate created,
  reviewed for whether a non-specialist could follow it.
- A structured interview question: "Describe a discussion where you helped
  a stakeholder understand an architectural topic. What did you use to
  explain it?"

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

Describe the reasoning behind architectural design decisions. Gather
information to inform decisions. Understand architectural governance and
assurance relevant to your work.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. This level describes and gathers rather than makes
decisions — a supporting, close-supervision role in the decision, not an
independent one.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Cannot explain why a design decision was made on a piece of work
  they were involved in.
- 1 — Beginner: Describes the reasoning behind a decision made by someone
  else, and gathers information for a decision when asked.
- 2 — Intermediate: Gathers information and contributes reasoning to a
  decision with some independence, and understands the governance process
  relevant to their work.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe an architectural decision you
  were involved in. What information did you gather, and how did that
  inform the reasoning?"
- A work sample: give a short scenario and ask what information the
  candidate would gather before a decision could be made.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What the framework expects at this level**

Help to frame a problem characterised by managed levels of complexity,
complication, or risk so that a solution can be created. Help to create
options for solving problems at an appropriate level of detail.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 1 — Beginner. The framework's own word is "help" — supporting
someone else's framing and option-creation, not owning it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not contributed to framing a problem or creating solution
  options.
- 1 — Beginner: Helps frame a straightforward problem and helps generate
  options, under supervision.
- 2 — Intermediate: Contributes independently to framing a managed-
  complexity problem, with a supervisor reviewing the result.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe a problem you helped frame. What
  was your contribution, and what did a more senior colleague add?"
- A work sample: present a simple, real-shaped problem and ask the candidate
  to help outline it and suggest an option.

### Skill: Technical design throughout the life cycle

**What the framework expects at this level**

Create technical designs characterised by managed levels of risk, impact, and
complexity. Provide guidance and support to teams using technical designs
throughout the life cycle. Adapt a technical design if needed during
delivery. Work with well-understood technology and identify appropriate
patterns.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. This bullet uses stronger, doing verbs than the
rest of this level's skill set — "create", "provide guidance", "adapt" — on
work that is already scoped to managed risk and well-understood technology,
which is a genuine Intermediate bar even at associate level.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: Has not produced a technical design used in real delivery.
- 1 — Beginner: Produces a technical design for managed-risk work with close
  supervision, and needs help adapting it when circumstances change.
- 2 — Intermediate: Independently creates a technical design for
  well-understood technology and managed risk, provides basic guidance to
  the team using it, and adapts it during delivery with occasional support.
- 3 — Advanced: Not expected at this level.
- 4 — Expert: Not expected at this level.

**Evidence to gather**

- A portfolio item: a technical design the candidate produced for real or
  training work.
- A structured interview question: "Describe a time your technical design
  had to change during delivery. What prompted the change, and what did you
  do?"
- A work sample: ask the candidate to sketch a technical design for a
  described, well-understood system.
