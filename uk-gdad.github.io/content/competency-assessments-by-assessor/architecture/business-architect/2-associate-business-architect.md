# Associate Business Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a
reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee has the baseline
abilities the **associate business architect** role level requires, under
the UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) Profession Capability Framework.
Use it when hiring into an associate business architect post, when checking
readiness to move up from associate to business architect, or as a periodic
capability check for someone already in the role.

It measures demonstrated skill against the framework's own description of
this specific role level. It is one input among several into that decision —
see Advisory below for why it must never stand alone.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything, so
you are judging the person against what an associate business architect
actually does — someone applying their knowledge with growing independence,
still supported and trained — rather than against the fully independent
business architect above them. Gather whatever evidence exists before you
start: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task,
direct observation, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, using
the same evidence sources where possible. For a hiring or promotion decision,
have more than one assessor score independently before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** This is an unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument. Used
inconsistently, it can produce unlawful adverse impact. Applying it the same
way to everyone, and having more than one assessor involved in a hiring or
promotion decision, materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be
influenced by anything other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same four-point scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 1 | Awareness | You can describe skill fundamentals and show basic knowledge of related tools |
| 2 | Working | You can apply the skill with some support and use appropriate tools |
| 3 | Practitioner | You can apply the skill independently, determine the best tools, and share your experience |
| 4 | Expert | You can lead organisational best practice and teach advanced techniques |

This is a **proficiency** scale, not a frequency scale: it rates how well the
person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example — Stakeholder relationship management.** A candidate rated 1
would only identify stakeholders when told who they are. A candidate rated 2
(the baseline expected at this level) identifies the important stakeholders
for a piece of work unprompted, communicates with them clearly and regularly,
and tailors that communication to what each stakeholder needs — with some
support building consensus where views differ. A candidate rated 3 would
already be resolving disagreement between stakeholders and building
relationships proactively with little support, which is more than this level
expects and is worth noting as a strength.

## About this role level

**Architecture role: Business architect.** A business architect develops an
integrated view of an enterprise, such as a business area or organisation,
and uses that view to help their organisation put its strategy in place.

In this role, a business architect will:

- understand and apply business architectural principles, techniques, and
  tools to business challenges
- ensure design work from projects and programmes aligns with strategy
- ensure business change is implemented effectively to meet strategy
- aggregate and disaggregate complex information from across the organisation
- communicate complex information about the organisation to stakeholders

**Role level: Associate business architect.** This is the second of four
levels in the business architect role, in order: trainee business architect,
associate business architect, business architect, and lead business
architect. An associate business architect uses their knowledge and
experience to address business scenarios, and continues to get support and
training for their role.

At this role level, an associate business architect will:

- apply architectural methods following direction and wider strategy to
  produce outputs
- break down complexity and produce clear outputs
- explain complex business problems using clear language and artefacts
- bring together disconnected information to inform decision making
- recognise issues that cut across different areas of the organisation and
  present opportunities for improvement

The framework names seven skills for this role level: Business architecture,
Communicating information, Consultancy, Making and guiding decisions, Methods
and tools, Stakeholder relationship management, and Strategic thinking.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Business architecture

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate business architect helps support the adoption of business
architecture, clearly communicates business architecture frameworks,
principles and techniques, supports the production of a multidimensional
view of the organisation, and assesses the impact of strategies on the
organisation, highlighting risks and issues.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. The framework moves this level from a trainee's
awareness under guidance to actively supporting adoption and assessing
impact — real application of the skill, but still support to the wider
practice rather than leading it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: explains a framework or technique when asked, but needs
  guidance to apply it or to assess impact.
- 2 — Working: communicates frameworks and techniques clearly, supports
  the production of a multidimensional view of the organisation, and
  highlights obvious risks and issues from a strategy's impact with some
  guidance.
- 3 — Practitioner: independently assesses the impact of a strategy on the
  organisation and identifies risks and issues others have missed.
- 4 — Expert: develops the organisation's business architecture practice and
  coaches others in applying it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give the candidate a short strategy summary and ask them to identify one or
  two risks or issues it might create for the organisation.
- A structured interview question: "Tell us about a time you explained a
  business architecture concept to someone who was not familiar with it. How
  did you make it clear?"
- If already in post, review a multidimensional view or artefact — a
  capability map, a value stream diagram — the person contributed to.

- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Communicating information

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate business architect works collaboratively in a group and builds
relationships with others, identifies issues through Agile "health checks"
and works with others to address them, manages stakeholder expectations, is
flexible and capable of proactive and reactive communication, and hosts or
moderates difficult discussions within the team or with senior stakeholders.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. The framework's own wording goes further than most
other skills at this level — hosting or moderating difficult discussions
with senior stakeholders is a real facilitation skill, not a supported task,
so the baseline sits above the level's general 2.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: works collaboratively in routine situations but needs
  support managing expectations or handling a difficult conversation.
- 2 — Working: builds working relationships, manages stakeholder
  expectations in most situations, and identifies issues through team health
  checks with some guidance on how to address them.
- 3 — Practitioner: is flexible between proactive and reactive communication as
  the situation demands, and hosts or moderates a difficult discussion with
  senior stakeholders competently.
- 4 — Expert: is sought out to lead the most difficult stakeholder
  conversations in the organisation and coaches others in doing the same.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about a difficult discussion you
  hosted or moderated, including with someone senior to you. What made it
  difficult, and what did you do?"
- Ask a referee: "How did this person handle disagreement or tension within
  the team?"
- If already in post, observe the person facilitating or contributing to a
  team health check or retrospective.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Consultancy

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate business architect provides advice, guidance and
recommendations based on their specialist knowledge and experience, proposes
methodologies to follow and approaches to implementation, and frames problems
so they can easily be understood, troubleshooting where required to support
the business in operating more effectively.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. This level moves from a trainee's guided,
precedent-referencing advice to advice grounded in the person's own
specialist knowledge — a real step up, but not yet the significant,
organisation-wide specialist knowledge expected further up the role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: gives advice mainly by referring to precedent, and needs
  support to propose a methodology or approach.
- 2 — Working: gives advice and recommendations grounded in their own
  knowledge and experience, proposes a suitable methodology, and frames a
  moderately complex problem clearly.
- 3 — Practitioner: gives well-reasoned advice on complex problems unprompted and
  troubleshoots effectively when things go wrong.
- 4 — Expert: is the organisation's go-to source of advice on business
  architecture problems and defines methodology for others to follow.

**Evidence to gather**

- Give the candidate a short, realistic business problem and ask them to
  frame it and propose an approach.
- A structured interview question: "Describe a piece of advice you gave that
  changed how a problem was approached."
- If already in post, review a recommendation document or methodology
  proposal the person has produced.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Making and guiding decisions

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate business architect makes decisions characterised by managed
levels of risk and complexity, recommends decisions as risk and complexity
increase, and resolves technical disputes between wider peers and indirect
stakeholders, considering all views and opinions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. The framework has this level making decisions
directly for the first time, provided the risk and complexity are managed —
a genuine increase in independence from a trainee's recommend-only remit.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: makes low-risk decisions alone but escalates as soon as risk
  or complexity rises, without judging whether escalation is needed.
- 2 — Working: makes decisions with managed risk and complexity, judges
  correctly when to recommend rather than decide as risk rises, and resolves
  disputes between wider peers by weighing the views involved.
- 3 — Practitioner: makes sound decisions on higher-risk, more complex questions
  with little support.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted with the organisation's most complex, highest-risk
  decisions in this area.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about a decision you made where
  getting it wrong would have mattered. How did you decide, and how did you
  know it was yours to decide?"
- Ask a referee: "Can you describe a dispute between colleagues this person
  helped resolve?"
- If already in post, review a documented decision, with its reasoning, that
  the person owned.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Methods and tools

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate business architect selects and uses the most appropriate tools
and techniques to support the planning, analysis, development, testing,
implementation and improvement of systems and services within a project.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. This level selects the method or tool rather
than following advice on which to use, as a trainee would — a real move to
independent judgement within the scope of a single project.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: selects a familiar method or tool for a routine task but
  needs guidance for anything unfamiliar.
- 2 — Working: selects and uses the most appropriate tool or technique
  for a project's planning, analysis, development, testing, implementation
  or improvement, checking with a colleague on less familiar cases.
- 3 — Practitioner: selects and applies the right method or tool across a wide
  range of situations without support.
- 4 — Expert: builds organisational capability in methods and tools and
  advises others on their use.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "You need to analyse a business process
  for a project. What would you use, and why?"
- Ask the candidate to critique a choice of method or tool made for a sample
  scenario, and suggest an alternative if appropriate.
- If already in post, review a piece of project work showing which methods or
  tools the person chose and why.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate business architect identifies important stakeholders and
communicates with them clearly and regularly, tailors communication to
stakeholders' needs and works with them to build relationships while meeting
user needs, builds and reaches consensus with stakeholders, and works to
improve stakeholder relationships using evidence to explain decisions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. This is a new skill at this level, and the
framework's wording describes someone actively managing relationships and
building consensus, which is more than a beginner's contact list but not yet
the strategic relationship-building expected of a business architect.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: identifies stakeholders when prompted and communicates with
  them, but does not yet tailor the communication or work towards consensus.
- 2 — Working: identifies important stakeholders unprompted, tailors
  communication to their needs, and works towards consensus with some
  support.
- 3 — Practitioner: builds and reaches consensus with stakeholders independently,
  including where views differ, and uses evidence to explain decisions
  persuasively.
- 4 — Expert: builds long-term strategic stakeholder relationships and
  coaches others in doing the same.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell us about a stakeholder relationship
  you had to build from scratch, or repair. What did you do?"
- Ask the candidate to map the stakeholders for a short scenario and say how
  they would communicate with each.
- If already in post, review a stakeholder communication plan or engagement
  log the person has kept.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level**

An associate business architect works within a strategic context and
communicates how activities meet strategic goals, and contributes to the
development of strategy and policies.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. Contributing to the development of strategy and
policies is a real step beyond a trainee's administrative support role,
while still falling short of defining strategy, which comes later in the
role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: explains, when asked, how work connects to strategic goals,
  but needs support to contribute to strategy or policy development.
- 2 — Working: communicates unprompted how activities meet strategic
  goals, and makes a defined contribution to developing strategy or policy.
- 3 — Practitioner: shapes strategic conversations and contributes substantively
  to strategy and policy development with little support.
- 4 — Expert: sets strategic direction and develops others' ability to think
  strategically.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Describe a piece of work you contributed
  to that fed into a wider strategy or policy. What was your part in it?"
- Ask the candidate to explain how a piece of work they know well connects to
  their organisation's current strategic goals.
- If already in post, review a strategy or policy document the person has
  contributed to.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner

One or two skills below target is not automatically a fail. Weigh how central
the skill is to an associate business architect's accountabilities —
Communicating information and Stakeholder relationship management sit close
to the core of how this level operates day to day — and consider whether the
shortfall is coachable quickly, since this level still carries formal support
and training. Record the reasoning behind your overall judgement, not only
the numbers.
