# Network Architect - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, an interview panel
member, or anyone else evaluating a candidate or an employee — who needs to
judge whether a person has the baseline abilities the Architecture
profession's network architect role level requires. It measures demonstrated
skill against the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of
this level, for a hiring decision, a promotion or progression board,
onboarding a new starter, or a periodic capability check. It is one input
into that decision, never the whole of it — see `## Advisory` before you
begin.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, so you know what the
framework actually expects before you look at any evidence. Then gather what
exists for this person: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work
sample or task, direct observation of them at work, or references from people
who have worked with them. At this level the framework expects independent
delivery and some mentoring of associate network architects, so look for
evidence of unsupervised work, not just supported project work.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to
evidence for a rating, record it as unknown rather than guess — an honest gap
teaches you more than a guess dressed up as a score.

**Stay consistent.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way,
using the same evidence sources wherever you can. For a hiring or promotion
decision, have more than one assessor score independently, then compare notes,
rather than settling on a single view first.

**Fairness.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one, used
inconsistently, can produce unlawful adverse impact even when nobody intends
it. Consistent, evidence-based administration by more than one assessor
materially reduces that risk. Never let a rating be influenced by anything
other than demonstrated, job-related evidence.

**Handling sensitive material.** Network architecture at this level routinely
touches live infrastructure, third-party vendor arrangements and security
controls. When you record evidence in `## Assessor notes and evidence log`,
describe what the person did and how they reasoned about it — not classified
material, live vulnerability detail, or specific security configuration.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point
proficiency scale — how well the person does the thing, on the evidence
available, not how often they do it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

**Worked example.** Take Skill: Systems design. At 2 — Intermediate, a
candidate can produce a competent design for a well understood system when
someone more senior reviews it before it proceeds. At 3 — Advanced, the
baseline for this level, they independently design systems of medium risk,
impact and business or technical complexity, select the design standards and
tools to use without being told which ones, and can review another person's
design well enough to catch a poor technology choice or an inefficient use of
resources. At 4 — Expert, they set the design standards that others,
including their reviewers, work to. Calibrate against evidence like this
before you start scoring.

## About this role level

Architecture role: Network architect. A network architect is responsible for
network designs and specifications, including cloud networks.

The role has three levels, in order: associate network architect, network
architect, and lead network architect. This assessment covers the second of
those.

Role level: Network architect. A network architect has responsibility for
network designs and specifications to support business strategies using
common tools.

At this role level, the framework expects someone to:

- research new technologies and solutions, and ensure appropriate levels of
  assurance
- plan, direct and co-ordinate activities, engaging with other stakeholders to
  manage and implement a programme
- agree SLAs (service level agreements) and engage with third party vendors
- have an understanding of most of the following network technologies: LAN
  (local area network), WAN (wide area network), wifi, data-centre LAN, SD
  (software-defined) networks, telephony, mobile solutions infrastructure,
  unified comms, network management solutions, network-specific security and
  remote access technologies and approaches
- coach and mentor associate network architects where appropriate
- collaborate with engineers to ensure systems are practically designed

The framework names five skills for this level: Governance and assurance, IT
infrastructure, Systems design, Systems integration, and Troubleshooting and
problem resolution.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What the framework expects at this level**

A network architect develops appropriate governance and assurance processes,
identifies and recommends improvements to them, and supports others in
following or applying them.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The verbs move from following, at associate level, to
developing and improving, plus supporting others — that is independent
ownership of governance, which sits at Advanced rather than Intermediate.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe what a governance or assurance process is for.
- 1 — Beginner: follows an existing governance process when directed to.
- 2 — Intermediate: applies an existing governance process correctly and
  without prompting, and can explain it to someone newer.
- 3 — Advanced: develops or improves a governance or assurance process for
  their own area, and helps colleagues follow it correctly.
- 4 — Expert: sets governance and assurance processes across a wider
  programme or organisation, and is the person others check a difficult
  governance judgement against.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a governance or assurance process they have
  designed or improved, and what problem it solved.
- Review a governance artefact they produced — a process document, a service
  assessment pack, an improvement proposal.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate supporting a colleague through
  a governance step, and how that went.

### Skill: IT infrastructure

**What the framework expects at this level**

A network architect designs and implements infrastructure solutions and
services — for example networking and physical infrastructure,
commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) software, open source packages and solutions,
or virtual and cloud computing.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Designing infrastructure, not only building or
supporting it, is independent architectural work, consistent with the
Advanced description of working independently with a strong grasp of the
skill.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not designed an infrastructure solution.
- 1 — Beginner: has helped implement an infrastructure design someone else
  produced, with close review.
- 2 — Intermediate: designs straightforward infrastructure components with a
  senior colleague checking the approach before it is built.
- 3 — Advanced: independently designs and implements infrastructure
  solutions and services, and takes responsibility for how well they perform
  once live.
- 4 — Expert: sets infrastructure design standards for others to follow and
  is consulted on the hardest infrastructure design problems.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review an infrastructure design the candidate produced end to end, and ask
  them to justify the key decisions in it.
- Ask about a piece of infrastructure they designed and implemented, and what
  happened once it went live.
- Ask a referee whether the person's infrastructure designs have needed
  significant intervention after go-live, and why.

### Skill: Systems design

**What the framework expects at this level**

A network architect designs systems characterised by medium levels of risk,
impact, and business or technical complexity, selects appropriate design
standards, methods and tools and ensures they are applied effectively, and
reviews the systems designs of others to ensure the selection of appropriate
technology, efficient use of resources and integration of multiple systems
and technology.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Reviewing other people's designs, on top of producing
their own for medium-risk, medium-complexity systems, is the clearest sign in
the whole matrix that this level works independently and is trusted with
judgement calls the associate level is not — a textbook Advanced description.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot judge whether a systems design is fit for purpose.
- 1 — Beginner: produces a systems design for review at every stage.
- 2 — Intermediate: designs a system of managed risk and complexity largely
  unassisted, but has not yet reviewed another person's design.
- 3 — Advanced: independently designs systems of medium risk, impact and
  complexity, selects the right standards and tools without being told which,
  and reviews colleagues' designs for technology choice, resource efficiency
  and integration.
- 4 — Expert: is the escalation point when a design review surfaces a
  disagreement, and shapes the design standards the review is measured
  against.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to walk through a design they produced and a design they
  reviewed, and what they changed in each.
- Review a design review comment thread or sign-off record the candidate
  produced.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate catching a poor technology
  choice or an inefficient design in someone else's work.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level**

A network architect defines the integration build, co-ordinates build
activities across systems, and understands how to undertake and support
integration testing activities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Defining and coordinating a build across systems is
active ownership, a clear step up from the associate level's ability to
explain and describe integration in the abstract, and matches the Advanced
description of working independently.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not taken responsibility for any part of an integration
  build.
- 1 — Beginner: contributes to one part of an integration build under
  direction.
- 2 — Intermediate: coordinates a small integration build with a senior
  colleague available to check progress.
- 3 — Advanced: defines an integration build and coordinates activities
  across systems independently, and supports the integration testing that
  follows.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted to define integration builds across complex,
  multi-system programmes and to resolve disputes about how systems should
  connect.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an integration build they defined and
  coordinated, including how they sequenced the work across systems.
- Review an integration plan or build schedule they produced.
- Ask a referee about the candidate's role in a recent integration testing
  effort — did they support it, or run it.

### Skill: Troubleshooting and problem resolution

**What the framework expects at this level**

A network architect breaks a problem down into its component parts to
identify and diagnose root causes, and troubleshoots and identifies problems
across different technology capabilities.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. The framework adds root-cause decomposition to the
associate level's troubleshooting, and expects it applied across several
technology capabilities without direction — independent, strong-grasp work
that matches Advanced rather than Intermediate.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: cannot describe a structured approach to diagnosing a fault.
- 1 — Beginner: diagnoses a familiar fault type when guided through it.
- 2 — Intermediate: diagnoses faults in a familiar technology area
  unassisted, but needs help breaking down a fault that spans several
  capabilities.
- 3 — Advanced: independently breaks a cross-capability problem into its
  component parts, diagnoses the root cause, and resolves it or hands it on
  with a clear diagnosis.
- 4 — Expert: is the escalation point for faults that have resisted
  diagnosis by others, and teaches root-cause analysis technique.

**Evidence to gather**

- Walk through a real or simulated multi-capability fault scenario and observe
  how the candidate decomposes it.
- Ask for an example of a root cause they identified that was not the first,
  more obvious, suspect.
- Ask a referee for an example of the candidate resolving a problem that
  spanned more than one technology area.
