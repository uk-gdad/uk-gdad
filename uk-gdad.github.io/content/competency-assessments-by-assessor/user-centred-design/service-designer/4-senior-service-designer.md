# Senior Service Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the
baseline a UK Government Digital and Data (GDAD) senior service designer needs. It is
written for an assessor — a line manager, a panel member, or a reviewer — not for the
person being assessed.

A service designer designs the end-to-end journey of a service, so that a user can
complete their goal and government can deliver its policy intent, across both digital
and offline channels. A senior service designer is an experienced designer who works
with minimal support and can influence and mentor others. This assessment measures
demonstrated capability against the framework's own description of that level, across
the seven skills the framework names for it.

It is one input among several into a hiring, promotion or development decision, never
the whole of one — see `## Advisory` for why.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for educational
purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed
or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this form must never be the sole basis
for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision. Before using it,
consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational
psychology or legal compliance colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything. Gather
whatever evidence exists: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or
task, direct observation, or references. This is the fourth of six levels the framework
publishes for this role — expect evidence of a designer who now works with minimal
support, can carry responsibility across complex services, and is starting to influence
and mentor others, rather than evidence of someone still being closely directed.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something the
person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. If you cannot point to evidence for a
rating, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, using the same
kinds of evidence where possible. For a hiring or promotion decision, have more than one
assessor score independently before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Never let a rating be influenced by anything other than demonstrated,
job-related evidence. See [Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use)
in the project specification for what this rules out.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix is rated on the same five-point proficiency scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | No demonstrated experience of this skill |
| 1 | Beginner | Just starting to learn the skill; requires close supervision |
| 2 | Intermediate | Knows the basics and can perform tasks with some guidance |
| 3 | Advanced | Has a strong grasp of the skill and can work independently |
| 4 | Expert | Demonstrates full mastery and can teach or guide others in the skill |

This rates how well the person does the thing, on the evidence available — not how often
they do it. A worked example, using this level's skill of Design communication:

- **2 — Intermediate**: they explained a moderately complex design problem to a
  stakeholder outside design, with some help from a more senior colleague preparing the
  explanation.
- **3 — Advanced**: they independently explained a genuinely complex problem to
  stakeholders outside design, documented the decision and its risks clearly, and built
  consensus on the approach by asking difficult questions that surfaced an unstated
  assumption.

A senior service designer's baseline for this skill, below, is 3.

## About this role level

**The role: service designer.** A service designer designs the end-to-end journey of a
service. This helps a user complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent.
The work may involve creating, or changing, transactions, products and content across
both digital and offline channels provided by different parts of government.

**The level: senior service designer.** The framework describes this level as an
experienced designer who works with minimal support and can influence and mentor
others. It is the fourth of six levels the framework publishes for this role, sitting
above associate service designer, junior service designer and service designer, and
below lead service designer and head of service design. At this level, you work with
service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts; potentially carry
responsibility across complex services; help set direction and embed good practice
within teams; and make important decisions based on research, understanding how that
research affects others.

**The skills this level names**, in the summary's order: Design communication;
Designing for everyone; Designing strategically; Designing together; Evidence-based
design; Iterative design; and Leading design. Leading design is explicitly qualified
here — the framework says you lead and coordinate design work "with support" — which is
a lighter expectation than the other six skills, and the baseline below reflects that.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a senior service designer to clearly explain complex problems and
ideas to teams or stakeholders outside of design; clearly document and communicate
design decisions, related risks and any unresolved issues; and build consensus around a
design approach, for example by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Explaining complex problems to non-design stakeholders and
building consensus by challenging assumptions is independent, unsupported communication
work, consistent with a level the framework says "works with minimal support".

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not explained a design problem to a stakeholder outside design.
- 1 — Beginner: has helped prepare an explanation of a design problem, led by someone
  more senior.
- 2 — Intermediate: has explained a moderately complex design problem to non-design
  stakeholders, with some support.
- 3 — Advanced: independently explains complex problems to teams or stakeholders
  outside design, documents decisions and unresolved risks clearly, and builds
  consensus by asking difficult questions.
- 4 — Expert: is sought out to resolve entrenched disagreement about a design approach
  across multiple teams or stakeholder groups.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a time you had to explain a complex
  design problem to a stakeholder who was not a designer. How did you approach it?"
- Ask for a design decision document or risk log they authored, if available.
- Ask for an example of building consensus on a design approach where the room started
  in disagreement.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a senior service designer to advise teams on how to design
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services; design and
deliver ethical content or services that consider the personal and social context of
users; and ensure a design meets appropriate standards, for example accessibility
regulations.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Advising other teams on inclusive and accessible design, rather
than only applying it to your own work, is independent, standards-facing practice.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not advised a team on inclusive, accessible or sustainable design.
- 1 — Beginner: has applied an accessibility standard to their own work under
  supervision.
- 2 — Intermediate: has advised a colleague on one inclusive design consideration, with
  guidance from someone more senior.
- 3 — Advanced: independently advises teams on inclusive, accessible and
  environmentally sustainable design, and ensures designs meet standards such as
  accessibility regulations.
- 4 — Expert: sets or measurably improves how multiple teams practise inclusive,
  accessible design.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about advising a team on making a service
  more accessible or inclusive. What did you recommend, and why?"
- Ask for an example of a design decision they made because of the personal or social
  context of users, not just the immediate task.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has been the person a team turned to for
  accessibility or inclusion advice.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a senior service designer to help a team understand how
user-centred design helps it meet its goals; help teams align their work to the goals
and vision of their organisation; use risks, opportunities and constraints in
technology, systems and policy to shape design; and identify and create new design
patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Aligning a team's design work to organisational goals, and
creating new patterns rather than only reusing existing ones, is independent,
judgement-driven work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not connected a design decision to an organisational goal.
- 1 — Beginner: has followed a strategic direction someone else set.
- 2 — Intermediate: has helped align one piece of design work to a team's goals, with
  guidance.
- 3 — Advanced: independently helps teams understand how their design work supports
  organisational goals, weighs technology, systems and policy constraints to shape
  design, and creates a new design pattern or component when an existing one does not
  fit.
- 4 — Expert: shapes design strategy across multiple teams or the whole organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a design pattern or component you
  created because nothing existing fitted the problem."
- Ask for an example of a decision shaped by a technology, systems or policy
  constraint, and what trade-off they made.
- Review a design strategy document or pattern library entry they authored, if
  available.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a senior service designer to advise others how to effectively plan
and run design sessions with a team, users or stakeholders; adapt a design session to
ensure a useful outcome; effectively involve the right people throughout the design
process; and work across team or profession boundaries, for example with policy teams.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Advising others on running design sessions, and working across
profession boundaries such as with policy teams, is independent facilitation beyond
running your own sessions.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not advised another designer on planning or running a design session.
- 1 — Beginner: has co-run a design session under someone else's lead.
- 2 — Intermediate: has run a design session independently, with support choosing who
  to involve.
- 3 — Advanced: independently advises others on planning and running design sessions,
  adapts a session in the moment to reach a useful outcome, and works across a
  profession boundary such as with a policy team.
- 4 — Expert: is the person other designers ask when a design session with senior
  leaders or a difficult stakeholder group needs to succeed.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a design session you had to adapt
  midway through to get a useful outcome."
- Ask for an example of working with a non-design profession, such as policy, to plan
  or run a session.
- Ask a referee whether other designers have asked the candidate for advice on
  planning a session.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a senior service designer to analyse, synthesise and clearly
explain complex evidence relevant to users or a service, for example large data sets;
help the team use design hypotheses effectively; and use complex research and data to
develop and test design ideas.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Analysing and explaining complex evidence, including large data
sets, and using it to develop and test design ideas, is independent analytical work.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not analysed complex evidence to inform a design decision.
- 1 — Beginner: has read a research summary someone else produced.
- 2 — Intermediate: has analysed a moderately complex data set with support, and used
  it to inform one design decision.
- 3 — Advanced: independently analyses and clearly explains complex evidence, such as a
  large data set, and uses it to develop and test design ideas; helps the team use
  hypotheses effectively.
- 4 — Expert: is the point of reference when a team's evidence about users is
  ambiguous, contradictory or unusually complex.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about a time complex or contradictory
  evidence changed the direction of a design."
- Ask for an example of a design hypothesis they helped a team test, and what they
  learned.
- Review a piece of analysis or a research synthesis they produced, if available.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a senior service designer to help other designers apply iterative
design principles and agile methodologies to their work; iterate and improve complex
designs based on successive rounds of research; independently prototype complex ideas
at an appropriate fidelity; and adapt designs quickly to changes in requirements,
priorities or user needs.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Advanced. Helping other designers apply iterative principles, and
independently prototyping complex ideas, sits above delivering your own iterations
alone.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not iterated a design based on a round of research.
- 1 — Beginner: has made a change to a design following direction from someone more
  senior.
- 2 — Intermediate: has iterated a design across successive rounds of research, with
  some support choosing prototype fidelity.
- 3 — Advanced: independently prototypes complex ideas at the right fidelity, iterates
  designs across research rounds, adapts quickly to changing requirements, and helps
  other designers apply iterative principles to their own work.
- 4 — Expert: coaches a team through a fundamental change of direction under real time
  pressure without losing design quality.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about helping a less experienced designer
  apply iterative design to a piece of work."
- Ask for an example of a prototype they built, and why they chose that fidelity.
- Ask for an example of a design that had to change quickly because requirements or
  user needs changed.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework asks a senior service designer to lead and coordinate design work in
their team, with support; communicate the value of user-centred design to the team;
support other designers; and work with digital and data leaders in the organisation,
when needed.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Intermediate. The framework itself qualifies leading and coordinating
design work here as done "with support" — a lighter expectation than this level's other
six skills, which is why this one baseline sits below the rest of this block.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 0 — None: has not coordinated design work across more than one person.
- 1 — Beginner: has supported one other designer informally, without a coordinating
  role.
- 2 — Intermediate: has led and coordinated design work in a team with support from a
  more senior designer or manager, communicated the value of user-centred design to the
  team, and supported at least one other designer.
- 3 — Advanced: independently leads and coordinates design work in a team, without
  needing support to do so, and engages digital and data leaders directly when needed.
- 4 — Expert: is trusted to coordinate design work across a team without oversight and
  is sought out by digital and data leaders directly.

**Evidence to gather**

- A structured interview question: "Tell me about coordinating design work across more
  than one person on your team. What support did you have, and what did you decide
  yourself?"
- Ask for an example of supporting another designer's development.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has represented design to a digital or data
  leader outside their immediate team.

The baseline is normally met when every skill is rated at or above its baseline target.
Note that Leading design has a lower target than the rest of this matrix, because the
framework's own wording for this level describes leading design work as done "with
support" — do not expect fully independent leadership evidence there specifically, even
though the other six skills sit at Advanced.

One or two skills below target is not automatically a fail. Weigh how central the skill
is to this level's core purpose — working with minimal support, carrying responsibility
across complex services, and beginning to influence and mentor others — consider
whether the shortfall is coachable quickly, and record the reasoning behind the
determination, not only the number.
