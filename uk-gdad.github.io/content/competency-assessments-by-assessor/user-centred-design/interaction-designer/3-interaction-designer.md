# Interaction Designer - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for an assessor — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or
a reviewer — judging whether a candidate or an employee meets the baseline
abilities required for the User-centred design role of Interaction designer,
at the Interaction designer role level. It measures demonstrated skill
against the UK GDAD Profession Capability Framework's own description of
this role level, for a hiring decision, a promotion board, onboarding, or a
capability check.

An interaction designer at this level is a confident and competent designer
who develops designs based on evidence of user needs and organisational
outcomes, is trusted to make good decisions, and knows when to ask for
support. This assessment looks for independent, evidence-grounded practice —
not the supervised first steps of a junior designer, and not yet the
cross-team influence expected of a senior interaction designer.

This assessment is one input among several into a decision, never the whole of
it. Read `## Advisory` before using it.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for
educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has
not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this
form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or
disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human
resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance
colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` first, then gather whatever
evidence exists: a portfolio of interaction flows, wireframes or prototypes,
a structured interview, a design task (for example, asking the candidate to
explain the decisions behind a real piece of work), direct observation in a
design session, or references.

**Score on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something
the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. Where you have no
evidence for a skill, record it as unknown rather than guess.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way. For a
hiring or promotion decision, have more than one assessor score independently
before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** Every rating and every piece of evidence you gather must be
about demonstrated, job-related behaviour — see Fair and lawful use in the
project specification. Never let a rating be coloured by anything other than
what the person actually did, said, or produced.

## Rating scale

Every skill in the competency matrix below is rated on the same four-point
scale. This is a **proficiency** scale — it rates how well the person does the
thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it or how they feel
about it.

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 1 | Awareness | You can describe skill fundamentals and show basic knowledge of related tools |
| 2 | Working | You can apply the skill with some support and use appropriate tools |
| 3 | Practitioner | You can apply the skill independently, determine the best tools, and share your experience |
| 4 | Expert | You can lead organisational best practice and teach advanced techniques |

**Worked example — Iterative design.** The framework expects an interaction
designer to "apply iterative design principles and agile methodologies to
your work," "iterate and improve a design based on successive rounds of
research," and "prototype your ideas at different fidelities to explore and
test designs." A candidate who can only make a small change to a design when
told exactly what to change is showing at most **1 — Awareness**. A candidate
who independently iterates a design across rounds of research, prototypes at
more than one fidelity, and reuses and adjusts existing patterns is showing
**2 — Working** — the baseline this level expects. A candidate who does
this for complex designs and adapts quickly when requirements shift is
showing evidence closer to **3 — Practitioner**, above this level's own baseline.

## About this role level

The User-centred design profession's Interaction designer role works out the
best way to let users interact with services, in terms of both overall flow
and the level of individual design elements.

The role has six levels, in order: Associate interaction designer, Junior
interaction designer, Interaction designer, Senior interaction designer, Lead
interaction designer, and Head of interaction design. This assessment is for
the third of them.

An **interaction designer** at this level is a confident and competent
designer who can develop designs based on evidence of user needs and
organisational outcomes. At this role level, they will be trusted to make
good decisions, recognise when to ask for further guidance and support,
contribute to the development of design concepts, and interpret
evidence-based research and incorporate it into their work.

The framework names seven skills for this role level: Design communication,
Designing for everyone, Designing strategically, Designing together,
Evidence-based design, Iterative design, and Leading design.

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to clearly explain problems and design
ideas to others, and clearly explain design decisions to others using
appropriate tools and methods.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. "Clearly explain" describes independent,
competent communication, a step beyond the guided description expected of a
junior designer, but short of the cross-boundary, consensus-building
communication a senior designer is expected to manage.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Explains a design decision when prompted, but the
  explanation is vague or needs a colleague to fill gaps.
- 2 — Working: Clearly explains a design problem and the reasoning
  behind an interaction decision, choosing an appropriate way to show it,
  such as an annotated wireframe or a short walkthrough.
- 3 — Practitioner: Explains complex design problems to stakeholders outside
  design, documents decisions and unresolved issues, and builds consensus by
  asking searching questions.
- 4 — Expert: Gains support for design strategy from senior leaders and
  coaches others in communicating design effectively.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to walk through a real interaction flow or prototype and
  explain the decisions behind it, as if to a stakeholder unfamiliar with
  design.
- Review a design rationale document or presentation they have produced.
- Ask about a time a design decision was challenged, and how they explained
  or defended it.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to work with others to design and
deliver inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or
services that meet the needs of all users, and to ensure a design meets
appropriate standards, for example accessibility regulations, with support.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. "Work with others to design and deliver" is
applied, collaborative practice, while "with support" on the standards
bullet keeps full, independent assurance out of reach.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: With guidance, checks a design against a basic accessibility
  checklist.
- 2 — Working: Independently applies accessible and inclusive design
  practice to an interaction flow, and asks for support to confirm the
  design meets formal standards.
- 3 — Practitioner: Advises other designers on inclusive design and ensures a
  design meets accessibility regulations without prompting.
- 4 — Expert: Measurably improves inclusive design practice across multiple
  teams.

**Evidence to gather**

- Review a prototype or flow the designer produced and ask how they checked
  it works for users with different access needs, including keyboard-only
  or screen-reader users.
- Ask about a specific accessibility standard and how they check for it.
- Ask about a design choice they changed because it excluded some users.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to align their design work to both the
goals and vision of their team and the strategic objectives of their
organisation, identify potential risks and use design to mitigate them,
identify opportunities and use design to take advantage of them, and
contribute to the development of design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. This moves beyond explaining why alignment
matters — a more junior framing — into actually aligning work and spotting
risk and opportunity, short of the senior designer's coaching role.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Says, when asked, how a specific piece of work supports the
  team's goals.
- 2 — Working: Independently aligns interaction design choices to team
  goals, and contributes new pattern or component ideas to a shared library.
- 3 — Practitioner: Helps a team understand how design contributes to its
  goals, and shapes design using risk, opportunity and constraints in
  technology, systems and policy.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds design in organisational strategy.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to describe a project where their interaction choices
  were driven by a team goal, not just personal preference.
- Review a contribution they made to a shared pattern library or component
  set.
- Ask about a risk they spotted in a design and what they did about it.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to effectively plan and run a design
session with their team, users or stakeholders, identify and engage the
right people throughout the design process, and give and receive
constructive design feedback.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. Planning and running a session independently,
and handling feedback both ways, is competent practice — a step up from
taking part in a session someone else runs, and short of advising others on
how to do it.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Takes part in a design session and gives feedback when
  prompted.
- 2 — Working: Plans and runs a design session, identifies who should
  be in the room, and both gives and receives constructive feedback.
- 3 — Practitioner: Advises others on running sessions, adapts a session that is
  not working, and works across team or profession boundaries.
- 4 — Expert: Runs sessions that include senior leaders and coaches others
  in collaborative design practice.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to describe a design session or crit they planned and
  ran, including who they invited and why.
- Observe them giving feedback on a colleague's work, if possible.
- Ask about a time they received difficult feedback on their design and what
  they did with it.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to analyse, synthesise and clearly
explain evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics
data, and to work with researchers or analysts to use evidence to inform,
develop and test a design idea.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. Analysing and explaining evidence, and working
with a researcher or analyst to test an idea, is applied, collaborative
practice — a step beyond explaining what evidence shows with support, and
short of handling complex evidence or framing hypotheses for a team.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Explains what a piece of evidence shows, with support.
- 2 — Working: Analyses and explains evidence relevant to a design, and
  works with a researcher or analyst to test a design idea.
- 3 — Practitioner: Analyses and explains complex evidence, for example large
  data sets, and helps a team frame and use design hypotheses.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds hypothesis-driven design practice and guides the
  organisation on using evidence.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to talk through a piece of user research or analytics
  data that shaped an interaction design decision.
- Ask how they worked with a user researcher or analyst on a project.
- Review a design that changed as a result of evidence, and ask what the
  evidence showed.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to apply iterative design principles and
agile methodologies to their work, iterate and improve a design based on
successive rounds of research, prototype their ideas at different
fidelities to explore and test designs, use a range of design tools and
techniques, and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. Iterating across rounds of research, working at
more than one fidelity, and adjusting existing patterns is competent,
independent practice — short of iterating complex designs or adapting
quickly to fast-changing requirements.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Makes a small change to a design following feedback, with
  guidance on what "iteration" means in practice.
- 2 — Working: Iterates a design across successive rounds of research,
  prototypes at more than one fidelity, and uses and adjusts existing
  patterns and components.
- 3 — Practitioner: Iterates complex designs, prototypes independently at the
  right fidelity for the question being asked, and adapts quickly when
  requirements change.
- 4 — Expert: Embeds iterative practice across teams and leads prototyping
  of complex ideas at any fidelity.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the designer to show two or three versions of an interaction flow and
  explain what changed and why, referencing research or feedback.
- Ask about the range of tools they use for different fidelities, for
  example a paper sketch versus a clickable prototype.
- Review a pattern or component they reused or adapted from a library.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level**

An interaction designer is expected to lead and coordinate design work in
their team, with support, communicate the value of user-centred design to
their team, support other designers, and work with digital and data leaders
in their organisation when needed.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 2 — Working. "With support" caps full independence, but
leading or coordinating a piece of work, even with support, is a step beyond
simply describing leadership responsibilities in the abstract, so
Working is the right baseline rather than Awareness.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Describes the value of user-centred design when asked.
- 2 — Working: With support, leads or coordinates a piece of design
  work in the team, communicates the value of user-centred design, and
  supports another designer.
- 3 — Practitioner: Leads and coordinates design work without needing support,
  advocates for user-centred design outside the design team, and mentors
  other designers.
- 4 — Expert: Sets a vision for design leadership across multiple teams or
  the organisation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask about a time the designer led or coordinated a piece of work, even a
  small one, and what support they needed.
- Ask how they have supported a more junior designer.
- Ask about a time they explained the value of user-centred design to
  someone outside the design team.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
