# Operational Control Manager - Competency Assessment

## Introduction

This assessment is for the person doing the assessing — a line manager, a hiring panel member, or a reviewer — who needs to judge whether a candidate or employee has the baseline abilities the operational control manager role level requires. It covers the IT operations role of command and control centre manager at its third level: someone accountable for day-to-day IT operations, acting as the escalation point for incidents and problems, within a function that proactively monitors live services and performance trends.

The competency matrix below turns the framework's own description of this role level into a rated, evidence-based instrument across the eleven skills it names. It is one input among several into a hiring, onboarding or development decision, never the whole of one — see `## Advisory` for why.

## Advisory

This competency assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a rating on this form must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance, or disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational psychology, or legal compliance colleagues.

## How to use this assessment

**Preparing.** Read `## About this role level` before you score anything. Gather whatever evidence exists ahead of time: a CV or portfolio, a structured interview, a work sample or task, direct observation, or references. This level carries a 24/7 or business-defined on-call accountability — score what the candidate has actually done on call, never their availability or personal circumstances around being on call, which are not part of this instrument.

**Scoring on evidence, not impression.** Every rating must trace to something the person did, said, produced, or was observed doing. Being the escalation point in name is not the same as having actually resolved an escalation — ask for the specific case.

**Consistency.** Score every candidate for this role level the same way, and for a hiring or promotion decision have more than one assessor score independently before comparing notes.

**Fairness.** An unvalidated, AI-drafted instrument like this one can produce unlawful adverse impact if it is used inconsistently or if ratings drift away from demonstrated, job-related evidence. Score against the behavioural indicators below, keep your evidence log, and use more than one assessor wherever you can — consistent, evidence-based administration is what materially reduces that risk. See [Fair and lawful use](../../../../uk-gdad-pcf-competency-assessments-by-assessor/spec/index.md#fair-and-lawful-use) for the full constraints this assessment is written to.

## Rating scale

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 1 | Awareness | You can describe skill fundamentals and show basic knowledge of related tools |
| 2 | Working | You can apply the skill with some support and use appropriate tools |
| 3 | Practitioner | You can apply the skill independently, determine the best tools, and share your experience |
| 4 | Expert | You can lead organisational best practice and teach advanced techniques |

This is a proficiency scale, not a frequency scale — it rates how well the person does the thing, on the evidence available, not how often they do it.

**Worked example — Incident management.** A candidate who diagnoses and resolves routine incidents independently sits at 2 or 3. A candidate who leads the investigation and resolution of incidents, acting as the named escalation point across IT operations, sits at 3 — this level's own baseline, since the framework describes this level as leading incident investigation and resolution. A candidate who additionally is called on for the organisation's most difficult incidents and mentors others in leading them is showing evidence closer to level 4.

## About this role level

Command and control centre managers proactively monitor live services and performance trends to identify potential problems or areas for improvement that can then be investigated. The role runs across four levels: operations analyst, senior operations analyst, operational control manager, and head of command and control.

The operational control manager level sits third of four, above senior operations analyst and below head of command and control. The framework describes this level as accountable for day-to-day IT operations, acting as the escalation point for incidents and problems. At this level, you are responsible for effective change management processes that maintain the availability of services, using proactive and preventative measures; you are on call 24/7 or according to business requirements; and you are allowed to address issues across IT operations.

The framework names eleven skills at this level: asset and configuration management (maintaining secure configuration and accurate information, controlling IT assets in one or more areas, verifying the location and state of IT assets), availability and capacity management (managing service components to meet business needs and key performance indicators (KPIs)), change management (managing high impact, complex change requests, and ensuring that release policies, procedures and processes are applied), community collaboration (working collaboratively in a group and actively networking with others, adapting feedback to ensure it is effective and lasting, using their topic to identify and rectify problems in the team dynamic, and identifying issues through Agile "health checks" with the team), continuity management (managing the runbooks and the processes around service continuity, and testing the runbooks to ensure that service availability is maintained), incident management (leading the investigation and resolution of incidents), ownership and initiative (taking accountability for issues that occur and being proactive in searching for potential problems, and achieving excellent user outcomes), problem management (initiating and monitoring actions to investigate patterns and trends, consulting specialists effectively, determining the appropriate resolution and assisting with its implementation, and determining preventative measures), service focus (seeing the bigger picture by taking groups of services and investigating how to get the best of underlying services), service management framework knowledge (holding a Level 3 service management framework qualification and demonstrating knowledge of the life cycle or capability elements of ITIL), and user focus (collaborating with user researchers, representing users internally, explaining the difference between user needs and desires, championing user research, and guiding others in prioritising the user story).

## Competency matrix

### Skill: Asset and configuration management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to maintain secure configuration and accurate information, to control IT assets in one or more areas, and to verify the location and state of IT assets.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. The wording is unchanged from the level below, but an operational control manager is expected to carry out this work with full independence across day-to-day operations, matching Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Updates configuration records accurately only with checking.
- 2 — Working: Maintains accurate records for their own area with some guidance on edge cases.
- 3 — Practitioner: Maintains secure, accurate configuration and asset information across the areas they are accountable for, entirely independently.
- 4 — Expert: Sets configuration and asset control standards other operational control managers follow.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe how they maintain configuration accuracy across day-to-day IT operations.

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to manage service components so that they meet business needs and key performance indicators (KPIs).

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. The wording is unchanged from earlier levels, but at this level a candidate is accountable for day-to-day operations as a whole, and expected to manage this independently, matching Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Reads KPI figures but needs help interpreting them.
- 2 — Working: Manages routine capacity actions with some guidance.
- 3 — Practitioner: Independently manages service components to meet KPIs across day-to-day operations, and anticipates and prevents breaches.
- 4 — Expert: Sets or redefines the availability and capacity standards for the operations centre.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they anticipated a capacity issue across operations and prevented a KPI breach.

### Skill: Change management

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to work collaboratively in a group and actively network with others, to adapt feedback to ensure it is effective and lasting, to use their topic to identify and rectify problems in the team dynamic, and to identify issues through Agile "health checks" with the team.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. Actively diagnosing and rectifying team dynamic issues is independent, applied leadership of collaboration, matching Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Participates in a team health check without acting on what it surfaces.
- 2 — Working: Networks and collaborates with support identifying underlying team dynamic issues.
- 3 — Practitioner: Actively networks across operations, adapts feedback so it lands effectively, and uses insight to identify and rectify team dynamic problems.
- 4 — Expert: Is asked to run or improve Agile health checks for other teams beyond their own.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a team dynamic issue they identified through a health check and how they rectified it.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner

### Skill: Continuity management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to manage the runbooks and the processes around service continuity, and to test the runbooks to ensure that service availability is maintained.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. Owning and testing runbooks is a clear step up from explaining basic continuity principles at earlier levels, matching independent, applied practice at Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Follows an existing runbook without maintaining or testing it.
- 2 — Working: Maintains a runbook with support, and has taken part in testing one.
- 3 — Practitioner: Manages runbooks and continuity processes independently, and runs tests that verify service availability is maintained.
- 4 — Expert: Owns continuity strategy and testing across the operations centre.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a runbook they manage and the last time they tested it, including what the test showed.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate's runbooks have proven reliable during a real event.

### Skill: Incident management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to lead the investigation and resolution of incidents.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. "Lead" marks a clear move from diagnosing incidents personally (the level below) to directing others through resolution as the named escalation point, matching Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Follows someone else's lead during an incident.
- 2 — Working: Leads the response to straightforward incidents with support available.
- 3 — Practitioner: Leads the investigation and resolution of incidents independently as the escalation point, co-ordinating others and communicating clearly throughout, including out of hours.
- 4 — Expert: Is called in to lead the operations centre's most difficult incidents and mentors others in incident leadership.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe an incident they led as the escalation point, including how they co-ordinated others and what they communicated.
- Ask a referee whether the candidate has led a major or out-of-hours incident and how that went.

### Skill: Ownership and initiative

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to take accountability for issues that occur, to be proactive in searching for potential problems, and to achieve excellent user outcomes.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. Proactively searching for problems ahead of time, rather than reacting once they occur, is independent, forward-looking practice matching Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Takes accountability for an issue when told to.
- 2 — Working: Takes accountability for issues in their own area, occasionally spotting problems early.
- 3 — Practitioner: Actively searches for potential problems ahead of time across day-to-day operations and consistently produces excellent outcomes for users.
- 4 — Expert: Builds proactive problem-spotting into how the whole operations centre works.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate for an example of a problem they found before anyone else reported it, and what they did about it.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to initiate and monitor actions to investigate patterns and trends, to effectively consult specialists where required, to determine the appropriate resolution and assist with its implementation, and to determine preventative measures.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. Initiating and monitoring investigation, and determining resolution independently, is a clear step above contributing to someone else's investigation, matching Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Contributes to a problem investigation someone else initiated.
- 2 — Working: Initiates a problem investigation with support and determines a resolution with guidance.
- 3 — Practitioner: Initiates and monitors problem investigations independently, consults specialists appropriately, and determines both the resolution and preventative measures.
- 4 — Expert: Sets the approach the operations centre uses for problem investigation.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a problem investigation they initiated, who they consulted, and how they arrived at both a resolution and a preventative measure.

### Skill: Service focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to see the bigger picture by taking groups of services and investigating how to get the best of underlying services.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. "See the bigger picture" across groups of services is a broader, more independent reading of this skill than at earlier levels, matching Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 3 — Practitioner: Investigates how to get the best from groups of underlying services independently, across day-to-day operations.
- 4 — Expert: Is the person others rely on to see how the whole operations estate's underlying services should best work together.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they improved outcomes by looking across a group of services rather than one in isolation.

### Skill: Service management framework knowledge

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to show they have a Level 3 service management framework qualification, and to demonstrate knowledge of the life cycle or capability elements of ITIL (Information Technology Infrastructure Library).

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. This level adds ITIL life cycle knowledge on top of the Level 3 qualification named at earlier levels, a broader and more applied grasp of the framework that matches Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Holds a Level 3 qualification but cannot describe how ITIL life cycle elements connect to their own work.
- 2 — Working: Holds Level 3 and can describe ITIL life cycle elements in general terms.
- 3 — Practitioner: Holds Level 3 and applies ITIL life cycle or capability knowledge directly to how they run day-to-day operations.
- 4 — Expert: Holds expert-level certification and teaches ITIL concepts to others.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to explain how an ITIL life cycle stage applies to an incident or change they have led.

**Rate this skill**

- [ ] 1 — Awareness
- [ ] 2 — Working
- [ ] 3 — Practitioner
- [ ] 4 — Expert

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level**

The framework expects an operational control manager to collaborate with user researchers and represent users internally, to explain the difference between user needs and the desires of the user, to champion user research to focus on all users, and to prioritise and define approaches to understand the user story, guiding others in doing so.

**Baseline for this role level**

Baseline: 3 — Practitioner. Championing user research and guiding others in prioritising the user story is independent leadership of this skill, matching Practitioner.

**Behavioural indicators**

- 1 — Awareness: Engages with user research when asked.
- 2 — Working: Represents users internally on straightforward matters.
- 3 — Practitioner: Champions user research, clearly distinguishes user needs from desires, and guides others in prioritising the user story.
- 4 — Expert: Sets how the wider operations centre approaches user focus and coaches others in it.

**Evidence to gather**

- Ask the candidate to describe a time they championed a user need against internal pressure to do something else.
- Ask them to explain, with a real example, the difference between what a user asked for and what they actually needed.

**Rate this skill**
