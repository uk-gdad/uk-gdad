# Senior DevOps engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior DevOps engineer on the technical
track in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. It has come from human
resources or your manager, and it exists to establish where the gap sits between
what you do now and what the organisation needs from someone at this level.

At senior the framework stops describing components and starts describing the
whole toolchain. You transform technical requirements into an effective DevOps
toolchain that enables product delivery; you make deployment strategies
repeatable, scalable and highly available; you coach and mentor; and you are the
deep technical knowledge that delivery teams call on when a problem will not
yield. That last part is the one that quietly eats the week, and it is worth
being honest about how much of your time it takes.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the ten skills gets an explanation, a note on what changes
at lead, a rating and some questions. Expect about 45 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send
from here — your answers never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your
organisation uses for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so
answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Where a question asks what you did, name your part** rather than the team's.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could pick up or did
  two jobs ago.
- **There is no target rating**, and a row of 4s is not the goal. Most senior
  DevOps engineers sit at 3 across their core skills and at 1 or 2 wherever
  their particular estate has never demanded it. That pattern is information.

## About this role level

**The role: development operations (DevOps) engineer.** Commonly called DevOps
engineers, development operations engineers support the development and
operation of software through tools, environments and practices — managing tools
and testing environments, central code control, maintaining development
standards, and writing software that automates systems.

**Your level: senior DevOps engineer.** At this level you deliver and integrate
software to form a complete DevOps toolchain, and you are responsible for
planning and designing large groups of stories. The framework expects that you:

- transform technical requirements into an effective DevOps toolchain to enable
  product delivery
- provide coaching and mentoring to more junior colleagues
- ensure that deployment strategies for products are repeatable, scalable and
  highly available
- have deep technical knowledge, providing support to delivery teams and solving
  complex problems

Note the word *complete*. At DevOps engineer you deliver components; here you
are answerable for whether the toolchain as a whole enables delivery — including
the parts you did not build.

**The two roles at this level.** The framework defines a technical role and a
management role with identical accountabilities. This form covers the technical
one. Three skills are worded differently:

| | Technical role (you) | Management role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Availability and capacity management | Manage service components to meet business needs and key performance indicators | Ensure the correct implementation of standards and procedures; identify capacity issues and implement changes; initiate remedial action |
| Development process optimisation | Work under guidance to identify opportunities and contribute to implementation | Analyse processes; identify and implement opportunities; lead and develop a team of experts; help set policy and standards |
| Service support | Identify, locate and fix **complex** service faults, and advise others on methodologies and types of service support | Identify, locate and fix service faults |

The pattern is worth knowing when choosing a track: the management role governs
capacity and owns process improvement as a programme; your role keeps the deeper
fault-finding and the advisory position on how support is done. If your job is
closer to that description, use the
[senior DevOps engineer (management) form](5-senior-devops-engineer-management.md)
and say so in Q46.

**Where this level sits.** The DevOps engineer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice DevOps engineer
2. Junior DevOps engineer
3. DevOps engineer
4. **Senior DevOps engineer** — you are here
5. Senior DevOps engineer (management)
6. Lead DevOps engineer
7. Lead DevOps engineer (management)
8. Principal DevOps engineer
9. Principal DevOps engineer (management)

Level 5 is not a step above level 4; it is the same level on a different track.
Your step up is lead DevOps engineer, at level 6, and only two of your ten
skills change there. Modern development standards moves from applying and
supporting to identifying, applying across complex programmes and leading
others. Systems integration changes most: from defining and co-ordinating the
integration build to *establishing standards and procedures across the service
product life cycle, ensuring practitioners adhere to them, and managing the
resources that make the integration function work*. The rest of the step is in
the accountabilities — acting as a technical product owner with medium to long
term strategies for product lines, and leading the sharing of knowledge.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether services stay up and cope with load — the services
your organisation runs, and the toolchain everyone else depends on.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you manage service components
so they meet business needs and key performance indicators.

**What that looks like here.** You know which indicators matter and can say why
those numbers were chosen. You have made a design decision on capacity grounds
rather than a configuration tweak. You know which parts of the estate have never
been tested by real load.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead DevOps engineer. The management
track at your own level is where this skill changes: ensuring standards and
procedures are implemented, and initiating remedial action.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how software gets built and released — the
pipeline, the feedback loops, the release path — so good work reaches users
faster and more safely.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you work under guidance to
identify process optimisation opportunities, and contribute to implementing the
solutions. "Under guidance" is the level's boundary, and it is a narrower
expectation than many senior DevOps jobs actually carry.

**What that looks like here.** You know the delivery numbers across the teams
you support. You have contributed to an improvement someone else was leading.
You can name what the last process change bought, in time or in failures
avoided.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead DevOps engineer — this skill does
not advance on the technical track. It advances on the management track at your
own level, where it becomes analysing processes, implementing improvements,
leading a team of experts and helping to set policy and standards. If process
ownership is what you want, that is worth raising.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Building systems that resist attack and contain damage, by
engineering controls against the threats that actually apply — including for the
pipeline, which is usually the most privileged thing you own.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design solutions and
services with security controls included, specifically engineered to mitigate
security threats.

**What that looks like here.** You can name your estate's real threats and the
control that answers each. You have held a delivery date over a security
concern. You know what a compromised build agent, runner or deployment
credential could reach, because you have thought it through rather than assumed.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead DevOps engineer. It changes at
principal, where you review and quality assure other people's solutions and lead
others in security practice.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code and configuration
understandable, testable and safe to change — applied to infrastructure and
pipelines as much as to application code.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you apply modern development
standards *and support others in applying them*. The second half is the senior
part, and it is where most of the evidence is missing.

**What that looks like here.** Your reviews teach. Delivery teams write better
pipeline code since you started reviewing it. You have set a convention and
explained the reasoning rather than asserting it.

**The next step.** One of the two skills that change at lead: identifying and
applying standards to support complex projects and programmes, and *leading*
others in applying them.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, disposably, so

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a

### Skill: Service support

**What this means.** Keeping live services and the toolchain working —
identifying, locating and fixing faults, and at this level being the person

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: identify, locate and

defeated someone else. You can work from a symptom to a cause across several

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of systems — components, boundaries,
technology — and the standards by which those decisions get made.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: design systems of
medium risk, impact and complexity; select appropriate design standards, methods
and tools and ensure they are applied effectively; and review other people's
designs for appropriate technology, efficient use of resources and integration
across systems.

**What that looks like here.** You have designed something substantial that
others built. You review designs and change their direction without overruling
their authors. You can say which of your own past decisions you would reverse.

### Skill: Systems integration

**What this means.** Making separate systems work together — defining the build,
co-ordinating it across teams, and testing the seams where things actually
break.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you define the integration
build, co-ordinate build activities across systems, and understand how to
undertake and support integration testing.

**What that looks like here.** You have owned integration with systems you do not
control — identity, artefact storage, secrets, cloud APIs, security scanning,
supplier services — and agreed the contract with the teams who own them. You
test failure paths, not only success.

**The next step.** The largest change at lead: establishing standards and
procedures across the service product life cycle and ensuring practitioners
adhere to them, and managing the resources that make the systems integration
function work. Integration stops being something you do and becomes something
you govern and staff.

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the people who use the service at the centre of
technical decisions. For you that is two audiences: the public who use the
services your toolchain supports, and the engineers who use the toolchain
itself.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: collaborate with user
researchers and represent users internally; explain the difference between user
needs and user desires; champion user research so it covers all users;
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story, guiding others
in doing so; and recommend the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You can connect a pipeline decision to what it
does for the public in the end. You treat your engineers' pain as evidence
rather than as complaint, and you have measured it. You have argued for a
technical decision on the grounds of what it does for users.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead DevOps engineer.
