# Apprentice developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome to this form.

It is for you if you work as an apprentice developer in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Someone in your organisation — usually human
resources, or your manager — has sent it to you so that you can say what you
already do, what you have not met yet, and what would help you most over the
next few months.

This is not a test. Nothing here is scored, marked or graded. Nobody is
comparing your answers with anyone else's. The whole point of an apprenticeship
is that you are learning on the job, so gaps are expected. Finding one is a good
outcome, not a bad one.

The form walks through the skills the Government Digital and Data Profession
Capability Framework names for an apprentice developer. For each skill it
explains what the skill means, what the framework expects of you at this level,
and what it looks like in real work. Then it asks you a few questions. You do
not need any other page open, and you do not need to look anything up.

Most people take about 30 to 45 minutes. You can stop and come back.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that
apply, and pick a rating where one is asked for. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

So pick whichever of these suits you:

- Fill it in here, then use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
  page, or print it. Clear answers empties the form afterwards.
- Copy the questions into a document or an email, and write your answers there.
- Answer in whatever tool your organisation already uses for development
  conversations.

Every question has a number, like **Q7**. If you answer by number, your answers
make sense on their own, and you do not have to copy the questions out.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **"I don't know" is a real answer.** It is one of the most useful answers you
  can give, because it tells people what to teach you.
- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a mark against you.
- **Small examples are fine.** A single ticket, a single pairing session or a
  single bug is plenty. You do not need a big story.
- **Nothing you write has to be perfect.** Notes and half-sentences are fine.
- **Ask who will read it.** If you are not sure who sees your answers or what
  they are used for, ask the person who sent it to you before you fill it in.
  That is a completely reasonable thing to ask.

## Rating scale

Several questions ask you to rate yourself on a skill. Use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It is about **what you do at work today** — not what you could probably manage,
  and not what you covered in a course. If you have only read about something,
  that is a 1.
- **There is no target rating.** An apprentice ticking mostly 1s and 2s is
  describing exactly what an apprenticeship looks like. Your ratings are a
  starting point, not a verdict.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs. In this role you write clean, secure
code following a test-driven approach, and you create code that is open by
default and easy for other people to reuse.

**Your level: apprentice developer.** An apprentice developer attends certified
training and develops skills on the job. The framework expects that you:

- spend a substantial portion of your time shadowing other people
- work with other developers to write code and tests
- build your knowledge
- follow a test-driven approach
- write code that is automatically tested

Notice what is *not* there. You are not expected to work alone, to own a
service, or to be responsible for security decisions. Shadowing and pairing are
the job at this level, not a sign that you are behind.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. **Apprentice developer** — you are here
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. Senior developer
5. Senior developer (management)
6. Lead developer
7. Lead developer (management)
8. Principal developer
9. Principal developer (management)

The next step for you is junior developer. Two things change there. First, some
skills you currently explain, you will start to *do* — security is the clearest
example. Second, two new skills appear that this level does not name at all:
systems design and systems integration. You do not need them yet.

**The skills this form covers.** The framework names seven skills for an
apprentice developer, and this form takes them in the framework's own order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Information security
3. Modern development standards
4. Programming and build (software engineering)
5. Prototyping
6. Service support
7. User focus

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the order the framework lists them. Each one explains what the
skill means, what is expected of you now, what it looks like in real work, and
what changes at the next level. Then you rate yourself and answer a few
questions.

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** "Availability" is whether a service is up and working for
the people using it. "Capacity" is whether it can cope with how many people use
it and how much they ask of it. Managing both means watching those things,
planning for them, and knowing what happens when they go wrong.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can *explain* the
processes. Explaining is the whole expectation here — you are not expected to
run them, plan capacity or be on call.

**What that looks like here.** You could tell a new starter what "the service is
down" actually means in your team. You know roughly what your team watches — a
dashboard, an alert, an error rate. You have heard someone explain why a service
slowed down under load, and you followed the explanation.

**The next step.** At junior developer, the framework describes this skill in
exactly the same words. So this is not the skill where your next step shows up.
It stays an explaining skill for a while.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Keeping information and services safe from people who
should not reach them, and from mistakes that expose them by accident. "Security
controls" are the specific things that do the protecting — passwords and
multi-factor sign-in, encryption, access permissions, code review, secrets kept
out of code.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can *explain* information
security and the security controls available to protect solutions and services.
The framework is explicit that at your level you are aware of security but not
responsible for it.

**What that looks like here.** You know why nobody puts a password or an API key
in the code. You know that some data in your service is more sensitive than
other data. You could name two or three controls your team relies on.

**The next step.** At junior developer this changes from explaining to doing:
you *use* information security practices and available controls to contribute to
protecting solutions and services. So this is a skill where the step up is real,
and worth thinking about now.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The shared ways of working that teams use so their code
stays understandable, testable and safe to change — version control, small
changes, code review, automated tests, automated builds, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can *explain why* modern
development standards matter. Not defend them in an argument, and not set them
for a team — explain the point of them.

**What that looks like here.** You could say why the team writes tests rather
than checking by hand. You could say why changes go through review. You know
what happens when you push code, even if you could not build that pipeline
yourself.

**The next step.** At junior developer you explain *the most important
principles and how they apply to your own work*, and you start applying them
under guidance. So the move is from "why does the team do this" to "here is how
I do this in my own work".

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** Writing the code itself: designing a small piece of it,
writing it, testing it, fixing it when it is wrong, and writing down enough for
the next person.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design, code, test,
correct and document simple programs or scripts *under the direction of others*.
The direction is part of the expectation. Working from someone else's steer is
what this level is.

**What that looks like here.** You pick up a small, well-described ticket. You
write a test first, then the code. You push it, someone reviews it, and you make
the changes they suggest. You leave a comment or a note where the next person
would otherwise be puzzled.

**The next step.** At junior developer the framework uses the same words for
this skill. What changes around it is the rest of the job — supervision instead
of direction, and more of the testing types.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building a rough version of something to learn from it —
on paper, in a slide, in clickable screens or in throwaway code. The point is
the learning, not the artefact. A prototype is meant to be thrown away.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things. That you can explain
what prototyping is, and why and when to use it. And that you understand how to
work in an open and collaborative environment — pair working, for example.

**What that looks like here.** You have seen your team try something rough
before building it properly. You can say why that was quicker than arguing about
it. You pair with someone and can work out loud rather than going quiet.

**The next step.** At junior developer you understand *when to use a specific
technique or method*, and you show the value of prototyping to your team. So it
moves from understanding the idea to choosing an approach and making the case
for it.

### Skill: Service support

**What this means.** Keeping a live service working for the people using it —
looking into faults, fixing them, and doing the routine work that stops faults
happening.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you help with investigating
and fixing service faults, completing defined activities under direction. In
other words: you take part, someone tells you which part, and someone more
experienced is leading.

**What that looks like here.** You are asked to check whether an error appears
in the logs, and you report back what you found. You sit in on an incident call
and follow what is happening. You pick up a small bug fix once the cause is
known.

**The next step.** At junior developer you help fix faults *following agreed
procedures* rather than direct instruction, and you carry out maintenance tasks
on service support infrastructure. Less "do this specific thing", more "follow
the runbook".

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the person who will use the service in mind while
you build it. "User experience analysis" is the work of finding out what people
actually need and where the service gets in their way. A "user story" is a short
description of a need, written from that person's point of view.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you show an awareness or
understanding of user experience analysis and its principles, and that you can
explain the purpose of user stories and the focus on user needs.

**What that looks like here.** You read the user story before you start the
ticket, and you can say who it is for. You have watched or heard about a user
research session. When something in the code seems odd, you can connect it back
to something a real person needs.

**The next step.** At junior developer the framework uses the same words. This
is a skill that deepens with exposure rather than changing shape at the next
level — the more research you sit in on, the more it means.
