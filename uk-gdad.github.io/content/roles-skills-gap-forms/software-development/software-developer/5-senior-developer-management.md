# Senior developer (management) - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior developer on the management track
in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your
manager has sent it to you so that you can set out what you do now, where the
gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework defines two roles at this level, a technical one and a management
one, and gives them the same accountabilities and almost the same skills. The
one skill it words differently is availability and capacity management, where
your version is about ensuring standards and procedures are implemented,
spotting capacity issues and initiating remedial action — responsibility for the
service holding up, rather than for one component of it. In practice the
management role usually carries people and delivery responsibility too, and
several questions here ask about that.

Nothing is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. The form takes each of the ten skills the framework names for you,
explains what it expects and what changes at lead developer (management), and
asks for a rating and an example. Expect to spend about 45 minutes. The awkward
questions are the ones worth answering.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick
boxes tick, and each skill has a rating to choose. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into
whatever your organisation uses for development conversations. Every question
is numbered, so answers travel back on their own. Clear answers empties the
form and deletes the saved copy, which is worth doing on a computer other
people use.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Answer for yourself, not for your team.** Where a question is about people
  you manage, it is asking what you do, not how they are performing.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could pick up or did
  in a previous job. Depth of current practice, not ceiling.
- **There is no target rating**, and a row of 4s is not the goal. On the
  management track it is common for hands-on ratings to drift down while
  standard-setting ratings rise. That drift is worth noticing rather than
  hiding: it tells you what you are becoming.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Development process optimisation**
>
> - [x] 2 — Working: I do it with support
>
> *Evidence:* I proposed splitting our release into two smaller ones and helped
> deliver it, but the analysis of where the delay actually came from was done by
> our delivery manager, not by me.

Note what makes it useful: named work, and an honest line about where the
support was.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs — writing clean, secure code following a
test-driven approach, and creating code that is open by default and easy for
others to reuse.

**Your level: senior developer (management).** A senior developer delivers and
integrates software to form a complete service. The framework expects that you:

- plan and lead development on sets of related stories
- have an understanding of the whole system, and take responsibility for
  teaching it to others
- work with other disciplines to understand what needs to be built
- coach and mentor more junior colleagues
- operate the production services you build
- find ways to improve system robustness, resilience and stability

**The two roles at this level.** The framework is explicit that there are two
roles here — a technical one and a management one — and it gives them identical
accountabilities. The difference in the published skills is a single one:

| | Technical role | Management role (you) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Availability and capacity management | Manage service components so they meet business needs and key performance indicators | Ensure the correct implementation of availability and capacity management standards and procedures; identify capacity issues and implement the required changes; initiate remedial action |

Everything else is worded the same for both. So the honest reading is that the
management role is the same craft with responsibility for the system holding up
and, usually, for the people who keep it holding up. If your work is closer to
the technical description, use the
[senior developer form](4-senior-developer.md) instead, and say so in Q46.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. Senior developer
5. **Senior developer (management)** — you are here
6. Lead developer
7. Lead developer (management)
8. Principal developer
9. Principal developer (management)

Note that level 5 is not a step above level 4. It is the same level on a
different track. Your step up is lead developer (management), at level 7.

That step changes two of your ten skills, and one of them changes a great deal.
Development process optimisation moves from contributing under guidance to
analysing processes, implementing improvements, leading a team of experts to
deliver them and setting policy and standards. Modern development standards
moves from applying and supporting to identifying, applying across complex
programmes and leading others. Everything else keeps its wording — including
programming and build, which does *not* advance on the management track the way
it does on the technical one. If deepening your hands-on engineering is what you
want, that is an argument for the technical track, and worth raising early
rather than late.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

2. Development process optimisation

4. Modern development standards

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether the service stays up for the people using it and
copes with the load put on it — and, in your version of this skill, whether the
standards and procedures for that are genuinely being followed rather than
merely documented.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things, and this is the one
skill worded differently from the technical role: that you ensure the correct
implementation of availability and capacity management standards and procedures;
that you identify capacity issues and implement the required changes; and that
you initiate remedial action.

**What that looks like here.** You know whether your team's runbooks match what
people actually do at three in the morning. You have spotted a capacity problem
from a trend rather than an alert, and got the change made. You have called for
remedial work and carried the argument for the time it took.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead developer (management). What grows
is the number of services and teams the standards have to hold across.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how software gets built — the pipeline, the
feedback loops, the review and release path — so good work reaches users faster
and more safely.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you work under guidance to
identify process optimisation opportunities, and contribute to implementing the
solutions. "Under guidance" is the level's boundary: spotting and contributing,
not owning the change programme.

**What that looks like here.** You noticed a bottleneck and said so with
numbers. You took a piece of a larger improvement and delivered it. You can name
what the last process change actually bought, in time or in defects.

**The next step.** The largest change on your track. At lead developer
(management) you analyse current processes, identify *and implement*
opportunities, lead and develop a team of experts to deliver service
improvements, and help establish requirements for change by setting policy and
standards. If you are aiming at lead, this is the skill to grow first.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Building services that resist attack and contain damage, by
engineering the controls in against threats that actually apply.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design solutions and
services with security controls included, specifically engineered to mitigate
security threats.

**What that looks like here.** You can name the threats that apply to your
service and the control that answers each. You have pushed back on a design, or
a deadline, because of what it exposed. You know what your service logs, where
those logs go, and what a leak would mean.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead developer (management). It changes
at principal, where you review and quality assure other people's solutions and
lead others in security practice.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code understandable, testable and
safe to change — version control, small changes, review, automated testing,
continuous integration, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you apply modern development
standards and support others in applying them.

**What that looks like here.** Your reviews teach. You have set a convention and
explained the reasoning rather than asserting it. Someone junior writes
differently since you started working with them.

**The next step.** One of the two skills that change at lead developer
(management): identifying and applying standards across complex projects and
programmes, and leading others in applying them. Supporting becomes leading, and
one team becomes several.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** The core craft: reviewing specifications, then designing,
coding, testing, and documenting software of real complexity with the right
standards and tools.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with others
when necessary to review specifications, and use the agreed specifications to
design, code, test and document programs or scripts of medium-to-high
complexity, using the right standards and tools.

**What that looks like here.** You still build things, and what you build is
picked up by people who were not in the room. You ask the questions that resolve
an ambiguous requirement before you write code against it.

**The next step.** This skill keeps the same wording at lead developer
(management) — it does not advance on your track. On the technical track it does:
lead developers advise on applying standards and methods, hold technical
responsibility across a whole project, and set the team's standards for tools and
techniques. If that progression is what you want, it is worth saying so now.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, quickly and
disposably, so decisions rest on evidence rather than argument.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others; that
you establish design patterns and iterate them; and that you use a variety of
prototyping methods and choose the most appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You ask your team for rough versions rather than
building them all yourself. You have ended a circular debate by asking for two
prototypes by Thursday. You throw prototypes away and say so out loud, so nobody
learns that rough work quietly becomes permanent.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead developer (management).

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of a system — components, boundaries,
and how they fit together — and choosing the standards, methods and tools that
shape it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design systems of medium
risk, impact and complexity; select appropriate design standards, methods and
tools and ensure they are applied effectively; and review other people's designs
for appropriate technology, efficient use of resources and integration across
systems.

**What that looks like here.** You have written a design someone else built. You
have reviewed a design and changed its direction. You can say why your system is
shaped as it is, including the parts you would not choose again.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead developer (management). It changes
at principal, where the risk band rises and design practice is controlled across

### Skill: Systems integration

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the people who use the service at the centre of
technical decisions, and knowing the difference between what they need and what
they ask for.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with user
researchers and can represent users internally; explain the difference between
user needs and user desires; champion user research so it covers all users;
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story and guide others
in doing so; and recommend the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You attend research rather than reading the
summary. You protect your team's time for it when delivery pressure rises. When
someone says "all users", you ask about the ones using assistive technology, on
a poor connection, or doing this once in their life under stress.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead developer (management).
