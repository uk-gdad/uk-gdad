# Principal developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a principal developer on the technical track
in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. It has come from human
resources or your manager, and it exists to establish where the gap sits between
what you do now and what the organisation needs from someone at this level.

Principal is the top of this role. The framework describes it as leading and
planning development across large or multiple teams: deep expertise applied
where it keeps several teams working effectively, capability built through
mobility, career paths and recruitment, and emerging technology identified,
tested and championed rather than merely admired. Almost everything at this
level is exercised through other people, which makes it the hardest level to
evidence honestly — the work shows up in other teams' output, in decisions that
were never needed, and in problems that did not happen.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of your ten skills gets an explanation of what the framework
expects, a note on how the management role at this level differs, a rating and
some questions. Expect 45 minutes to an hour. The questions are deliberately
uncomfortable in places; that is where the useful answers are.

- **"The organisation does this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**. At this level the temptation is to
  rate the reputation rather than the current practice; resist it.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you, and would stay changed. At principal a few 4s are expected;
  ten of them would be worth interrogating.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Prototyping**
>
> - [x] 2 — Working: I do it with support
>
> *Evidence:* I coach two leads on prototyping and it shows in their teams. But
> end-to-end strategic service design is not something I have done — our service
> designer holds that, and I have not tried to learn it.

Named work, and a clear line about where the practice stops.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs — writing clean, secure code following a
test-driven approach, and creating code that is open by default and easy for
others to reuse.

**Your level: principal developer.** A principal developer leads and plans
development across large or multiple teams. The framework expects that you:

- are an expert in many technical areas, or a specialist with very deep
  knowledge in a particular technical area
- use that knowledge, and work with related disciplines, to keep multiple teams
  working effectively
- develop capability by facilitating internal mobility, shaping career paths and
  recruiting talent, and ensuring they collaborate
- identify, test and champion the adoption of emerging technologies

The third of those is unusual for an engineering role and easy to underweight.
Internal mobility, career paths and recruitment are named as your work, not as
human resources' work that you occasionally help with. Several questions below
ask about it directly.

**There is no level above this one.** Nine levels exist in the software
developer role, and this is the highest on the technical track. Where people go
after principal is a question about other roles and other professions in the
framework, or about leadership outside it, and the framework does not define
that as a next step for this role — so this form does not invent one. What each
skill block gives you instead is how the management role at this level is worded
differently, which is published and is a real career choice.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. Senior developer
5. Senior developer (management)
6. Lead developer
7. Lead developer (management)
8. **Principal developer** — you are here
9. Principal developer (management)

**The two roles at this level.** Three skills are worded differently for the
management role: availability and capacity management, development process
optimisation, and programming and build. The pattern across them is that the
management role governs standards, sets improvement strategy and allocates
resources, while retaining the senior-level engineering expectation; your role
keeps the deeper engineering responsibility. If your job is closer to that
description, use the
[principal developer (management) form](9-principal-developer-management.md) and
say so in Q45.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether services stay up for the people using them and cope
with the load put on them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you manage service components
so they meet business needs and key performance indicators.

**What that looks like here.** The indicators your services are judged on are
ones you helped choose, and you can say why those numbers rather than others.
You make architectural decisions on capacity grounds across services rather than
within one. You know which of your organisation's services have never been
tested by real load.

**The management role differs.** It is worded around ensuring the correct
implementation of availability and capacity management standards and procedures,
identifying capacity issues and initiating remedial action — governance of the
practice rather than management of the components.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how software gets built, as a governed programme
of work rather than a series of good intentions.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: analyse current
processes; identify and implement opportunities to optimise them; lead and
develop a team of experts to deliver service improvements; and help to evaluate
and establish requirements for the implementation of changes by setting policy
and standards.

**What that looks like here.** You know your organisation's delivery numbers as
numbers. You have led an improvement with people assigned to it and a result you
can name. The policy that governs how teams release is one you helped write, and
you can say what it prevents.

**The management role differs.** It goes further into strategy: setting the
strategy and managing resource allocation for solution development programmes,
and working with client functions to establish business requirements and then
identify, propose, initiate and lead those programmes.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Building services that resist attack and contain damage —
and, at this level, assuring that other people's services do too.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design, review and
quality assure solutions and services, ensuring they are specifically engineered
to mitigate security threats, and that you lead others in using appropriate
information security practices.

**What that looks like here.** You review designs you did not write and find the
things their authors could not see. You have stopped something going live. The
security practice your teams follow is one you set, and people follow it when
you are not watching.

**The management role differs.** It is worded identically at this level.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code understandable, testable and
safe to change, applied across programmes rather than teams.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you identify and apply modern
development standards to support complex projects and programmes, and lead
others in applying them.

**What that looks like here.** The standards across your teams are ones you set
or inherited and then justified. You have retired a standard that had outlived
its reason, which is harder than adding one. New teams adopt the practice
without you being in the room.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** The craft, at the level where you are answerable for how it
is practised across projects rather than for what you personally produce.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you advise on the right way to
apply standards and methods to ensure compliance; maintain technical
responsibility for all the stages and iterations of a software development
project; and provide technical advice to stakeholders while setting team-based
standards for programming tools and techniques.

**What that looks like here.** You hold technical responsibility for work you did
not write, including the parts that are wrong. You give technical advice to
people who control money and time, in language they can act on. You still know
enough of the detail to be told when you are wrong.

**The management role differs.** Its expectation stays at the senior wording —
collaborating on specifications and building work of medium-to-high complexity.
This skill is the clearest place where the technical track goes further, and it
is worth knowing that when you choose between them.

### Skill: Prototyping

you. You can look at a whole service — not a component — and prototype how it
should work.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of systems at the highest risk band, and
governing how design gets done across an organisation.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: design systems
characterised by high levels of risk, impact and business or technical
complexity; control system design practice within an enterprise or industry
architecture; influence industry-based models for the development of new
technology applications; develop effective implementation and procurement
strategies consistent with business needs; and ensure adherence to relevant
technical strategies, policies, standards and practices.

**What that looks like here.** You have designed something where being wrong
would have been expensive and public. Design practice across teams follows an
architecture you help control. You have shaped how something gets bought, not
only how it gets built.

**The management role differs.** It is worded identically at this level.

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the people who use the service at the centre of
technical decisions, including the decisions taken far from them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with user
researchers and can represent users internally; explain the difference between
user needs and user desires; champion user research so it covers all users;
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story and guide others
in doing so; and recommend the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You still watch research, at a level where nobody
would notice if you stopped. You represent users in rooms where no user has ever
been discussed. When someone says "all users", you ask about the ones using
assistive technology, on a poor connection, or doing this once in their life
under stress — and you keep asking after it becomes tiresome.

**The management role differs.** It is worded identically at this level.
