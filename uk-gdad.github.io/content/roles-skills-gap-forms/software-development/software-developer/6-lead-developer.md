# Lead developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead developer on the technical track in
the UK Government Digital and Data profession. It has come from human resources
or your manager, and its purpose is to establish where the gap sits between the
work you do now and what the organisation needs from someone at this level, and
what would close it.

At lead, the framework stops describing delivery and starts describing
influence. You guide the ways in which the team works. You are involved in
deciding *whether* software should be written at all. You contribute to the
developer community beyond your own team, provide technical leadership, and
promote knowledge sharing and the adoption of good practice. Those are all
things that leave a trace in other people's work rather than in yours, which
makes them harder to evidence — and it is why several questions below ask what
survives your absence.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. The form takes each of the ten skills the framework names for a
lead developer, sets out what it expects and what changes at principal, and asks
for a rating and an example. Expect 45 minutes, and expect the useful answers to
be the uncomfortable ones.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send
from here — your answers never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your
organisation uses for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so
answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **At this level, "the team does this" is not an answer to "what do you do".**
  Where a question asks what you did, name your part in it.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could pick up, did two
  jobs ago, or once read a good paper about.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people.** "I set the approach and develop others
  in it" means someone else's practice changed because of you. At lead, that is
  a reasonable claim in some skills and an overreach in others; the difference
  is worth being honest about.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Development process optimisation**
>
> - [x] 2 — Working: I do it with support
>
> *Evidence:* I proposed and delivered the change to trunk-based development,
> but the analysis that identified it as our worst bottleneck came from our
> principal developer, and I would not have got there from the data on my own.

Named work, and an honest line about where the support was.

## About this role level

**The role: software developer.** A software developer designs, runs and
improves software that meets user needs — writing clean, secure code following a
test-driven approach, and creating code that is open by default and easy for
others to reuse.

**Your level: lead developer.** A lead developer leads development on one or a
small number of related project teams. The framework expects that you:

- guide the ways in which the team works
- are proficient in a wide range of technical systems
- are involved in identifying appropriate technology and approaches, deciding
  when software should be written
- contribute to the developer community
- provide technical leadership, coaching and mentoring your team
- promote knowledge sharing and the adoption of good practice

Two of those deserve attention because they are so often skipped. *Deciding when
software should be written* includes deciding when it should not — buying,
reusing, or solving the problem without code. And *contribute to the developer
community* means beyond your team: your organisation's community of practice,
cross-government work, or the wider profession.

**The two roles at this level.** The framework defines a technical role and a
management role at lead. This form is the technical one. The published
difference is in two skills: the management role's availability and capacity
management is about ensuring standards and procedures are implemented and
initiating remedial action, and its development process optimisation is much
broader — analysing processes, leading a team of experts, and setting policy and
standards. Its programming and build expectation, by contrast, stays at the
senior wording rather than advancing to yours. If your job is closer to that
description, use the
[lead developer (management) form](7-lead-developer-management.md) and say so in
Q45.

**Where this level sits.** The software developer role has nine levels:

1. Apprentice developer
2. Junior developer
3. Developer
4. Senior developer
5. Senior developer (management)
6. **Lead developer** — you are here
7. Lead developer (management)
8. Principal developer
9. Principal developer (management)

Your step up on this track is principal developer, at level 8. Four of your ten
skills change there, and they change substantially: development process
optimisation, information security, prototyping and systems design. The pattern
across all four is the same — from doing it well inside your teams to setting how
it is done beyond them, and from designing systems of medium risk to designing
systems of high risk and controlling design practice across an enterprise
architecture. The principal accountabilities add the rest: deep expertise applied
across multiple teams, developing capability through mobility and career paths
and recruitment, and championing the adoption of emerging technology.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Availability and capacity management
2. Development process optimisation
3. Information security
4. Modern development standards
5. Programming and build (software engineering)
6. Prototyping
7. Service support
8. Systems design
9. Systems integration
10. User focus

## Job skills

### Skill: Availability and capacity management

**What this means.** Whether services stay up for the people using them and cope
with the load put on them — measured and managed rather than discovered during
an incident.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you manage service components
so they meet business needs and key performance indicators.

**What that looks like here.** You know which indicators your services are held
to and where they sit. You have made an architectural decision on capacity
grounds. You can tell the difference between a service that is stable and a
service that has not yet been tested by real load.

**The next step.** Identical wording at principal developer. On the management
track, at both lead and principal, it is broader: ensuring the standards and
procedures are correctly implemented, and initiating remedial action.

### Skill: Development process optimisation

**What this means.** Improving how software gets built — the pipeline, the
feedback loops, the review and release path — so that good work reaches users
faster and more safely.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you work under guidance to
identify process optimisation opportunities, and contribute to implementing the
solutions. This is one of the few places where the framework asks less of a lead
than you might expect, and less than the management track at your own level.

**What that looks like here.** You have identified a bottleneck with evidence
rather than with irritation, and delivered part of the fix. You know what your
team's lead time actually is.

**The next step.** One of the four skills that change at principal, and the
largest change: analysing current processes, identifying *and implementing*
opportunities, leading and developing a team of experts to deliver service
improvements, and helping to establish requirements for change by setting policy
and standards. If principal is where you are heading, start here.

### Skill: Information security

**What this means.** Building services that resist attack and contain damage, by
engineering controls against the threats that actually apply.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design solutions and
services with security controls included, specifically engineered to mitigate
security threats.

**What that looks like here.** You can name your services' threats and the
controls that answer them. You have held a delivery date over a security concern
and carried the argument. Your teams know which decisions they must not take
alone.

**The next step.** At principal this becomes design, review *and quality
assurance* of solutions and services, plus leading others in using appropriate
information security practices. Reviewing other people's security work, and
being the person who sets the practice, is the step.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code understandable, testable and
safe to change — version control, small changes, review, automated testing,
continuous integration, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you identify and apply modern
development standards to support complex projects and programmes, *and lead
others in applying them*. Leading, not supporting: this is your skill to set.

**What that looks like here.** The standard your teams work to is one you chose
and can justify. You have changed a practice that people had stopped
questioning. New joiners can learn how things are done here without asking you.

**The next step.** Identical wording at principal developer. The scope grows with
the number of teams, not the description.

### Skill: Programming and build (software engineering)

**What this means.** The craft, at the level where you are answerable for how it
is practised rather than only for what you personally produce.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you advise on the right way to
apply standards and methods to ensure compliance; maintain technical
responsibility for all the stages and iterations of a software development
project; and provide technical advice to stakeholders while setting team-based
standards for programming tools and techniques.

**What that looks like here.** You are the person who answers "how should we
build this". You hold technical responsibility across a whole project, including
the parts you did not write. You give technical advice to people who are not
engineers, in language they can act on.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, disposably, so that
decisions rest on evidence rather than seniority.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others;
establish design patterns and iterate them; and use a variety of prototyping
methods, choosing the most appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You ask for prototypes rather than opinions. You
have ended a circular debate by asking for two versions by Thursday. The
patterns your teams reuse were established and then revised, not fixed once.

**The next step.** At principal it moves to sharing best practice and coaching
others in prototyping, and to looking at strategic service design end to end —
from prototyping a component to prototyping how a whole service should work.

### Skill: Systems design

**What this means.** Deciding the shape of systems — components, boundaries,
technology choices — and the standards by which those decisions get made.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design systems of medium
risk, impact and complexity; select appropriate design standards, methods and
tools and ensure they are applied effectively; and review other people's designs
for appropriate technology, efficient use of resources and integration across
multiple systems and technologies.

**What that looks like here.** You have designed something substantial and had
it built by people who were not you. You review designs and change their
direction without simply overruling their authors. You can say which of your own
past decisions you would reverse.

**The next step.** The largest change at principal alongside process
optimisation: designing systems of *high* risk, impact and complexity;
controlling design practice within an enterprise or industry architecture;
influencing industry-based models for new technology applications; developing
implementation and procurement strategies consistent with business needs; and
ensuring adherence to technical strategies, policies and standards. Note the
appearance of procurement — at principal, how things get bought becomes part of
how they get designed.

### Skill: User focus

**What this means.** Keeping the people who use the service at the centre of
technical decisions, and knowing the difference between what they need and what
they ask for.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with user
researchers and can represent users internally; explain the difference between
user needs and user desires; champion user research so it covers all users;
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story and guide others
in doing so; and recommend the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You attend research rather than reading the
summary, and you protect your teams' time for it when delivery pressure rises.
You have argued a technical decision on user grounds in a room where the
loudest argument was cost. When someone says "all users", you ask about the ones
using assistive technology, on a poor connection, or doing this once under
stress.

**The next step.** Identical wording at principal developer.
