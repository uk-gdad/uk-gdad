# Junior frontend developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome to this form.

It is for you if you work as a junior frontend developer in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can say what you already do, what you have not had a chance to try, and what
would help you most over the next few months.

Nothing here is scored, marked or graded, and nobody is comparing your answers
with a colleague's. Junior is a learning level by design — you deliver real
work, with guidance from more senior developers — so gaps are expected.

The form walks through the eleven skills the framework names for a junior
frontend developer. Two of them are new at this level compared with apprentice:
strategic thinking, and systems integration. Each skill is explained before you
are asked anything about it, so you will not need another page open.

Most people take about 40 minutes. You can stop and come back.

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into a document or an email, or answer
in whatever tool your organisation already uses. Every question has a number,
like **Q7**, so your answers make sense on their own. Clear answers empties
the form and deletes the saved copy. If other people use this computer, press
it when you have finished.

Before you start:

- **"I don't know" is a real answer**, and it tells people what to teach you.
- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a mark against you.
- **One real example beats a general claim.** A single component or bug is
  plenty.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers or what
  they are used for, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: frontend developer.** A frontend developer designs, builds and
improves website software that meets user needs. In this role you write clean,
accessible code following a progressive enhancement approach, create code that
is open by default and easy for others to reuse, design software that creates
meaningful interactions with users, and understand HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

**Progressive enhancement** means the basics work everywhere first — real HTML
that works without JavaScript — with improvements layered on top for browsers
that can take them. **Accessible** means it works for people using screen
readers, keyboards, magnification or speech input. Government services are used
by everybody, so both matter more here than in many jobs.

**Your level: junior frontend developer.** A junior frontend developer learns on
the job by delivering software components. The framework expects that you:

- work independently, with guidance from more senior developers, to identify
  user issues and needs
- write clean code that follows web standards
- build your understanding of accessibility
- build your knowledge
- develop software which meets user needs

Notice the shape of the first one: independently, *with guidance*. You are
expected to get on with work, and also expected to have someone to check with.

**Where this level sits.** The frontend developer role has six levels:

1. Apprentice frontend developer
2. **Junior frontend developer** — you are here
3. Frontend developer
4. Senior frontend developer
5. Lead frontend developer
6. Head of frontend development

Your next step is frontend developer. The pattern across most skills is the
same: things you currently explain, you start to do, and things you do under
direction, you start to do with agreed specifications instead.

**One thing to know about this level.** The framework's wording for two skills —
communicating information, and community collaboration — reads as more senior
than a junior job usually is. It talks about advocating for the team externally,
motivating teams and creating the right environment for them. Answer against
what you actually do; if the wording does not match your work, say so. That is
useful information, not a gap on your part.

### Skill: Accessibility

**What this means.** Making sure what you build works for everyone, including
people using a screen reader, navigating by keyboard, magnifying the screen,
needing high contrast, or finding movement difficult.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you understand users have
different accessibility needs, and that you build user interfaces that meet
accessibility standards and fix accessibility issues under the direction of
others.

**What that looks like here.** You reach for semantic HTML first. You check
keyboard navigation before asking for review. You have run an automated
accessibility check and understood what it reported — including which of its
findings were not real.

**The next step.** At frontend developer you champion accessibility to internal
stakeholders, offer design feedback to reduce the risk of failing accessibility
testing, and recommend the tools and methods to use. So it moves from building
correctly to influencing what other people build.

### Skill: Communicating information

**What this means.** Understanding what designers and other stakeholders are
asking for, being understood in return, and adjusting how you explain things
depending on who you are talking to.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: listen to the needs
of design and business stakeholders and interpret that information; take part in
discussions within a multidisciplinary team; be an advocate for the team
externally and manage differing perspectives; and shape and share communications
relevant to the audience.

Those last two are written for someone more experienced than a junior usually
is. Answer for what you actually do.

**What that looks like here.** You explain a technical constraint to a designer
in terms they can act on. You ask questions in team discussions rather than
after them. When two people want different things, you can describe both
positions accurately.

**The next step.** At frontend developer it becomes managing stakeholder
expectations, identifying issues through team health checks, and hosting or
moderating difficult discussions.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working well with the people around you, giving feedback
that people can act on, and noticing when a team is not working well.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: contribute to the
work of others; motivate and empower teams; create the right environment for
teams to work in and identify the best team makeup depending on the situation;
and recognise and deal with issues.

As with communicating information, that wording is pitched above a typical
junior job. Answer against your real work and say where it does not fit.

**What that looks like here.** You review other people's code and your comments
are useful. You notice when someone is stuck and quiet. You have said something
when a way of working was not helping.

**The next step.** At frontend developer it becomes active networking beyond the
team, adapting feedback so it lasts, and helping to stimulate the right
responses to problems in team dynamics.

### Skill: Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)

**What this means.** At this level, following the way your team has agreed to
work, and understanding why teams agree things.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you follow agreed good
practice for the team; show an awareness of the importance of team dynamics and
collaboration; and understand the importance of feedback. This is the same
wording as apprentice.

**What that looks like here.** You follow conventions without reminders and know
where they are written down. You act on feedback rather than defending against
it. You notice when a decision was made and by whom.

**The next step.** At frontend developer this changes sharply: understanding the
impact of decisions, making decisions of varying risk and complexity, and
resolving technical disputes between peers and stakeholders. It is one of the
biggest single jumps in the whole role.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The shared practices that keep code understandable and safe
to change — version control, small changes, code review, automated tests,
automated builds, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can explain the most
important principles and how they apply to your own work, and that you apply
them under guidance.

**What that looks like here.** Your branches, commits and pull requests follow
the team's conventions without reminders. You can say why the team keeps changes
small and point at a time it helped.

**The next step.** At frontend developer you apply the standards *and support
others in applying them*.

### Skill: Programming and build (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Building the interface: understanding the requirement,
writing the HTML, CSS and JavaScript, testing it, correcting it and documenting
it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with others
to understand and review software requirements, and that you design, code, test,
correct and document user interfaces using the right standards and tools, under
the direction of others.

**What that looks like here.** You are part of the conversation that decides
what a component should do, not only the one that builds it. You write tests for
your interfaces. You leave enough behind that someone else can change your work
safely.

**The next step.** At frontend developer the direction drops away, you review
specifications rather than receive them, and the interfaces become medium to
high complexity.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building a rough version of something to learn from it. The
point is the learning; the prototype is meant to be thrown away.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you understand when to use a
specific prototyping technique or method, and that you show the value of
prototyping to your team.

**What that looks like here.** You have built something rough to answer a
question rather than arguing about the answer. You can say why a paper sketch
suits one question and a coded prototype suits another.

**The next step.** At frontend developer prototyping becomes a team activity you
help run: soliciting prototypes from others, testing together, establishing
design patterns, and choosing between several methods.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What this means.** Understanding why your work exists — what the organisation
is trying to achieve, and how the thing you are building serves it. This skill
is new at your level; it does not appear at apprentice.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can explain the strategic
context of your work and why it is important, and that you support strategic
planning in an administrative capacity.

**What that looks like here.** You could say what your service is for and who
decided it mattered. You can connect a ticket to something bigger than the
ticket. You have helped prepare material for a planning session, even if you did
not run it.

**The next step.** At frontend developer you work within the strategic context
and communicate how activities meet strategic goals, and you contribute to
developing strategy and policies.

### Skill: Systems integration (frontend developer)

**What this means.** How your source code becomes the files a browser actually
downloads — the build that turns your CSS and JavaScript into static assets, how
those get delivered over the internet, and how different browsers and devices
end up with versions that work for them. This skill is new at your level.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: show an awareness of
how to build static assets from source code; and explain how browsers use and
present static assets to end users, and how different versions of an interface
can be delivered for different browsers and devices.

**What that looks like here.** You know what your build step does and roughly
why. You can explain what happens between a user typing an address and seeing
something on screen. You understand why a page might look different in an older
browser, and why progressive enhancement helps.

**The next step.** At frontend developer you build the static assets yourself
and test that the resulting interface works when delivered over the internet, and
you work on integration tests as part of a wider team.

### Skill: User focus (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Keeping the person who will use the page in mind while you
build it. "User experience analysis" is finding out what people need and where
the service gets in their way.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you show an understanding of
user experience analysis and its principles, and can explain the purpose of user
stories and the focus on user needs. This is the same wording as apprentice.

**What that looks like here.** You read the user story before starting and can
say who it is for. You have watched research. When a design seems awkward, you
can ask what need it is serving rather than assuming it is wrong.

**The next step.** At frontend developer it grows considerably: collaborating
with user researchers and interaction designers to represent user needs
internally, championing research that covers all users, and guiding others in
understanding the user story.

### Skill: Web performance optimisation

**What this means.** How quickly a page loads and responds, and what makes it
slow — large images, too much JavaScript, blocking requests, heavy fonts. It
matters most for people with older devices and poor connections.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: describe the basic
principles of web performance; explain why it is important; and help improve web
performance under supervision. This is the same wording as apprentice.

**What that looks like here.** You know a page that is fast on your laptop may
be slow on a five-year-old phone. You have looked at a performance measurement
of your own work. You have made something faster — a smaller image, a removed
dependency, a deferred script.

**The next step.** At frontend developer you explain how poor performance harms
user experience, and you help *identify and resolve* performance issues rather
than only helping improve them.
