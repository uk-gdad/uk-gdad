# Apprentice frontend developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome to this form.

It is for you if you work as an apprentice frontend developer in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Someone in your organisation — human
resources, or your manager — has sent it to you so that you can say what you
already do, what you have not met yet, and what would help you most over the
next few months.

This is not a test. Nothing here is scored, marked or graded, and nobody is
comparing your answers with anyone else's. An apprenticeship is a learning job,
so gaps are expected. Finding one is a good outcome.

The form walks through the nine skills the Government Digital and Data
Profession Capability Framework names for an apprentice frontend developer. Some
of them will be familiar — writing HTML, CSS and JavaScript — and some may be
new, like accessibility and web performance. Each one is explained before you
are asked anything about it, so you will not need another page open.

Most people take about 30 to 45 minutes. You can stop and come back.

## About this role level

**The role: frontend developer.** A frontend developer designs, builds and
improves website software that meets user needs. In this role you write clean,
accessible code following a progressive enhancement approach, create code that
is open by default and easy for others to reuse, design software that creates
meaningful interactions with users, and understand the three fundamental
frontend technologies: HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

Two of those phrases are worth explaining, because they shape everything else.
**Accessible** means the thing you build works for people using screen readers,
keyboard navigation, magnification, speech input, or simply an old phone on a
bad connection. **Progressive enhancement** means building so that the basics
work everywhere first — real HTML that works without JavaScript — and then
layering improvements on top for browsers that can take them. Government
services are used by everybody, so both matter more here than in many jobs.

**Your level: apprentice frontend developer.** An apprentice frontend developer
attends certified training and develops skills on the job. The framework expects
that you:

- spend a substantial portion of your time shadowing others
- work with other developers to write code and tests
- write code that follows web standards
- build your understanding of accessibility
- build your knowledge
- develop software which meets user needs

Notice that shadowing is listed first. It is the job at this level, not a sign
you are behind.

**Where this level sits.** The frontend developer role has six levels:

1. **Apprentice frontend developer** — you are here
2. Junior frontend developer
3. Frontend developer
4. Senior frontend developer
5. Lead frontend developer
6. Head of frontend development

Your next step is junior frontend developer. Two new skills appear there —
strategic thinking, and systems integration for frontend developers — and
several of the ones below grow. You do not need them yet.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Accessibility
2. Communicating information
3. Community collaboration
4. Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)
5. Modern development standards
6. Programming and build (frontend developer)
7. Prototyping
8. User focus (frontend developer)
9. Web performance optimisation

### Skill: Accessibility

**What this means.** Making sure what you build works for everyone, including
people who use a screen reader, navigate by keyboard, magnify the screen, need
high contrast, or find movement and flashing difficult.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: that you understand
accessibility and that users have different accessibility needs, and that you
help fix accessibility issues under the direction of others.

**What that looks like here.** You know what a screen reader is and have heard
one read a page. You know why a `<button>` is better than a styled `<div>`. When
someone points at an accessibility issue in your work, you understand what they
are pointing at and can fix it.

**The next step.** At junior you start *building* user interfaces that meet
accessibility standards, rather than only fixing issues when they are found. So
the move is from repair to prevention.

### Skill: Communicating information

**What this means.** Understanding what designers, product people, and other
stakeholders are asking for, and being understood in return.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you listen to the needs of
design and business stakeholders and interpret that information, and that you
take part in discussions within a multidisciplinary team.

**What that looks like here.** You sit in team discussions and say things in
them. When a designer explains an intention, you can turn it into a question
about the code. You ask when you do not follow, rather than nodding and hoping.

**The next step.** At junior this grows a lot: advocating for the team
externally, managing differing perspectives, and shaping communications for
different audiences.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working well with the people around you, and understanding
that how a team works together affects what it can build.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you understand the work of
others and the importance of team dynamics, collaboration and feedback.

**What that looks like here.** You know roughly what everyone in your team does
and why. You give and take feedback in code review without it feeling personal.
You notice when the team works well and when it does not.

**The next step.** At junior this jumps considerably, to contributing to other
people's work, motivating teams and helping to recognise and deal with issues.
That wording is written for someone with more experience than a junior usually
has; treat it as the direction of travel rather than a description of next
month.

### Skill: Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)

**What this means.** At this level, following the way your team has agreed to
work, and understanding why teams agree things in the first place.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you follow agreed good
practice for the team; show an awareness of the importance of team dynamics and
collaboration; and understand the importance of feedback.

**What that looks like here.** You follow the team's conventions without needing
reminders. You know where they are written down. You act on feedback rather than
defending against it.

**The next step.** At junior the framework describes this skill in exactly the
same words, so the change comes later — at frontend developer it becomes about
understanding the impact of decisions and making them.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The shared ways of working that keep code understandable
and safe to change — version control, small changes, code review, automated
tests, automated builds, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can explain the
importance of using modern development standards. Explaining is the whole
expectation.

**What that looks like here.** You could say why the team writes tests rather
than clicking through the page each time. You know what happens when you push
code, even if you could not build that pipeline yourself.

**The next step.** At junior you explain the most important principles *and how
they apply to your own work*, and start applying them under guidance.

### Skill: Programming and build (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Building the interface itself: designing a small piece of
it, writing the HTML, CSS and JavaScript, testing it, fixing it and documenting
it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design, code, test,
correct and document simple user interfaces using the right standards and tools,
under the direction of others. The direction is part of the expectation.

**What that looks like here.** You pick up a small, well-described ticket and
build the component. You use semantic HTML because you know why it matters. When
review comments come back, you understand the reasons behind them.

**The next step.** At junior you collaborate with others to understand and
review the requirements rather than receiving them, and the interfaces stop
being described as simple.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building a rough version of something to learn from it. The
point is the learning; the prototype is meant to be thrown away.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you can explain what
prototyping is, and why and when to use it, and that you understand how to work
in an open and collaborative environment — pair working, for example.

**What that looks like here.** You have seen your team try something rough
before building it properly. You can say why that was quicker than arguing. You
can work out loud when pairing rather than going quiet.

**The next step.** At junior you understand when to use a *specific* technique
or method, and show the value of prototyping to your team.

### Skill: User focus (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Keeping the person who will use the page in mind while you
build it. "User experience analysis" is the work of finding out what people need
and where the service gets in their way. A "user story" describes a need from
that person's point of view.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you show an understanding of
user experience analysis and its principles, and can explain the purpose of user
stories and the focus on user needs.

**What that looks like here.** You read the user story before you start and can
say who it is for. You have watched or heard about a research session. You can
connect something odd in the interface back to a real person's need.

**The next step.** At junior the framework uses the same words. This one deepens
through exposure — the more research you see, the more it means.

### Skill: Web performance optimisation

**What this means.** How quickly a page loads and responds, and what makes it
slow — large images, too much JavaScript, blocking requests, heavy fonts. It
matters most for the people with the worst devices and connections, which in a
government service is a lot of people.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: describe the basic
principles of web performance; explain why it is important; and help improve web
performance under supervision.

**What that looks like here.** You know that a page which is fast on your laptop
may be slow on an old phone. You have looked at a performance measurement of
something you built. You have made a change — a smaller image, a removed
library — that made a page faster.

**The next step.** At junior the framework uses the same words, so this is
another skill that deepens with exposure rather than changing shape.

## Legal compliance

Building government services comes with legal duties, and for frontend work one
of them is unusually direct: public sector websites and apps have to meet an
accessibility standard in law. At your level nobody expects you to be
responsible for that, but you should know it exists and know who to ask.

These questions ask about **training and support**, not about whether you get
the law right.
