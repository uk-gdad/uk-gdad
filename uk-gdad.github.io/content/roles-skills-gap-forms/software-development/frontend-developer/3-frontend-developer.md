# Frontend developer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a frontend developer in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, what you have not had the chance to do, and
what support would be most useful over the coming months.

Frontend developer is the level where the framework stops describing your work
as supervised. You build software components that form part of a product, you
write clean and well-tested code that follows web standards, you coach people
junior to you, and — the part most often left implicit — you collaborate closely
with other disciplines to develop designs and working prototypes. That last one
means the job now includes shaping what gets built, not only building it.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. The form takes each of the eleven skills the framework names for
you, explains what it expects and what changes at senior, and asks for a rating
and an example. Expect about 40 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick
boxes tick, and each skill has a rating to choose. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into
whatever your organisation uses for development conversations. Every question
is numbered, so answers travel back on their own. Clear answers empties the
form and deletes the saved copy, which is worth doing on a computer other
people use.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **One named piece of work beats a paragraph of description.**
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: frontend developer.** A frontend developer designs, builds and
improves website software that meets user needs. In this role you write clean,
accessible code following a progressive enhancement approach, create code that
is open by default and easy for others to reuse, design software that creates
meaningful interactions with users, and understand HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

**Your level: frontend developer.** At this level you build software components,
primarily in the form of user interfaces. The framework expects that you:

- collaborate and work closely with other disciplines to develop designs and
  working prototypes based on user needs, in support of organisational aims
- develop software components that form part of a product to meet user needs
- write clean, well-tested code that follows web standards
- coach and mentor more junior colleagues

**Where this level sits.** The frontend developer role has six levels:

1. Apprentice frontend developer
2. Junior frontend developer
3. **Frontend developer** — you are here
4. Senior frontend developer
5. Lead frontend developer
6. Head of frontend development

Your step up is senior frontend developer, and it is a narrower step than it
looks. Seven of the eleven skills below are worded identically at senior:
accessibility, communicating information, community collaboration, modern
development standards, programming and build, prototyping, and — nearly — user
focus. Four change: strategic thinking, systems integration, web performance
optimisation, and leadership and guidance. The rest of the difference sits in
the accountabilities: helping set direction and embed good practice within
teams, making decisions based on research, planning and leading development
across sets of related stories, understanding the whole system, and teaching
others.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Accessibility

### Skill: Accessibility

**What this means.** Making sure what your team builds works for everyone,
including people using screen readers, keyboards, magnification, speech input or
old devices — and, at this level, influencing decisions before they become
accessibility problems.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: build user interfaces
that meet a predefined set of standards; champion accessibility to internal
stakeholders; offer design feedback to mitigate the risk of failing
accessibility testing; and offer recommendations on the best tools and methods
to use when accessibility testing.

**What that looks like here.** You catch problems in a design review rather than
in a test report. You can explain to a product manager why a pattern will fail
and what to do instead. You know which of your tools find real issues and which
produce noise.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior frontend developer. It changes at
lead, where it becomes advocacy to *external* stakeholders and offering design
solutions rather than feedback.

### Skill: Communicating information

**What this means.** Working with people whose priorities differ from yours,
managing what they expect, and handling the conversations that are awkward.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: work collaboratively
in a group and build relationships; identify issues through team health checks
and work with others to address them; manage stakeholder expectations; be
flexible and capable of proactive and reactive communication; and host or
moderate difficult discussions within the team or with senior stakeholders.

**What that looks like here.** You tell people about a delay before they ask.
You have run a conversation where two people wanted incompatible things and
nobody left angry. You adjust how much detail you give depending on who is
listening.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior frontend developer. It changes at
lead: mediating and mending relationships, and speaking for the community to
large audiences inside and outside government.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working well beyond your immediate team, giving feedback
that lasts, and noticing when the way a team works is the actual problem.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work collaboratively
in a group, actively networking with others; adapt feedback to ensure it is
effective and lasting; use your subject to identify problems or issues in the
team dynamic and rectify them; and identify issues through team health checks and
help stimulate the right responses.

**What that looks like here.** You know frontend developers in other teams and
ask them things. Your feedback changes what someone does next time rather than
just this time. You have named a team problem that everyone could feel but
nobody had said.

### Skill: Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Making technical decisions, understanding what follows from
them, and settling disagreements between people who do not report to you.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: understand the
impact of decisions; make decisions characterised by different levels of risk
and complexity; and resolve technical disputes between wider peers and indirect
stakeholders, taking all views into account.

**What that looks like here.** You decide things rather than escalating them by
default. You can say what a decision cost as well as what it bought. You have
ended a technical argument between people who both had a point.

**The next step.** At senior the wording shifts rather than simply rising: making
decisions of *medium* risk and complexity and recommending decisions as risk
increases, plus contributing to best practice guidelines. If the dispute-resolution
part of your job is already heavy, that is worth saying — the published wording
does not capture it at senior.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What this means.** The practices that keep code understandable, testable and
safe to change — version control, small changes, review, automated testing,
continuous integration, working in the open.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you apply modern development
standards *and support others in applying them*. The second half is what makes
this a frontend-developer-level skill rather than a junior one.

**What that looks like here.** Your reviews explain rather than approve. You
have helped a junior colleague see why a change should be split up. Your work is
consistent enough that someone could learn the team's conventions from it.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior frontend developer. It changes at
lead: identifying and applying standards across complex projects and programmes,
and leading others in applying them.

### Skill: Programming and build (frontend developer)

**What this means.** The craft: reviewing specifications, then building, testing
and documenting user interfaces of real complexity with the right standards and
tools.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you collaborate with others
when necessary to review specifications, and use the agreed specifications to
build, test and document user interfaces of medium to high complexity, using the
right standards and tools.

**What that looks like here.** You take an interface that has genuine states —
loading, error, empty, partial, offline — and handle them all. You test at the
level that matters. What you build gets reused by people who did not talk to
you first.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior frontend developer. It changes at
lead: advising on how standards and methods should be applied, providing
technical advice to stakeholders, and setting team standards for tools and
techniques.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What this means.** Building rough versions to learn from, quickly and
disposably, so decisions rest on evidence rather than argument.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you approach prototyping as a
team activity, actively soliciting prototypes and testing them with others;
establish design patterns and iterate them; and use a variety of prototyping
methods, choosing the most appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You have built two versions in a day to settle a
disagreement. You ask designers for rough versions rather than finished ones.
The patterns your team reuses get revised when they stop fitting.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior frontend developer, and at lead.
It changes at head of frontend development, where it becomes coaching others and
looking at strategic service design end to end.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What this means.** Understanding what the organisation is trying to achieve,
and being able to say how your team's work serves it — including when it does
not.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you work within a strategic
context and communicate how activities meet strategic goals, and that you
contribute to the development of strategy and policies.

**What that looks like here.** You can explain your roadmap in terms of what the
organisation is trying to achieve. You have argued against a piece of work
because it did not serve that. You have contributed to a standard or policy
rather than only following one.

**The next step.** At senior this rises considerably: defining strategies and
policies, guiding others on working in the strategic context, and evaluating

### Skill: Systems integration (frontend developer)

**What this means.** How your source becomes what a browser downloads — the
static assets build, delivery over the internet, and testing that the interface
works once it gets there.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you build static assets from
source code and test that the resulting user interface works as expected when
delivered over the internet, and that you work on integration tests and support
configuration of the network part of the system as part of a wider team.

**What that looks like here.** You have changed the build rather than only used
it. You have debugged something that worked locally and failed in production —
caching, compression, a content security policy, a content delivery network. You
write and maintain integration tests.

**The next step.** At senior you *define* the static assets build, co-ordinate
all aspects of the integration, take responsibility for the tests around the
interface, and configure the network part yourself.

### Skill: User focus (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Representing the people who use the service inside the
team, and knowing the difference between what they need and what they ask for.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: collaborate
effectively with user researchers and interaction designers to represent user
needs internally; champion user research so it focuses on all users; and
prioritise and define approaches to understanding the user story, guiding others
in doing so.

**What that looks like here.** You attend research rather than reading the
summary. You have pushed for research with people the team was not thinking
about. You help others frame a user story rather than accepting whatever arrives.

**The next step.** At senior two things are added: explaining the difference
between user needs and user desires, and recommending the best tools and methods
to use.

### Skill: Web performance optimisation

**What this means.** How quickly a page loads and responds, what makes it slow,
and what that costs the people with the worst devices and connections.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: explain the
principles of web performance; explain how poor performance can negatively
affect user experience; help improve web performance; and help identify and
resolve performance issues.

**What that looks like here.** You can read a performance trace and say what is
actually slow rather than what looks slow. You have made a measurable
improvement and know the number before and after. You can explain to a
non-technical stakeholder why an extra script has a cost.

**The next step.** At senior you *identify and resolve* performance issues
yourself, collect data to monitor them, and guide others on the best software
and methods.

directly: public sector websites and apps must meet an accessibility standard in
