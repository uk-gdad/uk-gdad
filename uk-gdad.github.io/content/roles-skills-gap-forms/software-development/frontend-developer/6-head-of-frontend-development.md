# Head of frontend development - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a head of frontend development in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent
it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would
close them.

The framework describes this level as an expert practitioner with broad industry
experience — an expert in many technical areas, or a specialist with very deep
knowledge in one. What it adds beyond lead is not technical. You define and
assure best practice, influence and develop organisational strategy and
priorities with counterparts across government, ensure the right conditions and
environment for frontend developers to work effectively, and develop
departmental capability through internal mobility, career paths and recruitment.
Two of those — conditions, and capability — are jobs about people, and they are
the ones most often left out of a technical leader's development conversation.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the eleven skills gets an explanation, a rating and some
questions. Expect 45 minutes to an hour. The questions are deliberately
uncomfortable in places; that is where the useful answers are.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your
organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources staff, or
people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send
from here — your answers never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your
organisation uses for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so
answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"The organisation does this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name
  your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer** at this level too.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job
  description and not what you were excellent at three years ago.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you, and would stay changed if you left. Several are reasonable
  here. Eleven would be worth interrogating.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Web performance optimisation**
>
> - [x] 2 — Working: I do it with support
>
> *Evidence:* I advocate for performance budgets and they are in our standards,
> but I have not looked at a trace in two years and the coaching is done by two
> of our leads rather than by me.

Named practice, and a clear line about where it has faded. That is more useful
here than a high rating.

## About this role level

**The role: frontend developer.** A frontend developer designs, builds and
improves website software that meets user needs — writing clean, accessible code
following a progressive enhancement approach, creating code that is open by
default and easy to reuse, and understanding HTML, CSS and JavaScript.

**Your level: head of frontend development.** An expert practitioner with broad
industry experience, expert in many technical areas or a deep specialist in one.
The framework expects that you:

- define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring
  others
- influence and develop organisational strategy and priorities, collaborating
  with counterpart colleagues across government
- ensure the right conditions and environment for frontend developers to work
  effectively
- develop departmental capability by facilitating internal mobility, shaping
  career paths and recruiting talent

The third one deserves particular attention, because it is rarely measured.
*Conditions and environment* means the tooling, the access, the time, the
decision-making and the culture that determine whether good frontend developers
can do good work here — or leave.

**There is no level above this one.** The frontend developer role has six levels
and this is the highest. Where people go next is a question about other roles
and professions in the framework, or about leadership outside it, and the
framework does not define that as a next step for this role — so this form does
not invent one. What each skill block gives instead is what the framework asks
of you here, and where it differs from lead, which is published.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Apprentice frontend developer
2. Junior frontend developer
3. Frontend developer
4. Senior frontend developer
5. Lead frontend developer
6. **Head of frontend development** — you are here

Only three skills are worded differently from lead: community collaboration,
which becomes unblocking issues between departments and growing the community
itself; prototyping, which becomes coaching others and strategic service design
end to end; and web performance optimisation, which becomes advocacy and
coaching rather than resolution. The rest of the difference is in the
accountabilities above.

### Skill: Accessibility

**What this means.** Making sure what your organisation builds works for
everyone, and carrying that argument to people who do not report to you and do
not have to listen.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: show a strong
understanding of accessibility and act as an advocate for users with
accessibility needs; build user interfaces that meet a predefined set of
standards; champion accessibility to internal and external stakeholders; offer
design solutions to mitigate the risk of failing accessibility testing; and
recommend the best tools and methods for accessibility testing.

**What that looks like here.** Accessibility is in your standards, your
procurement questions and your definition of done, not only in your rhetoric.
You have argued it with a supplier or a partner department and changed something.
You know which of your services would fail an audit today.

**Same wording as lead frontend developer**, and there is no level above.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Unblocking the things that stop teams and departments
working, and building the professional community rather than only using it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things, and this is one of the

departments at the highest level; and coach the organisation on team dynamics

**What that looks like here.** You have resolved something between departments
that had been stuck for months. You coach other leaders on team dynamics, not
only your own teams. There is a frontend community in your organisation that is
bigger and more active than when you arrived.

**Different from lead frontend developer**, where the skill is about networking,
feedback and team-level dynamics rather than departmental unblocking and
community building.

### Skill: Leadership and guidance (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Leadership in the organisational sense: structures,
strategy, high-risk decisions, consensus between organisations, and the
psychology of how teams work.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Seven things: change organisational
structures to fixable and sustainable designs; lead on the strategy for the whole
organisation, marrying business needs with innovative analysis; make and justify
decisions characterised by high levels of risk, impact and complexity; build
consensus between organisations, public or private, or highly independent
stakeholders; solve and unblock issues between teams or departments at the
highest level; understand the psychology of a team and have strong mediation
skills; and coach the organisation on team dynamics and conflict resolution.

**What that looks like here.** You have changed how teams are arranged and it
held. You have made a decision where being wrong would have been public and
expensive. You have got organisations with different incentives to agree
something and stick to it.

what you actually do; few people do all seven.

### Skill: Programming and build (frontend developer)

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things, and this is another

at a whole service — every channel, every step, including the parts that are not
digital — and prototype how it should work.

**Different from lead frontend developer**, where prototyping is a team activity

### Skill: Systems integration (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Owning the path from source code to what a browser
downloads, across an estate rather than a service.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: define the static
assets build; co-ordinate all aspects of the integration and take responsibility
for the tests around the user interface; and configure the part of the system
that uses the network and test that it works as expected.

**What that looks like here.** The build and delivery approach across your
services is a deliberate design that someone could explain without you. You know
what happens across the estate when a shared dependency — a component library, a
delivery network, an identity provider — degrades.

**Same wording as lead frontend developer**, and there is no level above.

### Skill: User focus (frontend developer)

**What this means.** Representing the people who use the services, including in
rooms where no user has ever been mentioned.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: collaborate with user
researchers and interaction designers to represent user needs internally;
explain the difference between user needs and the desires of the user; champion
user research so it focuses on all users; effectively prioritise and define
approaches to understanding the user story, guiding others to do so; and offer
recommendations on the best tools and methods.

**What that looks like here.** You still watch research at a level where nobody
would notice if you stopped. You protect research capacity in planning rounds.
You keep asking about the users nobody is thinking about long after it has
become tiresome.

### Skill: Web performance optimisation

**What this means.** How quickly services load and respond, and what that costs
the people with the worst devices and connections — at this level, as something
you advocate for and coach rather than fix.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things, and this is the third
skill that changes from lead: advocate for web performance optimisation best
practices; coach and guide others; and actively collect data from multiple tools
to monitor and resolve performance issues.

**What that looks like here.** Performance is in your standards and your
planning, not only in incident reviews. You collect real-user data as well as
laboratory measurements and know where they disagree. Other people diagnose
performance because you taught them how.

**Different from lead frontend developer**, where the skill is identifying and
resolving issues yourself rather than advocating, coaching and monitoring.

## Legal compliance

Government services carry legal duties, and frontend work carries one very
directly: public sector websites and apps must meet an accessibility standard in
law and publish an accessibility statement. At this level you may be the person
whose assurance the organisation relies on. These questions ask about awareness,
training and support — not about whether you can state the law correctly, and
not as a check on your conduct.
