# Principal enterprise architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a principal enterprise architect in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent
it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would
close them.

Principal is the top of this role. The framework has you leading at the highest
level and being responsible for ensuring the strategy is developed, agreed and
followed: networking with senior stakeholders across enterprises, supporting
multiple communities and teams, inspiring other enterprise architects, horizon
scanning for external influences and risks, being responsible for the overall
direction of business and digital capabilities — and being responsible for the
creation, maintenance, and consumption of a Digital Twin.

That last one is unusual, and the framework does not define it further. Taken
plainly, it means a maintained model of the organisation — its capabilities,
systems, data, processes and dependencies — kept current enough that people can
ask questions of it and test changes against it before making them. Whether your
organisation means that, or something narrower, is worth agreeing explicitly
rather than assuming.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Each of the eight skills gets an
explanation, a rating and some questions. Expect 45 minutes to an hour.

| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job
  description and not what you were excellent at three years ago.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you and would stay changed if you left. Several are reasonable
  here; eight would be worth interrogating.

## About this role level

**The role: enterprise architect.** Enterprise architects are leaders working
across different levels of an organisation to translate business strategy into
business change and technical delivery — owning the architecture vision, strategy
and roadmaps across business, technology and data, establishing principles,
policies and standards, developing the architecture community, and horizon
scanning across industry.

**Your level: principal enterprise architect.** At this level you lead at the
highest level and are responsible for ensuring the strategy is developed, agreed
and followed. The framework expects that you:

- network and communicate with senior stakeholders across enterprises, and
  actively seek opportunities for improvement
- support multiple communities and teams
- find and use best practice
- find and use emerging technologies and approaches
- inspire other enterprise architects and help them understand how to meet
  organisational goals
- horizon scan for external influences or risks
- support successful delivery of the long-term strategy
- are responsible for the overall direction of business and digital capabilities
- are responsible for the creation, maintenance and consumption of a Digital Twin

**There is no level above this one.** The enterprise architect role has four
levels and this is the highest. Where people go next is a question about other
roles — chief technology or digital leadership, or leadership outside the
profession — and the framework does not define it as a next step for this role,
so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Enterprise architect
2. Senior enterprise architect
3. Lead enterprise architect
4. **Principal enterprise architect** — you are here

Three skills are yours alone rather than shared with lead: community
collaboration, which becomes unblocking issues between teams or departments at
the highest level and coaching the organisation on dynamics and conflict;
enterprise architecture, which becomes leading others who design, influencing
structures beyond the organisation, and ensuring organisation-wide change aligns
with the strategy; and making architectural decisions, which rises to high risk
and complexity, leading and evolving governance, and representing architectural
governance within wider governance such as legal or commercial. Where you sit
against those three is the real subject of this form.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Enterprise architecture
6. Making architectural decisions
7. Problem definition and shaping
8. Strategy design

## Job skills

Eight skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the framework
expects, what it looks like in practice, and where it differs from lead.

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What this means.** Looking beyond the organisation — at society, industry and
policy — and building architecture that survives what is coming.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: assess how trends in
society and industry practices might impact the organisation; work with people
outside your organisation to inform policies, strategies and standards;
anticipate changes to policy and build resilience through your architectural
work; and coach others in identifying important trends.

**What that looks like here.** You have a view on what will be true in five years
and have committed something to it. You work in cross-government or industry
forums where standards are actually set. Your architecture has absorbed a policy
change that hurt other organisations.

**Same wording as lead enterprise architect**, and there is no level above.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What this means.** Communicating and mediating across organisations, winning
support for high-risk decisions, and teaching others to do it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: communicate with
technical and non-technical stakeholders at all levels and across organisations,
using architecture communication techniques; mediate between people in difficult
architectural discussions; gain support from business and technical stakeholders
for architectural topics with high levels of risk, impact and complexity; and
coach and support others in architecture communication.

**What that looks like here.** You can hold a room of people who disagree and
have authority. You win backing for decisions whose payoff is years away. Your
architects present without needing you there.

**Same wording as lead enterprise architect**, and there is no level above.

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What this means.** Choosing how and from whom the organisation buys, and living
with the consequences for years.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: identify appropriate
contractual frameworks and approaches; and identify, evaluate and select
appropriate suppliers.

**What that looks like here.** You choose the purchasing route as deliberately as
the technology. You have selected a supplier and lived with the result. You know
which of your organisation's contracts are the hardest to leave, and what that
costs in architectural freedom.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Unblocking what is stuck between departments, and coaching
the organisation on how people work together.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things, and this is one of the
three skills that change from lead: solve and unblock issues between teams or
departments at the highest level; and coach the organisation on team dynamics and
conflict resolution, while also building and growing the community.

**What that looks like here.** You have resolved something between departments
that had been stuck for a year. You coach other leaders on conflict, not only
your own architects. The architecture community is bigger and more active than
when you arrived.

**Different from lead**, where the skill is networking, feedback and team-level
dynamics rather than departmental unblocking and building the community itself.

### Skill: Enterprise architecture

**What this means.** Leading the people who design, influencing structures beyond
your own organisation, and keeping organisation-wide change aligned to the
strategy.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: lead others designing
architectures that define the technology, people, process and other elements
needed to achieve organisation objectives; work with others beyond the
organisation to influence organisational structures, technology, people and
processes; and ensure organisation-wide changes align with the architectural
strategy.

**What that looks like here.** You lead architects rather than producing the
architecture. You have influenced how something works outside your own
organisation. Major change comes past the strategy rather than around it — and
you can say how you know.

**Different from lead**, which designs and drives use of its own architecture
rather than leading designers and reaching beyond the organisation.

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What this means.** Owning the highest-risk decisions, and owning the governance
that decides how such decisions get made.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: make and guide
architectural design decisions characterised by high levels of risk and
complexity; identify and address architectural risks across the organisation or
wider government; lead and evolve architectural governance and assurance; and
represent architectural governance as part of wider governance, for example legal
or commercial.

**What that looks like here.** You make the calls where being wrong is public and
expensive. Governance is yours to change, and you have changed it. You sit in
the wider governance — investment, legal, commercial — as the architectural voice
rather than being consulted afterwards.

**Different from lead**, which decides at medium risk and contributes to
governance rather than leading and evolving it.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What this means.** Leading the framing of the hardest problems, and teaching
others to frame their own.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: lead the framing of a
problem characterised by high complexity, complication or risk so that a solution
can be created; coach others in defining problems and describing appropriate
options for solutions; and help others challenge requirements and assumptions and
identify opportunities when defining problems and solution options.

**What that looks like here.** When something is a mess at the top of the
organisation, you are asked to say what the problem actually is. You coach rather
than reframing on people's behalf. You have made challenging assumptions a normal
thing to do rather than a brave one.

**Same wording as lead enterprise architect**, and there is no level above.

### Skill: Strategy design

**What this means.** Defining and connecting strategy across the organisation and
wider government, and clearing the way for it to happen.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: define and connect
strategies or visions across the organisation or wider government; and enable the
implementation of strategies or visions across the organisation or wider
government, for example by advocating for resources and removing blockers.

**What that looks like here.** You connect strategies written by people who never
spoke to each other. You spend credibility moving money and removing blockers.
The strategy is a thing people act on rather than a document that exists.

**Same wording as lead enterprise architect**, and there is no level above.
