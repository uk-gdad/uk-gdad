# Enterprise architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as an enterprise architect in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, what you have not had the chance to do, and
what support would be most useful over the coming months.

Enterprise architect is the first level of this role, and the framework describes
it as supporting other architects in developing the strategy. That word
*supporting* matters: the role's overall description is enormous — owning
architecture vision and roadmaps, establishing principles and standards,
horizon scanning across industry — and at this level you are learning to do that
work alongside people who already do it, not carrying it alone.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the eight skills gets an explanation, a note on what changes
at senior, a rating and some questions. Expect about 45 minutes.

Worth knowing:

- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a
  generous reading of a thin example.
- **Name a real piece of work** rather than describing what you generally do.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: enterprise architect.** Enterprise architects are leaders working
across different levels of an organisation to translate business strategy into
business change and technical delivery. The role's remit includes identifying
priorities for change, leading cross-cutting capabilities, owning the enterprise
architecture vision, strategy and roadmaps across business, technology and data —
including the current state, the target state and the transitions between them —
understanding the organisation's ecosystem and interdependencies, establishing
architectural principles, policies and standards, developing the architecture
community, and horizon scanning across industry.

**Your level: enterprise architect.** At this level you support other architects
in developing the strategy. The framework expects that you:

- network and communicate with stakeholders across domains or enterprises, and
  identify opportunities for improvement
- use best practice
- use emerging technologies and approaches
- learn to identify influences or risks
- play a part in successful delivery of the team's objectives

*Learn to identify influences or risks* is the honest phrasing for this level.
Nobody arrives able to see which external change will matter to their
organisation in two years; it is learned by watching people who can.

**Where this level sits.** The enterprise architect role has four levels:

1. **Enterprise architect** — you are here
2. Senior enterprise architect
3. Lead enterprise architect
4. Principal enterprise architect

Your step up is senior enterprise architect. Four of your eight skills change
there — enterprise architecture, making architectural decisions, problem
definition and shaping, and strategy design — and all four in the same direction:
the support drops away, the risk band rises, and you define rather than
contribute. The other four keep their wording; they change later, at lead.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Enterprise architecture
6. Making architectural decisions
7. Problem definition and shaping
8. Strategy design

## Job skills

Eight skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at senior.

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What this means.** Working for the organisation rather than for your immediate
piece of it, and noticing the slow-moving things that will matter later.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: work to support wider
organisational objectives beyond your immediate goals; track emerging internal
and external issues over time that could affect teams across the organisation;
and take action to solve or mitigate problems by influencing colleagues across
the organisation.

**What that looks like here.** You can name three things happening elsewhere in
the organisation that will affect your area within a year. You have raised
something outside your remit because it needed raising. You influence people who
do not report to anyone you report to.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior enterprise architect. It changes at
lead, where it becomes assessing how trends in society and industry might affect
the organisation, working with people outside it to inform policy, anticipating
policy change, and coaching others in spotting trends.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What this means.** Making architecture understandable and persuasive to people
who will never read a model — and to people who will read it very critically.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: lead the communication
of complicated, complex or risky architecture topics with technical and
non-technical stakeholders; communicate with senior stakeholders across your
organisation; adapt your message and communication techniques to your audience;
advocate on behalf of a team to other stakeholders; and manage stakeholder
expectations effectively.

**What that looks like here.** You can present the same architecture decision to
a delivery team and to a director and have both act on it. You have advocated for
a team's position in a room they were not in. You tell people about a problem
before it arrives.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior. At lead it becomes communicating
across organisations, mediating in difficult architectural discussions, gaining
support for high-risk topics, and coaching others.

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What this means.** Understanding how your organisation buys things, because
architecture decisions become purchases, contracts and dependencies that outlast
the decision.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: understand commercial
processes and the appropriate internal contacts within a government department;
and understand different sourcing strategies and when to apply them.

**What that looks like here.** You know who your commercial colleagues are and
when to involve them — before a design assumes a supplier, not after. You can
explain the difference between building, buying and using something already
bought, and when each makes sense.

**The next step.** Identical wording at senior. At lead it becomes a great deal
more: identifying appropriate contractual frameworks and approaches, and
identifying, evaluating and selecting suppliers.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working well with the people around you, and noticing when
the way a team works is the actual problem.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: contribute to the work
of others; motivate and empower teams; create the right environment for teams to
work in and identify the best team makeup for the situation; and recognise and
deal with issues.

**What that looks like here.** You make other people's work better rather than
only producing your own. You have said something when a team was not working
well. You know which mix of people a piece of architecture work actually needs.

**The next step.** At senior the wording shifts rather than rises: active
networking, adapting feedback so it lasts, using your subject to spot problems in
team dynamics, and stimulating the right responses to issues found in team health
checks.

### Skill: Enterprise architecture

**What this means.** Turning what the organisation is trying to achieve into a
design that covers technology, people and process — not just systems.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: turn an organisation's
goals into clear objectives, with limited direction; and design an architecture
that defines the technology, people, process and other elements needed to achieve
those objectives, with support.

**What that looks like here.** You can take a strategic ambition and say what it
would actually require. Your designs include the people and process changes, not
only the technical ones — which is what makes them enterprise architecture rather
than solution design.

**The next step.** At senior the support drops away, and you also work with
others across the organisation to make sure your design is actually used to
achieve the objectives — designing it is no longer enough.

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What this means.** Making decisions with real consequences, seeing the risks
they carry, and working within the governance that exists.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: work with others to
make architectural design decisions characterised by managed levels of risk and
complexity; identify and address architectural risks relevant to your team or
domain — business, data or security, for example; and engage with architectural
governance and assurance to manage decisions and risks, with support.

**What that looks like here.** You decide with others rather than alone, and the
reasoning is written down. You can name the architectural risks in your domain.
You take things to governance before they are irreversible.

**The next step.** At senior you *make and guide* decisions of medium risk,
address risks affecting multiple teams or domains, use governance without
support, and contribute to developing that governance.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What this means.** Turning a mess into a stated problem, and offering options
rather than a single answer.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: help to frame a problem
characterised by managed levels of complexity, complication or risk so that a
solution can be created; and help to create options for solving problems at an
appropriate level of detail.

**What that looks like here.** You can take a complaint or an ambition and turn
it into a problem statement people recognise. You produce options with
trade-offs rather than a recommendation dressed as the only possibility.

**The next step.** At senior you frame problems of medium complexity yourself,
produce architectural representations that give different teams a shared
understanding, and describe options so that delivery methods can be chosen.

### Skill: Strategy design

**What this means.** Helping build the direction the organisation will follow,
and the principles and roadmaps that carry it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: support the
development of a strategy or vision that aligns with organisational objectives;
challenge requirements and assumptions and identify opportunities to develop
strategy; support the implementation of a strategy or vision, for example through
a roadmap or plan; and use architectural principles, patterns and constraints
when appropriate.

**What that looks like here.** You have contributed to a strategy rather than
only read one. You ask the question that exposes an assumption nobody had tested.
You use the principles that exist rather than inventing your own each time.

**The next step.** At senior you *define* strategies or visions across teams,
direct implementation through roadmaps and plans, define architectural principles
and patterns, and maintain strategy in response to feedback.

## Legal compliance

Enterprise architecture decisions carry legal weight: what data services hold,
how accessible they are, how technology is bought, and how organisational change
affects people. These questions ask about awareness, training and support, not
about whether you can state the law correctly, and not as a check on your
conduct.
