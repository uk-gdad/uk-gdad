# Senior enterprise architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior enterprise architect in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. It has come from human resources or your
manager, and it exists to establish where the gap sits between what you do now
and what the organisation needs from someone at this level.

The framework describes a senior enterprise architect as supporting lead
architects in making sure the strategy is developed, agreed and *followed*. That
last word is where the work actually is. Strategies are agreed all the time;
whether anything is built differently as a result is a separate question, and at
this level it becomes partly yours.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the eight skills gets an explanation, a note on what changes
at lead, a rating and some questions. Expect about 45 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational
purposes. If you intend to use it in any way, please consult with your

## About this role level

**The role: enterprise architect.** Enterprise architects are leaders working
across different levels of an organisation to translate business strategy into
business change and technical delivery — owning the architecture vision, strategy
and roadmaps across business, technology and data, establishing principles,
policies and standards, developing the architecture community, and horizon
scanning across industry.

**Your level: senior enterprise architect.** At this level you support lead
architects in ensuring the strategy is developed, agreed and followed. The
framework expects that you:

- network and communicate with stakeholders across domains or enterprises
- identify opportunities for improvement
- support a community or team
- use best practice
- use emerging technologies and approaches
- play an active part in the team and understand how to deliver the team's
  objectives to meet organisational goals
- identify influences or risks
- play a part in successful delivery of the long-term strategy

Notice the change from the level below: identifying influences and risks is no
longer something you are *learning* to do. It is expected.

**Where this level sits.** The enterprise architect role has four levels:

1. Enterprise architect
2. **Senior enterprise architect** — you are here
3. Lead enterprise architect
4. Principal enterprise architect

Your step up is lead enterprise architect, where four skills change and the
direction is outward. Architect for the whole context moves from tracking issues
inside the organisation to assessing societal and industry trends, working
outside the organisation to inform policy, and anticipating policy change.
Commercial perspective moves furthest of all — from understanding sourcing to
identifying contractual frameworks and selecting suppliers. Architecture
communication adds mediation and coaching, and problem definition moves to
leading the framing of high-complexity problems and coaching others.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Enterprise architecture
6. Making architectural decisions
7. Problem definition and shaping
8. Strategy design

## Job skills

Eight skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at lead.

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What this means.** Working for the organisation rather than your own patch, and
tracking the slow-moving issues that will matter later.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: work to support wider
organisational objectives beyond your immediate goals; track emerging internal
and external issues over time that could affect teams across the organisation;
and take action to solve or mitigate problems by influencing colleagues across
the organisation.

**What that looks like here.** You have given up something your area wanted
because the organisation needed something else. You track issues over time rather
than reacting to them. You get things changed by influence, having no authority
over the people involved.

**The next step.** At lead this turns outward: assessing how trends in society
and industry practice might affect the organisation, working with people outside
it to inform policies, strategies and standards, anticipating policy change and
building resilience for it, and coaching others in spotting trends.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What this means.** Making architecture understandable and persuasive to
whoever is in the room, including when the news is unwelcome.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: lead the communication
of complicated, complex or risky architecture topics with technical and
non-technical stakeholders; communicate with senior stakeholders across your
organisation; adapt your message and communication techniques to your audience;
advocate on behalf of a team to other stakeholders; and manage stakeholder
expectations effectively.

**What that looks like here.** You lead the conversation on the difficult topics
rather than briefing someone else to. Senior people take your architectural
concerns seriously because you have been right before and said so plainly. You
manage expectations rather than absorbing them.

**The next step.** At lead it becomes communicating *across organisations* using
architecture communication techniques, mediating in difficult architectural
discussions, gaining support for topics with high risk and complexity, and
coaching others.

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What this means.** Understanding how your organisation buys, because
architecture decisions become contracts and dependencies that outlive them.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: understand commercial
processes and the appropriate internal contacts within a government department;
and understand different sourcing strategies and when to apply them. This is the
same wording as the level below, so the framework does not raise this expectation
until lead.

**What that looks like here.** You involve commercial colleagues before a design
assumes an answer. You can say what your existing contracts constrain — and what
they will cost to leave.

**The next step.** The largest change at lead: identifying appropriate
contractual frameworks and approaches, and identifying, evaluating and selecting
suppliers. If you are heading for lead, this is the skill with the furthest to
travel, and it is one you cannot practise alone.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working across teams and beyond them, giving feedback that
lasts, and dealing with the dynamics that stop work happening.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work collaboratively
in a group, actively networking with others; adapt feedback to ensure it is
effective and lasting; use your subject to identify problems or issues in team
dynamics and rectify them; and identify issues through team health checks and
help stimulate the right responses.

**What that looks like here.** Your network reaches beyond the people you must
work with. Feedback you give changes behaviour rather than only being received.
You have used an architectural conversation to surface a problem that was
actually about how a team worked.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead. It changes at principal, where it
becomes unblocking issues between teams or departments at the highest level and
coaching the organisation on team dynamics while building the community.

### Skill: Enterprise architecture

**What this means.** Designing how the organisation achieves its objectives
across technology, people and process — and making sure the design is actually
used.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: design an architecture
that defines the technology, people, process and other elements needed to achieve
organisation objectives; and work with others across the organisation to ensure
your design is used to achieve those objectives.

**What that looks like here.** Your architecture covers the operating model, not
only the systems. And you follow it into delivery — the second bullet is the one
that separates architecture that changes things from architecture that gets
approved.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead. It changes at principal, where you
lead others designing architectures, work beyond the organisation to influence
structures and processes, and ensure organisation-wide change aligns with the
architectural strategy.

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What this means.** Making and guiding consequential decisions, and using
governance to manage risk rather than to slow things down.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: make and guide
architectural design decisions characterised by medium risk and complexity;
identify and address architectural risks that affect multiple teams or domains;
use architectural governance and assurance to make design decisions and manage
technical risks at the appropriate level; and contribute to the development of
architectural governance and assurance.

**What that looks like here.** You decide and you also help others decide. You
can name the risks that cross domain boundaries — usually the ones nobody owns.
You have improved a governance process rather than only complying with one.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead. At principal it becomes high risk
and complexity, risks across the organisation or wider government, leading and
evolving governance, and representing architectural governance within wider
governance such as legal or commercial.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What this means.** Turning a mess into a stated problem, representing it so
different teams share an understanding, and setting out real options.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: frame a problem
characterised by medium complexity, complication or risk so that a solution can
be created; produce architectural representations that enable different teams to
have a shared understanding of problems throughout the life cycle; and describe
options for solving problems so that appropriate delivery methods can be decided.

**What that looks like here.** Your framing survives contact with the teams who
have to act on it. Your diagrams are used by people who were not in the room when
they were drawn. Your options include what each would mean for how the work gets
delivered, not only for the technology.

**The next step.** At lead you lead the framing of *high* complexity problems,
coach others in defining problems and describing options, and help others
challenge requirements and assumptions.

### Skill: Strategy design

**What this means.** Defining direction across teams, and the principles,
patterns and roadmaps that make it followable.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: define strategies or
visions across teams that align with organisational objectives; direct the
implementation of a strategy or vision, for example by creating roadmaps or
plans; define architectural principles and patterns; and develop or maintain
strategy in response to feedback and findings.

**What that looks like here.** You have written a strategy that teams work to.
The principles your organisation uses include ones you defined, and people can
say why each exists. When something is learned, the strategy changes rather than
the learning being noted.

**The next step.** At lead you define and *connect* strategies across the
organisation or wider government, and enable their implementation by advocating
for resources and removing blockers — the strategy stops being a document and
becomes a campaign.

## Legal compliance

how accessible they are, how technology is bought, and how organisational change
affects people. These questions ask about awareness, training and support, not
about whether you can state the law correctly, and not as a check on your
conduct.
