# Chief data architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a chief data architect in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

Chief is the top of this role. The framework has you setting the vision for the
organisation's use of data as directed by the appropriate governance body:
overseeing the design of multiple data models, accountable for supporting and
aligning to the data strategy, championing data architecture internally and at
the most senior levels across government, setting the standards and ways of
working for the data architecture community, accountable for assuring data models
at project and enterprise level, advising project teams, overseeing the full data
product life cycle, and responsible for making sure the organisation's systems
are designed in line with the enterprise data architecture.

That is a long list, and much of it is accountability for work other people do.
The useful answers below are the ones about what you actually do, not what you
are answerable for.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Each of the ten skills gets an
explanation, a rating and some questions. Expect 45 minutes to an hour.

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job
  description and not what you were excellent at three years ago.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you and would stay changed if you left. Several are reasonable
  here; ten would be worth interrogating.

## About this role level

**The role: data architect.** A data architect sets the vision for the
organisation's use of data, through data design, so that data is managed properly
and meets the organisation's needs.

**Your level: chief data architect.** At this level you set that vision, as
directed by the appropriate governance body. The framework expects that you:

- oversee the design of multiple data models and have a broad understanding of
  how each fulfils the needs of the organisation
- are accountable for supporting and aligning to the organisation's data strategy
- champion data architecture internally and by collaborating and communicating at
  the most senior levels across government
- set the standards and ways of working for the data architecture community
- are accountable for assuring data models at project or enterprise level
- provide advice to project teams and oversee the management of the full data
  product life cycle
- are responsible for ensuring the organisation's systems are designed in
  accordance with the enterprise data architecture

**There is no level above this one.** The data architect role has three levels
and this is the highest. Where people go next is a question about other roles and
professions — enterprise architecture, or data leadership outside this
profession — and the framework does not define it as a next step for this role,
so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Data architect
2. Senior data architect
3. **Chief data architect** — you are here

One thing to notice about the published skills at this level. *Data analysis and
synthesis* is worded identically at all three levels — profiling and source
system analysis, presenting clear insights. The framework does not release you
from doing it. In practice this is the skill that fades fastest at chief level,
and it is the one the questions below press on hardest.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating between the technical and non-technical
2. Communicating data
3. Data analysis and synthesis
4. Data governance (data architect)
5. Data innovation
6. Data modelling
7. Data standards
8. Metadata management
9. Problem management
10. Strategic thinking

### Skill: Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**What this means.** Being understood by people who do not think in data terms,
and interpreting what they mean when they describe what they need.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: listen to and
interpret the needs of technical and non-technical stakeholders and manage their
expectations; manage active and reactive communication; and support or host
difficult discussions within the team or with diverse senior stakeholders.

**What that looks like here.** You can turn a permanent secretary's ambition into
a data question without losing what was meant. You tell people about problems
before they discover them. You host the conversations nobody wants to chair.

**Same wording as senior data architect**, and there is no level above.

### Skill: Communicating data

**What this means.** Turning complexity into something that can be acted on, and
being honest about what is uncertain.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: turn complex data
into clear and well understood solutions which can be acted upon; share data
communication skills with the team and the organisation; and understand and
communicate different options, taking risks and uncertainties into account.

**What that looks like here.** Decisions get made on what you present. You teach
others to present rather than presenting for them. When you offer options, the
risks are stated plainly enough that nobody can later claim they were not warned.

**Different from senior data architect**, where the skill is choosing media and
shaping for the audience rather than producing actionable solutions and building
the capability in others.

### Skill: Data analysis and synthesis

**What this means.** Finding out what data actually contains — profiling and
source system analysis — and presenting insight colleagues can use.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things, and they are the same
two the framework expects of a data architect three levels below: undertake data
profiling and source system analysis; and present clear insights to colleagues to
support the end use of the data.

**What that looks like here.** You can still read a profiling result critically
and know what has been left out. You have looked at real data recently enough to
know whether what people tell you about it is true.

**No change anywhere in this role.** The framework never releases you from this
skill, which makes it the honest test of whether your expertise is current or
remembered.

### Skill: Data governance (data architect)

**What this means.** Making governance serve the organisation's direction, fit
with how the rest of the organisation is governed, and actually reduce risk.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: ensure data
governance supports changes to the organisational strategy; align data governance
with wider governance, for example budget; and assure corporate services by
understanding important risks and providing mitigation through assurance
mechanisms.

**What that looks like here.** When the strategy changes, governance changes with
it rather than lagging a year behind. Data decisions and money decisions happen
in the same conversation. You can name the top data risks and the mechanism that
mitigates each.

**Different from senior data architect**, which evolves and defines governance
and assures across business services rather than aligning it with strategy and
wider governance.

### Skill: Data innovation

**What this means.** Judging what is worth adopting and — the harder part — when.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you identify areas of
innovation in data tools and techniques, and recognise the appropriate timing for
adoption.

**What that looks like here.** You have said "not yet" to something good and been
right. You have also said "now" and moved before the organisation was
comfortable. You can explain the timing judgement, not just the technology.

**Different from senior data architect**, which understands the impact of
emerging trends rather than deciding when to act on them.

### Skill: Data modelling

**What this means.** Modelling itself, plus deciding how the organisation
organises its models and where they should align with others.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: understand the
concepts and principles of data modelling and produce relevant data models; work
across government and industry, recognising opportunities for reuse and alignment
of models in different organisations; and design the method to categorise data
models within the organisation.

**What that looks like here.** You still model when it matters. You know where
another department or an industry standard has already solved something you were
about to repeat. There is a way of organising models that you designed, and
people can find things in it.

**Different from senior data architect**, which models across subject areas and
aligns models internally — yours reaches outside the organisation and defines the
categorisation itself.

### Skill: Data standards

**What this means.** Deciding where standards are needed at all, setting them for
the organisation, and overseeing whether they hold — including in the wider
government context.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: create data standards
for the organisation; advocate for and oversee compliance with data policies and
standards; and decide where standards need to be set across the organisation, and
how to set them in the wider context of government.

**What that looks like here.** You have decided *not* to standardise something,
deliberately. Compliance is overseen rather than assumed. Where a cross-government
standard exists you adopt it instead of writing your own.

**Different from senior data architect**, which creates standards for subjects and
monitors compliance — yours decides where standards belong at all.

### Skill: Metadata management

**What this means.** Making the case for metadata, showing where it helps, and
governing it so it stays true.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: identify how metadata
repositories can support different areas of the organisation; communicate the
value of metadata repositories; and set up robust governance processes to keep
repositories up to date.

**What that looks like here.** Areas outside the data team use the repository
because you showed them what it answers. There is a process keeping it current
that does not depend on goodwill. You can make the value case in money or risk
terms.

**Different from senior data architect**, which designs repositories and advises
colleagues — yours is about reach, value and governance.

### Skill: Problem management

**What this means.** Anticipating problems, seeing where they fit in the bigger
picture, and building the ability to solve them in other people.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: anticipate problems
and defend against them at the right time; understand how a problem fits into the
larger picture; identify and describe problems, and help others to describe them;
and build problem-solving capabilities in others.

**What that looks like here.** You have spent effort on a problem before it
arrived and been able to justify it. You help people state a problem precisely
rather than solving it for them. Your architects diagnose better than they did a
year ago.

**Different from senior data architect**, which co-ordinates investigation and
prevention — yours is anticipation and capability building.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What this means.** Defining the direction others work to, and checking whether
it delivers.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: define strategies and
policies, providing guidance to others on working in the strategic context; and
evaluate current strategies to ensure business requirements are being met and
exceeded where possible.

**What that looks like here.** There is a data strategy, you wrote or shaped it,
and people outside the data function can describe it. It gets evaluated on a
rhythm. You have stopped something because the evaluation said so.

**Different from senior data architect**, which works within the strategic
context and contributes to strategy rather than defining and evaluating it.
