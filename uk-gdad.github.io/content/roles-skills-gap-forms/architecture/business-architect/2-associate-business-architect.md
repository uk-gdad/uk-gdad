# Associate business architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as an associate business architect in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent
it so that you can set out what you do now, what you have not had the chance to
do, and what support would be most useful over the coming months.

Associate is the level where you start producing rather than supporting. The
framework expects you to apply architectural methods following direction and
wider strategy, break down complexity into clear outputs, explain complex
business problems in plain language and artefacts, bring disconnected
information together to inform decisions, and recognise issues that cut across
different parts of the organisation. That last one is the distinctive part of
business architecture: seeing the thing nobody owns because it falls between
three directorates.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the seven skills gets an explanation, a note on what
changes at the next level, a rating and some questions. Expect about 35 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: business architect.** A business architect develops an integrated
view of an enterprise — a business area or a whole organisation — and uses that
view to help the organisation put its strategy in place. In this role you apply
business architectural principles, techniques and tools to business challenges;
make sure design work from projects and programmes aligns with strategy; make
sure business change is implemented effectively; aggregate and disaggregate
complex information; and communicate it to stakeholders.

**Your level: associate business architect.** An associate business architect
uses their knowledge and experience to address business scenarios, and continues
to get support and training. The framework expects that you:

- apply architectural methods following direction and wider strategy to produce
  outputs
- break down complexity and produce clear outputs
- explain complex business problems using clear language and artefacts
- bring together disconnected information to inform decision making
- recognise issues that cut across different areas of the organisation and
  present opportunities for improvement

**Where this level sits.** The business architect role has four levels:

1. Trainee business architect
2. **Associate business architect** — you are here
3. Business architect
4. Lead business architect

Your next step is business architect. A new skill appears there — strategic
design and business change — and the pattern across the rest is consistent: from
producing outputs to guiding how the organisation produces them. Communicating
information makes the largest jump, to mediating between people, mending
relationships and speaking for the community to large audiences.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Business architecture
2. Communicating information
3. Consultancy
4. Making and guiding decisions
5. Methods and tools
6. Stakeholder relationship management
7. Strategic thinking

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what that looks like in practice, and what changes at business architect.

### Skill: Business architecture

**What this means.** Describing how the organisation works in a structured way,
so people can reason about changing it — and making that description useful
enough that decisions actually rest on it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: help support the
adoption of business architecture; clearly communicate frameworks, principles
and techniques; support the production of a multidimensional view of your
organisation; and assess the impact of strategies on the organisation,
highlighting risks and issues.

**What that looks like here.** You can explain your framework to someone who
thinks architecture is a waste of time, without jargon. You have produced part of
a model that other people use. You have read a strategy and said, specifically,
what it would mean for capabilities, processes or information — including what
looks risky.

**The next step.** At business architect you apply *and develop* frameworks,
advise and guide the organisation on implementing its strategy, share the value
of business architecture, and produce clear multidimensional views to support
decision making.

### Skill: Communicating information

**What this means.** Working with people whose priorities differ from yours,
managing what they expect, and running the conversations that are awkward.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: work collaboratively
and build relationships; identify issues through team health checks and work
with others to address them; manage stakeholder expectations; be flexible and
capable of proactive and reactive communication; and host or moderate difficult
discussions within the team or with senior stakeholders.

**What that looks like here.** You tell people about a problem before they
discover it. You have run a conversation where two areas wanted incompatible
things. You pitch the same finding differently for a delivery team and for a
director without changing what is true.

**The next step.** The largest jump at the next level: mediating between people
and mending relationships, hosting discussions about high risk and complexity
under constrained timescales, and speaking for the community to large audiences
inside and outside government.

### Skill: Consultancy

**What this means.** Being asked for advice and giving it well: finding the real
problem, framing it so others can see it, and recommending an approach people
can act on.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: provide advice,
guidance and recommendations based on your own specialist knowledge and
experience; propose methodologies to follow and approaches to implementation; and
frame problems so they can easily be understood, troubleshooting where required,
to support the business in operating more effectively.

**What that looks like here.** You give advice in your own name rather than
relaying someone else's. You propose *how* to approach a problem, not just what
the answer might be. You have reframed a problem so that a stuck conversation
moved.

**The next step.** At business architect you advise on the basis of significant
specialist knowledge, and you lead the definition of guidance — informing how the
whole organisation approaches delivery or uses digital and data skills.

### Skill: Making and guiding decisions

**What this means.** Making the decisions that are yours, recommending the ones
that are not, and settling technical disagreements between people who do not
report to you.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: make decisions
characterised by managed levels of risk and complexity, and recommend decisions
as risk and complexity increase; and resolve technical disputes between wider
peers and indirect stakeholders, considering all views and opinions.

**What that looks like here.** You decide within your remit rather than
escalating by default. When you escalate, you bring a recommendation. You have
ended a disagreement between two areas by getting both positions stated
accurately.

**The next step.** At business architect the risk band rises to medium, and you
build consensus between services or independent stakeholders rather than
resolving disputes between them.

### Skill: Methods and tools

**What this means.** The techniques and software used to plan, analyse, develop,
test and improve systems and services — modelling notations, mapping techniques,
architecture repositories, workshop methods.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you select and use the most
appropriate tools and techniques to support planning, analysis, development,
testing, implementation and improvement of systems and services *within a
project*.

**What that looks like here.** You choose the technique to fit the question
rather than reaching for the same one each time. You can say why a value stream
map suited one problem and a capability model another. You know which of your
tools produce artefacts people actually read.

**The next step.** At business architect you build capability in methods and
tools across the organisation, ensuring they are adopted consistently and used
effectively.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What this means.** Knowing who has an interest in what you are doing, keeping
them informed in the way that suits them, and building enough trust that they
tell you things early. This skill is new at your level.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: identify important
stakeholders and communicate with them clearly and regularly; tailor
communication to their needs and work with them to build relationships while
meeting user needs; build and reach consensus with stakeholders; and work to
improve stakeholder relationships using evidence to explain decisions.

**What that looks like here.** You could list your stakeholders and say what each
one cares about. People tell you about changes before they are announced. You
have used evidence rather than authority to bring someone round.

**The next step.** At business architect this becomes working with the team to
develop and maintain an understanding of stakeholders, implementing
communications strategies, resolving issues and influencing, and building
long-term strategic relationships.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What this means.** Understanding what the organisation is trying to achieve,
being able to say how work serves it, and starting to shape the strategy rather
than only reading it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: work within a strategic
context and communicate how activities meet strategic goals; and contribute to
the development of strategy and policies.

**What that looks like here.** You can trace a piece of work to a strategic aim
in a way a director would recognise. You have contributed wording, evidence or
analysis to a strategy or policy. You have said when something does *not* serve
the strategy.

**The next step.** At business architect you define strategies and policies and
guide others on working in the strategic context, and you evaluate current
strategies to check business requirements are being met.
