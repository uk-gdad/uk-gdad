# Security architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a security architect in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, what you have not had the chance to do, and
what support would be most useful over the coming months.

Security architect is the first level of this role, and it is not a junior job.
You create and design security for systems and services, maintain the security
documentation, and develop architecture patterns and security approaches for new
technologies. You recommend controls, give specialist advice across teams, assess
the risk of proposed changes, influence people to apply security principles, and
review other people's work.

The distinctive difficulty of this job is that you are usually advising people
who can overrule you, about risks that may never materialise. Several questions
below are about that.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked, and no answer is compared with a
colleague's. Each of the seven skills gets an explanation, a note on what changes
at lead, a rating and some questions. Expect about 45 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: security architect.** A security architect designs and builds secure
solutions.

**Your level: security architect.** At this level you create and design security
for a system or service, maintain security documentation, and develop
architecture patterns and security approaches for new technologies. The framework
expects that you:

- recommend security controls and identify solutions that support a business
  objective
- provide specialist advice and recommend approaches across teams and various
  stakeholders
- communicate widely with other stakeholders
- advise on important security-related technologies and assess the risk
  associated with proposed changes
- inspire and influence others to execute security principles
- help review other people's work

*Support a business objective* is the phrase that separates a useful security
architect from an obstructive one. Controls that make the objective unreachable
are not secure; they just move the risk to whatever people do instead.

**Where this level sits.** The security architect role has three levels:

1. **Security architect** — you are here
2. Lead security architect
3. Principal security architect

Your step up is lead security architect. Six of your seven skills change there.
The clearest single change is in designing secure systems: you move from applying
patterns and principles to *developing* them. Security technology makes the
biggest jump, adding explaining how exploitable a vulnerability is, and leading
and influencing security technology in the industry. Risk work moves from
describing methodologies to applying them proportionately at higher impact.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Analysis
2. Communication (security architect)
3. Designing secure systems
4. Enabling and informing risk-based decisions
5. Research and innovation
6. Security technology
7. Understanding security implications of transformation

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at lead.

### Skill: Analysis

**What this means.** Working out what a problem really is and whether a proposed
solution actually addresses it — with enough rigour that the answer holds.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: apply the approach to
real problems and consider all relevant information; and apply appropriate rigour
to ensure a full solution is designed and achieves the business outcome.

**What that looks like here.** You look at the whole picture rather than the part
you were shown. You notice when a proposed control addresses the symptom and
leaves the exposure. You are rigorous without being exhaustive, because a review
that never finishes protects nobody.

**The next step.** At lead you monitor analysis and ensure it is reused for
similar problems, review solutions and identify areas for change, drive the
collection of the information being analysed, and feed back on policy and
requirements.

### Skill: Communication (security architect)

**What this means.** Explaining security and risk to people who do not share your
training, holding up under challenge, and getting to a decision people will
actually follow.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: demonstrate a deep
understanding of security concepts and apply them at a technical level;
effectively translate and accurately communicate security and risk implications
to technical and non-technical stakeholders; successfully respond to challenges;
and manage stakeholder expectations, adapting flexibly to reach consensus.

**What that looks like here.** You can explain a risk in terms of what would
happen to real people and real services, not in terms of a rating. When someone
pushes back, you engage rather than retreating into authority. You reach
agreements that survive contact with delivery pressure.

**The next step.** Identical wording at lead security architect. It changes at
principal: expert understanding at the highest levels of risk complexity,
communicating at the most senior levels, and managing expectations under high
risk and constrained timescales.

### Skill: Designing secure systems

**What this means.** Building security into system architecture by applying
established patterns and principles rather than inventing protection each time.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you design and review system
architectures through the *application* of patterns and principles.

**What that looks like here.** You reach for a known pattern and can say why it
fits and where it does not. Your reviews find the design problem rather than the
missing document. What you design can be operated by the people who will have to
run it.

**The next step.** At lead this single word changes: you design and review
architectures through the *development* of patterns and principles. You stop
consuming the patterns and start making them.

### Skill: Enabling and informing risk-based decisions

**What this means.** Helping the people who own risk decide well — which means
explaining impact honestly and accepting that the decision is theirs.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: work with risk owners
to advise and give feedback; advise on risk impact and whether it is within risk
tolerance; and describe different risk methodologies, how they are applied, and
the proportionality of risk.

**What that looks like here.** You know who actually owns each risk in your area
— often the hardest question to answer. You advise rather than decide, and you
say plainly when something is outside tolerance. You can size the assurance to
the risk instead of applying the same process to everything.

**The next step.** At lead you work with higher impact and more complex risks,
and you *apply* different risk methodologies in proportion to the risk rather
than describing them.

### Skill: Research and innovation

**What this means.** Keeping up with how security properties of technology are
changing, and working out what that means for your organisation.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: advise on developments
to security properties in technology; and identify new technologies and design
their use in a business context.

**What that looks like here.** You can say what a new technology changes about
your threat picture, not just what it does. You have designed the safe use of
something new rather than refusing it. You read or attend something beyond your
own estate.

**The next step.** At lead you *contribute to and inform* developments rather
than advising on them, design the use of new technology across the organisation,
and engage with the broader security community.

### Skill: Security technology

**What this means.** Knowing the technology itself — how systems work, where the
weaknesses are, and what a vulnerability actually means for a given design.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: explain the effect of
vulnerabilities on current and future designs; and share information on a range
of systems, though you may specialise in one.

**What that looks like here.** When a vulnerability is announced, you can say
what it means for your designs rather than forwarding the advisory. You have
breadth across several system types and depth in at least one. You share what you
know rather than holding it.

**The next step.** The biggest jump at lead: explaining complex system
architectures, identifying and explaining *how easy or difficult a vulnerability
is to exploit*, and leading and influencing security technology in the industry.
That last one takes you outside your organisation.

### Skill: Understanding security implications of transformation

**What this means.** Seeing what a change to how the organisation works means for
security — including the policy, legal and political consequences, not only the
technical ones.

**What the framework expects at this level.** That you interpret and apply an
understanding of policy and process, business architecture, and legal and
political implications, to assist the development of technical solutions or
controls.

**What that looks like here.** You can say what a proposed transformation does to
the threat picture and to the organisation's obligations. You know which of your
controls exist because of a policy or a legal duty rather than a technical
judgement — and can tell the difference.

**The next step.** At lead you interpret and apply this understanding across a
complex area, and you *start influencing* policy and process, business
architecture and legal and political implications rather than only responding to
them.

## Legal compliance

Security architecture is unusual: legal and policy implications are named
*inside* one of your skills. What you design carries statutory duties, government
security policy, and — in one important respect — the boundary of what anyone may
lawfully do to a computer system.

These questions ask about awareness, training and support, not about whether you
can state the law correctly, and not as a check on your conduct.
