# Lead solution architect - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead solution architect in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent
it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would
close them.

The framework describes a lead solution architect as responsible for a group of
solution architecture projects, or a single more complex area, possibly leading
teams of more junior solution architects. You build relationships with senior
stakeholders across multiple business or technical areas, identify improvement
opportunities proactively, support multiple projects or programmes, find and use
emerging technologies, and *develop* best practice for solution architecture
rather than following it.

Six of your eight skills change at this level compared with senior. The largest
is commercial: you now identify contractual frameworks and select suppliers,
which is a different kind of responsibility from anything below it — the
consequences last years and are difficult to unwind.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Each of the eight skills gets an
explanation, a note on what changes at principal, a rating and some questions.
Expect about 45 minutes.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"The teams do this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer** at this level too.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they
  are used for, or how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: solution architect.** A solution architect designs solutions for
problems that affect the organisation — defining the problem and outcomes,
scoping to requirements, working within constraints such as legislation and
finance, designing and documenting solutions that can be implemented, complying
with standards and governance, and managing risks and decisions transparently.

**Your level: lead solution architect.** At this level you are responsible for a
group of solution architecture projects, or a single more complex area, and you
may lead teams of more junior solution architects. The framework expects that
you:

- build relationships with senior stakeholders across multiple business or
  technical areas
- are proactive in identifying opportunities to improve the organisation
- support multiple architecture projects or programmes
- find and use emerging technologies and approaches
- develop best practice for solution architecture

**Where this level sits.** The solution architect role has five levels:

1. Associate solution architect
2. Solution architect
3. Senior solution architect
4. **Lead solution architect** — you are here
5. Principal solution architect

Your step up is principal solution architect. Four skills change: architect for
the whole context turns outward to societal and industry trends and to informing
policy beyond your organisation; community collaboration becomes unblocking
issues between departments at the highest level and coaching the organisation on
dynamics; making architectural decisions rises to high risk, risks across the
organisation or wider government, and leading and evolving governance; and
strategy design becomes defining and connecting strategies across the
organisation or wider government. The principal accountabilities add working with
technology partners to inform their roadmaps.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Architect for the whole context
2. Architecture communication
3. Commercial perspective
4. Community collaboration
5. Making architectural decisions
6. Problem definition and shaping
7. Strategy design
8. Technical design throughout the life cycle

## Job skills

Eight skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects,
what it looks like in practice, and what changes at principal.

### Skill: Architect for the whole context

**What this means.** Working for the organisation rather than your own group of
projects, and tracking the slow-moving things that will matter later.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: work to support wider
organisational objectives beyond your immediate goals; track emerging internal and
external issues over time that could affect the work of teams across the
organisation; and take action to solve or mitigate problems by influencing
colleagues across the organisation.

**What that looks like here.** You have given up something your projects wanted
because the organisation needed something else. You track issues rather than
reacting to them. You change things by influence, having no authority over the
people involved.

**The next step.** At principal this turns outward: assessing how trends in
society and industry might affect the organisation, working with people outside
it to inform policies and standards, anticipating policy change, and coaching
others in spotting trends.

### Skill: Architecture communication

**What this means.** Communicating and mediating across organisations, winning
support for high-risk decisions, and teaching others to do it.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: communicate with
technical and non-technical stakeholders at all levels and across organisations,
using architecture communication techniques; mediate between people in difficult
architectural discussions; gain support from business and technical stakeholders
for architectural topics with high levels of risk, impact and complexity; and
coach and support others in architecture communication.

**What that looks like here.** You have mediated where both parties had authority
and neither had to concede. You have won backing for something expensive whose
benefit arrives later. Your architects present better because you coached them
rather than rewriting their material.

### Skill: Commercial perspective

**What this means.** Choosing how and from whom your organisation buys — the
frameworks, the approaches, and the suppliers.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things, and this is the largest
change at your level: identify appropriate contractual frameworks and approaches;
and identify, evaluate and select appropriate suppliers.

**What that looks like here.** You choose the purchasing route as deliberately as
the technology, knowing what each costs in time and flexibility. You have written
evaluation criteria that discriminated on things that mattered. You can tell when
a requirement has been written around one product — including when you wrote it.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What this means.** Working across teams, giving feedback that lasts, and
dealing with the dynamics that stop work happening.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: work collaboratively
in a group, actively networking with others; adapt feedback to ensure it is
effective and lasting; use your subject to identify problems or issues in the
team dynamic and rectify them; and identify issues through team health checks and
help stimulate the right responses.

**What that looks like here.** Your network reaches beyond your projects. Your
feedback changes behaviour rather than only being heard. You use architectural
conversations to surface problems that are really about how people work.

**The next step.** At principal this becomes solving and unblocking issues
between teams or departments at the highest level, and coaching the organisation
on team dynamics and conflict resolution while building and growing the
community.

### Skill: Making architectural decisions

**What this means.** Making and guiding consequential decisions, and improving
the governance that manages architectural risk.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: make and guide
architectural design decisions characterised by medium risk and complexity;
identify and address architectural risks that affect multiple teams or domains;
use architectural governance and assurance to make design decisions and manage
technical risks at the appropriate level; and contribute to the development of
architectural governance and assurance.

**What that looks like here.** You guide more decisions than you make. You can
name the cross-team risks nobody owns, and you have found owners for some. You
have improved a governance process rather than only complying with it.

**The next step.** At principal this rises to high risk and complexity, risks
across the organisation or wider government, leading and evolving governance, and
representing architectural governance within wider governance such as legal or
commercial.

### Skill: Problem definition and shaping

**What this means.** Leading the framing of the hardest problems, and teaching
others to frame their own.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: lead the framing of a
problem characterised by high complexity, complication or risk so that a solution
can be created; coach others in defining problems and describing appropriate
options for solutions; and help others challenge requirements and assumptions and
identify opportunities when defining problems and solution options.

**What that looks like here.** When something is a mess, you are asked to say
what the problem actually is. You coach rather than reframing on people's behalf.
You have made challenging a requirement a normal thing to do rather than a brave
one.

### Skill: Strategy design

**What this means.** Defining direction across teams, and the principles and
roadmaps that make it followable.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: define strategies or
visions across teams that align with organisational objectives; direct the
implementation of a strategy or vision, for example by creating roadmaps or
plans; define architectural principles and patterns; and develop or maintain
strategy in response to feedback and findings.

**What that looks like here.** You have written a strategy teams work to. The
principles in use include ones you defined, and people can say why each exists.
When something is learned, the strategy changes rather than the learning being
noted.

**The next step.** At principal you define and *connect* strategies across the
organisation or wider government, and enable implementation by advocating for
resources and removing blockers.

### Skill: Technical design throughout the life cycle

**What this means.** Designing at high risk, leading others who design, and
improving the standards that designs are held to.

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: create technical
designs characterised by high risk, impact and complexity; lead and guide others
in creating technical designs that achieve organisational objectives; and use
feedback to optimise and refine standards for technical designs throughout the
life cycle.

**What that looks like here.** You own designs where being wrong would be public
and expensive. You guide other architects' designs without taking them over. What
delivery teaches you changes the standard, not just the one design — which is how
an organisation stops repeating a mistake.

**The next step.** Identical wording at principal solution architect.

suppliers — which brings procurement fairness and conflicts of interest directly
into your job. These questions ask about awareness, training and support, not
about whether you can state the law correctly, and not as a check on your
conduct.
