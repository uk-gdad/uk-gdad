# Major incident manager - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a major incident manager in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where
the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as **managing significant outages and crises** and reporting issues
to problem management. At this level you work closely with workplace services, infrastructure,
applications operators and communication managers. The framework also notes that in some departments
you may be the head of service.

A crisis is different from a busy day. The technical problem is often the smaller half: the rest is
keeping a lot of anxious people informed, deciding what to say publicly, protecting the people doing
the diagnosis, and making a call about restoring versus understanding.

Major incident manager is the top of this role. Eleven skills. Expect about 35 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: incident manager.** Incident managers restore normal service operation as quickly as
possible and minimise adverse effects on business operations.

**Your level: major incident manager.** You manage significant outages and crises and report issues to
problem management. You work closely with workplace services, infrastructure, applications operators
and communication managers. In some departments you may also be the head of service.

**There is no level above this one.** This role has two levels and this is the higher. Where people go
next is a question about other roles — IT service management, operations leadership, or wider service
ownership — and the framework does not define it as a next step, so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Incident manager
2. **Major incident manager** — you are here

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, *Continuity management* at your level is the only place in this role where you are asked to
**test the runbooks**. Continuity plans that have never been tested are documents rather than plans,
and you are the person the framework holds responsible for the difference.

Second, four skills are worded **identically at incident manager** below you — asset and configuration
management, problem management, service management framework knowledge, and community collaboration is
close in intent. The published step up is concentrated in incident management, continuity, service
focus, change, and availability.

**The eleven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Asset and configuration management
2. Availability and capacity management
3. Change management
4. Community collaboration
5. Continuity management

components.

**Same wording as incident manager.**

expensive minutes you will ever spend. If records are wrong, that is a finding worth raising outside
the incident.

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects.** Four things: work collaboratively in a group, actively networking with
others; **adapt feedback to ensure it is effective and lasting**; use your topic to identify problems or
issues in the team dynamic and rectify them; and identify issues through Agile health checks with the
team, and help stimulate the right responses.

**What that looks like here.** Incident reviews are where feedback either improves things or damages
people. A review that finds a person rather than a cause teaches everyone to be less forthcoming next
time, which makes the next incident longer.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects.** Five things: collaborate with user researchers and represent users
internally; explain the difference between user needs and the desires of the user; champion user
research to focus on all users; prioritise and define approaches to understand the user story, guiding
others in doing so; and offer recommendations on the best tools and methods to use.

**What that looks like here.** During a crisis, "who is affected and how badly" is the question that
should drive every decision. In practice organisations often prioritise by who is complaining loudest.
