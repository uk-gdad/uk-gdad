# Associate infrastructure operations engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello. This form is for you if you work as an associate infrastructure operations engineer in the UK
Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can say what
you can do now, what you cannot do yet, and what would help.

The framework describes this level as a **trainee**. You work in an established team and support
infrastructure operations engineers in their daily work, **under their direction**. That is the whole
expectation. Nobody is looking for you to be running things on your own.

This is a short form — you have only **four skills**, fewer than almost any other level in the framework. It
should take about 15 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational purposes. If you intend to use
it in any way, please consult with your organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources
staff, or people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

## How to fill in this form

This is a web page that does not save anything. There is no button to press and nothing gets sent from here.

Print it out, or copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your organisation uses for
these conversations. Every question has a number, so your answers make sense on their own.

A few things worth knowing:

- **You can skip questions.** A blank is not a mark against you.
- **"I do not know" is a real answer.** It is often the most useful one at this level, because it shows
  someone where to teach you next.
- **Do not put anything secret in here** — no passwords, no system details, no colleagues' names.
- **Ask who will read it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or how long
  they are kept, it is completely fine to ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

For a trainee, **0 and 1 are the normal answers**. A 2 means you have actually done the thing with someone
helping. If you are picking 3 or 4 for much of this, either you have been here a while or you are being
harder on the scale than it deserves — ask someone who knows your work.

> urgent ones. I have not been part of resolving one.

## About this role level

**The role: infrastructure operations engineer.** An infrastructure operations engineer supports, manages and
maintains **the core infrastructure that underpins production services**.

"Production" means the real thing — the systems people actually use, right now, not a test copy. That is why
this work has a particular feel to it: mistakes are visible immediately, and the safety comes from process
rather than from being clever.

**Your level: associate infrastructure operations engineer.** As a trainee, you work in an established team
and support infrastructure operations engineers in their daily work, under their direction.

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. **Associate infrastructure operations engineer** — you are here
2. Infrastructure operations engineer
3. Senior infrastructure operations engineer
4. Lead infrastructure operations engineer
5. Principal infrastructure operations engineer

Your next step is infrastructure operations engineer: responsible for the preparation and support of IT
operations solutions and services, physical or virtual, according to industry and organisational best
practice, standards, service requirements and key performance indicators throughout the product life cycle.

**One thing worth knowing about that step, and it is a big one.** You have **four skills**. The level above
has **twelve**. It adds asset and configuration management, availability and capacity management, change
management, coding and scripting, service focus, service management framework knowledge, technical
specialism, and testing — all at once. It also expects a **Level 3 service management framework
qualification**, which takes time to get.

That is the largest step in this role, and it is much better to know about it now than to meet it at a
promotion conversation. If you can start on the qualification and on scripting during your traineeship, you
will be in a very different position.

**The four skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Incident management
2. Ownership and initiative
3. Problem management
4. User focus

## Job skills

Four skills, in the framework's order. Each one gives you what the level asks for, what that means in plain
terms, and what changes at the level above.

### Skill: Incident management

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: **identify and register incidents, gathering the
required information and allocating it to the appropriate channel.**

**In plain terms.** Spot that something is wrong, log it properly, get the right information into the log,
and send it to the right place.

**Why the "gathering the required information" part matters more than it sounds.** Whoever picks the incident
up next only knows what you wrote. When it started, what you saw, what changed just beforehand, who reported
it, how many people are affected — a good log saves the next person half an hour, and a vague one costs them
one.

**The next step.** At infrastructure operations engineer this is worded identically. It stays the same for
two levels, which means you can get genuinely good at it here.

### Skill: Ownership and initiative

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **show an awareness of problem resolution
processes**; and **pass problems on to your team**.

**In plain terms.** Know how problems get dealt with here, and hand them over properly when you hit one you
cannot solve.

**This one is often misread.** "Pass problems on" is not a failure — it is the actual expectation at this
level. What matters is passing them on *well*, with what you tried and what you saw.

**The next step.** At infrastructure operations engineer you own an issue until a new owner has been found or
the problem is resolved. That is the real difference between the two levels.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **investigate problems in systems, processes and
services**, with an understanding of the level of a problem — for example, strategic, tactical or
operational; and **contribute to the implementation of remedies and preventative measures**.

**In plain terms.** Look into things that go wrong, get a sense of how big they are, and help with the fix.

**Incidents and problems are not the same thing.** An incident is this outage. A problem is the underlying
cause that will produce the next one too. Incident work restores service; problem work stops it recurring.
Teams under pressure do a lot of the first and very little of the second.

**The three levels of problem.** Operational means today's breakage. Tactical means the pattern behind
several breakages. Strategic means something about how the whole thing is built.

**The next step.** Worded identically at infrastructure operations engineer.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **show an awareness or understanding of user
experience analysis and its principles**; and **explain the purpose of user stories and the focus on user
needs**.

**In plain terms.** Understand that all this infrastructure exists so that somebody can do something, and
that the somebody is the point.

**Why this is in an infrastructure role.** It is easy in operations to think of the systems as the job.
Behind every production service is a person trying to claim a benefit, renew a licence, or do their own job
— and downtime lands on them, not on the server.

**A user story** is a short sentence describing what someone needs and why: "As a caseworker, I need to see
the full history so that I can answer a query in one call." It keeps the reason attached to the requirement.

**The next step.** A significant jump: at infrastructure operations engineer you identify and engage with
users to collate evidence of user needs, define research, and use quantitative and qualitative data.
