# Principal infrastructure operations engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a principal infrastructure operations engineer in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do
now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as **leading and directing IT operations specialist teams in monitoring,
operating and supporting technical solutions**. At this level you:

- **develop the strategic and tactical roadmaps** for technologies and services, ensuring they are
  future-proofed and the organisation derives maximum value from investment in technologies
- **own the operational relationships with suppliers**, ensuring services and products are aligned to
  industry best practice and regulatory and contractual requirements

Note that supplier ownership is stated flatly here. In the sibling infrastructure engineer role the same
accountability is qualified with "potentially". Yours is not.

This is the top of the role. Nothing above it in the framework tells you what good looks like, so the
questions here ask you to argue for your own judgement rather than measure yourself against a next step.

Twelve skills. Expect about 35 minutes.

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send from here — your answers never
touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your organisation uses for
development conversations. Every question is numbered, so answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **At this level, "I have not had the chance" often means the organisation has not asked.** That is worth
  recording as it is.
- **Do not include anything sensitive about contracts, security posture or people.**
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or how long
  they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

At this level the scale is worth reading carefully. A principal who rates everything 4 has stopped
distinguishing between what they are accountable for and what they are actually good at, and the gap between
those two things is where the useful conversation is.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

>

Confident where it is earned, plain about the constraint.

## About this role level

**The role: infrastructure operations engineer.** An infrastructure operations engineer supports, manages and
maintains the core infrastructure that underpins production services.

**Your level: principal infrastructure operations engineer.** You lead and direct IT operations specialist
teams in monitoring, operating and supporting technical solutions, with the accountabilities listed in the
introduction.

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. Associate infrastructure operations engineer
2. Infrastructure operations engineer
3. Senior infrastructure operations engineer
4. Lead infrastructure operations engineer
5. **Principal infrastructure operations engineer** — you are here

There is no level above this in the role. Movement from here is usually into IT service management, a head of
operations post, or technical architecture.

**Two things worth knowing about this level.**

First, **your service management qualification requirement is an expert certificate** — the third and largest
step in an escalation that runs Level 3, then Level 3 plus ITIL life cycle or capability elements, then
expert. It is the highest certification requirement anywhere in this role, and it is one of very few places
in the whole framework where a named expert-level certificate appears.

Second, **five of your twelve skills are worded identically at lead**: change management, coding and
scripting, incident management, ownership and initiative, and testing. What makes your level different is
concentrated in asset and configuration management, availability and capacity, problem management, service
focus, technical specialism and user focus — plus the roadmap and supplier ownership.

**The twelve skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Asset and configuration management
2. Availability and capacity management
3. Change management
4. Coding and scripting
5. Incident management
6. Ownership and initiative

### Skill: Asset and configuration management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **manage configuration items and related
information**; and **manage service compliance and risk**.

**A reframing from the four levels below**, where it was maintaining accurate information and verifying
location and state. Compliance and risk, now.

**What that looks like here.** Being able to answer, on demand and with evidence, what the organisation runs,
whether it is compliant, and what the exposure is.

**Stretching further.** Making the compliance position continuous rather than assembled when someone asks for
it.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: **give direction on which tools or methods to
use**; demonstrate experience in meeting the needs of users across a variety of channels; **bring insight and
expertise in how user needs have changed over time** to ensure they are met by the business; and **apply
strategic thinking to provide the best service for the end user**.

**A step up from lead**, where it was collaborating with researchers and championing research.

**What that looks like here.** Insight over time is the distinctive one. How people use services has changed
substantially, and an operation designed for one pattern serves another badly.
