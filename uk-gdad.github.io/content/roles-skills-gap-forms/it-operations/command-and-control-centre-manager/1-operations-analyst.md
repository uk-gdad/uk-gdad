# Operations analyst - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as an operations analyst in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where
the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes command and control centre managers as proactively monitoring live services and
performance trends to identify potential problems or areas for improvement. At this level you provide
administrative support to your manager and alert support staff if necessary.

Watching screens sounds passive and is not. The difference between an operations centre that catches
things early and one that reports them late is entirely about who is paying attention to the right
signals, and whether they feel able to raise something that might turn out to be nothing.

Eleven skills. Expect about 35 minutes.

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send from here — your answers
never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your organisation uses for
development conversations. Every question is numbered, so answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **Answer about the job you do**, not the one on the job description.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer.** With eleven skills, several blanks are expected.
- **Do not include anything sensitive about your systems.**
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or how long
  they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: command and control centre manager.** Command and control centre managers proactively monitor
live services and performance trends to identify potential problems or areas for improvement that can
then be investigated.

**Your level: operations analyst.** You provide administrative support to your manager and alert support
staff if necessary.

**Where this level sits.** This role has four levels:

1. **Operations analyst** — you are here
2. Senior operations analyst
3. Operational control manager
4. Head of command and control

Your next step is senior operations analyst: prioritising incoming messages for the team, ensuring
correct information flow, record keeping and data capture, detecting notifications, conducting monitor
checks, and being on call in emergencies.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Seven of your eleven skills are worded **identically at
senior operations analyst**. Only four change: asset and configuration management, change management,
incident management and problem management. If you are aiming for that step, those four are the whole
published list — and incident management in particular, which moves from registering incidents to
diagnosing and prioritising them.

**The eleven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Asset and configuration management
2. Availability and capacity management
3. Change management
4. Community collaboration
5. Continuity management
6. Incident management
7. Ownership and initiative
8. Problem management
9. Service focus
10. Service management framework knowledge
11. User focus

## Job skills

Eleven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects, what it looks like in
practice, and what changes at senior operations analyst.

### Skill: Asset and configuration management

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: track, log and correct information to protect
assets and components.

**What that looks like here.** When an alert fires you need to know what it belongs to and who owns it.
Records that are wrong send you to the wrong team, which costs time at exactly the wrong moment.

**The next step.** At senior you maintain secure configuration and accurate information, control IT
assets in one or more areas, and verify the location and state of assets.

### Skill: Change management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: implement change requests; and apply change
control procedures **under supervision**.

**What that looks like here.** In an operations centre, knowing what changes are planned tonight is what
lets you tell a real fault from an expected disruption. A change calendar you can see is worth a great
deal.

**The next step.** At senior you analyse and assess the impact of change, document change requests, and
action changes from them.

### Skill: Incident management

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: identify and register incidents, gathering the
required information and allocating them to the appropriate channel.

**What that looks like here.** The quality of what you record shapes everything that follows. Time
started, what is affected, what changed, who reported it — a good record saves the responder the first
twenty minutes.

**The next step.** At senior you **diagnose and prioritise** incidents, investigate their causes and find
resolutions. That is the biggest single change between the two levels.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: investigate problems in systems, processes and
services, with an understanding of the level of a problem; and contribute to the implementation of
remedies and preventative measures.

**What that looks like here.** You see the same alerts night after night. That pattern knowledge is
valuable and usually stays in the operations centre unless someone writes it down.

**The next step.** At senior you initiate and monitor investigations into patterns and trends, consult
specialists, determine resolutions and determine preventative measures.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: identify and engage with users or stakeholders
to collate user needs evidence; understand and define research that fits user needs; and use quantitative
and qualitative data about users to turn user focus into outcomes.

**What that looks like here.** Research-flavoured wording for a monitoring role. The practical version is
knowing which of the things you watch actually matter to people, so that a red light on a critical
service is treated differently from a red light on something nobody uses at 2am.
