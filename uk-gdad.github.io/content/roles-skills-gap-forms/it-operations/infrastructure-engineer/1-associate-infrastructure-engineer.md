# Associate infrastructure engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello. This form is for you if you work as an associate infrastructure engineer in the UK Government Digital
and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can say what you can do now,
what you cannot do yet, and what would help.

The framework describes this level as a **trainee**. You work in an established team and support
infrastructure engineers in their daily work, **under their direction**. That is the whole expectation. You
are not supposed to be running anything on your own yet.

That matters for how you fill this in. A lot of honest answers here will be "I have watched someone do this
but I have not done it myself", and that is exactly right for a trainee. Nobody is looking for you to claim
more.

Eight skills. Expect about 20 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational purposes. If you intend to use

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that
apply, and pick a rating where one is asked for. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page, or print it out, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation
uses for these conversations. Every question has a number, so your answers
make sense on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy. If other people use this computer, press it when you have finished.

A few things worth knowing:

- **You can skip questions.** A blank is not a mark against you.
- **"I do not know" is a real answer.** It is often the most useful one at this level, because it shows
  someone where to teach you next.
- **Do not put anything secret in here** — no passwords, no system details, no colleagues' names.
- **Ask who will read it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or how long
  they are kept, it is completely fine to ask before you fill it in.

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

For a trainee, **0 and 1 are the normal answers**. A 2 means you have actually done the thing with someone
helping. If you are picking 3 or 4 for much of this, either you have been here a while or you are being
harder on the scale than it deserves — ask someone who knows your work.

> not built or tested an interface myself.

## About this role level

**The role: infrastructure engineer.** An infrastructure engineer designs, builds, manages and supports the
infrastructure services that underpin all internal user services and services to the public.

That is worth reading twice. Infrastructure is the layer everything else sits on — servers, storage,
networks, the cloud platforms, the physical kit in data centres. When it works, nobody thinks about it. When
it does not, nothing else works either.

**Your level: associate infrastructure engineer.** As a trainee, you work in an established team and support
infrastructure engineers in their daily work, under their direction.

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. **Associate infrastructure engineer** — you are here
2. Infrastructure engineer
3. Senior infrastructure engineer
4. Lead infrastructure engineer
5. Principal infrastructure engineer

Your next step is infrastructure engineer: supporting and maintaining infrastructure solutions and services
as directed and according to departmental policy, and working with third parties.

**One thing worth knowing about that step.** You have **eight skills**. The level above has **twelve** — it
adds coding and scripting, service focus, and testing. That is a big jump, and it is worth knowing about now
rather than discovering it at a promotion conversation. If you can start picking up scripting during your
traineeship, you will be ahead.

**The eight skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. IT infrastructure
2. Information security
3. Modern development standards
4. Ownership and initiative
5. Problem management
6. Systems design
7. Systems integration
8. Troubleshooting and problem resolution

## Job skills

Eight skills, in the framework's order. Each one gives you what the level asks for, what that means in plain
terms, and what changes at the level above.

### Skill: IT infrastructure

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **support infrastructure solutions and services**,
and other computer, storage, networking, physical infrastructure, software, commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS)
software and open source packages and solutions; and **support virtual and cloud computing**, including
Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS) and Software as a Service (SaaS).

**In plain terms.** Help the engineers with the kit and the platforms. "Support" is the key word — you are
assisting, not owning.

**Three terms you will hear constantly:**

- **IaaS** — Infrastructure as a Service. You rent the machines and manage what runs on them.
- **PaaS** — Platform as a Service. You rent a platform and just deploy your code onto it.
- **SaaS** — Software as a Service. You rent finished software and use it.

**Commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS)** just means software bought ready-made rather than built here.

**The next step.** At infrastructure engineer you build, configure, administer and support solutions — doing
rather than assisting.

### Skill: Information security

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: **explain information security and the security
controls available to protect solutions and services.**

**In plain terms.** Be able to say what security is for and what tools exist to do it — firewalls, access
controls, encryption, patching, logging. Explaining, not implementing.

**Why it matters here.** Infrastructure is where most of the security controls actually live. Somebody
misconfiguring one server can undo a lot of policy written elsewhere.

**The next step.** At infrastructure engineer this is worded identically. At senior it becomes *using*
security practices and controls to contribute to protecting solutions.

### Skill: Modern development standards

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: **explain the importance of using modern development
standards.**

**In plain terms.** Understand why teams work the way they do — version control, automated testing,
infrastructure defined as code, small frequent changes rather than big rare ones.

**Why this is in an infrastructure role.** Infrastructure used to be built by hand. Now it is increasingly
written as code, versioned, and tested like software. That shift is why this skill is here from your first
day.

**The next step.** At infrastructure engineer you explain the most important principles and apply them under
guidance.

### Skill: Ownership and initiative

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **show an awareness of problem resolution
processes**; and **pass problems on to your team**.

**In plain terms.** Know how problems get dealt with here, and hand them over properly when you hit one you
cannot solve.

**This one is often misread.** "Pass problems on" is not a failure — it is the actual expectation at this
level. What matters is passing them on *well*: what you saw, when, what you tried, what changed just
beforehand.

**The next step.** At infrastructure engineer you own an issue until a new owner is found or it is resolved.
That is the real difference between the two levels.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **investigate problems in systems, processes and
services**, with an understanding of the level of a problem — for example, strategic, tactical or
operational; and **contribute to the implementation of remedies and preventative measures**.

**In plain terms.** Look into things that go wrong, get a sense of how big they are, and help with the fix.

**The three levels of problem.** Operational means today's breakage. Tactical means the pattern behind
several breakages. Strategic means something about how the whole thing is built. Being able to tell them
apart is more than half this skill.

**The next step.** At infrastructure engineer this is worded identically. It changes at senior.

### Skill: Systems design

### Skill: Systems integration

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **explain the process and principles of integrating
systems**; and **describe challenges of designing, building and testing interfaces between systems**.

**In plain terms.** Understand how separate systems are joined together, and why joining them is harder than
it looks.

**Why it is hard.** Two systems that each work perfectly can fail together — different data formats,
different timing, one going down while the other keeps sending. Most of the difficult problems in government
technology live in the joins.

**The next step.** At infrastructure engineer you build and test simple interfaces yourself.

### Skill: Troubleshooting and problem resolution

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **show an awareness of different technology
capabilities**; and **demonstrate basic troubleshooting capability**.

**In plain terms.** Know roughly what the different pieces do, and be able to work through a simple fault
sensibly.

**Basic troubleshooting, practically.** Check the obvious first. Change one thing at a time. Write down what
you tried. Know when to stop and ask.

**The next step.** At infrastructure engineer you troubleshoot and identify problems across different
technology capabilities — across the pieces, not within one.
