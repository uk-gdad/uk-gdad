# Principal end user computing engineer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a principal end user computing engineer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where
the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as **owning the strategy roadmap** and **leading on resourcing, learning
and development for the team**. You look at the bigger picture and understand trends in the business.

This is the top of the role. Nothing above it in the framework tells you what good looks like, so the
questions here ask you to argue for your own judgement rather than measure yourself against a next step.

Ten skills — one fewer than every level below you. Expect about 35 minutes.

## Advisory

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational purposes. If you intend to use
it in any way, please consult with your organization's appropriate teammates, such as with human resources
staff, or people and organizational development staff, or legal compliance staff.

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **At this level, "I have not had the chance" often means the organisation has not asked.** That is worth
  recording as it is.
- **Do not include anything sensitive about contracts, security posture or people.**
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or how long
  they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

At this level the scale is worth reading carefully. A principal who rates everything 4 has stopped
distinguishing between what they are accountable for and what they are actually good at, and the gap between
those two things is where the useful conversation is.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Technical specialism**
>
> - [x] 2 — Working: I do it with support
>
> *Evidence:* I can describe where end user computing is going, but I have not delivered a model for
> supporting future technologies alongside what we run now. Our roadmap is a device refresh schedule with
> ambitions attached.

Honest about the difference between having a view and having a plan.

## About this role level

**The role: end user computing engineer.** You manage the product life cycle of all service-raised incidents
and all service requests, using knowledge management, inform customers of progress, advise on workarounds,
and support and maintain end user services.

**Your level: principal end user computing engineer.** You own the strategy roadmap and lead on resourcing,
learning and development for your team. You look at the bigger picture and understand trends in the business.

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. Associate end user computing engineer
2. End user computing engineer
3. Senior end user computing engineer
4. Lead end user computing engineer
5. **Principal end user computing engineer** — you are here

There is no level above this in the role. Movement from here is usually sideways into IT service management,
infrastructure or a head of function post.

**One thing worth knowing about this level.** You have **ten skills where every level below you has eleven**.
The one that disappears is **service management framework knowledge** — the qualification requirement that
runs from associate to lead and stops before you. Whether that is a deliberate statement that strategy work
is not framed by ITIL, or an artefact of how the framework was written, the published text does not say. It
is recorded here rather than explained away.

**The ten skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Asset and configuration management
2. Change management
3. IT service reporting
4. Incident management
5. Ownership and initiative
6. Problem management
7. Service focus
8. Technical specialism
9. Testing
10. User focus

### Skill: Asset and configuration management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **manage configuration items and related
information**; and **manage service compliance and risk**.

**A change from lead**, where it was maintaining secure configuration, controlling assets and verifying
location and state. Yours is framed as compliance and risk.

**What that looks like here.** Being able to answer, on demand and with evidence, what the organisation
runs, whether it is compliant, and what the exposure is. Not a spreadsheet — a position you would defend to
an auditor.

**Stretching further.** Making the asset position a live input to financial and security planning rather
than something assembled when someone asks.

### Skill: IT service reporting

### Skill: Technical specialism

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **understand the direction of future
technologies**; and **deliver a model to support and maintain those future technologies and any databases
that coexist in the current environment**.

**The strategic core of the level.** Not just knowing where things are going, but having a delivery model for
getting there without abandoning what runs today.

**What that looks like here.** Cloud-managed devices, zero trust access, AI-assisted support, hybrid working
patterns — each with a plan for coexistence rather than a slide about the future.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: **give direction on which tools or methods to

user needs have changed over time**; and **apply strategic thinking to provide the best service**.

**What that looks like here.** Insight over time is the distinctive one. Working patterns have changed
substantially, and a device and software estate designed for one pattern serves another badly.
