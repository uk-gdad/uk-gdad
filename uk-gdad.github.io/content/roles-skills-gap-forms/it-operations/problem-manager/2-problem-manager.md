# Problem manager - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a problem manager in the UK Government Digital and Data profession.
Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are,
and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as picking up feeds from numerous sources, such as the service desk,
with the aim of putting continual improvements in place to stop incidents such as regular service
outages recurring. It expects you to resolve emerging and recurring problems; perform **root cause
analysis** to minimise the adverse impact of incidents caused by errors within the IT infrastructure;
work closely with major incident managers; and deal with problems and root cause analysis.

The hard part of this job is almost never the analysis. It is persuading an organisation to spend
capacity on something that is not currently on fire.

Problem manager is the top of this role. Eleven skills. Expect about 35 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: problem manager.** Problem managers resolve and control the root causes of incidents caused
by errors within the IT infrastructure, and work to prevent those incidents recurring.

**Your level: problem manager.** You pick up feeds from numerous sources, such as the service desk,
aiming to put continual improvements in place to stop incidents recurring. The framework expects that
you:

- resolve emerging and recurring problems
- perform root cause analysis to minimise the adverse impact of incidents caused by errors within the
  IT infrastructure
- work closely with major incident managers
- deal with problems and root cause analysis

**There is no level above this one.** This role has two levels and this is the higher. Where people go
next is a question about other roles — IT service management, operations leadership, or service
ownership — and the framework does not define it as a next step, so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Problem analyst
2. **Problem manager** — you are here

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Four of your eleven skills are worded **identically at
problem analyst** below you — asset and configuration management, availability and capacity management,
service management framework knowledge, and user focus. The qualification requirement does not rise
either: both levels are worded as a Level 3 service management framework qualification.

**The eleven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Asset and configuration management
2. Availability and capacity management
3. Change management
4. Community collaboration
5. Continuity management
6. Incident management
7. Ownership and initiative

9. Service focus

### Skill: Community collaboration

**What the framework expects.** Four things: work collaboratively in a group, actively networking with
others; **adapt feedback to ensure it is effective and lasting**; use your topic to identify problems or
issues in the team dynamic and rectify them; and identify issues through Agile health checks with the
team, and help stimulate the right responses.

**What that looks like here.** Root cause analysis that names people rather than causes shuts down the
flow of information you depend on. Whether teams tell you the awkward truth is the single biggest
determinant of how good your analysis can be.

### Skill: Problem management

**What the framework expects.** Two things: **ensure that the right actions are taken to investigate,
resolve and anticipate problems**; and co-ordinate the team to investigate problems, implement solutions
and take preventive measures.

**What that looks like here.** You are accountable for the right things happening rather than for doing
all of them. In practice the difficulty is that the people who must implement fixes usually report to
somebody else with different priorities.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects.** Three things: identify and engage with users or stakeholders to collate
user needs evidence; understand and define research that fits user needs; and use quantitative and
qualitative data about users to turn user focus into outcomes.

**Same wording as problem analyst.**

**What that looks like here.** The most persuasive case for fixing a problem is what it costs people.
Hours of rework, applications delayed, calls made — those numbers move priorities in a way that
technical descriptions do not.
