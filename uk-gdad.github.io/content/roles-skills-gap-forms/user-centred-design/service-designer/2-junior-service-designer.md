# Junior service designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a junior service designer in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now,
where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject or with
relevant work experience. It expects you to explain design decisions, work collaboratively and
**have responsibility for a service**; work independently after being given direction by more senior
designers; and independently identify user issues and important needs.

"Responsibility for a service" is a striking phrase at a junior level — in the equivalent
interaction and graphic design roles the wording is responsibility *as part of* a service. Service
design does not really divide into pieces, and that is reflected here.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating and some
questions. Expect about 25 minutes.

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick

- **Answer about the job you do**, not the one on the job description.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a generous reading of a
  thin example.

  long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: service designer.** Service designers design the end-to-end journey of a service. This
helps a user complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. Your work may involve
creating or changing transactions, products and content across both digital and offline channels,
provided by different parts of government.

**Your level: junior service designer.** You are a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject or
someone with relevant work experience. The framework expects that you:

- explain design decisions, work collaboratively and have responsibility for a service
- work independently after being given direction by more senior designers
- independently identify user issues and important needs

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate service designer
2. **Junior service designer** — you are here
3. Service designer
4. Senior service designer
5. Lead service designer
6. Head of service design

Your next step is service designer: a confident and competent designer developing designs based on
evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes, trusted to make good decisions.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, four of your seven skills are worded **identically at service designer** — design
communication, designing for everyone, evidence-based design and iterative design. The step up is
about judgement and trust rather than new craft.

Second: **the framework publishes the same seven skills, word for word, for service designers,
interaction designers and graphic designers**, at every level. The published skills cannot
distinguish the disciplines; the difference is the scope of what you are designing.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects, what it looks like
in service design, and what changes at service designer.

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: clearly explain problems and design ideas
to others; and clearly explain design decisions to others using appropriate tools and methods.

**Same wording at service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** A service map that shows where people fall out, how long each step
takes and who handles what is usually more persuasive than any argument. Showing an organisation its
own service honestly is a large part of this job.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: work with others to design and deliver
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services that meet the needs of all
users; and ensure a design meets appropriate standards, for example accessibility regulations, **with
support**.

**Same wording at service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** At service level, accessibility means more than a compliant web page.
It means an assisted digital route that works, a phone line that is actually answered, and a paper
option for people who need one — and knowing whether those exist in practice rather than in policy.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: explain the importance of aligning to
the strategy of an organisation; describe how your design work aligns to the wider goals and vision
of your team; and explain the value of using patterns and components in design.

**Same wording as associate service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Service patterns exist too — how someone proves identity, how a
decision is communicated, how an appeal works. Reusing one that has been tested is usually better
than inventing a new process.

**The next step.** At service designer you align your work to team goals and organisational
objectives, identify risks and use design to mitigate them, identify opportunities, and contribute to
developing patterns and components.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: explain different techniques for bringing
together perspectives from multiple people to inform a design; and explain the importance of
constructive feedback in the design process.

**Same wording as associate service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** The people who know how a service really works are usually the ones
handling exceptions — the caseworker with the spreadsheet of things the system cannot do. Getting
them into a design conversation is worth more than any amount of documentation.

**The next step.** At service designer you plan and run design sessions with your team, users or
stakeholders, identify and engage the right people throughout, and give and receive constructive
feedback.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: analyse, synthesise and clearly explain
evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics data; and work with researchers or
analysts to use evidence to inform, develop and test a design idea.

**Same wording at service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Complaints, repeat contacts, appeals and casework volumes are all
evidence about the service design. A step that generates a lot of phone calls is telling you
something specific.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: apply iterative design principles and
agile methodologies to your work; iterate and improve a design based on successive rounds of
research; prototype your ideas at different fidelities to explore and test designs; use a range of
design tools and techniques; and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Same wording at service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Prototyping a service can mean acting it out. Role-playing a phone
call, drafting the letter someone would receive, or walking through the steps with a caseworker
finds problems that no screen prototype reaches.
