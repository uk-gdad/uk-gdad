# Head of service design - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a head of service design in a UK government organisation. Human
resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are,
and what would close them.

The framework describes this role as an expert practitioner with broad industry experience who can
define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others. It expects you to
influence both design and organisational strategy and priorities; collaborate with counterpart
colleagues across government; and **focus on ensuring the right conditions and environment for
designers to work effectively**.

For service design those conditions are unusual: designers need access to operational settings,
casework data, policy colleagues and the authority to convene people who do not report to anyone in
digital. Without that a service designer produces maps that describe problems nobody has the standing
to fix.

Head of service design is the top of this role. Seven skills. Expect about 30 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: service designer.** Service designers design the end-to-end journey of a service. This
helps a user complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. Your work may involve
creating or changing transactions, products and content across both digital and offline channels,
provided by different parts of government.

**Your level: head of service design.** You are an expert practitioner with broad industry experience
who can define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others. The
framework expects that you:

- influence both design and organisational strategy and priorities
- collaborate with counterpart colleagues across government
- focus on ensuring the right conditions and environment for designers to work effectively

**There is no level above this one.** This role has six levels and this is the highest. Where people
go next is a question about other roles — user-centred design leadership, digital leadership, or
leadership outside the profession — and the framework does not define it as a next step, so this form
does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Associate service designer
2. Junior service designer
3. Service designer
4. Senior service designer
5. Lead service designer
6. **Head of service design** — you are here

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Five of your seven skills are worded **identically at
lead service designer**. Only designing strategically and leading design are yours alone.

And the framework publishes the same seven skills, word for word, for heads of service design,
interaction design and graphic design. Nothing in the published skills reflects that your discipline
spans organisations, channels and policy while the other two do not — which is worth knowing if the
skills are used to compare roles or set pay.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Job skills

like in practice. There is no level above, so these say what the skill demands rather than what comes
next.

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects.** Four things: gain support for design decisions and design strategy
from senior leaders; communicate design strategy across organisational boundaries; coach others in
how to effectively communicate design; and develop or introduce more effective methods of
communicating design and working in the open.

**Same wording as lead service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** A service map showing waiting times, failure demand and where people
give up is the most persuasive document in government, because it describes the organisation's own
performance in terms nobody can dispute.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects.** Three things: work with others to ensure design becomes part of the
development and implementation of your organisation's strategy; work with leaders to ensure design is
included in policy, technology and delivery processes **early enough to inform effective decision
making**; and enable use of patterns and components across the organisation.

**This is one of the two skills that distinguish your level from lead.**

**What that looks like here.** For service design, "early enough" means during policy design. Once a
policy specifies who must prove what, by when, to whom, the service is largely determined and design
can only make the resulting process less painful.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects.** Four things: embed a practice of hypothesis-driven design in a team;
guide the organisation in how to use evidence to improve services; analyse, synthesise and use
evidence to improve the way the organisation works; and ensure user insights are shared with and used
by the wider organisation and across government.

**Same wording as lead service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Your organisation knows how long people wait, how often it gets
decisions wrong, and how much of its contact volume is caused by its own service design. Whether
those facts reach the people who could act on them is largely your decision.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects.** Four things: embed the practice of iterative design and agile working
in teams or the wider organisation; lead on prototyping complex ideas at any fidelity; help teams
adapt quickly to changes in requirements, priorities or user needs; and **iterate delivery processes
or team structures to help teams work effectively**.

**Same wording as lead service designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Iterating team structures is where a head of service design can do
something no one else can: moving a responsibility so that the person who causes a problem is the
person who feels it.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects.** Four things: set a vision for and lead design across multiple teams or
a whole organisation; work effectively with senior leaders across your organisation or wider public
sector; support a culture of learning and experimentation; and **iterate and measurably improve
design maturity within your organisation and its partners**.

**This is the second of the two skills that distinguish your level from lead.** Note the phrase *and
its partners* — for service design, maturity has to improve in the organisations you depend on too.

**What that looks like here.** Service design maturity is visible in specific things: whether
services are designed end to end, whether non-digital channels are treated as part of the service,
whether anyone measures the whole journey, and whether policy and operations expect to be involved.
