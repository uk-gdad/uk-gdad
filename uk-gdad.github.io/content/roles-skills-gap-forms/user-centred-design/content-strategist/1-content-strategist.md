# Content strategist - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a content strategist in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what
you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes a content strategist as someone who takes an overview of content
and how it is produced, designing governance, workflows and taxonomies in a user-centred
context and working with senior stakeholders, user researchers and content, service and
interaction designers. At this level you are an **expert practitioner in content strategy
and taxonomy design**. It expects you to develop governance, processes and workflows based
on delivering strategy and policy intent; manage relationships and interdependencies with
other content roles; shape and structure the architecture of content based on user-centred
design principles; and work with lead content designers to organise and manage teams to
deliver outcomes.

Most content problems in government are not writing problems. They are problems of
structure, ownership and process — several teams publishing overlapping content nobody
maintains, in an order that made sense to the organisation and not to anyone else. That is
your territory.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Six skills, each with an explanation, a rating and
some questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of
  you and would stay changed if you left. Delivering through others is written into your
  level, so several are reasonable.

## About this role level

**The role: content strategist.** You take an overview of content and how it is produced. In
this role you will:

- design governance, workflows and taxonomies in a user-centred context
- work with senior stakeholders, user researchers and content, service and interaction
  designers

**Your level: content strategist.** You are an expert practitioner in content strategy and
taxonomy design. The framework expects that you:

- develop governance, processes and workflows based on delivering strategy and policy
  intent
- manage relationships and interdependencies with other content roles
- shape and structure the architecture of content based on user-centred design principles
- work with lead content designers to organise and manage teams to deliver outcomes

**One level, no ladder.** The framework publishes this role with a single level. There is
no junior content strategist and no head of content strategy, so progression here means
moving to a different role — usually into content design leadership — rather than up this
one. That is worth raising in a development conversation, because single-level roles are
easy to overlook when development budgets are set.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Content strategist is one of the few
user-centred design roles with no *Technical understanding* skill, and the only one built
around taxonomy and governance rather than producing content. Your closest counterparts are
lead and head content designers, and where responsibilities meet is worth agreeing
explicitly rather than discovering.

**The six skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working
2. Prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management
4. Strategic thinking
5. User focus
6. User-centred content design

## Job skills

Six skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the framework expects and what
it looks like in practice. Because this role has one level, these say what the skill demands
rather than what comes next.

### Skill: Agile working

**What the framework expects.** Three things: identify and compare the best processes or
delivery methods to use, including measuring and evaluating outcomes; help the team to
decide the best approach; and help teams to manage and visualise outcomes, prioritise work
and adhere to agreed minimum viable product, priorities and scope.

**What that looks like here.** Content strategy work does not fit two-week sprints
comfortably — a taxonomy redesign delivers nothing visible for weeks and then changes
everything. Helping teams choose an approach that suits the work, rather than forcing the
work into the approach, is the skill.

### Skill: Prototyping

**What the framework expects.** Two things: understand when to use a specific prototyping
technique or method; and show the value of prototyping to your team.

**What that looks like here.** Content structures can be prototyped — card sorting, tree
testing, a paper navigation — and doing so before committing is far cheaper than discovering
after launch that nobody finds anything. This is the smallest skill in your set and often
the most neglected in strategy work.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects.** Three things: **direct** the stakeholder relationship
strategy for your teams; ensure stakeholders' objectives are set and support teams to meet
them; and influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to resolve issues and enable
progress.

**What that looks like here.** Content governance takes publishing freedom away from people
who currently have it. That negotiation is the hardest part of this job, and it is usually
won by showing what the current arrangement costs users rather than by asserting authority.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects.** Four things: give direction on which tools or methods to
use; demonstrate experience in meeting the needs of users across a variety of channels;
bring insight and expertise in how user needs have changed over time to ensure they are met
by the business; and apply strategic thinking to provide the best service for the end user.

**What that looks like here.** Content structured around how the organisation is arranged
rather than how people look for things is the most common failure in government content, and
it is invisible from inside. Search terms and research are what expose it.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects.** Six things: show a deep understanding of end-to-end journeys
and how content is affected within these journeys; identify where journey fixes or content
improvements need to be made; take responsibility for assuring the quality of content from
more junior colleagues, and coaching and guiding them to improve; ensure content is regularly
reviewed and evaluated, contributing to continuous improvement and iteration; encourage a
continuous improvement mindset in teams and more junior content colleagues; and deliver
through others, and direct and critique their work.

**What that looks like here.** *Regularly reviewed and evaluated* is the item everything
else depends on. Government content estates accumulate pages nobody has read since
publication, and a review process that actually runs is worth more than any amount of new
writing.
