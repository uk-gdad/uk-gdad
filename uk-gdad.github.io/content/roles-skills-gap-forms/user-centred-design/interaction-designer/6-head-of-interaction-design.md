# Head of interaction design - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a head of interaction design in a UK government organisation. Human
resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are,
and what would close them.

The framework describes this role as an expert practitioner with broad industry experience who can
define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others. It expects you to
influence both design and organisational strategy and priorities; collaborate with counterpart
colleagues across government; and **focus on ensuring the right conditions and environment for
designers to work effectively**.

For interaction design that last one has a specific meaning: designers need prototyping
environments, access to real system behaviour, research with real users including those in
difficult circumstances, and permission to change a journey after it has launched. Without those,
even good designers produce guesswork.

Head of interaction design is the top of this role. Seven skills. Expect about 30 minutes.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"The designers do this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer** at this level too.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or
  how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of you and
  would stay changed if you left.

## About this role level

**The role: interaction designer.** An interaction designer works out the best way to let users
interact with services, in terms of both overall flow and at the level of individual design
elements.

**Your level: head of interaction design.** You are an expert practitioner with broad industry
experience who can define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others.
The framework expects that you:

- influence both design and organisational strategy and priorities
- collaborate with counterpart colleagues across government
- focus on ensuring the right conditions and environment for designers to work effectively

**There is no level above this one.** This role has six levels and this is the highest. Where people
go next is a question about other roles — user-centred design leadership, digital leadership, or
leadership outside the profession — and the framework does not define it as a next step, so this
form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Associate interaction designer
2. Junior interaction designer
3. Interaction designer
4. Senior interaction designer
5. Lead interaction designer
6. **Head of interaction design** — you are here

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Five of your seven skills are worded **identically at
lead interaction designer** — design communication, designing for everyone, designing together,
evidence-based design and iterative design. Only designing strategically and leading design are
yours alone, and both are about organisational reach.

It is also worth knowing that the framework publishes the same seven skills, word for word, for
heads of interaction design, graphic design, and service design. Nothing in the published skills
distinguishes the three disciplines at any level.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the framework expects and what it
looks like in practice. There is no level above, so these say what the skill demands rather than
what comes next.

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects.** Four things: gain support for design decisions and design strategy
from senior leaders; communicate design strategy across organisational boundaries; coach others in
how to effectively communicate design; and develop or introduce more effective methods of
communicating design and working in the open.

**Same wording as lead interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** The most persuasive thing available to you is a working prototype of
a badly designed journey next to a better one. Senior leaders who will not read a paper will engage
with two minutes of clicking.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects.** Three things: work with others to ensure design becomes part of the
development and implementation of your organisation's strategy; work with leaders to ensure design
is included in policy, technology and delivery processes **early enough to inform effective decision
making**; and enable use of patterns and components across the organisation.

**This is one of the two skills that distinguish your level from lead.**

**What that looks like here.** In interaction design, "early enough" usually means before a
technology platform is chosen and before a policy specifies exactly what must be collected. After
those two decisions, the journey is largely determined.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects.** Four things: advise others how to effectively plan and run design
sessions with a team, users or stakeholders; adapt a design session to ensure you achieve a useful
outcome; effectively involve the right people throughout the design process; and work across team or
profession boundaries, for example with policy teams.

**Same wording as lead interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** At head level the boundary that matters most is with operations —
the teams who handle what your journeys produce. Design that ignores the back office produces
services that look efficient and create queues elsewhere.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects.** Four things: embed a practice of hypothesis-driven design in a team;

**Same wording as lead interaction designer.**

fail to complete public services. Whether that reaches policy colleagues, and whether it ever
reaches other departments designing the same kind of transaction, is largely your decision.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects.** Four things: set a vision for and lead design across multiple teams
or a whole organisation; work effectively with senior leaders across your organisation or wider
public sector; support a culture of learning and experimentation; and **iterate and measurably
improve design maturity within your organisation and its partners**.

**This is the second of the two skills that distinguish your level from lead.**

**What that looks like here.** Design maturity for interaction design is measurable in specific
ways: whether designers are in teams from the start, whether prototypes are tested before build,
whether live services are iterated, and whether journeys are designed with operations rather than
at them.
