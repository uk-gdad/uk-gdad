# Senior interaction designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a senior interaction designer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do
now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as an experienced designer who works with minimal support and
can influence and mentor others. It expects you to work with service managers and programme
directors to develop design concepts; potentially have responsibility across complex services; help
set direction and embed good practice within teams; and make important decisions based on research,
understanding how that research impacts others.

*Complex services* in interaction design usually means one of three things: many possible paths
through a journey, a transaction that has to reconcile with a legacy system, or a service where the
same user returns over months and the state has to be right when they do.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating and some
questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

This skills gap form is created by AI and is work in progress for educational purposes. If you

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send from here — your
answers never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your organisation uses for
development conversations. Every question is numbered, so answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"The team does this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a generous reading of a
  thin example.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or
  how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of you and
  would stay changed if you left. Mentoring is written into your level, so one or two are
  reasonable.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Iterative design**
>
> - [x] 3 — Practitioner: I do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions
>
> *Evidence:* I prototype complex conditional journeys in code by myself and adapt them quickly when
> rules change. Helping other designers work iteratively is something I do by example rather than
> deliberately.

Named practice, and a clear line about where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: interaction designer.** An interaction designer works out the best way to let users
interact with services, in terms of both overall flow and at the level of individual design
elements.

**Your level: senior interaction designer.** You are an experienced designer who works with minimal
support and can influence and mentor others. The framework expects that you:

- work with service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts
- potentially have responsibility across complex services
- help set direction and embed good practice within teams
- make important decisions based on research and understand how this research impacts others

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate interaction designer
2. Junior interaction designer
3. Interaction designer
4. **Senior interaction designer** — you are here
5. Lead interaction designer
6. Head of interaction design

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, **your published wording for *Leading design* is identical to the interaction designer's
below you** — including "lead and co-ordinate design work in your team, **with support**" — despite
your level description saying you work with minimal support and mentor others. Leading is the one
skill where your level shows no progression at all.

Second, the framework publishes the same seven skills, word for word, for interaction designers,
graphic designers and service designers at every level. Everything that makes senior interaction
design distinct is in the work rather than the wording.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects, what it looks like
in interaction design, and what changes at lead interaction designer.

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: clearly explain complex problems and
ideas to teams or stakeholders outside of design; **clearly document and communicate design
decisions, related risks and any unresolved issues**; and build consensus around a design approach,
for example by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions.

**What that looks like here.** Complex journeys accumulate compromises — a step that exists only
because of a system limitation, a question asked twice because two teams need it. Recording those
honestly, so the next designer knows what is deliberate and what is residue, is the most valuable
documentation in interaction design.

**The next step.** At lead you gain support for design decisions and strategy from senior leaders,
communicate design strategy across organisational boundaries, coach others in communicating design,
and introduce more effective methods of communicating and working in the open.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: advise teams on how to design inclusive,
accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services; design and deliver ethical content
or services that consider the personal and social context of users; and ensure a design meets
appropriate standards, for example accessibility regulations.

**What that looks like here.** The *with support* qualifier has gone. And *personal and social
context* asks something specific of interaction design: a journey that demands documents someone may
not have, or forces a disclosure in a public place, or assumes a stable address, meets every
technical standard and still excludes people.

**The next step.** At lead you measurably improve inclusive, accessible and sustainable design
practice across teams or your organisation, and help others understand and mitigate the influence of
privilege and power on design.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: help a team understand how user-centred
design helps it meet its goals; help teams align their work to the goals and vision of their
organisation; use risks, opportunities and constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape
design; and identify and create new design patterns and components.

**What that looks like here.** *Use constraints to shape design* rather than work around them. A
system that cannot store a value, a rule that requires an exact date — treating those as design
inputs rather than obstacles usually produces a better journey than pretending they are not there.

**The next step.** A clear step up at lead interaction designer: planning and running design sessions
that include **senior leaders or stakeholders**, helping teams connect across **organisational**
boundaries, coaching other designers in involving stakeholders of any level, and developing or
introducing more effective ways to design together.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: advise others how to effectively plan and
run design sessions with a team, users or stakeholders; adapt a design session to ensure you achieve
a useful outcome; effectively involve the right people throughout the design process; and **work
across team or profession boundaries, for example with policy teams**.

**What that looks like here.** Policy colleagues often arrive with a rule and a request to build a
journey for it. Turning that into a conversation about whether the rule can be expressed in a way
people can actually satisfy is where senior interaction design earns its place.

**The next step.** A clear step up at lead interaction designer: planning and running design sessions
that include **senior leaders or stakeholders**, helping teams connect across **organisational**
boundaries, coaching other designers in involving stakeholders of any level, and developing or
introducing more effective ways to design together.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: analyse, synthesise and clearly explain
complex evidence relevant to users or a service, for example large data sets; help your team use
design hypotheses effectively; and use complex research and data to develop and test design ideas.

**What that looks like here.** In transactional services the data is rich: every step, every error,
every abandonment. The skill is combining it with research so you know not only where people leave
but why.

**The next step.** At lead you embed hypothesis-driven design in a team, guide the organisation in
using evidence to improve services, and ensure user insights are shared across government.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: help other designers apply iterative
design principles and agile methodologies to their work; iterate and improve complex designs based
on successive rounds of research; **independently prototype complex ideas at an appropriate
fidelity**; and adapt designs quickly to changes in requirements, priorities or user needs.

**What that looks like here.** Prototyping a conditional journey with branching logic, realistic
data and error states — independently — is the technical core of this level. It is also what lets
research test the hard parts rather than the happy path.

**The next step.** At lead you embed iterative design across teams or the organisation, lead on
prototyping complex ideas at any fidelity, help teams adapt to change, and iterate delivery
processes or team structures.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: lead and co-ordinate design work in your
team, **with support**; communicate the value of user-centred design to your team; support other
designers; and work with digital and data leaders in your organisation, when needed.

**Identical wording to interaction designer**, the level below — including *with support*, and
despite your level description saying you work with minimal support and mentor others.

**What that looks like here.** Most senior interaction designers lead without support. If you do,
you are beyond this wording, and it is worth saying so plainly.

**The next step.** At lead you lead and co-ordinate design work in a team without the qualifier,
advocate for user-centred design with leaders outside digital and data, create an inclusive and
productive environment for designers, mentor and coach, and iterate design processes.
