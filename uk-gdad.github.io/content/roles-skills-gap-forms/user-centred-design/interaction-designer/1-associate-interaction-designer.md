# Associate interaction designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you work as an associate interaction designer in the
UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you
can say what you do now, what you would like to get better at, and what would help.

The framework describes this level plainly: as a trainee in an entry-level position, working under
supervision, you need aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. That is the whole
level description — there is no separate list of accountabilities, which makes the first section
of this form more important than usual.

Interaction design in government is about **flow and elements together**: working out the best way
to let people interact with a service, both the overall path through it and each individual thing
they have to do. The question "what is the one thing this screen is asking?" is the heart of the
craft.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. There are no right answers. Seven skills, each with a
plain explanation, a rating and a couple of questions. Take about 25 minutes.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen. Type into the boxes, tick the boxes that
apply, and pick a rating where one is asked for. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

So: when you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page, or print it, or copy your answers into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so your answers
make sense on their own. Clear answers empties the form and deletes the saved
copy, which is worth doing on a computer other people use.

A few things worth knowing before you start:

- **You can skip anything.** Leaving a question blank does not count against you.
- **Answer about what you actually do**, not what you are supposed to be learning.
- **"I have not done this yet" is the right answer at a trainee level** for a lot of these.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who will see your answers, what they will be used
  for, or how long they will be kept, it is completely reasonable to ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Some questions ask you to rate yourself. Use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you studied.
- **0s and 1s are completely normal here.** Every one of your seven skills is worded as *explain*
  or *describe*. At this level the framework asks you to understand the practice before you own
  it, so a 1 is frequently the exact published expectation.

Here is what a filled-in answer looks like:

> **Iterative design**
>
> - [x] 1 — Awareness: I know what it is and why it matters
>
> *Evidence:* I understand why designs get tested and changed rather than finished in one go, and
> I have watched our designer rework a flow after research. I have not prototyped anything myself.

That is a good answer. It says what is understood and exactly where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: interaction designer.** An interaction designer works out the best way to let users
interact with services, in terms of both overall flow and at the level of individual design
elements.

**Your level: associate interaction designer.** As a trainee in an entry-level position, working
under supervision, you need aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role.

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. **Associate interaction designer** — you are here
2. Junior interaction designer
3. Interaction designer
4. Senior interaction designer
5. Lead interaction designer
6. Head of interaction design

Your next step is junior interaction designer: explaining design decisions, working
collaboratively, holding responsibility as part of a service, working independently after
direction from more senior designers, and identifying user issues and needs.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, three of your seven skills — designing strategically, designing together and leading
design — are worded **identically at junior interaction designer**. They do not change when you are
promoted.

Second, and worth knowing early in your career: **the framework publishes exactly the same seven
skills, with exactly the same wording, for interaction designers, graphic designers and service
designers** — at every level. The only published difference between the three roles is the
one-sentence role description. So the skills cannot tell you what makes interaction design
different from its neighbours; the craft is in the work, not in the framework text. This form asks
about the work.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain the importance of articulating
design decisions to others; and describe some methods for explaining design decisions.

**What that means in interaction design.** The most common decisions you will need to explain are
about splitting: why one question per page rather than a long form, why this step comes before
that one, why an optional field was removed. These look like small choices and change completion
rates substantially.

**What changes at junior.** You would clearly explain problems and design ideas to others, and
clearly explain design decisions using appropriate tools and methods.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain the importance of making
government content and services inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable; explain
different access needs that users can have, and ways to meet them; and describe how privilege and
power influence the design and delivery of content and services.

**What that means in interaction design.** Concretely: can the whole flow be completed with a
keyboard alone, does focus move sensibly, are errors announced rather than only shown in red, and
does the journey work for someone who has to stop halfway and come back tomorrow? Government
services are used by people in difficult circumstances, and an interaction that assumes a calm
uninterrupted session excludes many of them.

**What changes at junior.** You would work with others to design and deliver inclusive, accessible
and sustainable services, and ensure a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility
regulations, with support.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain the importance of aligning to
the strategy of an organisation; describe how your design work aligns to the wider goals and
vision of your team; and explain the value of using patterns and components in design.

**Same wording at junior interaction designer.**

**What that means in interaction design.** Patterns matter more here than almost anywhere. A
government service that uses the standard date input, the standard error summary and the standard
question page is instantly familiar to someone who has used any other government service. Novelty
costs users effort.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain different techniques for
bringing together perspectives from multiple people to inform a design; and explain the importance
of constructive feedback in the design process.

**Same wording at junior interaction designer.**

**What that means in interaction design.** Flows get designed with content designers, developers
and often policy colleagues in the room, because what a screen can ask depends on what the rules
allow and what the system can store. Sketching together on a wall or a shared board resolves in an
hour what a document would take a fortnight to settle.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain how research and analytics
inform user-centred design; frame your ideas as design hypotheses to be tested; and identify
common sources and types of data that inform user-centred design.

**What that means in interaction design.** Your work is unusually testable. Whether people can
complete a step, where they abandon, which error they trigger repeatedly — all of it is observable
in research and in analytics. Watching someone fail to complete a form you designed is the fastest
learning available in this job.

**What changes at junior.** You would analyse, synthesise and clearly explain evidence relevant to
users or a service, and work with researchers or analysts to inform, develop and test a design
idea.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: describe the basics of iterative
design methodologies such as agile; explain how iteration supports user-centred design; and
describe some ways to prototype ideas at different levels of realism, or fidelities, and when to
use them.

**What that means in interaction design.** Prototypes here are usually clickable, because a flow
cannot be judged from a static picture — you have to move through it. Many government interaction
designers prototype in HTML using a departmental or cross-government prototype kit, which is a
useful thing to start learning now.

**What changes at junior.** You would apply iterative design principles and agile methodologies,
iterate based on successive rounds of research, prototype at different fidelities, use a range of
tools and techniques, and use and iterate patterns and components.
