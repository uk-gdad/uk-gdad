# Junior interaction designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a junior interaction designer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do
now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject, or someone
with some relevant work experience, or both. It expects you to explain design decisions; work
collaboratively; have a responsibility as part of a service; work independently after being given
direction by more senior designers; and independently identify user issues and important needs.

**Responsibility as part of a service** is the phrase that matters. A flow you design is not a
picture — it is the path someone takes to claim something they are entitled to, report something
they are required to report, or find out whether they qualify. Getting the order of the questions
wrong has consequences for real people.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating and some
questions. Expect about 25 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: interaction designer.** An interaction designer works out the best way to let users
interact with services, in terms of both overall flow and at the level of individual design
elements.

**Your level: junior interaction designer.** You are a graduate with a degree in a relevant
subject, or someone with relevant work experience, or both. The framework expects that you:

- explain design decisions
- work collaboratively
- have a responsibility as part of a service
- work independently after being given direction by more senior designers
- independently identify user issues and important needs

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate interaction designer
2. **Junior interaction designer** — you are here
3. Interaction designer
4. Senior interaction designer
5. Lead interaction designer
6. Head of interaction design

Your next step is interaction designer: a confident and competent designer who develops designs
based on evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes, trusted to make good decisions, and
contributing to design concepts.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, four of your seven skills are worded **identically at interaction designer** — design
communication, designing for everyone, evidence-based design, and iterative design. Only designing
strategically, designing together and leading design change. The step up is about judgement and
trust rather than new craft.

Second: **the framework publishes exactly the same seven skills, word for word, for interaction
designers, graphic designers and service designers**, at every level. The published skills cannot
distinguish the three disciplines. Everything specific to interaction design — flows, states,
errors, transactions — lives in the work rather than in the framework text.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects, what it looks like
in interaction design, and what changes at interaction designer.

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: clearly explain problems and design ideas
to others; and clearly explain design decisions to others using appropriate tools and methods.

**Same wording at interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** A prototype is usually your best communication tool. Walking a
stakeholder through the actual journey — including what happens when something goes wrong — ends
arguments that a written specification would prolong.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: work with others to design and deliver
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services that meet the needs of
all users; and ensure a design meets appropriate standards, for example accessibility regulations,
**with support**.

**Same wording at interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** The interaction-specific checks are concrete: keyboard-only
completion, sensible focus order, error messages that are announced and that say what to do,
timeouts that do not lose someone's work, and a journey that can be paused and resumed.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: explain the importance of aligning to
the strategy of an organisation; describe how your design work aligns to the wider goals and vision
of your team; and explain the value of using patterns and components in design.

**Same wording as associate interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Government patterns exist because they have been tested repeatedly —
addresses, dates, names, payment. Departing from them needs a reason better than "ours is
different", and being able to say when a departure is justified is the skill.

**The next step.** At interaction designer you align your work to team goals and organisational
objectives, identify risks and use design to mitigate them, identify opportunities, and contribute
to developing patterns and components.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: explain different techniques for bringing
together perspectives from multiple people to inform a design; and explain the importance of
constructive feedback in the design process.

**Same wording as associate interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Interaction design is constrained by what the back-end system can
actually do and by what the rules allow. Designing a flow without a developer and a policy
colleague in the room usually produces something that has to be redesigned.

**The next step.** At interaction designer you plan and run design sessions with your team, users or
stakeholders, identify and engage the right people throughout, and give and receive constructive
feedback.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: analyse, synthesise and clearly explain
evidence relevant to users or a service, for example web analytics data; and work with researchers
or analysts to use evidence to inform, develop and test a design idea.

**Same wording at interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Drop-off points, repeated validation errors and back-button use are
all evidence about your design. A question that triggers the same error for a third of people is
not a user problem, it is a design problem.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: apply iterative design principles and
agile methodologies to your work; iterate and improve a design based on successive rounds of
research; prototype your ideas at different fidelities to explore and test designs; use a range of
design tools and techniques; and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Same wording at interaction designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Prototyping in code is common in government interaction design, and
worth building towards if you have not — a realistic clickable journey gets far better research
findings than a set of screens someone has to imagine moving between.
