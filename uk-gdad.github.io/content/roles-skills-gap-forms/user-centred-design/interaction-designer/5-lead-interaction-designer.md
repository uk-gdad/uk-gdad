# Lead interaction designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead interaction designer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do
now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as an expert practitioner who influences and mentors others. It
expects you to work with service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts; set
direction and **assure the quality of design delivery across teams**; and lead multiple or highly
complex services.

Assuring interaction design quality across teams is a specific problem: the failures you are
looking for are structural rather than visual. A journey that works for the common case and traps
people in an unusual one looks perfectly fine in a screenshot.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating and some
questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of you and
  would stay changed if you left. Mentoring and setting direction are written into your level, so
  several are reasonable.

## About this role level

**The role: interaction designer.** An interaction designer works out the best way to let users
interact with services, in terms of both overall flow and at the level of individual design
elements.

**Your level: lead interaction designer.** You are an expert practitioner who influences and mentors
others. The framework expects that you:

- work with service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts
- set direction and assure the quality of design delivery across teams
- lead multiple or highly complex services

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate interaction designer
2. Junior interaction designer
3. Interaction designer
4. Senior interaction designer
5. **Lead interaction designer** — you are here
6. Head of interaction design

Your next step is head of interaction design: influencing both design and organisational strategy,
collaborating with counterparts across government, and focusing on the conditions and environment
designers need to work effectively.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Five of your seven skills are worded **identically at
head of interaction design** — design communication, designing for everyone, designing together,
evidence-based design and iterative design. Only designing strategically and leading design change,
and both change towards organisational reach rather than deeper craft.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: gain support for design decisions and
design strategy from senior leaders; communicate design strategy across organisational boundaries;
coach others in how to effectively communicate design; and develop or introduce more effective
methods of communicating design and **working in the open**.

**Same wording at head of interaction design.**

**What that looks like here.** Publishing what your teams have learned about journeys — which
patterns worked, which failed and why — is unusually valuable in interaction design, because other
departments are designing the same kinds of transaction and repeating the same mistakes.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: **measurably improve** the practice of
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable design in multiple teams or across your
organisation; and help others understand and mitigate the influence of privilege and power on the
design and delivery of content and services.

**Same wording at head of interaction design.**

**What that looks like here.** In interaction design, privilege and power show up as journeys built
for the applicant the organisation finds easiest to deal with. The person with a stable address, a
smartphone, documents to hand and time to complete in one sitting is the implicit default in most
transactional services.

### Skill: Designing strategically

levels.

with leaders so design informs policy and technology decisions early enough to change them, and

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: advise others how to effectively plan and
run design sessions with a team, users or stakeholders; adapt a design session to ensure you achieve
a useful outcome; effectively involve the right people throughout the design process; and work
across team or profession boundaries, for example with policy teams.

**Same wording at head of interaction design.**

**What that looks like here.** In interaction design the person most often missing is whoever runs
the operational process on the other side — the caseworker whose queue your journey feeds. Journeys
designed without them create work that nobody planned for.

### Skill: Evidence-based design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: embed a practice of hypothesis-driven
design in a team; guide the organisation in how to use evidence to improve services; analyse,
synthesise and use evidence to improve the way the organisation works; and **ensure user insights
are shared with and used by the wider organisation and across government**.

**Same wording at head of interaction design.**

**What that looks like here.** Transactional services generate the strongest evidence in
government — every abandonment is a recorded fact. Whether that evidence reaches the people who set
the rules is a different question, and mostly your job.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: embed the practice of iterative design
and agile working in teams or the wider organisation; **lead on prototyping complex ideas at any
fidelity**; help teams adapt quickly to changes in requirements, priorities or user needs; and
iterate delivery processes or team structures to help teams work effectively.

**What that looks like here.** Leading on prototyping at any fidelity means you can still do it.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: lead and co-ordinate design work in a
team; advocate for user-centred design with **leaders outside of digital and data**; create an
inclusive, productive environment for designers to work in; mentor and coach other designers; and
iterate and improve design processes within your team.

**What that looks like here.** The *with support* qualifier that persisted through the two levels
below has gone. Advocating outside digital and data — to policy, operations and legal — is where
interaction design stops being a build activity and starts shaping what gets built.

**The next step.** At head you set a vision for and lead design across multiple teams or a whole
organisation, work with senior leaders across the organisation or public sector, support a culture of
learning and experimentation, and measurably improve design maturity.
