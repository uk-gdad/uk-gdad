# Junior content designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a junior content designer in the UK Government Digital
and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what
you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as a graduate with a degree in a relevant subject, or
someone with some relevant work experience in content creation. It expects you to explain
content decisions; work collaboratively; work independently after being given direction by
more senior content designers; independently identify user issues and needs; support content
team administration and publishing processes; and engage with and contribute to the
cross-government content community.

**Explaining content decisions** is first on that list for a reason. Anyone can change a
sentence; being able to say why the new version is better — in terms of the user, the
evidence or the style guide, rather than taste — is what makes you a content designer rather
than an editor.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Six skills, each with an explanation, a rating and
some questions. Expect about 25 minutes.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for people to understand
and use. That can mean working on a single piece of content or on the end-to-end journey of a
service, to help users complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. In this
role you will:

- make sure appropriate content is shown to a user in the right place and in the best format
- start from discovery and work closely with user researchers, service designers and
  interaction designers

**Your level: junior content designer.** You are a graduate with a degree in a relevant
subject, or someone with relevant work experience in content creation. The framework expects
that you:

- explain content decisions
- work collaboratively
- work independently after being given direction by more senior content designers
- independently identify user issues and needs
- support content team administration and publishing processes
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government content community

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate content designer
2. **Junior content designer** — you are here
3. Content designer
4. Senior content designer
5. Lead content designer
6. Head of content design

Your next step is content designer: responsible for creating, evaluating, updating and
reviewing content at all stages of the end-to-end journey, comfortable using evidence and
research to make decisions, and contributing to style guides and content patterns.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** **Five of your six skills are worded identically
at associate content designer**, the level below. Only *Strategic thinking (content design)*
rises. So the published skills barely distinguish you from a trainee, and what actually
changes is in the accountabilities — explaining decisions, working independently after
direction, and identifying user needs yourself.

The step to content designer is much larger. Every one of the six skills changes, and several
change substantially. That is the real gap to plan for.

**The six skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Job skills

Six skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects, what it looks
like in practice, and what changes at content designer.

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: demonstrate experience of working
in Agile teams, including an awareness of Agile tools and how to use them; advise colleagues
on how and why Agile methods are used and provide a clear, open and transparent framework in
which teams can deliver; adapt and reflect and be responsive to feedback; and see outside of
the process.

**Same wording as associate content designer.**

**What that looks like here.** *See outside of the process* is the interesting one — noticing
when the way of working is producing content that ticks a box rather than solving a problem,
and saying so.

**The next step.** At content designer this changes substantially: applying an Agile mindset,
working in a fast-paced evolving environment with an iterative approach, being unafraid to
take risks and learn from mistakes, ensuring the team has situational awareness of each
other's work, and using a range of tools to plan and manage your work.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: show an awareness of prototyping and
explain why and when to use it; and understand how to work in an open and collaborative
environment, by pair working for example.

**Same wording as associate content designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Content in a realistic prototype behaves differently from
content in a document — the line length changes, the surrounding elements compete, and the
thing that read well suddenly does not. Seeing your words in context before they are agreed
saves rework.

**The next step.** At content designer you understand different prototyping techniques from
paper sketches to coded prototypes and use them to visualise content in context, choose the
best option for different audiences, and show the value of prototyping to the team.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** One thing: understand who your stakeholders are,
what evidence is relevant to them and the importance of managing relationships with them.

**Same wording as associate content designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Content design is largely a negotiation with people who wrote
the original words. Knowing what evidence moves each of them — research, analytics, the style
guide, an example from another department — is what turns a disagreement into a decision.

**The next step.** At content designer you identify important stakeholders and communicate
clearly and regularly, tailor communication to their needs, build and reach consensus, and use
evidence to explain decisions.

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: support the creation and
development of strategies and policies; contribute to iterating and improving processes and
guidelines; and understand how activities meet strategic goals.

**This is the only skill that rises from associate**, where it was awareness of strategic
context and supporting planning administratively.

**What that looks like here.** Contributing to processes and guidelines means noticing that
something your team does repeatedly could be written down once — a pattern, a checklist, a
standard way of handling a recurring content problem.

**The next step.** At content designer you contribute to content strategies and policies,
create content patterns or standards, support content improvement projects, and focus on
outcomes rather than solutions and activities.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: show an awareness or understanding of
user experience analysis and its principles; and see the purpose of user stories and focus on
user needs.

**Same wording as associate content designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Your accountabilities go further — you are expected to
*independently identify user issues and needs*, which is more than the skill wording says. If
you are doing that, it is worth recording.

**The next step.** At content designer this becomes substantial: identifying tasks that give
insight into a problem, forming hypotheses, understanding the range of users who might access
content, translating user stories into content, and turning insight into outcomes.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: demonstrate basic but decent
editorial standards; show an awareness of style standards; and work well under supervision.

**Same wording as associate content designer.**

**What that looks like here.** The craft basics: plain language, short sentences, the right
heading structure, front-loaded links, and consistency with the style guide. These are not
constraints on good writing — for content people need rather than want to read, they *are*
good writing.

**The next step.** At content designer this becomes the longest skill in the role: designing
content to meet user needs, making complex language and processes easy to understand, robust
experience in writing, editing and publishing, understanding accessibility requirements and
assistive technologies, using data and research to evaluate content, applying standards
consistently, understanding content life cycle management, and working autonomously.
