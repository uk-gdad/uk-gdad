# Lead content designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead content designer in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you
do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as an expert practitioner and leader who directs a team of
content designers, assures content quality across whole teams, and makes sure content aligns to
strategy and objectives while meeting the needs of users. It expects you to work closely with
service managers, programme directors and senior stakeholders to resource teams, resolve
problems and develop future projects; and to promote the content design discipline, engage with
the cross-government community and keep up to date with industry changes.

Only two accountabilities, and the first is largely about **resourcing**. Content design is
usually the discipline with the fewest people relative to the work, and arguing for enough of
them is a permanent feature of this job.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Six skills, each with an explanation, a rating and
some questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

## How to fill in this form

This is a static web page. There is nothing to type into and nothing to send from here — your
answers never touch this site.

Print it, copy the questions into a document, or paste them into whatever your organisation uses
for development conversations. Every question is numbered, so answers travel back on their own.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **"My team does this" is not an answer to "what do you do".** Name your part.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer** at this level too.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or
  how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of you
  and would stay changed if you left. Directing a team and assuring quality across teams are
  written into your level, so several are reasonable.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Content concepts and prototyping**
>
> - [x] 2 — Working: I do it with support
>
> *Evidence:* I make the case for prototyping content and my teams do it. My own hands-on
> prototyping has not moved on since I stopped designing daily.

Named practice, and a clear line about where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: content designer.** Content designers make things easier for people to understand
and use. That can mean working on a single piece of content or on the end-to-end journey of a
service, to help users complete their goal and government deliver a policy intent. In this role
you will:

- make sure appropriate content is shown to a user in the right place and in the best format
- start from discovery and work closely with user researchers, service designers and
  interaction designers

**Your level: lead content designer.** You are an expert practitioner and leader who directs a
team of content designers. You assure content quality across whole teams and make sure content
aligns to strategy and objectives while meeting the needs of users. The framework expects that
you:

- work closely with service managers, programme directors and senior stakeholders to resource
  teams, resolve problems and develop future projects
- promote the content design discipline, engage with the cross-government community and keep up
  to date with industry changes

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate content designer
2. Junior content designer
3. Content designer
4. Senior content designer
5. **Lead content designer** — you are here
6. Head of content design

Your next step is head of content design: developing a content strategy for the organisation,
leading a team capable of executing it, working with senior stakeholders to influence
organisational strategy, and championing good content design practice within government and
industry.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, **five of your six skills are worded identically at head of content design.** Only
*Content concepts and prototyping* differs — and it differs in your favour: yours is the
content designer wording, while the head's is the **associate and junior** wording, the lowest
in the role.

Second, your published wording for prototyping is itself lower than the senior content
designer's below you, which is the most demanding in the role. So on that skill the ladder runs
senior, then you, then head — in descending order. That is how the framework publishes it.

The practical consequence: the difference between lead and head is almost entirely in the
accountabilities — organisational strategy, leading a team to execute it, and championing the
discipline in government and industry.

**The six skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working (content design)
2. Content concepts and prototyping
3. Stakeholder relationship management (content design)
4. Strategic thinking (content design)
5. User focus (content design)
6. User-centred content design

## Job skills

Six skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the level expects, what it looks

### Skill: Agile working (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: coach and lead teams in Agile and
Lean practices, determining the right approach for the team or project; evaluate and review the
approach through the life of a project and iterate and pivot accordingly; think of new and
innovative ways of working to achieve the right outcomes; act as a recognised expert and
advocate the approaches proposed, continuously reflecting on the team's work and constructively
challenging them to improve; and **regularly assess and review capability within teams and
ensure individuals have the skills needed to deliver**.

**Same wording at head of content design.**

**What that looks like here.** That last item is a capability audit in all but name — knowing
who on your teams can do what, and where the gaps would bite if a particular person left.

### Skill: Content concepts and prototyping

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: understand different prototyping
techniques, from paper sketches to coded prototypes, and use them to visualise content in
context; choose the best option to make content understandable to different audiences and
demonstrate a proposed approach; and show the value of prototyping to the team.

**This is the content designer wording**, lower than the senior content designer's below you —
and higher than the head of content design's, which matches the trainee levels. That is how the
framework publishes it, and it means this skill's ladder is not in ascending order at the top of
this role.

**What that looks like here.** At lead the useful contribution is making sure prototyping
happens at all — teams under pressure argue about wording in documents because it feels faster,
and it is not.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: manage long-term strategic
relationships with stakeholders, identifying where new connections need to be made and existing
ones nurtured; direct the strategic approach for stakeholder relationships, establishing
stakeholder objectives and ensuring they are clearly represented; **act as a point of escalation
if stakeholder relationships break down or become challenging for more junior members of the
team**; influence important senior stakeholders and arbitrate when blockers are escalated; and
facilitate discussions across high-risk and complex areas or projects under constrained
timelines.

**Same wording at head of content design.**

**What that looks like here.** Being the escalation point for relationships that have broken
down is the item that protects your people. A content designer being worn down by a stakeholder
should be able to hand that over rather than endure it.

### Skill: Strategic thinking (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: help lead the design and
implementation of strategies, evaluating their impact and progress to ensure business objectives
and user needs are met; and lead and direct strategic content improvement projects, focusing
effort in areas of greatest priority.

**Identical at senior content designer and at head of content design** — the same text across
all three top levels of this role.

**What that looks like here.** At lead the distinctive part is directing improvement across
teams rather than within one, which means choosing between teams' priorities and being able to
justify it.

### Skill: User focus (content design)

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: give direction on which tools or
methods are best for teams to use; demonstrate extensive experience in meeting the needs of users
across a variety of channels; understand complex user journeys and direct solutions to meet
different needs within them; bring insight and expertise in how user needs have changed over time
to ensure they are still relevant and being met; and apply strategic thinking to providing the
best service for the user while also considering business needs and the wider cross-government
context.

**Same wording at head of content design.**

**What that looks like here.** *A variety of channels* includes letters, phone scripts and
face-to-face guidance. Content designers often improve the digital route and leave the letter
that arrives afterwards untouched, contradicting it.

### Skill: User-centred content design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Six things: direct the approach to content life
cycle management so content is regularly reviewed and evaluated by teams; oversee teams' work to
ensure the right content is being produced to meet user needs; demonstrate extensive experience
in creating, iterating, managing and overseeing content across multiple channels; encourage teams
to review and evaluate the effectiveness of processes and systems and support them to iterate;
**be accountable for the production of high quality, user-focused content**; and identify gaps in
content design skills and capability, and help teams grow and develop.

**Same wording at head of content design.**

**What that looks like here.** Being accountable for quality across teams means having a view on
content you have not read. A review approach that runs without you is the only version that
works.
