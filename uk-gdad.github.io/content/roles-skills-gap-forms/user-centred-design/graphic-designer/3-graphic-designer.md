# Graphic designer - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a graphic designer in the UK Government Digital and Data
profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now,
where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as a confident and competent designer who can develop designs
based on evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes. It expects you to be trusted to make
good decisions; recognise when to ask for further guidance and support; contribute to the
development of design concepts; interpret evidence-based research and incorporate it into your
work; support the quality of design delivery across teams; and **lead multiple or highly complex
services**.

That last accountability is a large one for this point in the ladder, and it sits oddly beside
"recognise when to ask for guidance". Both are true: you are trusted, and you are not expected to
be alone.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Seven skills, each with an explanation, a rating and
some questions. Expect about 30 minutes.

## How to fill in this form

You can fill this form in on screen: the boxes below take typing, the tick
boxes tick, and each skill has a rating to choose. Your answers are saved in
this browser, on this device, so you can close the page and come back to them.
Nothing is sent anywhere.

When you have finished, use Export TSV or Export as JSON at the top of the
page to take your answers with you, or print the page, or copy them into
whatever your organisation uses for development conversations. Every question
is numbered, so answers travel back on their own. Clear answers empties the
form and deletes the saved copy, which is worth doing on a computer other
people use.

Worth knowing:

- **Skipping a question means nothing.** A blank is not a finding.
- **Answer about the job you do**, not the one on the job description.
- **"I haven't had the chance" is a real answer**, and more useful than a generous reading of a
  thin example.
- **Ask who reads it.** If nobody has told you who sees your answers, what they are used for, or
  how long they are kept, ask before you fill it in.

## About this role level

**The role: graphic designer.** A graphic designer creates the graphic elements that underpin
interaction and service design. You use layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure
that content is legible and readable and that users see and understand interactions.

**Your level: graphic designer.** You are a confident and competent designer who can develop
designs based on evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes. The framework expects that
you:

- be trusted to make good decisions
- recognise when to ask for further guidance and support
- contribute to the development of design concepts
- interpret evidence-based research and incorporate this into your work
- support the quality of design delivery across teams
- lead multiple or highly complex services

**Where this level sits.** This role has six levels:

1. Associate graphic designer
2. Junior graphic designer
3. **Graphic designer** — you are here
4. Senior graphic designer
5. Lead graphic designer
6. Head of graphic design

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Four of your seven skills are worded **identically at
junior graphic designer** — design communication, designing for everyone, evidence-based design
and iterative design. What changed on promotion into this level was designing strategically,
designing together and leading design, plus a much larger set of accountabilities.

That has a practical consequence worth noticing: your accountabilities now include leading
multiple or highly complex services and supporting design quality across teams, while
*Designing for everyone* is still worded as meeting standards **with support**. If you are the
person other people rely on for accessibility judgements, you are working beyond that wording.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: clearly explain problems and design
ideas to others; and clearly explain design decisions to others using appropriate tools and
methods.

**Same wording as junior graphic designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Leading complex services means explaining decisions to people who
did not attend the workshop, arrived late, and have a different priority. Being able to restate
the reasoning from scratch, without irritation, is most of this.

### Skill: Designing for everyone

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: work with others to design and deliver
inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable content or services that meet the needs of
all users; and ensure a design meets appropriate standards, for example accessibility
regulations, **with support**.

**Same wording as junior graphic designer**, including the *with support* qualifier — which sits
oddly against accountabilities that have you leading complex services.

**What that looks like here.** The graphic decisions that determine accessibility are yours:
contrast, type scale, target sizes, focus states, and whether meaning ever depends on colour
alone. If you are the person others check with, that is beyond the published wording.

**The next step.** Senior levels take this further into influencing standards and practice.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: align your design work to both the
goals and vision of your team and the strategic objectives of your organisation; identify
potential risks and use design to mitigate them; identify opportunities and use design to take
advantage of them; and contribute to the development of design patterns and components.

**What that looks like here.** *Use design to mitigate risk* is the item worth dwelling on. A
clearer warning, a better-signposted deadline, a form that makes an error hard to make — these
prevent problems that would otherwise arrive as complaints or failed applications.

**The next step.** At senior graphic designer you help a team understand how user-centred design helps
it meet its goals, help teams align their work to the organisation's vision, use risks, opportunities
and constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design, and identify and create new design
patterns and components.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: effectively plan and run a design
session with your team, users or stakeholders; identify and engage the right people throughout
the design process; and give and receive constructive design feedback.

**What that looks like here.** Running a session is the new item at your level. The most useful
ones get non-designers sketching — people who cannot draw produce clearer requirements in five
minutes of sketching than in an hour of discussion.

**The next step.** At senior you advise others how to plan and run design sessions, adapt a session to
achieve a useful outcome, involve the right people throughout, and work across team or profession
boundaries — with policy teams, for example.

### Skill: Iterative design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: apply iterative design principles and
agile methodologies to your work; iterate and improve a design based on successive rounds of
research; prototype your ideas at different fidelities to explore and test designs; use a range
of design tools and techniques; and use and iterate design patterns and components.

**Same wording as junior graphic designer.**

**What that looks like here.** Across multiple services the discipline is knowing when to stop
iterating — a design that is good enough and consistent with everything else usually beats a
better one that is unique.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: lead and co-ordinate design work in
your team, **with support**; communicate the value of user-centred design to your team; support
other designers; and work with digital and data leaders in your organisation, when needed.

**What that looks like here.** This is the first level where leading appears as doing rather than
explaining. Supporting other designers usually starts informally — being the person people show
work to before a critique.
