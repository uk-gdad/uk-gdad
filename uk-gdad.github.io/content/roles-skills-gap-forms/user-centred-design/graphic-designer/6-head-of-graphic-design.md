# Head of graphic design - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a head of graphic design in a UK government organisation. Human
resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where the gaps
are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this role as an expert practitioner with broad industry experience who
can define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others. It expects
you to influence both design and organisational strategy and priorities; collaborate with
counterpart colleagues across government; and **focus on ensuring the right conditions and
environment for designers to work effectively**.

That third accountability is unusual and worth taking seriously. Most head-of roles are described
in terms of output; yours is described in terms of the conditions other people work in. Designers
do their best work when they have research access, time to iterate, tools that work and
permission to show unfinished things — and securing those is your job rather than theirs.

Head of graphic design is the top of this role. Seven skills. Expect about 30 minutes.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of you and
  would stay changed if you left. The honest test here is whether design maturity would hold
  after your departure.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

> **Designing strategically**
>

>
> *Evidence:* Patterns are used across three of five directorates and I am working on the rest.
> Getting design into policy decisions early enough to change them is something I argue for and
> have not yet won.

Named practice, and a clear line about where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: graphic designer.** A graphic designer creates the graphic elements that underpin
interaction and service design. You use layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure
that content is legible and readable and that users see and understand interactions.

**Your level: head of graphic design.** You are an expert practitioner with broad industry
experience who can define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring
others. The framework expects that you:

- influence both design and organisational strategy and priorities
- collaborate with counterpart colleagues across government
- focus on ensuring the right conditions and environment for designers to work effectively

**There is no level above this one.** This role has six levels and this is the highest. Where
people go next is a question about other roles — user-centred design leadership, digital
leadership, or leadership outside the profession — and the framework does not define it as a next
step, so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Associate graphic designer
2. Junior graphic designer
3. Graphic designer
4. Senior graphic designer
5. Lead graphic designer
6. **Head of graphic design** — you are here

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Four of your seven skills are worded **identically at
lead graphic designer** — design communication, designing for everyone, evidence-based design and
iterative design. Three are yours alone: designing strategically (embedding design in
organisational strategy and enabling patterns across the organisation), designing together
(running sessions with senior leaders and connecting teams across boundaries), and leading design
(setting a vision across a whole organisation and measurably improving design maturity).

Those three, plus your accountabilities, are what actually distinguishes this job.

**The seven skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Design communication
2. Designing for everyone
3. Designing strategically
4. Designing together
5. Evidence-based design
6. Iterative design
7. Leading design

## Job skills

Seven skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the framework expects and what it
looks like in practice. There is no level above, so these say what the skill demands rather than
what comes next.

### Skill: Design communication

**What the framework expects.** Four things: gain support for design decisions and design strategy
from senior leaders; communicate design strategy across organisational boundaries; coach others in
how to effectively communicate design; and develop or introduce more effective methods of
communicating design and working in the open.

**Same wording as lead graphic designer.**

**What that looks like here.** At head level, gaining support usually means translating design
into the language of whatever the organisation is worried about — cost, risk, complaints,
capacity — without pretending that is why design matters.

### Skill: Designing strategically

**What the framework expects.** Three things: work with others to ensure design becomes part of
the development and implementation of your organisation's strategy; work with leaders to ensure
design is included in policy, technology and delivery processes **early enough to inform effective
decision making**; and enable use of patterns and components across the organisation.

**This is one of the three skills that distinguish your level from lead.**

**What that looks like here.** *Early enough* is the whole argument. Design brought in after a
policy is decided and a technology chosen can only improve the surface; brought in before, it can
change what gets built at all.

### Skill: Designing together

**What the framework expects.** Four things: effectively plan and run design sessions that include
senior leaders or stakeholders; help teams connect and work across organisational boundaries;
coach other designers in how to involve stakeholders of any level in the design process; and
develop or introduce more effective ways to design together.

**This is the second of the three skills that distinguish your level from lead.**

**What that looks like here.** Getting senior leaders to participate in a design session — rather
than be presented to — changes how they think about the work afterwards more than any deck does.

### Skill: Leading design

**What the framework expects.** Four things: set a vision for and lead design across multiple
teams or a whole organisation; work effectively with senior leaders across your organisation or
wider public sector; support a culture of learning and experimentation; and **iterate and
measurably improve design maturity within your organisation and its partners**.

**This is the third of the three skills that distinguish your level from lead.**

**What that looks like here.** Design maturity is measurable — whether design is present in
decisions, whether research happens routinely, whether patterns are reused, whether designers are
recruited and retained. Having a view of where your organisation sits, and where it was two years
ago, is what makes the rest of this credible.
