# Head of accessibility - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a head of accessibility in a UK government organisation.
Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set out what you do now, where
the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this role as an expert practitioner who can create an accessibility
vision or strategy for an organisation. It expects you to define and assure best practice
while influencing, leading and mentoring others; influence and develop organisational
strategy and priorities, working with counterpart colleagues across government; ensure the
right conditions and environment for accessibility specialists to work effectively; develop
departmental capability by supporting internal movement of staff between roles, shaping
career paths and recruiting talent; **understand and own the overall approach to
accessibility risk, including how accessibility risk is communicated and how corporate legal
compliance risk is managed within the organisation**; and engage with and contribute to the
cross-government accessibility community.

That risk accountability is the sharpest in this role, and possibly in this profession. You
own how an organisation understands and communicates a risk that has a specific statutory
duty attached to it.

Head of accessibility is the top of this role. Six skills. Expect about 30 minutes.

| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job description. Owning
  a risk is not the same as having the authority to reduce it, and that gap is what this
  form is for.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed because of
  you and would stay changed if you left.

>

## About this role level

**The role: accessibility specialist.** You provide support, advice and guidance to other
roles in the Government Digital and Data profession about how to create accessible digital
services. You use knowledge of accessibility guidelines, provide technical expertise, and
bring the voice of disabled users to ensure that product teams make digital services that
can be used by everyone.

**Your level: head of accessibility.** You are an expert practitioner who can create an
accessibility vision or strategy for an organisation. The framework expects that you:

- define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others
- influence and develop organisational strategy and priorities, working with counterpart
  colleagues across government
- ensure the right conditions and environment for accessibility specialists to work
  effectively
- develop departmental capability by supporting internal movement of staff between roles,
  shaping career paths and recruiting talent
- understand and own the overall approach to accessibility risk, including how it is
  communicated and how corporate legal compliance risk is managed
- engage with and contribute to the cross-government accessibility community

**There is no level above this one.** This role has four levels and this is the highest.
Where people go next is a question about other roles — user-centred design leadership,
digital leadership, or leadership outside the profession — and the framework does not define
it as a next step, so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Junior accessibility specialist
2. Accessibility specialist
3. Senior accessibility specialist
4. **Head of accessibility** — you are here

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, **your level has six skills; senior has seven.** *Consultancy* does not appear at
your level at all. Advising is plainly a large part of what a head of accessibility does,
but the framework does not publish it as an expectation here.

Second, **your published wording for *Technical understanding (accessibility)* is the same
as the accessibility specialist's**, two levels below — and less than senior's, which names
edge cases, deep knowledge of technologies such as Accessible Rich Internet Applications,
the usability boundary, and confidence with a range of assistive technologies. That is how
the framework publishes it. If you are the organisation's deepest technical authority on
accessibility — and heads often are — you are working beyond the published wording, and it
is worth saying so.

**The six skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Communicating information
2. Governance and assurance (accessibility)
3. Leadership and guidance
4. Technical understanding (accessibility)
5. Testing
6. User focus

## Job skills

Six skills, in the framework's order. Each block gives what the framework expects and what
it looks like in practice. There is no level above, so these say what the skill demands
rather than what comes next.

### Skill: Communicating information

**What the framework expects.** Three things: mediate between people and mend relationships,
communicating with stakeholders at all levels; manage stakeholder expectations and host or
moderate discussions about high risk and complexity, even within constrained timescales; and
speak on behalf of and represent the community to large audiences inside and outside of
government.

**What that looks like here.** *Mend relationships* is unusual wording and telling.
Accessibility work generates friction — findings that arrive late, releases delayed,
teams that feel judged — and someone has to repair what the work costs.

### Skill: Governance and assurance (accessibility)

**What the framework expects.** One thing: analyse governance and assurance systems and add
appropriate measures to ensure accessibility is built into **all project, programme and
change activity**.

**What that looks like here.** *All* is the ambitious word. Digital delivery is usually the
easy part; the change programmes, the bought-in systems and the internal tools are where
accessibility is least considered and where staff with disabilities are most affected.

### Skill: Leadership and guidance

**What the framework expects.** Seven things: change organisational structures to fixable
and sustainable designs; lead on the strategy for the whole organisation, marrying business
needs with innovative analysis; make and justify decisions characterised by high levels of
risk, impact and complexity; build consensus between organisations, public or private, or
highly independent and diverse stakeholders; solve and unblock issues between teams or
departments at the highest level; understand the psychology of a team and have strong
mediation skills; and coach the organisation on team dynamics and conflict resolution.

**What that looks like here.** This is the widest leadership expectation in the role and
sits oddly alongside the absence of a consultancy skill. Changing organisational structures
is a large claim for a head-of role, and worth being honest about whether you can.

### Skill: Technical understanding (accessibility)

**What the framework expects.** Six things: demonstrate knowledge and communicate the
requirements of accessibility standards and legislation; advocate appropriate technical
solutions to a range of accessibility issues; perform detailed audits of websites, services
and documents, and document findings clearly for others to work from; be an advocate for the
people affected by accessibility barriers across the department; provide introductory
awareness and training about accessibility; and demonstrate confidence using one or more
assistive technologies, and knowledge of others.

**A note on the wording.** This is the **accessibility specialist** wording, two levels
below, and less demanding than senior's. That is how the framework publishes it, recorded
here rather than adjusted.

**What that looks like here.** In practice, whether your organisation's technical
accessibility judgement is sound depends on whether you or your seniors hold the depth —
and if it is you, that is beyond what this skill describes.

### Skill: Testing

**What the framework expects.** Three things: manage the planning of system and acceptance
tests, co-ordinating both functional and non-functional specifications; provide authoritative
advice and guidance on test planning; and identify process improvements and contribute to the
definition of best practice.

**What that looks like here.** Accessibility belongs in acceptance criteria rather than in a
separate test phase. If it is still a stage that happens near the end, that is the process
improvement worth making.

### Skill: User focus

**What the framework expects.** Four things: give direction on which tools or methods to use;
demonstrate experience in meeting the needs of users across a variety of channels; bring
insight and expertise in how user needs have changed over time to ensure they are met by the
business; and apply strategic thinking to provide the best service for the end user.

**What that looks like here.** *Across a variety of channels* matters especially in
accessibility, because someone excluded from the digital route ends up on the phone or in
person — and those channels are rarely assessed for accessibility at all.
