# Lead product manager - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you work as a lead product manager in the UK Government
Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that
you can set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this level as responsible for a product portfolio or an
end-to-end service, and for the delivery of product quality and value across it.
You are a member of the management team for your product area, and you provide
product leadership and direction across multiple teams. It expects you to manage
people and manage products; maintain the high-level roadmap; take part in a range
of programme management activities; be involved in recruiting product managers at
all levels and contractors; and provide leadership in your programmes and across
the local community, working closely with the head of product where one exists.

*An end-to-end service* is the phrase that changes the job. A service does not
stop at the boundary of your team, your directorate or your digital channel — it
includes the phone call, the letter, and the caseworker at the other end.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Nine skills, each with an explanation, a
rating and some questions. Expect about 35 minutes.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job
  description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you and would stay changed if you left. Several are reasonable at
  lead, and the framework's own wording for four of these skills is coaching and
  leading.

## About this role level

**The role: product manager.** A product manager makes sure that their products
provide value and achieve the right outcomes, by balancing user and business
needs. In this role you will:

- work with multidisciplinary teams, bringing together their views, user needs
  and business needs, to frame the problem
- work with multidisciplinary teams to provide the right outcomes for users and
  the organisation
- make decisions on priorities for your teams
- ensure teams comply with appropriate standards

**Your level: lead product manager.** You are responsible for a product portfolio
or end-to-end service, and for the delivery of product quality and value. You
work with teams to identify, implement and improve strategies and tactics. You
are a member of the management team for your product area and provide product
leadership and direction across multiple teams. The framework expects that you:

- manage people and manage products
- maintain the high-level roadmap
- be involved in a range of programme management activities relevant to your
  product portfolio
- be involved in recruiting product managers at all levels and contractors
- provide leadership in your programmes and across the local community, working
  closely with the head of product where they exist

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. Associate product manager
2. Product manager
3. Senior product manager
4. **Lead product manager** — you are here
5. Head of product

Your next step is head of product: supporting every product manager in the
organisation, leading on product strategy, and setting and raising standards for
the profession there.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** Six of your nine skills are worded
**identically** at head of product. Only three change: creating value for money
(you would own a business case and iterate it, and build the case for an
organisational approach to value), managing product outcomes (using data to
influence senior stakeholders' priorities and challenging teams to demonstrate
outcomes), and product leadership (co-ordinating the community, leading on
coaching and mentoring, and seeking reuse across government rather than across
your organisation).

The published skills say the step from lead to head is mostly about scope, money
and the profession — not about a different craft. That is useful to know before a
promotion conversation, in either direction.

**The nine skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile and Lean practices
2. Applying user-centred insights
3. Creating value for money
4. Life cycle management
5. Managing product outcomes
6. Product leadership
7. Product management
8. Stakeholder relationship management
9. Strategic ownership

### Skill: Agile and Lean practices

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: coach and lead teams
in Agile and Lean good practices; create and tailor the right approach for a
team, challenging, evaluating and iterating the approach through the life cycle;
experiment with new and innovative ways of working to improve delivery across the
organisation; and act as a recognised expert and advocate for Agile and Lean
approaches.

**What that looks like here.** You are the person other areas ask about ways of
working. You have tried something new at organisational scale and can say whether
it worked. "Recognised expert" means recognised by other people, not by yourself.

**The next step.** Identical wording at head of product.

### Skill: Applying user-centred insights

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: advocate for a range

### Skill: Creating value for money

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: advocate for user
needs, ensuring user value is balanced against cost and value for the
organisation; determine the benefit of a product and persuade others it is the
right product to use; write or contribute to business cases; communicate
business-value propositions; and help others make value-based decisions,
influencing the direction of development.

**Same wording as senior product manager.**

**What that looks like here.** At portfolio scale this becomes about trade-offs
between products rather than within one — persuading people that the right answer
is to fund one thing properly rather than three things thinly.

**The next step.** At head of product you *develop and own* a business case and
iterate it throughout the life cycle, coach others on identifying benefits, and
build the case for an organisational approach to value through product strategy.

### Skill: Life cycle management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: lead and coach teams
through different phases of the life cycle; ensure effective support for the
product or service and its users; predict, prevent, mitigate and manage potential
risks at different phases; and contribute to the assessment of other teams,
providing guidance and support.

**What that looks like here.** *Ensure effective support for the product and its
users* is easy to skim past and is often where portfolios fail — live services
with no route for a user who is stuck. Assessing other teams means sitting on
panels and giving guidance rather than verdicts.

### Skill: Managing product outcomes

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: lead product teams to
create effective methods for measuring product outcomes; advocate for data-driven
decision making; help others prioritise to make better choices; use data to drive
continuous improvement, increasing quality and user experience; and identify
opportunities to share and connect product data.

**Same wording as senior product manager.**

**What that looks like here.** Across a portfolio, the useful question is whether
your products measure things in comparable ways. If each team invented its own
definitions, the portfolio view is fiction.

**The next step.** At head of product you use data to make prioritisation
decisions for multiple or complex products, use data to influence the priorities
of senior stakeholders, guide and challenge teams to make better use of data and
to demonstrate achievement of outcomes, and find opportunities to connect product
data across government.

### Skill: Product leadership

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: seek opportunities for
reuse and learning across your organisation; represent product management in your
area in conversations with senior stakeholders; protect teams from unnecessary
distractions and requests to change direction; coach and manage others in product
management; and lead some product management community activities and participate
in cross-government communities.

**Same wording as senior product manager.**

**What that looks like here.** As a member of the management team you are the
product voice where portfolio decisions get made — which means the protection
your teams get is decided in rooms they are not in.

**The next step.** At head of product you seek reuse across *government*,
co-ordinate the product management community, lead on coaching and mentoring for
the organisation, lead activities in cross-government communities, and represent
product management for the whole organisation.

### Skill: Product management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Five things: set the strategy and
vision for your products, collaborating with others; create a prioritised roadmap
for your products and use it to discuss product strategy at all levels; plan
prioritised work across multiple teams, minimising dependencies and risks;
support multiple teams in delivering quality outcomes in the right way; and coach
and mentor others in product management.

**Same wording at senior, lead and head.** This skill does not change across the
top three levels of the role.

**What that looks like here.** The high-level roadmap is yours, and its job is to
make trade-offs visible rather than to promise dates.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: direct the
stakeholder relationship strategy for your teams; ensure stakeholders' objectives
are set and support teams to meet them; and influence and negotiate with senior
stakeholders to resolve issues and enable progress.

**What that looks like here.** There is a deliberate relationship strategy rather
than a collection of individual habits, and your product managers know what their
stakeholders are trying to achieve. You negotiate at levels they cannot reach.

### Skill: Strategic ownership

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: support and coach
others in creating and implementing a successful long-term strategy and tactical
approach that others agree with; influence and persuade stakeholders to support
delivery of the strategy; support with strategic decision making; and ensure
strategic alignment across the organisation.

**What that looks like here.** You have stopped writing all the strategy and
started making other people better at writing theirs. Alignment across the
organisation means noticing when two areas are pulling against each other and
doing something about it.

**The next step.** Identical wording at head of product.
