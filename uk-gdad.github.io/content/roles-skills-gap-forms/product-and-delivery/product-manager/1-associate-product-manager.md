# Associate product manager - skills gap form

## Introduction

Hello, and welcome. This form is for you if you work as an associate product
manager in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Human resources or your
manager has sent it so that you can say what you do now, what you would like to
get better at, and what would help.

An associate product manager is responsible for features, components or part of a
product, and contributes to the delivery of value. The framework says this can be
an entry-level role — for civil servants moving into a digital role, or for people
on emerging talent schemes. If you arrived here from a completely different job,
you are exactly who this level is for.

The framework expects you to work with product managers to advocate for user
needs, manage a smaller product with support or a subset of features of a larger
one, and learn product management approaches and methods. **Learning is written
into the level.** That is unusual, and it means saying "I am still learning this"
is not an admission — it is the job description.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. There are no right answers. This is
your chance to say what you need, and it works best when you are honest about the
gaps.

There are nine skills. Each gets a plain explanation, a rating, and a couple of
questions. Take about 30 minutes. You can stop and come back.

Some questions ask you to rate yourself. Use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |

| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you could probably manage
  or what you learned on a course.
- **At this level, 1s and 2s are exactly right.** The framework says you manage a
  product *with support*. A 2 means "I do this with support" — that is your
  level, not a shortfall. Nobody should expect a row of 4s here, and a form full
  of them would be less believable, not more impressive.

## About this role level

**The role: product manager.** A product manager makes sure that their products
provide value and achieve the right outcomes, by balancing user needs and
business needs. That balancing is the heart of the job. In this role you will:

- work with multidisciplinary teams, bringing together their views, user needs
  and business needs, to frame the problem
- work with multidisciplinary teams to provide the right outcomes for users and
  the organisation
- make decisions on priorities for your teams
- ensure teams comply with appropriate standards

**Your level: associate product manager.** You are responsible for features,
components or part of a product, contributing to the delivery of value. The
framework expects that you:

- work with product managers to advocate for user needs throughout the
  organisation, prioritising delivery of greatest value
- manage a smaller product with support, or a subset of features of a larger
  product
- learn product management approaches and methods

**Where this level sits.** This role has five levels:

1. **Associate product manager** — you are here
2. Product manager
3. Senior product manager
4. Lead product manager
5. Head of product

Your next step is product manager. The difference is real but not enormous: you
would lead a product through the whole life cycle rather than a part of one, work
with less support, and be responsible for product quality and value rather than
contributing to it. Interestingly, the framework describes *that* level as an
entry point too — for people coming from other digital roles.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, three of your nine skills are worded at this level as *explain* rather
than *do* — life cycle management, managing product outcomes, and product
leadership. The framework is asking you to understand these before you own them.
Being able to explain what each life cycle phase is for is a genuine achievement
at this level.

Second, some of the other skills are worded surprisingly ambitiously for an
entry-level role. *Applying user-centred insights*, for example, asks you to
decide when you need more user insight and when you have enough to make a
decision — which is a judgement most people take years to develop. That is the
framework's text, quoted as published. If it reads as more than you do today,
that is the framework being ambitious, not you falling short.

**The nine skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile and Lean practices
2. Applying user-centred insights
3. Creating value for money
4. Life cycle management
5. Managing product outcomes
6. Product leadership
7. Product management

### Skill: Agile and Lean practices

**What the framework expects at your level.** Four things: demonstrate experience
in applying Agile principles in practice; provide a clear, open and transparent
framework in which teams can deliver; show an awareness of Agile tools and start
to use them intelligently; and visualise and make visible the work of the team.

**What that means day to day.** Agile is a way of working in short cycles,
showing progress often, and changing course when you learn something. Your part
of it is keeping the work visible — a board people can look at, a plan nobody has
to ask about — and being open when something is not going well. "Using tools
intelligently" mostly means not letting the ticket tracker become the point.

**What changes at product manager.** You would compare and select delivery
methods rather than working within one, encourage experimentation, and help teams
measure and visualise their own outcomes.

### Skill: Applying user-centred insights

**What the framework expects at your level.** Four things: identify and engage
users and stakeholders to identify user needs and trends; decide when you need
more user insights and when you have enough to make a decision; work with others
to define the problem and research goals; and use quantitative and qualitative
user insights to improve product or service outcomes.

**What that means day to day.** Knowing who uses your product and what they are
trying to do — and using what user researchers find rather than what you assume.
Quantitative means numbers, qualitative means what people said; both matter, and
they often disagree.

**A note on the wording.** "Decide when you have enough insight to make a
decision" is a hard judgement, and the framework asks it of you at entry level.
Most people at this stage take that decision to someone more experienced. If that
is you, rate honestly and say so.

**What changes at product manager.** You would represent users in internal
discussions using insight, advocate for research with all types of user, and use
user needs to agree priorities with the team.

### Skill: Creating value for money

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: clearly describe the
value of your work; determine or estimate the benefits your work is expected to
deliver; and identify opportunities for reuse, saving time and money.

**What that means day to day.** Being able to say what your work is *for* in
terms someone outside the team would recognise — usually time saved, mistakes
avoided, or something people can now do that they could not before. Reuse means
noticing when something already exists rather than building it again.

**What changes at product manager.** You would estimate the value of each item on
a roadmap, compare effort to value when prioritising, and escalate risks to value
when they appear.

### Skill: Life cycle management

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things, both of them explaining
rather than doing: explain what each phase of the product or service life cycle
is for; and explain how the needs of the product vary across phases, and how a
team responds to them.

**What that means day to day.** Government digital work runs through phases —
discovery, alpha, beta, live, and eventually retirement. Each phase asks a
different question. Discovery asks whether there is a real problem; alpha tries
things cheaply; beta builds the real thing with real users; live keeps it running
and improves it. Teams look and behave differently in each.

**What changes at product manager.** You would manage delivery through the phases
with some support, decide whether a product should move to the next phase, and
make sure it is developed against the service standard for that phase.

### Skill: Managing product outcomes

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: explain the
importance of identifying and measuring product outcomes; explain the need for
baseline data; and identify and explain the priority outcomes for your product or
feature.

**What that means day to day.** An outcome is a change in the world — fewer
people needing to phone for help, applications completed correctly first time.
It is not the same as an output, which is a thing you shipped. Baseline data is
the measurement you take *before* you change anything, so you can tell later
whether it worked. Teams forget the baseline constantly and then cannot prove
anything.

**What changes at product manager.** You would work with others to define key
performance indicators, analyse trusted data to make decisions, and explain
success metrics to stakeholders.

### Skill: Product leadership

**What the framework expects at your level.** Three things: describe how product
management works with other roles in a multidisciplinary team; explain how user
and business needs should be balanced in product development; and attend product
management community activities.

**What that means day to day.** Understanding what everyone else on the team is
for — the delivery manager, the user researcher, the designers, the developers,
the analysts — and how product management fits alongside them. And turning up to
the product community, wherever your organisation runs one. That last one is the
easiest item in this whole form to actually do.

**What changes at product manager.** You would advocate for a product-led
approach, protect the team from distraction and changes of direction, and
mediate with stakeholders rather than mainly observing.

### Skill: Product management

**What the framework expects at your level.** Four things: create and prioritise
actionable goals for your teams, balancing user needs and business perspectives;
make or recommend an evidence-based decision using insight and data; use a range
of product management methods, such as prioritisation and roadmapping; and work
with others in multidisciplinary teams to plan and deliver high-quality product
increments.

**What that means day to day.** This is the craft of the job. A roadmap is a
plan of what you intend to do and roughly when, held loosely. Prioritisation is
deciding what comes first when everything cannot. An evidence-based decision is
one you can explain by pointing at something rather than at your instinct.

**What changes at product manager.** You would investigate and validate what adds
value now and later, own a prioritised roadmap and use it to discuss strategy
with stakeholders, and manage scope under uncertainty.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at your level.** Four things: identify important
stakeholders and communicate with them clearly and regularly; tailor
communication to stakeholders' needs and work with them to build relationships
while meeting user needs; build and reach consensus with stakeholders; and work
to improve stakeholder relationships using evidence to explain decisions.

**What that means day to day.** A stakeholder is anyone with an interest in your
product — a policy team, an operations manager, a senior sponsor. Tailoring means
the same news told differently to different people, without changing what is
true. Using evidence to explain decisions is what keeps a disagreement about
priorities from becoming a disagreement about you.

**What changes at product manager.** You would work with the team on a shared
understanding of stakeholders and a communications strategy, and resolve issues
rather than escalate them.

### Skill: Strategic ownership

**What the framework expects at your level.** Two things: explain how you make
decisions that are aligned to the strategy of your organisation; and explain the
importance of having a strategy.

**What that means day to day.** Being able to connect what you do this sprint to
what the organisation says it is trying to achieve. If you cannot find that
connection, it is worth asking — sometimes the strategy is not well communicated,
and sometimes the work genuinely has drifted.

**What changes at product manager.** You would work with users and stakeholders
to develop a strategy and vision rather than align to one, and review and iterate
it regularly.
