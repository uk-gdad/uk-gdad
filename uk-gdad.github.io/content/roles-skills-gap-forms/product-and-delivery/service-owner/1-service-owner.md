# Service owner - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a service owner in the UK Government Digital and
Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can set
out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes a service owner as an accountable leader who brings
together a multidisciplinary team to ensure delivery of a quality service. You
advocate for your service and teams, and develop an open and trust-based culture.
You are accountable for the quality, performance, benefits and outcomes of the
service, and you provide clear end-to-end direction, prioritisation, risk
management and decision making in line with department, policy and user outcomes.

It expects you to ensure a strategy is in place that delivers the intended vision,
outcomes and benefits; prioritise areas of development, balancing user needs,
business requirements and policy; ensure appropriate governance and assurance is
in place; ensure appropriate funding is in place; and create the culture and
environment for the team to do their best work.

Two words in that list carry most of the job. **Accountable** means the buck
genuinely stops with you. **End-to-end** means the whole service — the phone
call, the letter, the caseworker, the appeal — not the digital part of it.

Nothing here is scored, marked or ranked. Nine skills, each with an explanation, a
rating and some questions. Expect about 35 minutes.

## Rating scale

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not what you are accountable for on
  paper. Accountability and capability are different things, and the gap between
  them is exactly what this form is for.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you and would stay changed if you left the service.

A filled-in answer looks like this:

>

> major incidents. The operating model itself was designed before I arrived and I

Named practice, and a clear line about where it stops.

## About this role level

**The role: service owner.** You are accountable for the quality, performance,
benefits and outcomes of a service, and you provide clear end-to-end direction,
prioritisation, risk management and decision making for teams, in line with
department, policy and user outcomes. In this role you will:

- define success and quality measures for a service, driving continuous
  improvement towards these measures
- inform and influence stakeholders, getting consensus that enables work to
  progress
- ensure the delivery of change that realises the outcomes and benefits of your
  service

**Your level: service owner.** The framework publishes this role with a single
level. You are an accountable leader who brings together a multidisciplinary team
to ensure delivery of a quality service; you advocate for your service and teams,
and develop an open and trust-based culture. The framework expects that you:

- ensure a strategy is in place that delivers the intended vision, outcomes and
  benefits
- prioritise areas of development, balancing user needs, business requirements
  and policy
- ensure appropriate governance and assurance is in place
- ensure appropriate funding is in place
- create the culture and environment for the team to do their best work

**One level, no ladder.** There is no junior or senior service owner in the
framework, so progression here means moving to a different role rather than up
this one. That is worth raising in a development conversation, since single-level
roles are easy to overlook when development budgets are allocated.

**Two things worth knowing about this role.**

First, four of the five accountabilities begin with *ensure*. That is deliberate:
much of this job is making sure something exists and is adequate, rather than
producing it yourself. Ensuring is not the same as delegating — it means you are
answerable for the quality of the thing whether or not you wrote it.

Second, *create the culture and environment for the team to do their best work*
is the accountability nobody measures and everybody notices. It rarely appears in
a performance conversation, and it is usually the first thing people mention when
a service owner leaves.

**The nine skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Adapting to delivery methodologies
2. Applying user-centred insights
3. Financial ownership
4. Governance and assurance
5. Leading performance and benefits
6. Life cycle management
7. Operational management
8. Stakeholder relationship management
9. Strategic ownership

### Skill: Adapting to delivery methodologies

**What the framework expects.** Four things: set and iterate standards for
working within different delivery methodologies; actively promote collaboration
and agreement so the team delivers value at the right time; experiment with new
and innovative ways of working to enhance delivery outcomes; and research new and
evolving delivery methodologies, identifying how your role would work within
them.

**What that looks like here.** A service usually contains teams working very
differently — an agile digital team, an operational team on a rota, a policy team
on its own cycle. Your job is not to make them the same, but to make them fit
together. The last item is unusual and worth noticing: the framework asks you to
work out how *your own role* would change under a new methodology.

### Skill: Applying user-centred insights

**What the framework expects.** Four things: advocate for a range of effective
research approaches across the organisation; coach others in making decisions
that meet user needs across a range of channels; advocate for continuous use of
user insights in teams; and use user insights to make strategic decisions to
provide the best user experience.

**What that looks like here.** *Across a range of channels* is the end-to-end
point again. The users who struggle most with a government service are often the
ones who never appear in digital analytics — people who phoned, gave up, or asked
someone else to do it for them.

### Skill: Financial ownership

**What the framework expects.** Five things: ensure a business case is in place
that provides sustainable funding; identify measurable benefits that represent
good value for money; allocate money and make spending decisions across competing
priorities; enhance and own relationships with suppliers to increase quality and
deliver good value outcomes; and act as an escalation point, working with
stakeholders to manage financial and commercial risk.

**What that looks like here.** *Sustainable* funding is the hard word. Plenty of
services are funded to be built and not funded to be run, and noticing that early
is one of the most valuable things a service owner does. Owning supplier
relationships means the supplier's account manager knows you, not only the
commercial team.

### Skill: Governance and assurance

**What the framework expects.** Three things: ensure appropriate and proportionate
governance and assurance is in place and continuously improved, aligned to
organisational and government needs; guide teams through governance and assurance
processes, such as service assessments, so standards are met; and share the
team's governance and assurance processes so stakeholders trust them to deliver
value in a sustainable way.

**What that looks like here.** *Proportionate* is the operative word — governance
that costs more than the risk it manages is a failure even when it is followed
perfectly. The third item is subtle: publishing how you govern is what earns the
freedom to be governed lightly.

### Skill: Leading performance and benefits

**What the framework expects.** Three things: ensure appropriate measures are in
place to deliver valuable outcomes to users and agreed business benefits; ensure
metrics are used to monitor, manage and prioritise delivery and inform decision
making; and demonstrate the value of the service and build understanding with
stakeholders by ensuring effective data and insights are shared.

**What that looks like here.** The test is whether your metrics ever change a
decision. Many services measure conscientiously and decide by instinct anyway.
Benefits realisation is the other trap — benefits promised in a business case get
quietly forgotten unless someone keeps checking.

### Skill: Life cycle management

**What the framework expects.** Four things: work and consult with the right
people at the right time to move through the life cycle and deliver value; use
evidence to decide when a team should continue, change direction or stop;
identify tools and techniques required at different phases; and guide colleagues
and stakeholders through different phases.

**What that looks like here.** Deciding to stop something is easier to write than
to do when you are the accountable owner and stopping looks like failure. Guiding
stakeholders through phases usually means explaining, repeatedly, that a live
service is never finished.

### Skill: Operational management

**What the framework expects.** Five things: ensure a scalable operating model is
in place to provide a resilient, high-quality service; co-ordinate teams from
operations, policy, digital and delivery to operate and iterate a service; ensure
teams understand the importance of efficient service operations; identify and
overcome operational constraints; and act as the escalation point for major
operational issues.

**What that looks like here.** This skill is what distinguishes a service owner
from a product owner. Co-ordinating operations, policy, digital and delivery
together is the actual work — four groups with different rhythms, different
managers and different definitions of urgent. Being the escalation point for major
operational issues means the worst days are yours.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects.** Three things: direct the stakeholder relationship
strategy for your teams; ensure stakeholders' objectives are set and support teams
to meet them; and influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to resolve
issues and enable progress.

**What that looks like here.** A service crosses organisational boundaries, so
your stakeholders include people with no reason to prioritise you. Directing the
strategy means your teams are not each inventing their own approach to the same
senior person.

### Skill: Strategic ownership

**What the framework expects.** Four things: support and coach others in creating
and implementing a successful long-term strategy and tactical approach that others
agree with; influence and persuade stakeholders to support delivery of the
strategy; support with strategic decision making; and ensure strategic alignment
across the organisation.

**What that looks like here.** *That others agree with* is the demanding clause.
A service strategy nobody outside your team endorses is a document, not a
strategy. Alignment across the organisation means noticing when another part of
the organisation is about to do something that will hurt your service.
