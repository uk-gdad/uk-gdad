# Head of portfolio - skills gap form

## Introduction

This form is for you if you are a head of portfolio in the UK Government Digital
and Data profession. Human resources or your manager has sent it so that you can
set out what you do now, where the gaps are, and what would close them.

The framework describes this role as leading a team of digital portfolio
managers and making sure the portfolio of digital projects delivers the best
possible outcomes. You are expected to lead the tracking and governing of digital
projects; ensure the right controls are used; address risks and deal with or
escalate issues; provide leaders with insights into the portfolio's future so
they can make informed decisions; build and lead a diverse, skilled and motivated
team of portfolio managers; ensure the portfolio team meets the needs of the
organisation; and support the professional development of the portfolio
community, including line management and coaching.

*Insights into the portfolio's future* is the phrase that separates this job from
reporting. Anyone can describe what has happened; the value here is in telling
leaders what is about to.

Head of portfolio is the top of this role. Nothing here is scored, marked or
ranked. Each of the six skills gets an explanation, a rating and some questions.
Expect about 35 minutes.

Where a question asks for a rating, use this scale:

| Value | Rating | Meaning |

| 0 | None | You have not worked with this |
| 1 | Awareness | You know what it is and why it matters |
| 2 | Working | You do it with support |
| 3 | Practitioner | You do it unsupported, and handle the usual exceptions |
| 4 | Expert | You set the approach and develop others in it |

Two things about the scale:

- It describes **what you do at work today**, not the remit on your job
  description.
- **A 4 is a claim about other people** — that someone else's practice changed
  because of you and would stay changed if you left. Several are reasonable here.

## About this role level

**The role: digital portfolio manager.** A digital portfolio manager co-ordinates
and manages a group — a portfolio — of digital projects, using an iterative and
flexible approach to help define governance processes for that portfolio.

**Your level: head of portfolio.** You lead a team of digital portfolio managers
and ensure the portfolio delivers the best possible outcomes. The framework
expects that you:

- lead the tracking and governing of digital projects
- ensure the right controls are used
- address risks and deal with, or escalate, issues
- provide leaders with insights into the portfolio's future so they can make
  informed decisions
- build and lead a diverse, skilled and motivated team of digital portfolio
  managers
- ensure the portfolio team is meeting the needs of the organisation
- support the professional development of the portfolio community, including line
  management and coaching

**There is no level above this one.** This role has four levels and this is the
highest. Where people go next is a question about other roles — delivery
leadership, digital leadership, or leadership outside the profession — and the
framework does not define it as a next step, so this form does not invent one.

**Where this level sits:**

1. Digital portfolio analyst
2. Digital portfolio manager
3. Senior digital portfolio manager
4. **Head of portfolio** — you are here

Two skills are yours rather than shared with senior: stakeholder relationship
management, which becomes directing the relationship strategy for your teams; and
strategic thinking, which becomes defining strategies and policies and evaluating
whether they are meeting business requirements. Communicating analysis is worded
differently rather than obviously more demanding.

**One thing worth knowing about this role.** *Agile working* is worded identically
at all four levels, including yours. A head of portfolio is described in exactly
the terms used for an analyst on that skill. If you lead agile practice across
your area — and most people in this job do — you are working beyond the published
expectation.

**The skills this form covers**, in the framework's order:

1. Agile working
2. Communicating analysis and insight
3. Governance and assurance
4. Planning
5. Stakeholder relationship management
6. Strategic thinking

### Skill: Agile working

**What the framework expects at this level.** Four things: show an awareness of
Agile methodology and how to apply the principles in practice; take an open-minded
approach; explain why iteration is important; and iterate quickly.

**Same wording as every level of this role**, including analyst. That is the
framework's text. If you are leading agile practice across your portfolio, you are
working beyond the published expectation and it is worth recording.

**What that looks like here.** The portfolio's own machinery — reporting,
governance, prioritisation — is iterated rather than defended. Teams are not
forced into one shape to make the portfolio easier to report on.

### Skill: Communicating analysis and insight

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: communicate relevant,
compelling stories using the most appropriate medium; and make complex
recommendations at a tactical and strategic level.

**What that looks like here.** Leaders act on what you tell them. Your
recommendations carry consequences, not just observations. You know which medium
lands with which audience — a paper, a conversation, a single number.

**Different from senior**, whose wording is longer and explicitly includes
communicating negative as well as positive information and sharing data
communication skills across government. Yours is shorter — which does not mean
those things stop mattering.

### Skill: Planning

**What the framework expects at this level.** Six things: understand the
environment and prioritise the most important or high value tasks; use data to
inform planning; manage complex internal and external dependencies; provide
delivery confidence; remove blockers or impediments that affect plans and develop
a plan for difficult situations; and ensure teams plan appropriately for their
capacity.

**What that looks like here.** You give leaders a confidence level they can rely
on. You see the dependencies nobody else has joined up. You say when the
portfolio is planned beyond the organisation's capacity — which is the most
useful and least welcome thing this role can say.

**Same wording as senior digital portfolio manager**, and there is no level above.

### Skill: Stakeholder relationship management

**What the framework expects at this level.** Three things: direct the stakeholder
relationship strategy for your teams; ensure stakeholders' objectives are set and
support teams to meet them; and influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders
to resolve issues and enable progress.

**What that looks like here.** There is a deliberate relationship strategy rather
than a set of individual habits. Your portfolio managers know what their
stakeholders are trying to achieve. You negotiate at levels your team cannot
reach.

**Different from senior**, which works with the team on understanding and
communications rather than directing the strategy and negotiating at senior
level.

### Skill: Strategic thinking

**What the framework expects at this level.** Two things: define strategies and
policies, providing guidance to others on working in the strategic context; and
evaluate current strategies to ensure business requirements are being met and
exceeded where possible.

**What that looks like here.** There is a portfolio strategy or prioritisation
policy that you wrote, and people work to it. You evaluate whether strategy is
delivering rather than assuming. You have recommended stopping something because
the evaluation said so.

**Different from senior**, which works within the strategic context and
contributes to strategy rather than defining and evaluating it.

## Legal compliance

Portfolio work handles information about projects, people and money, and portfolio
records may be disclosable. Portfolio governance is often where accessibility,
equality and public money obligations get checked — or missed. These questions ask
about awareness, training and support, not about whether you can state the law
correctly.
