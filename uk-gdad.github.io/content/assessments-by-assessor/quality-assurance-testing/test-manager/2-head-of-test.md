# Head of Test - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor evaluating a candidate for head of test within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are running a formal promotion panel, screening an external candidate for a director-adjacent post, or simply want a structured way to test whether someone is ready for the role, this guide equips you with a set of workplace job-specific assessment materials — and the answer key that goes with them.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. At head of test level, that means far more than abstract puzzles. The assessments in this guide are job-specific: present the candidate with the kinds of artefacts a head of test genuinely handles — testing policies and organisational standards, portfolio quality dashboards, workforce and capability plans, supplier strategies, business cases for new technology, and communications to boards and cross-government communities — and ask them to reason about the kinds of decisions a head of test genuinely makes, such as establishing policy for the test function, setting the testing strategy, owning continuous improvement, and ensuring that teams across the organisation have the right capabilities.

Why do these assessments matter at this level in particular? A head of test's decisions shape not one release but an organisation's entire relationship with quality. The role sets standards and influences organisational decisions for test types, techniques, design, and execution; researches new technology and new techniques to implement change; builds the QAT community and provides organisational leadership; and may represent test capabilities to audiences inside and outside government. Assessments of cognitive ability, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement map directly onto those demands, and testing them well tells you whether a candidate can detect the inconsistency between two policy documents before it becomes two years of divergent practice, interrogate a portfolio dashboard whose aggregation hides a failing programme, extract precisely what a Cabinet Office standard does and does not mandate, and choose the wisest available action when a director, a supplier, and organisational principle pull in three directions.

This document is organised into four main assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why employers use it for senior quality leadership roles, a mapping of the assessment dimensions to the specific named skills in the head of test capability framework, a substantial set of items with full worked answers and explanations for you to hold as the answer key, guidance on administering the section, and common pitfalls candidates fall into. Items progress from easy through moderate to hard within each section — though even the easy items are set in the territory a head of test occupies: policy, portfolio, capability, and community.

Administer the assessment fairly and consistently. Use the same items and the same time limit for every candidate you assess for this role level, so that results are comparable across candidates rather than an artefact of which questions someone happened to receive. Keep the worked answers and explanations back as your answer key: do not share them with the candidate before or during the sitting, only afterwards when you score the responses and debrief. After scoring, debrief the candidate — walk them through what the item was testing and why the key answer is the strongest one, in the same spirit the explanations below are written: not as a verdict but as a lesson, so the sitting is worth something to the candidate even where their answer differed from the key. And treat the result as one input among several: weigh it alongside interviews, work samples, references, and track record, and never let a single sitting of unvalidated material be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance, or disciplinary decision — the Advisory section below says this formally, and it is worth holding in mind throughout.

Here is how to get the most from this guide before a sitting. First, read each "About this assessment" section so you understand the format the candidate is likely to face and how to present it. Second, work through the items yourself before you administer them, so you can time the sitting realistically and answer procedural questions with confidence. Third, treat every explanation as a mini-lesson for the debrief: even where a candidate answers correctly, the walkthroughs connect technique to the strategic responsibilities of the role, and several address the subtle failure modes — aggregation traps, measurement distortions, governance ambiguities — that senior candidates meet more often than anyone below them. Finally, use the pitfalls sections to calibrate what a strong versus a weak answer looks like, and consider discussing the trickier items with a fellow assessor before you rely on your own judgement alone.

Take your time preparing, and use this material with care. A candidate for head of test has likely spent a career assuring the quality of other people's work; the least you owe them is an assessment administered thoughtfully and scored fairly. Let's begin.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes the head of test role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Unlike a generic intelligence test, present the candidate with the materials of senior quality leadership — testing policies and organisational standards, portfolio dashboards, capability matrices, technology evaluation papers, and governance submissions — and ask them to reason quickly and accurately about them.

Typical format: an online or paper-based, timed sitting lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Score objectively against the answer key below rather than impressionistically; where you have access to a norm group or a commercial platform, use it, but the worked items here stand on their own. If you are running this as a timed sitting, consider recording a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score, and weigh cognitive results alongside interviews, staff-facing exercises and track record — they carry real weight precisely because they are one of the few instruments in a process that cannot be prepared as a narrative.

At head of test level, cognitive assessment is relevant in a distinctive way. The reasoning the role demands is mostly *second-order*: a head of test rarely checks an individual test case, but constantly checks the systems that check — does a policy, applied by a hundred people who will read it less carefully than its author, produce the behaviour intended? Does a portfolio dashboard's aggregation method preserve or destroy the signal a board needs? Does the argument for adopting an AI-assisted testing tool actually support the conclusion, or only decorate it? When a head of test makes strategic decisions on new or improved test types and techniques, an error propagates to every team that follows the standard; when they set principles for test analysis across the organisation, an ambiguity becomes a hundred local interpretations. This is why cognitive ability is assessed at this level: the blast radius of a reasoning failure is the whole function, and the sheer volume of papers, dashboards and proposals crossing a head of test's desk means their reasoning must be not only sound but fast.

A note on fairness and reasonable adjustments: reputable assessment platforms used across the Civil Service comply with accessibility standards, and candidates at every level are entitled to reasonable adjustments — extra time, screen reader compatibility, alternative formats. Ask the candidate in advance whether they need any, and build them into the sitting; a candidate for a role that sets inclusive standards for others should experience an inclusive process themselves.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in the head of test capability framework:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to the **Managing, reporting and resolving defects** skill and ownership of continuous improvement: leading and coaching others in using defect patterns and trends to make tactical and strategic recommendations means recognising organisational-scale patterns — a defect class recurring across unrelated teams, a portfolio trend that only appears when data is disaggregated, an improvement that plateaus wherever a particular practice is missing.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Designing and executing tests** at this level — setting standards and making strategic decisions about test types and techniques requires reasoning validly from policy, evidence and constraint to consequence — and to **Test analysis**, where the role sets standards and principles whose logical implications must be foreseen before an organisation lives with them.
- **Error checking** maps to ownership of **establishing policy and setting testing strategy**: policies, standards and board papers must be internally consistent, and consistent with each other, because at this level the documents are the decisions — an undetected contradiction between a test policy and an environments standard becomes two departments' divergent practice.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the duty to **make important decisions** and to **ensure teams have the right capabilities**: allocating attention, budget and senior people across a portfolio of competing demands is the daily exercise of impact-weighted judgement.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to ownership of **continuous improvement, researching new technology and new techniques to implement change**: evaluating pilots, disentangling confounded evidence, and designing organisational interventions that survive contact with reality are problem solving at the scale this role occupies.

### Assessment items

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a standards taxonomy**

A candidate's QAT standards library uses a fixed identifier scheme: `QAT-<domain>-<serial>`, where the domain code is ST (strategy), PL (planning), EX (execution), EN (environments) or RP (reporting), and the serial is a three-digit number unique within its domain. A newly merged library from a partner organisation contains:

1. QAT-PL-014
2. QAT-EX-101
3. QAT-EN-07
4. QAT-RP-220
5. QAT-ST-003

Which entry breaks the scheme?

- A) Entry 2
- B) Entry 3
- C) Entry 4
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each entry component by component: prefix `QAT`, a valid two-letter domain code, then a three-digit serial. Entries 1, 2, 4 and 5 conform. Entry 3 uses a two-digit serial ("07" rather than "007"), breaking the three-digit rule. The check is trivial in isolation — but it earns its place in a head-of-test assessment because the scenario is merging two organisations' standards libraries, and identifier drift is exactly the kind of small inconsistency that silently breaks cross-references, automated compliance tooling, and audit trails at scale. Setting standards for test design and execution across an organisation includes caring, institutionally, about the boring conformance details — not because the head of test checks them personally, but because they commission the tooling and governance that does. In the assessment, the skill being sampled is disciplined element-wise comparison at speed; in the role, it is knowing that structure errors compound faster than content errors.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a testing policy**

A candidate's organisational testing policy states: "No service may enter public beta without an assurance review by the QAT function, unless the service is classified as an internal tool, in which case a self-assessment approved by the responsible test manager suffices." The candidate learns: "Service X entered public beta without a QAT assurance review." Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) Service X breached the testing policy.
- B) Service X is an internal tool with an approved self-assessment.
- C) Either Service X is an internal tool with an approved self-assessment, or the policy was breached.
- D) The responsible test manager approved a self-assessment for Service X.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The policy is a conditional with a single carve-out. From the facts given, you cannot determine whether Service X qualifies for the carve-out, so neither A nor B is guaranteed individually — each is merely possible. D asserts one component of the carve-out as fact without evidence. Only the disjunction in C must hold in every scenario consistent with the facts. The discipline — conclude exactly what the information guarantees, no more — is one the role exercises constantly as the owner of policy: when a compliance report lands on a head of test's desk showing an apparent breach, the professional first move is to check whether an exception legitimately applies, not to open with an accusation. At this level it matters doubly, because a leader's reaction to apparent breaches sets the tone for how the whole organisation treats their policy — as a reasoned instrument with legitimate exception routes, or as a stick. A strong candidate's answer shows exactly this order: deduce first, then inquire, then act.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a portfolio summary**

A portfolio quality report prepared for a candidate states: "Across our 4 programmes, 26 releases shipped this quarter: Alpha 8, Beta 6, Gamma 7, Delta 5. Escaped defects totalled 18: Alpha 4, Beta 3, Gamma 6, Delta 4. Overall escaped-defect rate: 0.69 per release, an improvement on last quarter's 0.80." Which figure is internally inconsistent?

- A) The release total: the programme figures sum to 25, not 26.
- B) The escaped defect total: the programme figures sum to 17, not 18.
- C) The rate: 18 ÷ 26 is 0.90, not 0.69.
- D) The comparison: 0.69 cannot legitimately be compared with 0.80.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify each aggregate against its parts. Releases: 8 + 6 = 14; 14 + 7 = 21; 21 + 5 = 26 — matches the stated 26, so option A is wrong. Escaped defects: 4 + 3 = 7; 7 + 6 = 13; 13 + 4 = 17 — against a stated total of 18. That is the inconsistency: option B. The rate check: 18 ÷ 26 = 0.692 ≈ 0.69, so the rate is correctly computed *from the stated totals* — option C's claim is false. Option D is a distractor: comparing this quarter's rate with last quarter's is exactly what the figure is for, provided the definitions held steady. The most instructive feature of the item is that the rate is arithmetically correct but built on a wrong input. If the true defect count is 17, the true rate is 17 ÷ 26 = 0.65 — the improvement claim survives, indeed strengthens, but only by luck; had the error run the other way, a "correct" division could have manufactured a false improvement. An arithmetically correct conclusion on unchecked inputs is the most dangerous kind of figure in a board pack, because a spot-check of the headline calculation passes it. A head of test consumes more aggregated data than almost anyone else in the organisation, so this item tests the habit that keeps board reporting trustworthy: check parts against wholes before checking the calculation.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation at head-of-function level**

It is Monday morning. Four items compete for a candidate's attention:

1. The departmental digital board meets Wednesday and has asked the candidate to present the quality position on a flagship programme reporting AMBER; their current draft rests on a dashboard their deputy flagged late Friday as possibly double-counting fixed defects.
2. A test manager has resigned in a programme with no deputy; their director asks how continuity will be maintained.
3. A cross-government QAT community session convened by the candidate runs Thursday; their keynote on AI-assisted testing is half-drafted.
4. Procurement needs the candidate's comments on the testing services framework renewal "within two weeks"; the renewal will bind the function for three years.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4 — verify the board evidence first, stabilise the succession gap, deliver the community commitment, then give the strategic renewal proper time this fortnight
- B) 4, 1, 2, 3 — the three-year commitment is the biggest decision, so it comes first
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4 — external commitments carry reputational weight and Thursday is closer than it looks
- D) 2, 1, 3, 4 — people issues always take precedence over papers

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weigh impact against urgency for each item. Item 1 is urgent (Wednesday) and carries the highest immediate stakes: presenting a board position built on possibly double-counted data risks misinforming a flagship decision and, if the flaw surfaces later, damaging the credibility on which every future quality report trades — verification cannot wait. Item 2 is genuinely important (succession is the head of test's named responsibility) and needs a same-day holding action — an interim arrangement and a conversation with the director — though the full resolution will take longer; it sensibly comes second. Item 3 has a fixed Thursday deadline but a half-drafted keynote is a bounded, recoverable task. Item 4 is the *largest* decision but not this morning's: "within two weeks" gives room to schedule the deep review the three-year commitment deserves — and option A explicitly protects that time rather than treating item 4 as deferred-means-ignored. Option B mistakes magnitude for urgency: spending Monday on the framework while flawed evidence heads for Wednesday's board optimises the long term by damaging the short. Option C elevates reputation over decision integrity. Option D applies a people-first rule mechanically; the resignation needs a same-day signal, not a same-day solution, and the board evidence problem is also a people issue — the deputy flagged it and deserves to see it acted on. Impact-weighted urgency, with holding actions for what cannot be finished today, is the senior pattern this item tests.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across an organisation**

Reviewing annual escaped-defect data across a candidate's organisation's five delivery areas, the candidate notices: areas whose teams run risk-based regression scoping average 2.1 escaped defects per quarter per team; areas without it average 2.3 — a modest difference. But when teams are split by whether they also hold a weekly cross-functional defect triage, the pattern sharpens: teams with *both* practices average 1.2; teams with either one alone average 2.6; teams with neither average 2.4. What is the most defensible reading?

- A) Risk-based scoping is useless — its headline effect is only 0.2.
- B) The practices appear to interact: together they are associated with markedly fewer escapes, while either alone is associated with none of that benefit (and possibly worse than neither); this pattern warrants investigation before any rollout mandate.
- C) Weekly triage alone drives the improvement and should be mandated organisation-wide immediately.
- D) The data is contradictory and should be discarded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The headline comparison (2.1 versus 2.3) masks structure that appears on disaggregation: the combination of practices is associated with roughly half the escape rate of any other configuration, while single practices show no benefit at all. That is an interaction pattern — the practices may be complements, each enabling the other (risk-based scoping decides *where* to look; cross-functional triage turns findings into fixes) — and it is precisely the kind of signal that aggregated dashboards destroy, which is why option A's headline-only reading fails. Option C attributes the benefit to one practice when the data shows singles of either kind performing *worse* than the combination and no better than neither. Option D confuses "surprising" with "contradictory". But note option B's crucial verb: *associated*. This is observational data — teams that adopt both practices may simply be the more mature teams, and maturity, not the practices, may drive the result. So the finding warrants investigation (Which teams? Adopted when? What else do they share?) and perhaps a deliberate pilot, before any mandate. As the owner of continuous improvement who makes strategic decisions on techniques, this two-step — disaggregate to find the real pattern, then withhold causal judgement until the evidence supports it — is the core cognitive move of the job.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction in capability planning**

A candidate is building a capability uplift plan. Constraints: (i) the new performance-engineering training cannot run until the training supplier is on contract; (ii) contracting the supplier takes one full quarter from approval; (iii) approval can only be sought after the workforce plan is signed off; (iv) the workforce plan will be signed off at the end of Q1; (v) the flagship programme needs trained performance engineers in place by the start of Q4, and training a cohort takes one quarter. Can the flagship's need be met, and what is the critical path?

- A) Yes, with a quarter to spare: approval Q2, contract Q2, train Q3.
- B) Yes, exactly on time: approval at the start of Q2, contracting through Q2, training through Q3, engineers in place at the start of Q4 — with zero slack, so any slip breaks the commitment.
- C) No: the earliest engineers can be trained is the end of Q4.
- D) Cannot be determined without knowing the cohort size.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the dependencies forward. Sign-off comes at the end of Q1 (iv), so approval can be sought at the start of Q2 (iii). Contracting consumes one full quarter (ii): the supplier is on contract at the end of Q2. Training can then run through Q3 (i, v's one-quarter duration), completing at the end of Q3 — engineers in place at the start of Q4, exactly when needed. So the need is met with zero slack: every step must start the moment its predecessor finishes, and any slip — a delayed sign-off, a contracting overrun, a cancelled cohort — lands directly on the flagship. Option A invents a spare quarter by overlapping approval and contracting, which constraint (ii) forbids ("one full quarter *from approval*"). Option C misses that the chain does fit. Option D raises a real planning question that the stem's constraints nonetheless do not require — cohort duration is given as one quarter regardless. The head-of-test insight this item tests is what a candidate does with a zero-slack answer: a leader who reports "yes, we can meet it" and one who reports "yes, with zero slack — here are the three risks on the critical path and the mitigation for each" are giving the same arithmetic and profoundly different advice. A strong candidate's answer treats ensuring teams have the right capabilities as mostly the discipline of running this chain backwards from every major commitment early enough to buy slack while slack is still purchasable.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking across two policy documents**

A candidate's testing policy (owned by the candidate) states: "Production-like environments must be used for all performance testing; anonymised production data is required." Their data handling standard (owned by the chief data officer, referenced by the candidate's policy) states: "Anonymised production data may be used in non-production environments only where a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) covering that use is in place. Where no DPIA covers the use, synthetic data must be used." A delivery team asks: "We have no DPIA for the performance environment. Which document do we follow?" What is the correct analysis?

- A) The testing policy wins because performance testing is a testing matter.
- B) The data handling standard wins because data protection is legally weightier, so the team must performance test with synthetic data — and the documents do not actually conflict for this team's situation, but the candidate's policy's unconditional wording invites the confusion and should be amended to reference the DPIA condition.
- C) The documents conflict irreconcilably; the team should pause performance testing until the owners meet.
- D) The team should proceed with anonymised data and complete the DPIA retrospectively.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the two texts as a system. The standard permits anonymised production data only under a DPIA; absent one, synthetic data is mandatory. The testing policy requires anonymised production data unconditionally — but it *references* the data handling standard, and the standard's condition governs the data question. For this team, today, the operative answer is clear: no DPIA, therefore synthetic data (while pursuing a DPIA if production data is genuinely needed for realism). So option C's "irreconcilable conflict" overstates — a coherent reading exists — and pausing performance testing is disproportionate. Option A resolves a legal question by organisational turf, exactly backwards. Option D — act first, assess later — inverts the entire purpose of an impact assessment and would put the organisation in breach. What elevates B above a merely correct reading is its final clause: the diagnosis that the head of test's *own* document is the defective one. Its unconditional "is required" contradicts the conditional regime it references, and every team that reads only that policy will make the team's mistake. Owning policy means owning the ambiguities a policy creates: the senior error-checking skill this item rewards is not just resolving the conflict for one team but tracing it to the document that generated it and fixing it at source — one amendment instead of a hundred clarification emails.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Evaluating a pilot design**

A vendor proposes a three-month pilot of an AI-assisted test generation tool. Their design: the tool is given to a candidate's three strongest automation teams; success is defined as "a 30% increase in test cases generated per sprint compared with those teams' previous quarter". As the owner of researching new techniques, what is the strongest single objection?

- A) Three months is too short for any technology pilot.
- B) The design measures volume of test cases generated, which is not a quality outcome — more generated cases may mean redundant, trivial or unmaintainable coverage — and by piloting on the strongest teams with a before/after comparison, any improvement is confounded with team quality and time effects; success should instead be defined on outcome measures (defect detection, escaped defects, maintenance cost) with a comparison group.
- C) AI-generated tests are inherently unreliable, so the pilot should be declined.
- D) The pilot should include every team, not three, so results generalise.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two independent flaws sit in the vendor's design, and option B names both. First, the success metric: "test cases generated per sprint" measures activity, not value — a generator that emits a thousand shallow assertions triples the count while adding maintenance burden and no detection power. Whatever a pilot measures is what a vendor will optimise, so a volume metric practically commissions the wrong behaviour; success criteria must live at the outcome level — defects found earlier, escapes reduced, engineer time freed — because those are what a strategic decision will actually be about. Second, the comparison structure: strongest teams, before/after. Strong teams improve anyway; quarters differ in workload and release content; and selecting the best teams builds in an expectation of success (and no evidence about the average team, which is where organisational value lives). A concurrent comparison — matched teams with and without the tool — controls what before/after cannot. Option A is a rule of thumb, not an analysis; three months with the right measures beats twelve with the wrong ones. Option C prejudges the substantive question the pilot exists to answer — scepticism is a reason to design the evaluation well, not to refuse evidence. Option D inflates cost and risk before value is shown; generalisation comes from *representative* pilot teams, not universal ones. Making strategic decisions on new techniques is only as good as the evidence designs a head of test insists on — and vendors will bring designs built to pass. Rebuilding them is the job this item tests for.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling of assurance reviews**

A candidate must schedule three assurance reviews — Programme K (K), Programme L (L), and Programme M (M) — across five working days, Monday to Friday, at most one review per day. Constraints: (i) K's review must precede M's, because M consumes K's shared-platform findings; (ii) the independent reviewer engaged for L is available only on Tuesday and Friday; (iii) at least one clear working day is needed between K's review and M's, for the platform team to respond to K's findings; (iv) K's review cannot be on Monday, because K's evidence pack arrives Monday evening; (v) M's review must be held no later than Thursday, because M's gate submission is due Friday morning. On which day must L's review take place?

- A) Tuesday
- B) Wednesday
- C) Friday
- D) Either Tuesday or Friday

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Anchor on the most constrained chain — K and M — and propagate. K cannot be Monday (iv), so K's earliest day is Tuesday. M needs a clear working day after K (iii) and must precede Friday (v): if K is Tuesday, Wednesday is the clear day and M can sit on Thursday — feasible. Test any later K: K on Wednesday pushes M to Friday at the earliest (Thursday clear), breaching (v); K on Thursday or Friday leaves M nowhere at all. So K must be Tuesday and M must be Thursday — the K–M chain is fully forced. Now place L: the reviewer offers only Tuesday and Friday (ii); Tuesday is taken by K, so L must be Friday. Every constraint checks out: K Tuesday, Wednesday clear, M Thursday, L Friday, one review per day. Options A and D fail because Tuesday is necessarily occupied by K in every feasible schedule; option B offers a day L's reviewer never had. The technique is the one that scales to real portfolio scheduling: identify the most constrained elements first (here, the K–M dependency squeezed between a Monday exclusion and a Thursday deadline), let them fix the frame, and slot the flexible elements into what remains. Working the other way — placing the flexible item first — is how real assurance calendars end up demanding days that independent reviewers were never available to give. At this level a head of test rarely builds such schedules personally, but approves them, and the ability to re-derive a schedule's logic in a minute is what lets a strong candidate spot the infeasible ones before they are published.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Portfolio resource allocation under risk**

A candidate's function has 12 unallocated tester-months this quarter. Four bids compete: Programme W (legally mandated go-live, highest-harm risks partially uncovered, bid: 6 tester-months, risk reduction assessed as high); Programme X (popular internal tool, low harm, bid: 4, risk reduction low); Programme Y (citizen-facing service with a rising escaped-defect trend, bid: 5, risk reduction high); Programme Z (early discovery phase, bid: 2, risk reduction modest now but shapes testability for two years). Which allocation best reflects risk-based portfolio leadership?

- A) W: 6, X: 4, Z: 2 — first come, first served among fundable bids.
- B) W: 6, Y: 5 — the two high-risk-reduction bids, with 1 tester-month held back; X and Z decline.
- C) W: 6, Y: 4, Z: 2 — fund the two high-risk-reduction bids (trimming Y by one tester-month in agreement with its test manager), fund the small testability investment in Z, decline X with reasons and a self-service offer.
- D) Fund all four at 75% each so every programme gets something.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rank by risk reduction per tester-month, then handle the remainders deliberately. W is non-discretionary in impact terms: legally mandated, highest-harm, high reduction — fund fully (6; remaining: 6). Y is high reduction against an actively worsening citizen-facing trend — fund at 4 with its scope re-planned in agreement with its test manager (remaining: 2). Z is the strategic sleeper: 2 tester-months in discovery buys testability — contract-level test hooks, environment design, requirements traceability — whose absence would cost far more than 2 tester-months to retrofit; declining Z is how organisations end up with the late-engagement crises every head of test knows. X is low harm and low reduction: decline, but with reasons and an offer of standards, tooling and self-service support — a declined bid handled well builds more trust than a grudging half-funding. Option B leaves a tester-month idle and Z's cheap, compounding investment unmade — an allocation that optimises this quarter's risk sheet while quietly borrowing from the next two years. Option A allocates by queue position, which is administration, not leadership. Option D — peanut-buttering — is the classic portfolio failure: every programme under-resourced, the mandated programme now carrying uncovered legal risk so that a low-harm tool could have three tester-months it cannot convert to value. Guiding teams across an organisation in optimising approaches by using appropriate data means exactly this: rank by marginal risk reduction, protect strategic investments with long payoffs, and decline transparently — never evenly.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Deduction across a portfolio risk register**

A candidate's portfolio risk register lists four organisational quality risks: Q1 (regulatory miscalculation reaching citizens), Q2 (personal data exposure in test environments), Q3 (accessibility regression across shared components), Q4 (release delays from environment contention). Mitigations and their status: the calculation regression standard (mitigates Q1) is adopted by all teams; the test data policy (mitigates Q2) is published but adopted by 3 of 7 teams; the shared component accessibility gate (mitigates Q3) is fully operational; the environment booking system (mitigates Q4) was decommissioned last month pending a replacement that is not yet live; exploratory audits (mitigate Q2 and Q4) ran across all teams last quarter but are unscheduled this quarter. Which risks currently have *no fully operational* mitigation?

- A) Q2 and Q4
- B) Q4 only
- C) Q2, Q3, and Q4
- D) Q1 and Q2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Assess each risk against every mitigation mapped to it, in current status. Q1: the calculation standard is adopted by all teams — fully operational; covered. Q2: the test data policy is published but adopted by only 3 of 7 teams — partial adoption is not full operation (four teams' environments sit outside its protection); the exploratory audits also map to Q2 but are unscheduled this quarter — a lapsed control, not an operational one. Q2 therefore has no fully operational mitigation. Q3: the accessibility gate is fully operational; covered. Q4: the booking system is decommissioned and its replacement is not live — a mitigation that existed last month protects nothing now; the audits mapping to Q4 are likewise unscheduled. Q4 uncovered. Answer: Q2 and Q4. The two traps: counting a *published* policy as an operational control (publication is a milestone; adoption is the mitigation — a distinction that separates paper compliance from real risk reduction), and counting last quarter's audits as if controls persist without being run. This is the deduction that turns a controls list into a risk position: at board level a strong candidate's answer would frame this as "two of our four organisational quality risks are currently unmitigated in practice — one by an adoption gap we can close with escalation, one by a tooling gap with a replacement date we must now treat as a risk deadline." Registers describe intentions; the job is to keep deducing the *current* truth from them.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Measurement logic and Goodhart's law**

Two years ago a candidate set an organisational target: "each team's regression automation coverage must reach 80% of regression cases". All teams now report 80%+ coverage. Yet escaped defects are flat, and audit sampling finds that several teams reached the target by reclassifying hard-to-automate cases out of their regression packs, shrinking the denominator. Which conclusion best captures the systemic lesson?

- A) Teams behaved dishonestly and should be disciplined; the target is sound.
- B) The target worked: 80% coverage was achieved everywhere.
- C) The measure stopped being a good measure once it became a target: coverage-of-pack invited denominator management, so the metric should be redesigned closer to the outcome (for example, coverage of risk-weighted scenarios with pack composition governed, paired with escaped-defect trends) — and the reclassified cases reviewed for restoration.
- D) Automation coverage should no longer be measured, since any metric will be gamed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The pattern is Goodhart's law in the field: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure, because the system optimises the measure rather than the outcome it proxied. The metric's design carried the seed — a ratio target controls only the ratio, and the cheapest path to a ratio is often the denominator; "80% of the regression pack" is achievable by improving automation *or* by shrinking the pack, and only one of those helps citizens. Option A misdiagnoses a systems problem as a character problem: reclassification decisions were probably locally defensible one at a time ("this case is flaky/manual-only/rarely run"), and disciplining teams for optimising the metric a leader set teaches the organisation to hide optimisation, not to stop it. Option B mistakes the map for the territory — the flat escaped-defect line is the territory. Option D overcorrects into measurement nihilism; the lesson is not "never measure" but "design measures that are expensive to game and paired with outcome checks": govern pack composition (the denominator) separately, weight coverage by risk, and always display the proxy beside the outcome it serves. In the framework's terms this is influencing improvements to quality processes informed by trends, applied reflexively to the leader's own instruments — the head of test's distinctive obligation is to test the tests, including the metrics. Expect items at this level to probe whether a candidate reasons about measurement as a system with incentives, not as arithmetic.

### Tips for administering this section

- **Time-box consistently.** Cognitive items reward speed as well as accuracy; give roughly a minute per question and note whether the candidate flags and returns to the multi-constraint items rather than stalling on them — that triage under time pressure is itself part of what the item measures.
- **Watch for second-order review.** The strongest answers treat a document, dashboard or standard as something other people will apply less carefully than the candidate does — note whether a candidate spontaneously asks "who else reads this, and what will they do with it?" when working through a policy or standards item.
- **Listen for the inference chain, not just the answer.** Ask the candidate to talk through their reasoning on the deduction and scheduling items ("sign-off ends Q1, so approval starts Q2..."). A candidate who reaches the right answer by a shortcut that happens to work is a different signal from one who can walk the chain.
- **Note whether a candidate reports flat or graduated confidence.** On the zero-slack scheduling item and others like it, a strong candidate's answer names the risk in a "yes, but" form rather than a flat "yes" — that distinction is itself diagnostic and worth recording in your notes.
- **Score aggregation and mix-effect items carefully.** Several items are built so that a plausible, calculation-correct answer is nonetheless the wrong one; mark against the key rather than against whether the candidate's arithmetic is internally consistent.
- **Keep conditions identical across candidates.** Same time limit, same items, same access to a calculator or paper for the multi-constraint items, for every candidate you assess at this role level — comparability depends on it.

### Common pitfalls in candidate answers

- **Answering from the candidate's own organisation instead of the stem.** A candidate who imports context from their current employer rather than reasoning from the stem's stated rules is showing exactly the failure mode the item is designed to catch — note it, and probe it gently in debrief.
- **Trusting an arithmetically correct conclusion built on unchecked inputs.** Watch for candidates who verify only the final calculation and skip checking the input totals — the most dangerous figure in any paper is a correct calculation on inconsistent data, because it passes a spot-check of the headline number alone.
- **Confusing "published" with "operational" and "adopted" with "effective".** A candidate's answer that treats a status word as a fact rather than interrogating it is a signal worth noting.
- **Mistaking magnitude for urgency.** A candidate who reaches for the biggest decision on the desk rather than the one that must happen this morning is showing a common senior prioritisation error.
- **Forcing a solution onto an over-constrained problem.** The scored behaviour on the scheduling items is detecting infeasibility, not manufacturing an answer that quietly breaks a constraint — a candidate who does the latter under time pressure is worth a follow-up question.
- **Headline-reading aggregated data.** A candidate who accepts a portfolio average without asking to disaggregate it is missing exactly the interaction effects several items are built around.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numerical reasoning assessment evaluates a candidate's ability to analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical data drawn from the real demands of the role. It is emphatically not a mathematics exam: the arithmetic rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, weighted averages, and rates of change. What it tests is whether the candidate can extract the right numbers from realistic leadership material — function budgets, workforce plans, supplier contract schedules, tooling business cases, portfolio dashboards, and capability survey results — and reason with them quickly and correctly.

Typical format: a strictly timed sitting, often 20 to 30 minutes for 15 to 25 questions, with a calculator allowed or provided. The priority is interpretation over mental arithmetic: the hard part is choosing what to calculate, reading scale qualifiers such as "in £ thousands" or "FTE", and avoiding traps like mixing financial-year and calendar-year figures, comparing rates across shifted denominators, or accepting a blended average where a weighted one is required. Question inputs mimic the data formats of the target role — the spreadsheets that underpin a spending review submission, a contract renewal, or a workforce plan.

At head of test level, numerical reasoning is the language in which strategy becomes real. Establishing policy and setting testing strategy sounds qualitative, but every strategic choice a head of test makes is ultimately expressed in numbers someone else will scrutinise: the budget line for the automation platform, the FTE mix between permanent staff and suppliers, the payback period in the business case for a tool, the escaped-defect trend cited as evidence that the strategy is working. A head of test is also the last line of numerical defence for their function: supplier renewal figures, vendor ROI claims, and portfolio dashboards all arrive on the desk having been prepared by people with incentives, and the difference between a head of function who recomputes and one who accepts headline figures is, over a few years, the difference between a well-defended budget and a quietly eroded one. When making the case for investment to directors and finance partners, the numbers will be probed by professionals who probe numbers for a living — and when ensuring teams have the right capabilities, the workforce arithmetic of attrition, recruitment lead times and training throughput is what converts intent into staffed teams.

Employers therefore use numerical reasoning assessments for senior quality leadership roles to check accuracy under time pressure, sound choice of calculation and base, and — most distinctively at this level — judgement about what a number can and cannot support. The items below exercise all three, using the data this role genuinely handles, and give you the worked answer key to score them against.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Budget and investment arithmetic** maps to ownership of **setting the testing strategy** and **owning continuous improvement**: strategies are funded or starved on percentages, payback periods and per-unit costs a head of test must compute and defend line by line.
- **Workforce and capability calculations** map to the duty to **ensure that teams have the right capabilities** and responsibility for the function's talent pipeline: attrition rates, recruitment lead times, training throughput and FTE ratios are the arithmetic of capability.
- **Supplier and contract figures** map to leadership of the function's commercial relationships: contract values, service credits, day-rate blends and renewal comparisons must be verified from raw data, never accepted from a supplier's summary.
- **Portfolio quality metrics** map to the **Managing, reporting and resolving defects** skill at organisational scale: leading others in using defect patterns and trends strategically requires handling rates, mix effects and shifting denominators honestly across many teams at once.
- **Evidence evaluation for new techniques** maps to the duty to **research new technology and new techniques to implement change**: vendor claims, pilot results and survey data all arrive as numbers, and strategic decisions are only as good as the reading of them.
- **Presenting figures to boards** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: choosing the honest, comprehensible number — and saying plainly what it shows and what it cannot — is how numerical rigour becomes organisational trust.

### Assessment items

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of a function budget**

A candidate's QAT function's annual budget is £6.8 million. The board has agreed that 4.5% of it should be ring-fenced for continuous improvement, including research into new tools and techniques. How much is the ring-fenced amount?

- A) £272,000
- B) £306,000
- C) £340,000
- D) £360,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 4.5% of £6,800,000 = 0.045 × 6,800,000 = £306,000. Sanity-check by scaling: 1% of the budget is £68,000, so 4.5% is 4.5 × £68,000 = £306,000 — the same answer by a second route. Option A is 4% and option C is 5%: both catch candidates who round the percentage before multiplying rather than after. The habit of computing 1% first and scaling is worth making automatic: it is fast, it is resistant to decimal-place slips, and it gives a leader a feel for the money ("each 1% of the budget is £68k") that serves them in live negotiations — when a finance partner proposes trimming "half a per cent", a candidate with this habit knows immediately that the conversation is about £34,000 of improvement work, and can say so.

**Question 2 (easy) — Ratio across an organisation**

A candidate's organisation has 540 software engineers and 90 test specialists across all delivery areas. What is the ratio of engineers to test specialists in simplest form, and — if the engineering workforce grows to 600 while the target ratio is held at that same level — how many test specialists would be required?

- A) 5:1; 120 specialists
- B) 6:1; 100 specialists
- C) 6:1; 110 specialists
- D) 9:1; 67 specialists

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Simplify the ratio: 540 ÷ 90 = 6, so the ratio is 6:1. Apply it to the grown workforce: 600 ÷ 6 = 100 specialists, an increase of 10 over today's 90. Option A missimplifies the ratio (540:90 is not 5:1 — that would require 450 engineers); option D divides the wrong way at the second step. Ratio maintenance is bread-and-butter workforce planning arithmetic for a head of function: engineering growth is usually announced without its testing consequence attached, and the leader who can say in the meeting "a 60-engineer uplift implies 10 more test specialists at our current ratio — funded or not?" converts a staffing surprise into a planning conversation. Note also what the ratio does *not* settle: whether 6:1 is the *right* ratio, which depends on automation maturity, risk profile and delivery model — a strategic question the arithmetic informs but cannot answer.

**Question 3 (easy) — Percentage change in a portfolio metric**

Escaped defects across a candidate's whole portfolio numbered 96 last financial year and 72 this financial year. What is the percentage change?

- A) A fall of 24%
- B) A fall of 25%
- C) A fall of 33%
- D) A fall of 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100 = ((96 − 72) ÷ 96) × 100 = (24 ÷ 96) × 100 = 25%. The original (earlier) year is always the base. Option A reports the absolute fall (24 defects) as a percentage — a category error. Option C divides the change by the *new* figure (24 ÷ 72 = 33%), inflating the improvement — the flattering error a candidate should recognise in drafts prepared by people keen to show progress. Option D confuses "fell to 75%" with "fell by 75%". When this figure heads for an annual quality statement, precision matters twice over: once for accuracy, and once because a director who catches a change-base error in a flagship number will discount the rest of the pack. Stating the base in the sentence — "down 25% on last year's 96" — is what keeps the ambiguity off the page.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Weighted average day rate**

A candidate's function's delivery capacity comprises 30 permanent test specialists at an effective cost of £310 per day, 12 contractors at £500 per day, and 8 supplier-provided engineers at £625 per day. What is the weighted average cost per person-day across the function?

- A) £406
- B) £445
- C) £478
- D) £512

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weight each rate by headcount. Total daily cost = (30 × £310) + (12 × £500) + (8 × £625) = £9,300 + £6,000 + £5,000 = £20,300. Total headcount = 30 + 12 + 8 = 50. Weighted average = £20,300 ÷ 50 = £406. Option C is the classic trap: the unweighted average of the three rates, (310 + 500 + 625) ÷ 3 = £478.33 ≈ £478 — a figure £72 a day adrift because it ignores that permanent staff are three-fifths of the workforce and the expensive supplier engineers are less than a sixth of it. Option B splits the difference and corresponds to no valid calculation, which is itself a lesson: distractors are often engineered to sit plausibly between the right answer and the classic error. Scale the £72-a-day gap to see why this matters strategically: across 50 people and roughly 220 working days a year, using the unweighted rate would misstate the function's annual cost base by about £790,000 — comfortably the size of error that discredits a spending review submission or distorts a make-versus-buy comparison. When a finance partner asks for "the blended day rate", the correct response computes it as total cost over total heads, states the mix it is built on, and recomputes it whenever the mix shifts — a blended rate quietly inherited from last year's workforce shape is wrong the day the workforce changes.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Licence model break-even**

A test management platform offers two pricing models: per-seat at £280 per user per year, or an enterprise licence at £42,000 per year for unlimited users. A candidate's function currently has 130 prospective users and expects growth. At what user count does the enterprise licence become cheaper, and what should be paid this year on the cheaper model for 130 users?

- A) Break-even at 150 users; pay £36,400 per-seat this year
- B) Break-even at 150 users; pay £42,000 enterprise this year
- C) Break-even at 140 users; pay £39,200 per-seat this year
- D) Break-even at 160 users; pay £36,400 per-seat this year

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Break-even is where per-seat cost equals the enterprise price: £42,000 ÷ £280 = 150 users. Below 150 users, per-seat is cheaper; above it, enterprise wins. At 130 users, per-seat costs 130 × £280 = £36,400 — £5,600 less than the enterprise licence — so this year the per-seat model is the economical choice, giving option A. Option B pays £5,600 for growth that has not happened yet; options C and D miscompute the break-even (£42,000 ÷ £280 is exactly 150). What the break-even figure hands a head of test beyond this year's decision is a trigger point for review. The strategic move is to buy per-seat now and diarise a re-evaluation when the user count approaches 150 — or to use the arithmetic in negotiation ("we are 20 users short of your enterprise case; price it at £36,000 and we will commit today"). Vendors present enterprise licences as simplification; the tooling strategy owner's job is to know exactly where simplification starts costing money, and that is always a division away.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Attrition and the recruitment pipeline**

A candidate's function has 60 test specialists. Annual attrition runs at 15%. Recruitment takes 4 months from advert to start date, and historically one in three shortlisted campaigns' offers convert to a start. To hold headcount flat over the year, how many leavers must be replaced, and — if each campaign yields on average 2 accepted offers that convert to starts — how many campaigns must be run?

- A) 9 leavers; 5 campaigns
- B) 9 leavers; 3 campaigns
- C) 6 leavers; 3 campaigns
- D) 12 leavers; 6 campaigns

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Leavers = 15% of 60 = 0.15 × 60 = 9 per year. Campaigns: each campaign converts on average 2 starts, so 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5 campaigns — which must round *up* to 5, because a half campaign cannot be run and four campaigns yield only 8 starts against 9 leavers. Option B rounds down and quietly plans to shrink; option C misreads the attrition rate; option D doubles the leaver count. Two leadership readings follow from the numbers. First, the 4-month lead time means campaigns must be launched *before* resignations land — at 9 leavers spread across a year, roughly one campaign every ten weeks, run continuously rather than reactively, or the function operates permanently 3–4 heads light. Second, the arithmetic prices retention: if a retention initiative cut attrition from 15% to 10%, that is 3 fewer replacements — at a typical £8,000–£15,000 fully-loaded cost per campaign-and-onboarding, the initiative's budget case writes itself. Ensuring teams have the right capabilities is, in large part, running this arithmetic before the gaps appear rather than after.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Supplier service credit verification**

A candidate's testing services contract has an annual value of £1.2 million, invoiced monthly in equal amounts. The contract provides that if the supplier's monthly delivery falls below 90% of committed tester-days, a service credit of 5% of that month's invoice applies; below 80%, the credit rises to 12%. In March the supplier committed 250 tester-days and delivered 195. What credit is due?

- A) £5,000
- B) £12,000
- C) £8,400
- D) No credit — delivery was above 80%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work the steps in order. Monthly invoice = £1,200,000 ÷ 12 = £100,000. Delivery percentage = 195 ÷ 250 = 0.78 = 78% — below the 80% threshold, so the 12% tier applies, not the 5% tier. Credit = 12% of £100,000 = £12,000. Option A applies the wrong tier (78% is below both thresholds, and the lower band's higher credit governs); option D misreads 78% as above 80% — check: 80% of 250 is 200 committed days, and 195 delivered is 5 days short of even that line. Option C (8.4%-ish of the invoice) has no basis in the schedule — beware options that average two tiers, because tiered contracts never average. The verification habit matters because in real life this calculation arrives done by the supplier, not by the head of test, and the two most common "errors" in supplier-prepared credit calculations are tier misclassification at the boundary and a conveniently recomputed committed-day baseline. As head of function, the leader owns the commercial relationship: recomputing the percentage from the committed figure *in the contract*, not the one in the report, is what keeps the £7,000 difference between tiers in the public purse.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Training budget coverage**

A candidate has £54,000 of training budget. A certification course costs £1,350 per person; an advanced automation bootcamp costs £2,700 per person. The candidate wants to put 24 people through certification and spend the remainder on bootcamp places. How many bootcamp places can be funded, and what percentage of the 60-person function receives *some* funded training this year (assuming no person does both)?

- A) 8 places; 53%
- B) 8 places; 40%
- C) 10 places; 57%
- D) 12 places; 60%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Certification spend = 24 × £1,350 = £32,400. Remainder = £54,000 − £32,400 = £21,600. Bootcamp places = £21,600 ÷ £2,700 = 8. Trained people = 24 + 8 = 32. Percentage of function = 32 ÷ 60 = 0.533… = 53% (to the nearest whole per cent). Option B computes the percentage on the bootcamp cohort's arithmetic wrongly (24 ÷ 60 = 40% counts only certification); option C stretches the remainder to 10 places, which would need £27,000. The two-step discipline — compute the committed spend, then divide the true remainder — prevents the commonest budget-planning slip, which is dividing the whole budget by the blended cost. And the 53% figure is the strategically loaded one: it shows that on this allocation, nearly half the function receives no funded development this year — the number a workforce survey will surface later as a grievance and a retention risk. Heads of function who compute the coverage percentage at planning time, not at survey time, get to choose the trade-off deliberately: fewer bootcamp places, cheaper certifications, or a bigger budget bid with this exact arithmetic as its evidence.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Automation investment with a ramp**

A proposed organisation-wide automation platform costs £180,000 to implement in year 1. Savings ramp up as teams onboard: £60,000 in year 1, £120,000 in year 2, and £150,000 in year 3 and each year after. Maintenance costs £30,000 per year from year 1 onwards. What is the cumulative *net* position at the end of each of the first three years, and in which year does the investment break even?

- A) Year 1: −£150,000; year 2: −£60,000; year 3: +£60,000; breaks even during year 3
- B) Year 1: −£120,000; year 2: −£30,000; year 3: +£90,000; breaks even during year 3
- C) Year 1: −£150,000; year 2: −£30,000; year 3: +£90,000; breaks even during year 2
- D) Year 1: −£120,000; year 2: +£30,000; year 3: +£150,000; breaks even during year 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Build the net flow year by year, remembering maintenance runs every year. Year 1: savings £60,000 − maintenance £30,000 − implementation £180,000 = net −£150,000. Year 2: savings £120,000 − maintenance £30,000 = +£90,000 for the year; cumulative −£150,000 + £90,000 = −£60,000. Year 3: savings £150,000 − maintenance £30,000 = +£120,000 for the year; cumulative −£60,000 + £120,000 = +£60,000. The cumulative position crosses zero during year 3 (it needs £60,000 of year 3's £120,000 net inflow — halfway through the year on an even-flow assumption). Option B forgets year 1 maintenance; option D nets maintenance against implementation only once and never again; option C computes the years correctly but misreads its own cumulative column — a reminder that the break-even claim must be checked against the running total, not the annual flow. The ramp is the substantive modelling point: flat-savings business cases (3 × £150,000 against £180,000) look dramatically better than ramped reality, and vendors know it. When this case goes to a board, the ramped cumulative line — negative for two years — is the honest picture, and a candidate who presents it unprompted, before a finance partner reconstructs it for them, is doing the work that makes the next business case easier to land.

**Question 10 (challenging) — A mix effect across the portfolio**

A candidate's annual report compares defect resolution performance across two years. In year 1, the portfolio resolved 200 simple defects averaging 3 days each and 100 complex defects averaging 12 days each. In year 2, it resolved 100 simple defects averaging 2.5 days and 200 complex defects averaging 11 days. A draft slide reads: "Average resolution time worsened from 6.0 to 8.2 days — resolution performance has deteriorated." What is the accurate assessment?

- A) The slide is right: 8.2 is worse than 6.0, so performance deteriorated.
- B) Both defect types were resolved *faster* in year 2 (simple: 3 → 2.5 days; complex: 12 → 11 days); the overall average rose only because the mix shifted toward complex defects, so the slide's conclusion is wrong and the report should show per-type trends alongside the mix change.
- C) The year 2 average is miscalculated and performance actually improved to 5.5 days overall.
- D) The figures cannot be compared because the volumes differ.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify the averages first. Year 1: (200 × 3 + 100 × 12) ÷ 300 = (600 + 1,200) ÷ 300 = 1,800 ÷ 300 = 6.0 days. Year 2: (100 × 2.5 + 200 × 11) ÷ 300 = (250 + 2,200) ÷ 300 = 2,450 ÷ 300 = 8.17 ≈ 8.2 days. The slide's arithmetic is correct — and its conclusion is still wrong. Within each type, year 2 is faster: simple defects improved from 3 to 2.5 days, complex from 12 to 11. The overall average rose solely because the workload mix inverted, from one-third complex to two-thirds complex — and complex defects take longer even when handled better. This is Simpson's paradox in operational clothing: every component improved while the aggregate deteriorated. Option A trusts the aggregate; option D gives up on comparison when disaggregation resolves it cleanly. The reporting standard a head of function should set follows directly: whenever workload mix can shift, aggregate averages must be accompanied by per-segment trends and a mix statement, or the organisation will punish teams for improvements and reward deteriorations, depending on which way the mix happened to move. Leading others in using defect trends to make strategic recommendations means, above all, refusing to let an aggregate speak unexamined for its parts.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Capability survey with shifting bases**

A candidate's annual capability self-assessment asks teams to rate their test analysis maturity as "developing", "practising" or "leading". Results: year 1 — 40 teams responded: 20% leading, 50% practising, 30% developing. Year 2 — 25 teams responded: 32% leading, 48% practising, 20% developing. A draft board paper states: "Leading-maturity teams rose from 20% to 32% — a 60% improvement in top-level capability." Which critique is most important?

- A) The percentage-point rise is 12, not 60, so the paper understates the improvement.
- B) In absolute terms, leading teams went from 8 (20% of 40) to 8 (32% of 25) — no change at all — and 15 teams stopped responding; until it is known which teams dropped out, the claimed improvement is unsafe, and the "60% improvement" framing (a relative change of percentages) compounds the overstatement.
- C) Self-assessment data is always invalid and should never reach a board.
- D) The paper is sound: 32% is genuinely 60% higher than 20%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to counts before believing any survey trend. Year 1 leading teams: 20% × 40 = 8. Year 2: 32% × 25 = 8. The absolute number of leading teams is identical; the percentage rose only because the response base collapsed from 40 to 25. And the missing 15 responses are unlikely to be random — teams that skip a capability survey are plausibly the less engaged, lower-maturity ones, which would inflate year 2's percentages precisely through non-response bias. The "60% improvement" is a relative change of percentages (12 points on a base of 20), rhetorically dramatic and doubly detached from reality. Option D endorses the arithmetic while ignoring the base; option A quibbles the framing but misses the deeper flaw; option C throws away a usable instrument — self-assessment data is weak evidence, not non-evidence, and it improves sharply when response rates are managed and bases are reported. The standard to set: survey results reach a board only with response counts alongside percentages, year-on-year base comparison, and a stated view on non-response. As the leader who sets standards and principles for test analysis across the organisation, the same evidential discipline demanded of defect data should apply to capability data — especially when it flatters the leader presenting it.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Real-terms budget arithmetic**

A candidate's function's budget was £6.0 million this year. The spending settlement offers a flat cash budget of £6.0 million for each of the next two years. Cost inflation for the function (pay awards, contractor rates, licence uplifts) is forecast at 4% per year. What is the real purchasing power of the year-2 settlement in today's terms, and what percentage cut in real activity does the two-year settlement imply by year 2?

- A) £5.76 million; a 4% real cut
- B) £5.55 million; a 7.5% real cut
- C) £5.54 million; an 8% real cut
- D) £6.00 million; no cut — the budget is protected

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Deflate the flat cash by compound inflation. After one year of 4% inflation, £6.0 million buys what £6.0m ÷ 1.04 = £5.769 million buys today. After two years: £6.0m ÷ (1.04²) = £6.0m ÷ 1.0816 = £5.547 million ≈ £5.55 million. The real cut by year 2 = (6.0 − 5.547) ÷ 6.0 = 0.453 ÷ 6.0 = 7.55% ≈ 7.5%. Option A applies one year of inflation to a two-year question; option C uses simple (added) rather than compound inflation — 8% versus the correct 7.55%, close enough to look right and wrong enough to matter at this scale (£27,000); option D mistakes flat cash for protection, which is precisely the framing a settlement letter will use. The leadership consequence: a "flat" settlement is a rolling real reduction of roughly £450,000 by year 2 — about 7 to 8 tester-year equivalents at the function's blended rates — and the time to say so is at settlement, not at the point teams discover they cannot recruit. Heads of function who can walk a finance director through compound deflation in three lines negotiate from evidence; those who cannot, absorb the cut and answer for its consequences. This is the arithmetic behind advocating for test capabilities where it matters most: in the room where budgets are set.

### Tips for administering this section

- **Cover the senior six.** Percentage of an amount, percentage change on the correct base, weighted averages, break-even division, compound growth and deflation, and tiered threshold calculations cover nearly every item in this section — a candidate who is reflexive on these frees their timed attention for interpretation, where the marks and the traps both live.
- **Allow a calculator, but score interpretation.** The arithmetic is deliberately simple; the discriminating skill is choosing what to calculate and from what base. Note in your scoring whether a wrong answer came from a calculation slip or from choosing the wrong base or the wrong figures — they are different signals about the candidate.
- **Watch for percentage-to-count conversion.** Survey results, maturity distributions and adoption rates are where senior-level traps hide — collapsing bases, mix shifts, non-response bias are invisible in percentages and obvious in counts. A candidate who converts spontaneously is showing strength worth noting.
- **Listen for the candidate naming the base.** A strong answer states the denominator out loud — which year, which invoice, which population — before computing. In this section, as in real board packs, wrong bases outnumber wrong arithmetic, so this is a useful thing to prompt for in debrief if a candidate does not do it unprompted.
- **Note whether a candidate estimates before computing.** "Flat cash against 4% for two years — call it 8% off six million, roughly £480k" is a five-second sanity check that catches keying errors; candidates who do this before reaching for the calculator are demonstrating the estimation skill they will need live in meetings with no calculator and no pause button.
- **Keep timing consistent, then debrief untimed.** Run the timed sitting strictly, then walk through every explanation slowly and without a clock in the debrief. Speed and depth are different things to assess, and conflating them under one time pressure loses information.

### Common pitfalls in candidate answers

- **Averaging averages.** A candidate who takes the unweighted mean of day rates, resolution times or team scores — right only when group sizes are equal, which they never are in workforce and portfolio data — is showing a pattern worth flagging: weighting by headcount, volume, or spend is the expected move.
- **Trusting an aggregate across a shifting mix.** Watch for a candidate who accepts an overall average without asking whether the underlying mix moved. An overall average can worsen while every component improves, and vice versa; a strong candidate disaggregates before concluding.
- **Wrong-base percentage changes.** Change ÷ original, not change ÷ new — and watch for a candidate who presents a relative change of percentages ("a 60% improvement" from 20% to 32%) as if it were a change in reality.
- **Simple-interest thinking in a compound world.** Inflation, growth, and attrition compound. A candidate who adds percentages across years is understating every multi-year effect, always in the direction that flatters a flat settlement.
- **Tier and threshold misreads.** Service credits, pricing bands and target gates turn on which side of a line a number falls. A strong candidate computes the boundary value explicitly (80% of 250 is 200) rather than judging proximity by eye.
- **Forgetting recurring costs and ramps.** Business-case traps at this level rarely sit in the multiplication — they sit in the omitted maintenance line, the assumed instant adoption, and a savings figure that starts at full run-rate in month one. A strong candidate models the ramp and presents the negative years unprompted.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate understands, evaluates, and draws conclusions from written material of the kind the role requires them to process every day. Typical items present a short passage — a standard, a policy, a contract clause, board minutes, a vendor claim — followed by statements the candidate must classify as **true** (the passage guarantees it), **false** (the passage contradicts it), or **cannot say** (the passage neither guarantees nor contradicts it), together with inference questions and judgements about clear professional writing.

Typical format: a timed sitting of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 items. The governing discipline is strict: the candidate must answer from the passage alone, not from professional knowledge. Make this instruction explicit when you administer the section — the constraint can feel artificial to a senior candidate who knows how these documents usually read, but it mirrors a real necessity: policies, contracts and standards bind by what they say, not by what their drafters probably intended, and the gap between the two is where compliance failures, commercial disputes and governance surprises are born.

At head of test level, words are the role's primary instrument of action. A head of test establishes policy — a document. They set testing strategy — a document. They set standards for test types, techniques, design and execution, and principles for test analysis across the organisation — documents, every one, that hundreds of people will read without the author in the room to explain what they meant. Upward and outward, the texture is the same: digesting functional standards and central guidance to keep policy aligned with government expectations; reading supplier frameworks whose clauses determine what the commercial levers actually are; consuming board papers and assurance reports whose careful hedges ("substantially mitigated", "expected to complete") carry more meaning than their headlines; and producing submissions where a single overclaimed sentence can commit the function to an assurance position it cannot honour. A head of test also speaks for the QAT community to large audiences inside and outside government — and representing others raises the standard again, because their words become the community's position.

Employers use verbal reasoning assessments for senior quality leadership because imprecise reading at this level propagates furthest: a misread standard becomes a non-compliant policy; an ambiguous principle becomes a hundred divergent local practices. The passages below are drawn from the genres a head of test genuinely handles, and each is followed by the classification key for you to score against.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to ownership of **establishing policy**: policy questions arrive as "does the standard require us to…?", and the professional answer distinguishes what a text mandates, what it permits, and what it is silent about — the exact three-way classification the assessment scores.
- **Comprehension of standards and central guidance** maps to **Designing and executing tests** and **Test analysis** at this level: setting organisational standards that must nest inside government-wide frameworks requires parsing conditional, qualified, exception-laden prose without adding or subtracting obligations.
- **Contract and framework language** maps to leadership of the function's **supplier relationships**: options, obligations, notice periods and remedies turn on modal verbs and defined terms, and the head of function is the last reader before signature.
- **Inference control** maps to **Managing, reporting and resolving defects** strategically: turning assurance reports and defect trends into recommendations for boards requires knowing precisely when the evidence supports a conclusion and when it is merely consistent with it.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: moderating difficult, high-risk discussions and representing the community to large audiences depends on choosing formulations that carry technical truth across the boundary without distortion — and on recognising, in others' drafts, the sentence that will mislead a board.

### Assessment items

**Passage 1 (for Questions 1–3).** Extract from a cross-government quality standard:

"Each organisation must appoint a named individual accountable for test policy. Organisations must ensure that testing of citizen-facing services is planned from the discovery phase onwards. Where an organisation adopts automated testing at scale, it should publish internal guidance on the maintenance of automated test assets. Departures from this standard are permitted only where approved by the organisation's digital board and recorded in a register available to internal audit."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "An organisation that has not published internal guidance on maintaining automated test assets is necessarily in breach of the standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, if the organisation tests citizen-facing services

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two features of the text defeat the statement, and either alone would suffice. First, the guidance provision applies only "where an organisation adopts automated testing at scale" — an organisation below that threshold has no such obligation, so non-publication cannot be *necessarily* a breach. Second, and more precisely, the provision says "should", not "must": in the drafting conventions of standards, "should" marks a recommendation, not a mandate, so even an at-scale organisation without the guidance falls short of good practice rather than into breach. The statement's "necessarily" makes it false. This must/should distinction is one a policy owner lives by: when teams ask "do we have to?", the answer depends on which modal verb the standard chose, and heads of function who conflate them either over-burden their organisation with phantom obligations or under-comply with real ones. A strong candidate reads standards the way auditors will: obligation by obligation, verb by verb.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "A departure from the standard that has been approved by the organisation's digital board, but not recorded in the register, is not a permitted departure."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because board approval is the substantive requirement

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The final sentence sets two conjoined conditions: departures are permitted "only where approved by the organisation's digital board *and* recorded in a register available to internal audit". "Only where" makes the pair of conditions jointly necessary — approval without recording satisfies one condition but not both, so the departure is not permitted. The statement is true. Option D voices the intuition the question is built to test: that approval is the "real" requirement and recording is mere administration. The text draws no such hierarchy, and neither will an auditor — registers exist precisely so that internal audit can verify governance happened, and an unrecorded approval is, for assurance purposes, indistinguishable from no approval. As the person who owns their organisation's equivalent register, a head of test needs the lesson in both directions: hold departures to both conditions, and when designing exception routes into policy, decide deliberately which conditions are conjoined — because "and" and "or" in an exceptions clause will be enforced exactly as written.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "The standard requires testing of internal-facing services to be planned from the discovery phase onwards."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, by reasonable extension of the citizen-facing rule

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The planning obligation is scoped: "testing of *citizen-facing* services" must be planned from discovery. About internal-facing services the passage says nothing — it neither extends the obligation to them nor exempts them. A requirement cannot be inferred from silence, so the statement is not true; but nor does the passage contradict it — a fuller version of the standard might well cover internal services elsewhere. The correct classification is cannot say. Option D names the exact temptation: "reasonable extension" is how experienced professionals import their own good practice into a text — and while planning internal services' testing early is excellent practice, the *standard as quoted* does not require it, and the difference matters when writing the compliance statement a digital board will sign. The three-way discipline earns its keep precisely here: "the standard requires it", "the standard forbids it", and "the standard is silent, and our policy chooses to…" are three different sentences, and senior credibility lies in never letting the third masquerade as the first.

**Passage 2 (for Questions 4–6).** Clause from a testing services framework agreement:

"The Authority may, on giving thirty days' written notice, require the Supplier to replace any key personnel whose performance is materially unsatisfactory, provided the Authority has first given the Supplier written notice of the performance concerns and a period of no fewer than twenty working days to remedy them. The Supplier shall bear the costs of any replacement under this clause. Nothing in this clause limits the Authority's right to terminate for material breach under clause 14."

**Question 4 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "The Authority can require immediate replacement of a key person the moment their performance becomes materially unsatisfactory."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, provided the Supplier bears the costs

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The clause builds a mandatory sequence before any replacement requirement: first, written notice of the performance concerns; then a remedy period of at least twenty working days; and then the replacement itself requires thirty days' written notice. "Immediate replacement the moment performance becomes unsatisfactory" contradicts both the remedy-period precondition ("provided the Authority has first given…") and the notice requirement, so the statement is false. Option D is a distractor wired to the cost sentence, which allocates expense but creates no shortcut through the procedure. The practical reading habit for a head of function: contract rights are usually *processes*, not switches — the right actually held is "notice, then twenty working days, then thirty days' notice", a minimum of roughly ten weeks from first concern to enforced replacement. Knowing that timeline in advance changes management behaviour: a head of test documents performance concerns in writing early — starting the contractual clock — rather than discovering, mid-crisis, that the "right to replace" is a quarter of a year long. The final sentence matters too: the replacement route sits alongside, not instead of, termination for material breach — parallel remedies a head of test should never let a supplier present as alternatives.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "If the Supplier remedies the performance concerns within the remedy period, the Authority cannot require replacement under this clause."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because the thirty-day notice right stands independently

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the conditional structure precisely. The clause permits replacement of personnel "whose performance is materially unsatisfactory", provided notice and a remedy period were given. If the concerns are remedied — performance is no longer materially unsatisfactory — the natural reading is that the ground for replacement falls away. But does the text *guarantee* that? Strictly, the clause does not state what happens after a successful remedy: it does not say "and the concerns remain unremedied at the end of the period" as an express condition of proceeding. On the natural reading the answer would be true; on a hostile literal reading, arguably the qualifying condition ("is materially unsatisfactory") must still hold at the time of the requirement, which supports true as well — yet the clause never addresses remediation outcomes explicitly, so the strictly supportable classification is cannot say. This is a deliberately hard item: the lesson is not that natural readings are wrong, but that a clause which forces a reader to choose between a natural and a literal reading is a *defectively drafted clause* — and noticing that is the head-of-function skill. In an assessment, classify by what the text guarantees (cannot say); at the negotiating table, fix the drafting: "where the concerns are not remedied within the period, the Authority may then require replacement" costs eleven words and removes the ambiguity that would otherwise be argued about, expensively, at the worst possible moment.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Inference from silence**

Statement: "If key personnel are replaced under this clause, the Supplier must provide a replacement with equivalent skills and experience."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because that is standard practice in government frameworks

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The clause obliges the Supplier to bear the *costs* of replacement, and says nothing about the qualities of the replacement — no equivalence requirement, no approval right over the incoming person, no transition obligations. Equivalence provisions are common in well-drafted frameworks, which is exactly what makes option D seductive: a senior reader's professional experience says such a term is "usually there". But the passage is the universe, and it is silent — cannot say. The finding worth taking from the exercise is the gap itself: a replacement clause without an equivalence or approval provision lets a supplier swap a strong engineer for a weak one, at their cost but at the organisation's risk, and the time to notice that is before signature. This is why heads of function read framework schedules personally rather than delegating them entirely to commercial colleagues: the commercial team checks that the clause works legally; only the head of test can check that it works *operationally*, because only they know that the thing being protected is the capability of a test team, not just the cost of a substitution. Silence in contracts is never neutral — it allocates risk to whoever failed to notice it.

**Passage 3 (for Questions 7–9).** Extract from a programme assurance report to a candidate's organisation's digital board:

"The programme's quality position has improved since the last review. The regression pack for the core journey is now fully automated, and nightly runs have been stable for six weeks. Performance testing against the agreed load model is complete, with all targets met. However, the load model was agreed in January, before the policy change that is expected to widen eligibility. The test data strategy remains dependent on a data-sharing agreement that is still in legal review. On current evidence the panel assesses residual quality risk as moderate, trending favourably, and expects the programme to be releasable in Q3 subject to resolution of the data-sharing agreement."

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "Performance testing has demonstrated that the service will meet its targets under the demand expected after the eligibility change."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because all targets were met

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states that performance testing is complete "against the agreed load model" with all targets met — and then immediately undermines the currency of that model: it "was agreed in January, before the policy change that is expected to widen eligibility". Wider eligibility plausibly means higher demand than the January model assumed — but the passage never quantifies the change, never states the model is invalid, and never reports testing against post-change demand. So the statement is neither guaranteed (the testing demonstrably did not target post-change demand — but whether the service *will* meet targets under it is simply unaddressed) nor contradicted: cannot say. Option D repeats the report's most quotable phrase while detaching it from its qualifying context, which is exactly how assurance headlines get misused in board rooms. The professional reflex this trains: when a report says "complete against the agreed X", interrogate the vintage of X. Panels choose words like "agreed load model" carefully — completeness against a stale baseline is a true statement concealing an open risk, and the head of test who catches the January date is the one who asks the board's most valuable question: "complete against *which* demand?"

**Question 8 (moderate) — Fact versus judgement**

Which element of the report is a professional judgement rather than a reported fact?

- A) Nightly automated runs have been stable for six weeks.
- B) The data-sharing agreement is still in legal review.
- C) Residual quality risk is moderate and trending favourably.
- D) The load model was agreed in January.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A, B and D are verifiable states of the world: run logs, the legal team's tracker, and the January agreement record could each confirm or refute them. "Residual quality risk is moderate, trending favourably" is an assessment — a conclusion the panel formed by weighing those facts through unstated criteria: how much the eligibility change undermines the load model, how likely legal review is to conclude in time, what "moderate" even means on the panel's scale. Distinguishing the two is the core skill of *consuming* assurance as a board-level reader: a reader can accept every fact in this report and still legitimately reach a different risk judgement, because the judgement is exactly the part where expertise, appetite and framing enter. It is equally the core skill of *producing* assurance: reports commissioned by a head of test should label assessments as assessments, state the criteria behind rating words, and never let "moderate" do the work of an argument. Leading and coaching others in reporting — a named element of the defect management skill — depends on teaching this fact/judgement line first, because reports that blur it transfer risk decisions from accountable boards to anonymous adjectives.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Evaluating the argument**

The panel's conclusion — "releasable in Q3 subject to resolution of the data-sharing agreement" — rests on the evidence in the passage. Which is the strongest critique of that conclusion *as stated*?

- A) The conclusion is sound: automation is stable, performance targets are met, and the only open item is named.
- B) The conclusion's single named condition (the data-sharing agreement) is not the only open risk in the panel's own evidence: the January load model predates the eligibility change, so the performance result may not cover Q3's actual demand — yet the releasability statement does not condition on revalidating it.
- C) The conclusion fails because six weeks of stability is too short a period to prove automation reliability.
- D) The conclusion fails because "moderate" risk should always preclude a releasability statement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test the conclusion against the report's own contents. The panel itself surfaced two open issues: the stale load model and the unresolved data-sharing agreement. Its releasability statement conditions on only one of them. That asymmetry is the weakness: a reader acting on the headline will treat legal resolution as the sole gate, while the panel's own evidence says the performance result may not describe the demand the service will actually meet — the eligibility change could invalidate the one test area currently marked green. Option B names this internal gap precisely, which is what "critique as stated" demands. Option A accepts the headline and inherits the gap. Options C and D substitute the reader's own thresholds (how much stability is enough, what "moderate" should preclude) for analysis of the argument's structure — imported standards, not critique, and both are contestable as rules. The pattern worth taking into practice: the most dangerous sentence in any assurance report is the conditional conclusion whose conditions are *fewer than its own findings* — and the fastest quality check a head of test can run on a report, or teach a panel they commission, is to count the "however" clauses and confirm each one either appears in the conditions or is explicitly retired in the text.

**Passage 4 (for Questions 10–12).** Extract from a vendor's whitepaper, sent to a candidate by their chief digital officer with the note "worth a look?":

"Organisations adopting our AI-driven test generation platform report up to 70% reductions in test design effort. In a recent study of twelve enterprise customers, teams using the platform released 40% more frequently than industry benchmarks. The platform eliminates the test maintenance burden through self-healing scripts, and requires no specialist skills, making dedicated test engineers optional for most delivery teams. Leading government organisations are already adopting the platform."

**Question 10 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "According to the passage, every organisation adopting the platform reduced test design effort by 70%."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, within the twelve-customer study

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage claims "up to 70% reductions" reported by (unspecified) adopting organisations. "Up to" states a ceiling, not a norm: it is consistent with one organisation achieving 70% and every other achieving 5%, and it directly contradicts "every organisation… by 70%". The statement misattributes a maximum to the whole population, so it is false — the passage itself, read carefully, refutes it. Option D compounds the error by attaching the 70% figure to the twelve-customer study, which the passage links only to release frequency, not design effort — a detail-splicing trap that mirrors how marketing claims blur together on second retelling. Reading vendor material with this precision is not pedantry; it is the first filter of the duty to research new technology. "Up to X%" is the single most reliable marker of selected-best-case evidence, and the immediate professional question it should trigger is: what was the *median* reduction, over what population, measured how? A vendor who can answer has evidence; a vendor who re-quotes the ceiling has a brochure.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Evaluating evidence quality**

A candidate's chief digital officer asks: "The 40% more frequent releases figure — how solid is that?" Based only on the passage, which reply is most accurate?

- A) "Solid: twelve enterprise customers is a reasonable sample, and 40% is a large effect."
- B) "Weak as presented: the comparison is against 'industry benchmarks', not against the same teams before adoption or matched non-adopting teams — so the customers may simply be organisations that release frequently anyway; twelve self-selected customers of the vendor is a sample built for survivorship; and release frequency is not a quality outcome, so even a real effect would not tell us testing improved."
- C) "Meaningless: no vendor study can ever carry evidential weight."
- D) "Solid enough to proceed: government organisations are already adopting it, which corroborates the claim."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Take the claim apart as evidence. The comparator is "industry benchmarks" — a population average — while the sample is twelve of the vendor's own enterprise customers: organisations that chose, funded and persisted with the platform, which selects for delivery maturity independent of any tool effect. Nothing in the design isolates the platform's contribution: no before/after on the same teams, no matched comparison, no statement of how the twelve were chosen from the customer base (the study may omit the customers who churned — survivorship in its purest form). And the endpoint is release *frequency* — a tempo metric that can rise for many reasons and says nothing direct about defect detection or escaped defects, the outcomes a decision would actually turn on. Option B assembles all three problems and stays honest about what follows: weak *as presented*, which invites better evidence rather than closing the door. Option C overshoots into nihilism — vendor studies can be probative when designs are sound and data is shared. Option D mistakes social proof for evidence; "leading organisations are adopting" is the oldest pressure-frame in procurement. This is the researching-new-techniques duty in verbal form: the head of test's value in the tooling market is not enthusiasm or scepticism but the ability to say precisely *why* a claim is weak and precisely *what* evidence would repair it.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Writing the upward summary**

A candidate must reply to their chief digital officer in three or four sentences. Which reply best serves a senior, non-specialist reader?

- A) "The whitepaper's claims rest on 'up to' figures, benchmark comparisons, and self-selected samples; the self-healing and no-specialists claims are unevidenced and the latter contradicts our capability strategy. The underlying technology is worth evaluating properly: I propose a structured pilot on two representative teams with outcome measures we define — defect detection, escaped defects, maintenance effort — and a decision point in six months. I will send a one-page evaluation design this week. No commitment should be made ahead of that evidence."
- B) "Marketing fluff — 'up to 70%', benchmark games, survivorship bias, and the 'no specialist skills' line is aimed at cutting test engineers. Recommend we ignore."
- C) "Very promising — significant reported gains in design effort and release frequency, and government adoption is under way. Recommend we open commercial discussions."
- D) "The claims raise interesting methodological questions regarding comparator validity, sample selection and endpoint relevance which merit further epistemological consideration before any procurement pathway is initiated."

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The CDO's implicit questions are: is this real, what should we do, and who is doing it. Option A answers all three in senior register: it dispatches the evidence problems in one compressed sentence (trusting the reader with conclusions, not a tutorial), flags the strategic conflict with the function's capability plans, converts scepticism into a *constructive next step* — a pilot on the candidate's own terms with outcome measures they define — and closes with a clear commitment and a guard-rail against premature procurement. That conversion is the crucial move: at head-of-function level, "no" to weak evidence must arrive fused with "here is how we would generate strong evidence", or the function becomes one that blocks rather than one that knows. Option B reaches broadly sound conclusions in a register that spends the candidate's credibility and gives the CDO nothing actionable — and dismissiveness reads as threat-response, inviting the suspicion that testing is defending its headcount rather than the organisation's interests. Option C forwards the brochure's own frame upward, unexamined. Option D hides the analysis inside abstraction — words like "epistemological" in an executive reply are how experts talk to themselves in public. Speaking on behalf of the community to senior audiences means exactly this discipline: compress the technical case, surface the strategic stake, and always hand the decision-maker a well-shaped next step.

### Tips for administering this section

- **Insist on strict passage-only reading.** The commonest senior-candidate error is "true by reasonable extension" — a confident import of their own professional experience into a passage that does not say it. Make the rule explicit before the sitting: classify from the text alone.
- **Watch for the modal verbs.** Must, shall, should, may, "only where", "provided that" — the standards and contract items turn on exactly these words. A candidate's answer that flattens "should" into "must", or treats a compound condition as satisfied by half of it, is showing a specific and worth-noting error.
- **Note whether a candidate reads conditionals both ways.** A strong candidate checks a rule's contrapositive and identifies whether its conditions are conjoined ("and") or alternative ("or") before answering. Misreading the conjunction is the classic senior misstep this section is built to surface.
- **Score the fact/judgement item on the distinction, not the surface answer.** Item 8 is diagnostic of whether a candidate can tell a verifiable state of the world from an assessment dressed as one — this distinction matters more than any single correct classification elsewhere in the passage.
- **Ask candidates to name the baseline.** "Complete against the agreed model", "compared with industry benchmarks", "since the last review" — a strong candidate's answer interrogates the vintage of the comparator rather than accepting the headline.
- **Use the upward-summary item to assess register, not just content.** The strongest reply compresses the technical case, states the stake, and hands over a next step, in language a non-specialist senior reader could act on — score register as well as substance.

### Common pitfalls in candidate answers

- **Importing professional knowledge into the passage.** An experienced candidate knows what frameworks usually contain and what standards usually mean — and the passage may deliberately differ from that. A candidate who answers from what they know rather than what the text says is showing the commonest senior error in this section.
- **Quantifier and attribution blindness.** Watch for a candidate who treats "up to", "as many as" or "reported" as if they meant "every" or "confirmed". A ceiling is not a norm; a report of a claim is not the claim's truth; twelve customers are not the market.
- **Conflating "should" with "must" and "approval" with "approval plus recording".** Obligations have exact shapes. A candidate who flattens a recommendation into a mandate, or a compound condition into its most salient half, is producing confident wrong answers in the test and would produce non-compliant policies in the job.
- **Letting headlines detach from qualifiers.** "All targets met" and "releasable in Q3" both carry load-bearing conditions in their own passages. A strong candidate re-reads the sentence before and after any quotable phrase before answering.
- **Critiquing the argument the candidate would have made, not the one given.** The strongest critique evaluates a conclusion against the passage's own evidence and conditions — internal consistency — not against the candidate's private thresholds for stability windows or risk appetite.
- **Register errors in the rewrite item.** The best upward summary is neither dismissive, credulous, nor abstract: it compresses the analysis, states the stake, and offers a shaped next step. An answer that scores points, forwards hype, or hides in vocabulary is signalling itself as weak.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks the candidate to evaluate possible responses — typically identifying the most effective and least effective actions, ranking the options, or rating each option's effectiveness. Unlike the other three assessments, an SJT has no arithmetic and no passage to parse: it measures professional judgement — the ability to weigh competing pressures and choose actions that serve users, staff, the organisation and the public interest at once.

Typical format: 12 to 25 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed. Score against the key below, agreed by subject-matter experts and calibrated to the target level — which changes the assessment's character fundamentally at this level. At head of function level, the scored behaviours are organisational leadership behaviours: setting and defending policy, exercising judgement where governance is ambiguous, building capability and community, holding evidential integrity against senior pressure, managing propriety in commercial relationships, and developing the leaders below rather than substituting for them. Options that would score well two levels down — escalate, seek direction, defer to seniority — often score poorly here, because in most rooms a head of test occupies, they are the escalation route, and the assessment wants to see them carry that weight.

For a head of test, the SJT is the closest of the four assessments to the actual texture of the job. The framework describes a role that establishes policy and sets testing strategy, owns continuous improvement, makes important decisions, builds the QAT community and provides organisational leadership, ensures teams have the right capabilities, and may advocate for test capabilities inside and outside government. Almost nothing in that list is a technique; nearly all of it is judgement exercised on people, institutions and evidence under pressure. The scenarios below are drawn from the recurring dilemmas of the level: assurance positions colliding with political deadlines, policy exceptions demanded by the powerful, capability strategies threatened by vendor narratives, propriety at the commercial boundary, succession choices, and the slow, deliberate work of building a community across organisational lines.

Two patterns will help you score consistently. First, strong senior answers tend to hold two things simultaneously that weaker answers trade off: evidence held steady and decision-makers genuinely helped; authority exercised and people developed; risk named honestly and a workable path offered. Second, weak answers at this level have characteristic shapes: capitulation dressed as pragmatism, confrontation dressed as integrity, delegation dressed as empowerment when it is actually abdication, and process dressed as action. Learning to recognise the costume will help you score borderline answers consistently across candidates.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Assurance-under-pressure scenarios** map to the duty to **make important decisions** and to **manage stakeholder expectations and moderate difficult discussions about high-risk topics within constrained timescales**: the item probes whether the candidate can hold the quality evidence steady while helping accountable owners find governable paths through political deadlines.
- **Policy and exception scenarios** map to ownership of **establishing policy for the test function**: policies are tested not when they are written but when someone powerful wants an exception, and the scored behaviour is principled flexibility — exception routes used properly, never quietly.
- **Capability and workforce scenarios** map to **ensuring that teams have the right capabilities** and responsibility for the function's talent: succession, development, and the defence of professional skills against cost narratives.
- **Community and advocacy scenarios** map to **building the QAT community and providing organisational leadership**, and to **speaking on behalf of, and representing the community to, large audiences inside and outside the organisation** — including the judgement calls about openness, credit and candour that representation entails.
- **Coaching scenarios** map to the leadership form of the role's named skills — **leading and coaching others** in defect management, test analysis and test design: the item watches whether a candidate grows leaders or quietly replaces their judgement with their own.
- **Commercial propriety scenarios** map to leadership of supplier relationships and research into new technology: procurement fairness, hospitality boundaries, and evidence-led evaluation are where a head of function's integrity is most visible and most consequential.

### Assessment items

**Question 1 (moderate) — The ministerial deadline and the assurance position**

A ministerial commitment fixes a service's public launch for the 15th. A candidate's assurance review, completed today (the 1st), concludes that the highest-harm risks are well covered but that testing of the eligibility calculation against complex household circumstances — the service's most sensitive outcome — cannot complete before the 22nd. The senior responsible owner (SRO) says: "The date is immovable. I need your assurance statement to support launch."

- Option A: Provide the statement — the SRO owns the risk, and the highest-harm risks are covered.
- Option B: State that assurance cannot be provided before the 22nd and that launching on the 15th would be against the candidate's advice, in writing, and leave the decision with the SRO.
- Option C: Give the SRO an honest, decision-ready position: what is assured by the 15th, what is not, and the options that could make the 15th governable — a phased launch excluding complex household cases until the 22nd, enhanced post-launch monitoring with a rapid-correction route, or launch with the residual risk explicitly accepted and recorded at SRO level — with the candidate's recommendation among them.
- Option D: Escalate past the SRO to the digital board, asking it to move the date.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** The head-of-test move is to refuse the binary the SRO's request implies. Option C holds the evidential line — nothing is assured that is not assured — while doing the senior work of converting an assurance gap into decision architecture: scope-shaped options (the phased launch protects exactly the users whose cases are untested), honestly priced residual risk, and accountability recorded at the level that owns it. That is "moderating difficult discussions about high risk topics within constrained timescales" as the framework means it. Option B is honest but incomplete at this level: a written "not before the 22nd" protects the candidate while leaving the SRO alone with a political commitment and no path — integrity as self-defence rather than as service, and the surest way to teach SROs to stop inviting testing in early. Option D spends escalation before partnership has been attempted, and asks a board to move what may genuinely be immovable rather than helping shape what is movable: scope. Option A is the least effective: an assurance statement that asserts what the evidence does not support is the one asset the function can never recover — its statements' meaning. Signed once under pressure, every future statement becomes negotiable, and the eligibility failures that surface among complex households will surface alongside the candidate's signature. Never lend the evidence; always lend the help.

**Question 2 (moderate) — The powerful director's policy exception**

A candidate's testing policy requires independent assurance review before any citizen-facing launch. The director of a flagship programme — politically favoured, chronically late — asks the candidate to waive the review for their launch: "Your policy's exception route exists for exactly this. We're weeks behind, the delivery team is strong, and the chief executive mentioned this programme in her all-staff. I need the waiver today."

- Option A: Grant the waiver — the exception route exists, the team is strong, and the political context is real.
- Option B: Refuse outright: policy is policy, and flagship programmes especially must model compliance.
- Option C: Apply the exception route as designed rather than as demanded: require the programme's risk case in writing today, assess it on its merits against the criteria the route specifies, offer a proportionate alternative if the full review genuinely cannot fit (for example, a focused two-day review of the highest-harm journeys), and record whatever is decided in the exceptions register — making clear that political salience is not among the route's criteria.
- Option D: Tell the director the waiver will be granted if the chief executive personally requests it.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Exception routes are where policy either proves itself or dissolves. Option C treats the route as the piece of governance it is: evidence in writing, criteria applied, proportionate alternatives explored, outcome recorded where audit can see it. The focused-review offer matters — principled flexibility means the policy's *purpose* (independent eyes on citizen-facing risk) is preserved even when its full form cannot be, which is how policies survive contact with real delivery. The closing point in C is the quietly crucial one: naming that political salience is not a criterion protects not just this decision but the route itself, because every waiver granted on salience re-prices all future waivers. Option B mistakes rigidity for integrity — a policy whose exception route is never genuinely available is a policy teams will learn to route around rather than through, and "policy is policy" refuses the assessment the route itself promises. Option A grants on the stated grounds — lateness, team confidence, executive attention — none of which is a risk argument. Option D is the least effective: it outsources the candidate's own accountability upward while converting a governance decision into a political favour — if the chief executive asks, the waiver becomes unrefusable regardless of risk, designing the mechanism of the policy's own collapse and teaching every director the cheat code. Owning policy means the criteria are the head of test's to apply, especially when it would be more comfortable if they were someone else's.

**Question 3 (moderate) — The test manager's public misjudgement**

In a programme board attended by a candidate, one of their test managers presents a release recommendation the candidate can see is built on a flawed reading of the defect trend — the data is accurate, but they have read a fall in *found* defects as an improvement in quality, when execution volume halved in the same period. The board is minutes from endorsing the recommendation. The test manager reports to the candidate and is, in general, one of their strongest people.

- Option A: Say nothing in the meeting; correct the analysis with the test manager afterwards and, if needed, have the recommendation revisited by email.
- Option B: Intervene now, but as a question that hands the insight to them: "Before we conclude — how did execution volume move over the same period? Worth checking the find *rate* rather than the count before we treat the fall as improvement." Then support whatever correction they make, and debrief privately afterwards.
- Option C: Intervene now with the correction stated plainly as the candidate's own analysis, since the board must not decide on a flawed basis.
- Option D: Let the recommendation stand — undermining the candidate's test manager in front of a board does more damage than one imperfect release decision.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Two duties collide: the board must not decide on a false basis (a duty that cannot wait for the corridor), and the test manager's authority is an asset the head of test is responsible for growing, not spending. Option B honours both: the question format puts the missing variable into the room — discharging the duty to the decision — while leaving the correction itself to the test manager, who gets to demonstrate rather than lose competence; the private debrief then converts the moment into coaching on the denominator discipline. This is what "leading and coaching others in using defect patterns and trends" looks like when the stakes are live. Option C discharges the decision-duty but takes the analysis away from the strongest person in the room, in public — survivable once, corrosive as a pattern: boards learn to wait for the head of test's view, and test managers learn to bring their analysis to the head of test before daring to hold one themselves. Option A protects the person by deceiving the board — every minute the flawed recommendation stands endorsed is organisational risk, and unpicking an endorsed decision by email costs more credibility than a well-crafted question would have. Option D is the least effective: it prices a citizen-affecting release decision below a moment of managed discomfort, and it misunderstands what undermines people — a respectful question in the room does not; discovering later that their head of profession watched them mislead a board and said nothing, does.

**Question 4 (challenging) — The budget cut and the capability strategy**

A 15% cut to a candidate's function's budget is mandated. Their finance partner's draft simply removes the two cheapest lines: the entire training and professional development budget, and the community-of-practice programme (events, cross-department secondments, the annual conference). Delivery capacity is untouched. The candidate must respond this week.

- Option A: Accept the draft — it protects delivery, which is what the organisation notices, and development can pause for a year.
- Option B: Counter-propose a portfolio answer: model two or three cut shapes with their consequences over a three-year horizon (for example, a supplier-capacity reduction absorbed through risk-based scope prioritisation, a slower automation build-out, a trimmed but intact development and community budget), show that erasing capability investment converts a one-year saving into attrition, skill decay and a weaker pipeline, and bring the options to the decision with the candidate's recommendation.
- Option C: Refuse the cut and escalate: quality investment should be protected, and the function cannot absorb 15%.
- Option D: Accept the 15% but take it evenly — every budget line reduced by 15%, so no activity is singled out.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** The head-of-function failure here would be to let the *shape* of the cut be decided by what is administratively easiest to delete. Option B does the leadership work: it accepts the mandate (refusing a mandated cut, as option C does, converts a resource negotiation into an insubordination story and forfeits the seat at the table where the shape is decided), but contests the draft with modelled alternatives and multi-year consequences — the only argument finance partners can actually use. Its substance is the framework in action: ensuring teams have the right capabilities is a forward obligation, and the training and community lines are the *capability pipeline* — cheap on a spreadsheet precisely because their value lands in later years, which is why they are always the first lines a one-year lens deletes. Option D's even shave feels equitable and is actually the abdication of portfolio judgement — it thins every activity below effectiveness thresholds rather than deciding what the function will stop doing, and "we cut everything a bit" is the answer of a leader who declined to lead. Option A is the least effective: it trades the function's future for a quiet year — twelve months later the organisation has a delivery-shaped function with rising attrition, no succession depth, a stalled community, and a workforce that heard clearly what the organisation invests in when money tightens. Cuts are strategy under compulsion; the scored behaviour is shaping them, not suffering them.

**Question 5 (challenging) — The vendor at the boundary**

A candidate is midway through a formal evaluation of test automation platforms; three vendors are in contention. The account director of one contender — whose product the candidate's teams mildly prefer so far — invites the candidate to speak at their flagship user conference (travel and accommodation covered), and mentions they could "arrange early access to the new AI features for your teams, outside the evaluation, no strings".

- Option A: Accept both — speaking builds the candidate's community profile, early access is free evaluation data, and their judgement is not for sale.
- Option B: Decline both while the procurement is live, briefly and without drama; report the approach to the candidate's commercial team as procurement rules require; ensure any early-access opportunity is offered to all three vendors through the evaluation framework or not at all; and revisit the speaking invitation, on the candidate's own expenses policy, once the procurement has concluded.
- Option C: Decline the early access but accept the conference slot — speaking is community advocacy, which the role explicitly encourages.
- Option D: Accept early access for the candidate's teams but decline the conference — the access benefits the evaluation while the conference only benefits the candidate.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** During a live procurement, the question is never only "is my judgement influenced?" but "would a losing bidder, a National Audit Office reviewer, or a journalist reasonably perceive advantage?" — because procurement fairness is a perception standard enforced in hindsight. Option B gets every element right: decline cleanly, *report* (the approach itself — paired hospitality and selective benefit, offered mid-evaluation to the decision owner — is information the commercial team needs, and may itself say something about the vendor), neutralise the genuine evaluative value of early access by routing it equally through the framework, and preserve the legitimate advocacy opportunity by time-shifting it past the conflict rather than abandoning it. Option C treats the conference as separable because it maps to the community duty — but a covered-costs keynote at one bidder's flagship event, mid-evaluation, is a public signal of alignment whatever the candidate's intentions, and the advocacy role is precisely why it signals so loudly. Option D launders the benefit through "it helps the teams": selective access from one bidder contaminates the evaluation's comparability — it is the *most* procurement-damaging half of the offer, not the least. Option A is the least effective: it accepts both benefits on the strength of self-assessed incorruptibility, which is the one defence that has never survived an audit. At this level, propriety is not a constraint on leadership; it is the reason a candidate's recommendation will be believed when it matters.

**Question 6 (challenging) — The vendor narrative and the workforce**

A candidate's chief digital officer, under efficiency pressure, has read the whitepaper claiming AI test generation makes "dedicated test engineers optional" and asks the candidate to model a 30% reduction in test specialists over two years, adding: "I'm not asking whether — I'm asking how."

- Option A: Refuse to model it: the premise is vendor marketing, and modelling a bad idea legitimises it.
- Option B: Model exactly what was asked and submit it — the CDO owns workforce strategy and was explicit about the terms.
- Option C: Do the work, honestly and on the candidate's own professional terms: model the 30% scenario *with its consequences priced* — risk exposure by portfolio area, escaped-defect sensitivity, the evidence base (and its gaps) for the AI-productivity assumption — alongside an alternative scenario where AI adoption re-shapes roles toward test engineering and analysis with a smaller, phased headcount effect tied to *demonstrated* productivity gains from a pilot; present both with a recommendation, and have the workforce conversation with the CDO on that evidence.
- Option D: Model the reduction as asked, but quietly brief the candidate's test managers to begin documenting why it will fail, so the record is ready when it does.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** "I'm not asking whether — I'm asking how" is a frame, and the senior skill is to serve the requester without accepting the frame's hidden premise (that the vendor's productivity claim is established fact). Option C does the commissioned work — refusing outright, as option A does, converts a winnable evidence conversation into a compliance dispute lost by memo — but does it as a professional rather than a clerk: the 30% scenario arrives priced in risk and stated assumptions, next to a genuine alternative that takes AI seriously (this matters: an alternative that is merely "change nothing" confirms the suspicion that testing resists efficiency) while tying headcount effects to demonstrated rather than advertised gains. That is ensuring teams have the right capabilities exercised at the moment it is hardest: not defending headcount, but insisting that workforce decisions consume evidence at the same standard a head of test would demand of a release decision. Option B executes the frame unexamined — a head of profession who models a 30% cut without pricing its risk has silently endorsed the premise, and the model will travel with their name on it. Option D is the least effective: outward compliance plus a curated failure dossier is organisational bad faith — it spends the leader's integrity, conscripts test managers into it, and positions the function to profit from a failure it declined to prevent. The whole option set turns on one distinction: influence the decision openly with evidence, or manage it covertly with positioning. At this level, the first is the job; the second, its betrayal.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The concealed test results**

A test analyst three levels below a candidate asks to speak privately. They show the candidate evidence that their programme's test lead — outside the candidate's line, reporting into the programme — has been excluding failed accessibility test results from the reports that reach the programme board, "to keep the RAG green until the fixes land". The analyst is frightened of being identified: the test lead controls their next assignment.

- Option A: Thank them, protect their identity, verify the evidence independently and quickly (the reports and raw results will speak for themselves), then act on what verification shows: correct the reporting to the programme board through its SRO, address the test lead's conduct through the proper route, and follow through personally on safeguarding the analyst from retaliation — while treating the deeper signal (a reporting culture where concealment felt survivable) as a systemic issue for the candidate's function's standards.
- Option B: Tell the analyst that as the issue sits outside the candidate's line, they should raise it through the programme's own escalation route, which is the proper channel.
- Option C: Confront the test lead directly today with the analyst's evidence and demand an explanation before deciding anything.
- Option D: Quietly arrange for the accessibility results to be re-run and included in future reports, without addressing the past concealment or the test lead's conduct — the forward-looking fix avoids a damaging dispute.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Falsified quality reporting is the one offence a head of test can never triage away: the function's entire authority rests on reports meaning what they say, and accessibility results concern users who are least able to absorb the consequences of concealment. Option A sequences the duties correctly: verify first (protecting both the accused and the accuser from an error), correct the *decision-making record* through the SRO — the board has been governing on false information and must know — pursue conduct through proper process rather than personal confrontation, and treat retaliation-proofing the analyst as personal follow-through, not a hope. Its last clause is what makes it head-of-function judgement: one concealment that felt survivable is evidence about culture, and culture belongs to the head of test. Option C acts on unverified evidence, identifies the analyst almost arithmetically (who else saw the raw results?), and converts a governance failure into a personal duel. Option D fixes the future while ratifying the past — the board's prior decisions stand on concealed data, the conduct is condoned by silence, and the analyst learns that courage changes nothing. Option B is the least effective, and its politeness is what makes it lethal: "outside my line, use the proper channel" is jurisdictional correctness deployed against a frightened person who has just taken the largest professional risk of their career to bring truth to the head of their profession. They came to the head of test *because* the channel runs through the person they fear. Organisational leadership of the QAT community means every tester in the building is inside the head of test's duty of care, whatever the reporting lines say — the moment that stops being true, no one brings the next concealment forward.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Representing the community: the failure story**

A candidate is invited to give a keynote at a cross-government conference on quality engineering. The most valuable talk the candidate could give — by far the most requested topic in the community — is an honest account of their organisation's automation programme, including the eighteen months it lost to a tooling decision the candidate personally got wrong. Their communications team prefers "a positive capability story"; a director worries that "airing failures invites press interest"; the candidate's own test managers are quietly watching what they choose.

- Option A: Give the sanitised capability talk — the community benefit of one honest talk does not justify organisational risk.
- Option B: Decline the keynote to avoid the tension altogether.
- Option C: Give the honest talk without engaging communications — professional communities deserve candour, and asking permission invites refusal.
- Option D: Work the tension rather than picking a side: engage the communications team early with a draft, frame the story as "what we learned and what we would do differently" with the lessons made reusable for other departments, address the press-risk question concretely (what is genuinely sensitive versus merely uncomfortable), and give the candid talk within those agreed bounds — crediting the teams who did the recovering.

**Most effective: D. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests whether a candidate understands what representing a community to large audiences actually involves: not just speaking, but carrying an organisation's trust while doing it. Option D does the mature version: candour *with* governance rather than candour *versus* governance. Engaging communications early converts a veto-holder into a collaborator; distinguishing the sensitive from the merely uncomfortable is usually where the entire disagreement dissolves (directors rarely object to lessons — they object to surprises); and crediting the recovery teams turns a personal confession into an organisational achievement. The talk that results is nearly as candid as option C's and infinitely more durable — because option C, the least effective, buys one honest keynote at the price of every future one: bypassing the organisation's own processes to discuss its failures publicly is a trust breach that will be discovered (keynotes are recorded), and afterwards no sensitive story from that leader will be cleared again, which is a poor trade for a community they intend to serve for years. It also models, for the test managers watching, that candour requires going around the organisation — the opposite of the culture the leader is meant to be building. Option A wastes the community's most requested learning and teaches the leader's own people that failure stories are unspeakable — a direct hit to the incident-review culture quality depends on. Option B avoids the tension by abandoning both the community and the influence the role exists to exercise. Leaders who can tell their failure stories *well* — honestly and institutionally — give their whole profession permission to learn in public.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The succession decision**

The deputy head of test role is vacant for a candidate holding this post — the natural successor pipeline for their own role. Two internal candidates stand out. Candidate One is the head of test's longest-serving test manager: loyal, steeped in the head of test's methods, strong delivery record, similar background and style; interviewing them will be comfortable and their appointment popular with the old guard. Candidate Two came from a service management background three years ago: a sharper strategic thinker on the evidence of their portfolio work, stronger with data and with senior stakeholders, but less embedded in the community — and their appointment would surprise people. The head of test holds decisive influence over the panel's recommendation.

- Option A: Back Candidate One — continuity, loyalty and community acceptance matter for a deputy, and their delivery record has earned it.
- Option B: Back Candidate Two — the sharper strategic capability is what the function's future needs.
- Option C: Before backing anyone, notice the shape of the head of test's own pull toward Candidate One — similarity, comfort, loyalty — and deliberately re-anchor the decision in the role's forward requirements: define what the function needs its next leader to do in three years' time, ensure the process tests both candidates against *that* (not against resemblance to the head of test), take the panel's assessment seriously as a check on their own bias, and invest properly in the development of whichever candidate is not appointed.
- Option D: Split the difference: appoint Candidate One as deputy, and create a new "head of strategy" role for Candidate Two so nobody is disappointed.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The scenario's real subject is not which candidate — the stem deliberately leaves that answerable either way — but whether the candidate recognises succession as the decision where a leader's biases compound longest. Option C is keyed highest because it treats the candidate's own pull toward the similar candidate as data about *themselves*: affinity bias ("mini-me" succession) is the best-documented failure mode in leadership pipelines, and it operates precisely through the feelings option A lists as virtues — loyalty, comfort, continuity, popularity. The counter-measures in C are the real ones: specify the future role before comparing people to it, let the process genuinely test rather than ratify, use the panel as an external check on the decision-maker's own judgement, and treat the unsuccessful candidate's development as part of the decision's cost. Note that C does not pre-decide against Candidate One — a re-anchored process might still choose them, legitimately; what it forecloses is choosing them *because they are familiar*. Option B at least reasons from capability, but it commits the mirror error: deciding before the process, then using the process as theatre. Option D is the least effective despite its emollient surface: it invents an unfunded role to avoid a decision, fragments the function's leadership to spare the decision-maker's own discomfort, tells Candidate Two they were the sharper strategist *and* not chosen, and — worst — leaves the succession question unanswered while appearing to answer it. Taking responsibility for talent and succession means exactly the discomfort option D is engineered to avoid: choosing, on the function's future needs, with the leader's own reflection consciously in view.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The community that outgrew its founder**

Three years ago a candidate founded the cross-department QAT community of practice; it now has 400 members. Lately its energy has shifted: a group of senior test engineers is driving the agenda toward deep automation engineering topics the candidate no longer practises hands-on, sessions they do not attend are the best-attended, and this month the organising group proposed — politely — that the community's chair should rotate among practitioners rather than remain with them. The candidate still funds it, sponsors it at board level, and represents it externally.

- Option A: Retain the chair: the community needs senior air cover, and the candidate's sponsorship is why it exists — rotation can wait until it is more mature.
- Option B: Support the rotation gladly and restructure the candidate's own role around what the community actually needs from them now — sponsorship, funding protection, board-level advocacy and external representation — while letting practitioners own the technical agenda; treat the proposal as evidence the community has succeeded, and say so publicly.
- Option C: Support rotation of the chair but retain approval rights over the agenda and speakers, so quality and reputational risk stay managed.
- Option D: Step back entirely — hand over the chair, the funding case, and the external representation, since the community has clearly outgrown the candidate.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Building the QAT community and providing organisational leadership — the framework's phrase — has a maturity curve, and this scenario tests whether a candidate knows which end of it they are on. A community whose members drive their own agenda, fill sessions without the founder, and ask to govern themselves is not a problem: it is the *success condition*, and option B is the only option that reads it that way. Its restructuring is precise rather than sentimental — the community still genuinely needs what only the founder can give (budget protection, board advocacy, external voice), and it no longer needs what practitioners now do better (technical agenda, chairing). Saying so publicly converts a potentially awkward transition into a story about what success looks like, which itself builds the community. Option A holds the chair on air-cover grounds the stem has already answered — sponsorship and chairing are separable, and "rotation when mature" from a founder is how communities learn their governance is decorative. Option D over-rotates into abandonment: the funding case and external representation are head-of-profession functions, and dropping them does not empower the community — it orphans it at board level, where practitioners have no standing. Option C is the least effective, and the subtlest failure: rotation-with-approval-rights grants the title of self-governance while retaining its substance — practitioners will read the approval gate accurately within weeks, the best contributors (the senior engineers driving the energy) will disengage first, and the credibility of "letting go" will have been spent without its benefits. Founders' final act of leadership is building things that no longer need them — and recognising the moment, gracefully, when that has happened.

### Tips for administering this section

- **Score as the office, not the individual.** The key is written to the standards of the role — establish policy, set strategy, build community, ensure capabilities, make important decisions — not to any one person's habits. When a candidate's answer differs from the key, check it against the office's standards before deciding it is wrong.
- **Look for both goods held together.** At head-of-function level, the strongest answers almost always refuse a trade the weaker ones accept: evidence and helpfulness, authority and development, candour and governance, compliance and challenge. An answer that preserves both sides of a tension is usually the stronger one.
- **Learn to spot the costume.** Capitulation dresses as pragmatism, confrontation as integrity, abdication as empowerment, avoidance as diplomacy, and control as quality assurance. Naming the costume on a candidate's chosen option helps you score it consistently and explain the scoring in debrief.
- **Weigh second-order effects in scoring.** Senior actions are precedents: ask of a candidate's answer, "what would this teach every director, supplier, test manager and frightened analyst who heard about it?" An answer that solves the immediate scenario while corrupting the incentive landscape should score lower than the key's rationale suggests at first glance.
- **Calibrate with a second assessor where you can.** Rank a sample of candidate answers independently, then compare with a colleague, ideally another senior test or quality leader. Expert calibration on situational judgement is trained by comparison, not by working alone.
- **Keep the citizen in the frame when discussing borderline answers.** In every scenario touching releases, reporting or resources, the strongest answers keep the citizen in view: untested eligibility cases, concealed accessibility failures, services launched on lent assurance. An answer that loses sight of that is usually the weaker one, whatever else it gets right.

### Common pitfalls in candidate answers

- **Lending the evidence.** A candidate's answer in which assurance statements, severity ratings, RAG statuses or survey figures flex to ease a stakeholder's problem is showing an integrity trade — and at head-of-function level the trade is permanent, because a leader's statements' meaning, once spent, does not return.
- **Integrity without help.** The mirror error: an answer that protects the candidate's own position in writing while leaving the accountable owner alone with an ungovernable problem. Senior integrity is measured by the quality of the paths a leader builds, not only the lines they hold.
- **Escalating what is theirs to decide.** The head of the function is being assessed: an answer that passes policy exceptions, evidential disputes or community conflicts upward — or sideways to "proper channels" — before exercising their own office usually marks the abdication pattern the key penalises.
- **Spending people to win rooms.** Public corrections of a candidate's own managers, analysts exposed to retaliation, communities governed by approval rights: any answer that converts a person's trust into a tactical asset fails the leadership test even when it wins the moment.
- **Executing a frame rather than examining it.** "The date is immovable", "I'm asking how, not whether", "the exception route exists for exactly this" — powerful requests arrive pre-framed. The scored behaviour is serving the requester while pricing the frame's hidden premise, openly.
- **Founder's grip.** In community, succession and delegation scenarios, watch for a candidate who retains control because the thing they built still feels like theirs. The role's verbs — build, ensure, advocate, coach — all point at other people's capability, and the key rewards answers where other people end up larger.

## Conclusion

You now have a complete set of materials for administering a head of test assessment: the cognitive items that probe second-order review — checking the systems that check, from policy consistency and portfolio aggregation to pilot designs and the metrics that go wrong when they become targets; the numerical items that test whether a candidate keeps budgets, workforce plans, supplier credits, business cases, and survey claims honest under scrutiny; the verbal items that test whether a candidate reads standards, contracts and assurance reports for exactly what they guarantee, and can write an upward summary that carries technical truth across the boundary intact; and the situational judgement scenarios that test whether a candidate holds assurance steady against ministerial deadlines, applies policy exception routes as governance rather than favours, defends capability against cost narratives with evidence rather than defensiveness, and knows when a community is ready to govern itself.

**Scoring.** Mark each item against the correct answer or key given in its explanation, and use the explanation to understand why that answer is the strongest one — this matters most on the situational judgement scenarios, where more than one option can look reasonable at a glance and the explanation sets out what separates the scored answer from the near misses. Where a candidate's reasoning differs from the explanation but reaches a defensible answer for defensible reasons, note that rather than marking it simply wrong; the explanations are a guide for judgement, not a checkbox key, and this material has not been piloted or normed, so treat close calls with proportionate caution. Keep a record of scores and your notes on reasoning for each candidate — both for your own consistency across a hiring or promotion round and in case the process is later reviewed.

**Debrief.** After scoring, go through the assessment with the candidate: what each section was testing, where their answers matched the key and where they diverged, and why the key answer is considered strongest. This is worth doing generously — the explanations in this guide are written to teach as well as to score, and a candidate who understands why an answer was weak learns something useful whether or not they are appointed. Where you cannot appoint or promote, a thoughtful debrief is often the most valuable part of the process for the candidate.

**Administer consistently.** Use the same items and the same time limits for every candidate you assess against this role level, in the same conditions as far as you reasonably can, and offer the same reasonable adjustments on request. Keep the answer key back from every candidate until their own scoring and debrief. And hold the result as one input among several: weigh it alongside interviews, work samples, references and track record, and never let it be the sole basis for a decision about someone's hiring, promotion, pay, performance, or employment — see the Advisory section above.

Before you use this with a real candidate, work through the items yourself. Time yourself running a section, read every explanation, and note which items feel like they need adjusting for your own organisation's context — a supplier framework clause, a budget figure, or a governance structure that does not quite match how your organisation works. Treat this guide as a starting point that a competent assessor reviews and adapts, not a finished instrument to run unmodified. Good luck administering it — and remember that a fair process is worth as much as an accurate one.

The items that feel hardest to score are often the ones testing capabilities your organisation is only now learning to value explicitly.*
