# Delivery Manager - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for delivery manager, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At this level a candidate is accountable for the performance of the team: building and maintaining it, identifying obstacles, focusing effort on what matters most, encouraging continuous improvement, and coaching others in Agile and Lean tools. The material is job-specific: it uses the artefacts a delivery manager genuinely handles — sprint and flow metrics, burndown and cumulative flow data, dependency maps, budget reports, supplier correspondence, service standard guidance, and stakeholder communications — rather than abstract puzzles.

This document covers four types of assessment, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and problem solving using delivery artefacts.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — percentages, rates, averages, forecasts, budgets, and flow metrics, with every calculation worked step by step.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — comprehension and inference from the passages a delivery manager actually reads: guidance extracts, supplier letters, governance papers, and reports.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — realistic dilemmas of team leadership, stakeholder management, and escalation, with most-effective and least-effective formats.

Each section explains what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, maps its dimensions to the named skills in the role, gives a substantial set of practice questions progressing from easy to challenging with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, then gives administration tips and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Offer any unscored practice questions the platform provides. At this level a candidate is expected to run a team's world largely without supervision, so several situational judgement items deliberately present more than one defensible option — score for the quality of the reasoning and the recognition of what is actively harmful, not only a single "correct" letter. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace material rather than abstract items. Instead of shape sequences, a candidate reasons about flow metrics, working agreements, dependency chains, release rules, and delivery data, quickly and accurately.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is typically norm-referenced and often adaptive. Most platforms offer unscored practice questions before the timer starts; encourage the candidate to take them.

This kind of assessment matters for a delivery manager because the role is, at its core, structured noticing and reasoning under time pressure. A delivery manager who can look at a cumulative flow diagram and locate the bottleneck, hear three stand-up updates and detect the contradiction, or hold four constraints in mind while re-planning a release, converts raw information into team performance. At this level, expect scenarios about independent delivery and coordination: accountability for team performance, running its cadence, managing dependencies that cross team boundaries, and deciding what to fix, delegate, or escalate.

### How this assessment maps to the role

Each cognitive dimension corresponds to named skills in the role:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Agile and Lean practices**: helping teams measure, evaluate and visualise outcomes means seeing the recurring signal in noisy delivery data.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Planning** and **Maintaining delivery momentum**: managing complex internal and external dependencies is sustained conditional reasoning.
- **Error checking** maps to **Making a process work** and **Financial management**: identifying unnecessarily complicated processes, and monitoring cost and budget, both depend on spotting the inconsistency.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the accountability to focus the team on what is most important to delivery, and to **Planning**: prioritising the most important or high value tasks where urgency and impact rarely align neatly.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Life cycle management** and **Team dynamics and collaboration**: identifying whether a product should move to the next phase, and recognising and dealing with issues in a team, are both exercises in decomposing a messy situation into a tractable decision.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in flow data**

A team's board shows item counts by state at the end of each of the last four weeks: Week 1 — To do 20, In progress 4, In review 2, Done (cumulative) 10. Week 2 — 18, 4, 5, 12. Week 3 — 16, 4, 9, 13. Week 4 — 15, 4, 13, 14. Which state shows the clearest unhealthy trend?

- A) To do
- B) In progress
- C) In review
- D) Done

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "To do" falls steadily and "In progress" is stable — both healthy. "In review" rises 2, 5, 9, 13 — items entering review far faster than they leave it, a compounding queue, while "Done" increments slow. This tests the classic bottleneck signature on a cumulative flow view: the team looks busy and work in progress looks controlled, yet finished value slows because review capacity does not match development capacity.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a working agreement**

A team's definition of done states: "A story is done only when its code is peer reviewed, its automated tests pass, and the product owner has accepted it in the demo environment." At review, a developer says story S is done; later it emerges the product owner has not seen story S. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The developer lied.
- B) Story S's tests failed.
- C) Story S does not meet the definition of done as stated.
- D) The definition of done is too strict.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The definition is a conjunction of three conditions; product owner acceptance has not happened, so at least one required condition is unmet. Option A over-reaches — a mistaken claim is not a lie; B invents an unstated fact; D is an opinion. This tests deducing exactly what a conjunctive rule entails and no more, since definitions of done exist so that "done" is a fact, not a feeling.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a delivery report**

A monthly report paragraph: "The team completed 58 of 64 planned points (91%). Both quarterly objectives remain on track. The payments integration is blocked pending supplier credentials, which we expect within a week; this blocks objective 2's final milestone. No risks require escalation this month." Which two sentences are in tension with each other?

- A) Sentences 1 and 2
- B) Sentences 2 and 3
- C) Sentences 3 and 4
- D) Sentences 1 and 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sentence 2 claims both objectives are on track; sentence 3 describes a live blocker on objective 2's final milestone — a reader cannot tell which sentence to believe. This tests catching internal inconsistency in a report before it is sent, since stakeholders forgive amber status but not discovering that green and amber were both asserted in the same paragraph.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across competing demands**

At 9:00 Monday, four items compete: (1) the deployment pipeline has been failing since Friday evening, blocking a fix for a live defect affecting users; (2) a director's office needs delivery data for a parliamentary question response by 16:00 today; (3) sprint planning is at 11:00 and the backlog has not been refined; (4) a supplier's project manager wants to discuss a contract variation this week. Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 2, 1, 3, 4 — parliamentary business always comes first
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4 — unblock the user-affecting release, get the PQ data moving, then prepare planning, then schedule the supplier
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4 — the nearest meeting first
- D) 1, 3, 2, 4 — team ceremonies outrank external requests

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 has live user harm accumulating and needs only a short first action to get moving; item 2 has a hard same-day deadline but item 1's first action costs little time; item 3 has slack before 11:00; item 4 says "this week." This tests sequencing by impact-weighted urgency and spending personal effort where only the candidate can act, delegating the rest — not by proximity or category.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in recurring impediments**

An impediment log over six sprints shows "waiting for security review" in sprints 1, 3, 4, and 6 (4–6 days each), "test environment refresh delayed" in sprints 2 and 4, and "key stakeholder unavailable for sign-off" once. What is the most valuable conclusion?

- A) The team raises too many impediments and should be more self-sufficient.
- B) Security review waits are a systemic pattern costing roughly a week most sprints, and warrant a structural fix rather than sprint-by-sprint chasing.
- C) One sprint shows stakeholder availability is the biggest problem.
- D) The impediments are varied, so no pattern exists.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Security review waits appear in four of six sprints at real cost each time — a persistent, expensive pattern that has become a system property, warranting a structural response (a service-level expectation, earlier engagement, a capacity conversation) rather than repeated chasing. This tests identifying organisational processes that repeatedly tax delivery, rather than blaming the team or promoting a single occurrence over a repeated pattern.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction with release constraints**

Release rules: (i) releases happen only Tuesdays and Thursdays; (ii) a release requires a completed regression run, taking two full working days, finishing before release day; (iii) no releases during the last week of the fiscal quarter; (iv) the change advisory board (CAB) must approve on the working day immediately before release. The regression run starts Monday of week 1; the last week of the quarter is week 2. What is the earliest possible release day?

- A) Thursday of week 1
- B) Tuesday of week 2
- C) Tuesday of week 3
- D) Thursday of week 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Regression finishes end of Tuesday, week 1, making the earliest possible release day Wednesday onward; the next permitted day under rule (i) is Thursday of week 1; CAB approval on Wednesday is possible; week 1 is not the embargoed week. This tests checking every rule in sequence without inventing constraints the rules do not contain — over-constraining a plan delays value just as surely as under-constraining risks failure.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a dependency map**

A dependency register: (1) Team A needs the identity API from Team B by 1 June; (2) Team B will deliver it on 15 June; (3) Team A's payment milestone, requiring the identity API, is due 10 June; (4) the programme plan lists Team A's payment milestone as green. Assuming entries 1–3 are accurate, what is wrong with entry 4?

- A) Nothing; milestones can be green while dependencies are outstanding.
- B) The milestone cannot credibly be green: its required dependency arrives five days after the milestone's due date.
- C) Entry 4 should say the milestone is complete.
- D) Entry 2 must be wrong, because Team B is always late.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The API arrives five days after the milestone needs it, so the milestone is on course to be missed — a green status asserts the opposite of what the register's own facts imply. This tests catching internal contradiction between a plan's status and its own recorded facts, which drift apart because they are updated by different people at different times.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Problem solving with work-in-progress limits**

A team of five developers has agreed a WIP limit of six items in progress. A stakeholder urgently requests a small, genuinely time-critical fix; the board already shows six items in progress. Which option best respects both the urgency and the team's system?

- A) Add the fix as a seventh item; limits are guidelines.
- B) Refuse the fix until an item finishes, whatever that takes.
- C) Apply the team's expedite approach: pause or finish one current item to make room, make the trade visible on the board, and review at the retro whether an explicit expedite lane policy is needed.
- D) Have the most junior developer do the fix secretly, off the board.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C threads the needle — the urgent item enters, but something else visibly pauses to pay for it, so the limit's protective function survives and the interruption's cost is explicit. Option A quietly abolishes the limit; B honours the rule but not its purpose; D produces invisible work and a board that no longer tells the truth. This tests making exceptions visible and priced, never silent and free.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

Four events must fit in one five-day week: sprint review (R), retrospective (T), sprint planning (P), and a stakeholder demo (D). Constraints: (i) R before T before P; (ii) the product owner attends R, P, and D, but is away Monday and Tuesday; (iii) D must be at least two days after R; (iv) at most one event per day. On which day must D take place?

- A) Wednesday
- B) Thursday
- C) Friday
- D) It cannot be scheduled this week

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** R, P, and D all need the product owner, available only Wednesday–Friday; the ordering rule forces R–Wednesday, T–Thursday, P–Friday, filling all three available days, leaving none for D — and D would need Friday under rule (iii), which P already occupies. This tests exhaustive constraint checking rather than pattern-matching to a plausible-looking answer; the honest output of the reasoning is that something must move.

**Question 10 (hard) — Diagnosing a velocity anomaly**

A team's velocity over eight sprints: 30, 31, 29, 30, 44, 45, 43, 46. The jump coincides with sprint 5, when the team switched estimation approach after two new members joined. The product owner says: "The team is 50% faster!" Stakeholders want the roadmap re-forecast using velocity 45. What is the soundest interpretation?

- A) The team became 50% more productive; re-forecast with 45.
- B) The scale changed: post-sprint-5 points are not the same unit as pre-sprint-5 points, so compare forecasts only within the new baseline, and re-estimate remaining roadmap work in the new scale before re-forecasting.
- C) The new members caused the increase; velocity will keep rising.
- D) Velocity is meaningless and should not inform forecasts.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two flat plateaus with a single step exactly coinciding with a re-estimation and team change is far more plausibly a re-calibration of the unit than a genuine productivity leap. This tests recognising a changed measurement scale and its practical consequence: forecasting from the new baseline is fine only if the remaining backlog is re-estimated in the same new scale, since mixing scales inflates the forecast dangerously.

**Question 11 (hard) — Prioritising by cost of delay**

Four backlog items, one deliverable per sprint: (W) saves £8,000/month once live, no deadline; (X) required for a regulation taking effect in four sprints, after which the service cannot legally operate without it; (Y) fixes a defect costing about £3,000/month in extra operational cost, with a working phone-based workaround; (Z) a director's pet reporting feature, no measured value. Which ordering best reflects cost of delay and risk?

- A) Z, X, W, Y — seniority first
- B) X, W, Y, Z — legal deadline first (it consumes the available runway), then highest monthly saving, then the smaller saving, then the unmeasured request
- C) W, Y, X, Z — money first, compliance later
- D) X, Y, W, Z — legal first, then user-facing defect, then savings, then the request

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** X must start soon since it needs runway against a hard legal deadline that money comparisons cannot capture; W's higher monthly cost of delay places it ahead of Y; Z goes last pending evidence of value. This tests ordering by quantified cost of delay and risk category together, rather than by seniority (A), ignoring a legal deadline's urgency (C), or a plausible-sounding but unquantified ordering (D).

**Question 12 (hard) — Spotting the flawed improvement argument**

At a retro, a team member argues: "Our defect count doubled in the two sprints after we started pairing on complex stories. Pairing is clearly hurting quality — we should stop." Two other facts are known: the team also began recording defects in the tracker (instead of fixing them silently) at the same time as the pairing experiment, and the two sprints included the riskiest integration work of the quarter. What is the strongest response to the argument?

- A) Agree: the numbers speak for themselves, and doubling is decisive.
- B) Point out that two simultaneous changes (defect recording and work risk profile) confound the comparison, so the defect count cannot yet attribute cause; propose a fairer evaluation before deciding.
- C) Overrule the discussion: pairing is best practice and is not up for debate.
- D) Compromise by pairing half as often, splitting the difference.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With three candidate causes changing at once — pairing, measurement, and work risk — the doubling supports no single attribution. This tests naming confounds and proposing a fairer evaluation (comparing escaped defects, comparing against similar-risk work, or running longer) rather than accepting the flawed inference, overruling the team's ownership of its own practice, or treating a causal question as a negotiation.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate diagnoses a bottleneck from where work queues up**, not from who appears busy.
- **Watch for whether a candidate checks a status against its own supporting evidence** rather than accepting a contradictory report at face value.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes a measurement or scale change from a genuine performance change** in a metric.
- **Note whether a candidate verifies a candidate answer against every stated constraint**, including the unglamorous ones.
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that stops at the first satisfying option** in a multi-constraint question rather than checking every constraint.
- **Missing when a candidate accepts a status report that contradicts its own underlying facts.**
- **Crediting an answer that treats a step-change in a metric as a performance change** without checking whether measurement or scale changed.
- **Missing when a candidate lets seniority or recency drive a prioritisation decision** rather than impact-weighted urgency and cost of delay.
- **Crediting an answer that accepts a causal claim without checking for confounding changes.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and confidently a candidate works with the numbers this role genuinely produces: velocity and throughput, capacity, burn rates, budgets, supplier costs, defect rates, and forecast arithmetic. The operations are percentages, ratios, averages, rates, and differences, applied to realistic delivery data under time pressure, where selecting the right figures matters as much as the arithmetic.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 30 minutes, with 15 to 25 questions, each presenting data followed by a calculation or comparison. A calculator is often permitted; offer rough paper.

This kind of assessment matters for a delivery manager because the role's credibility runs on numbers handled well: whether a plan fits capacity, whether a burn rate fits a budget, whether a trend supports a forecast. A delivery manager who mis-reads a percentage in a governance paper, or forecasts from an inflated average, spends the team's credibility.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages, ratios and proportions** map to **Agile and Lean practices** and reporting on delivery: completion rates, defect ratios, and utilisation are the everyday units of delivery conversation.
- **Averages, medians and trends** map to **Planning**: using data to inform planning means choosing the representative number, and knowing when an average misleads.
- **Rate and capacity arithmetic** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: managing pace and tempo quantitatively.
- **Budget and cost calculations** map to **Financial management**: monitoring cost and budget, balancing cost versus value, and reporting financial delivery all reduce to day rates, run rates, variances, and comparisons done correctly.
- **Supplier cost comparison** maps to **Commercial management**: as the point of contact for contracted suppliers, sanity-checking quotes and variations before they reach commercial colleagues.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: translating a data table into one accurate sentence for a stakeholder is a numeric act before it is a verbal one.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Sprint completion percentage**

A team planned 55 points and completed 44. What percentage of the plan was completed?

- A) 75%
- B) 80%
- C) 82%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 44 ÷ 55 = 80%. This tests dividing completed by planned, keeping the direction firm — inverting gives 125%, which should immediately look wrong for a sprint that left work unfinished.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average throughput**

A team finished 12, 15, 11, 14, and 13 work items in the last five weeks. What is the average weekly throughput?

- A) 12 items
- B) 12.5 items
- C) 13 items
- D) 14 items

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Sum = 65; ÷5 = 13. This tests a basic average, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting that throughput often forecasts more robustly than story-point velocity since it does not depend on estimation calibration.

**Question 3 (easy) — Capacity after absences**

A team of 6 has a 10-working-day sprint. One member is on leave all sprint, another for 4 days, and a public holiday removes 1 day for everyone present. How many person-days of capacity remain?

- A) 41 person-days
- B) 42 person-days
- C) 45 person-days
- D) 46 person-days

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 60 (maximum) − 10 (fully absent member) − 4 (partial leave) − 5 (holiday for the remaining 5) = 41. This tests explicit, careful accounting of who loses what — a common error subtracts the holiday for all 6 people rather than the 5 not already fully absent.

**Question 4 (easy) — Budget run rate**

A 6-month budget of £480,000 has spent £172,000 after 2 months. At the same monthly rate, what will total spend be at 6 months?

- A) £480,000
- B) £498,000
- C) £516,000
- D) £524,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Monthly rate = £86,000; × 6 = £516,000. This tests projecting a run rate rather than answering "how much has been spent" — the run-rate figure, identified early, leaves months to correct course or raise a variance, unlike the same calculation done at month 5.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Defect rate comparison**

Release 1: 40 stories shipped, 6 defects found in 30 days. Release 2: 55 stories, 7 defects. Which release had the lower defect rate per story, and what was it?

- A) Release 1, at 0.15 defects per story
- B) Release 2, at 0.127 defects per story
- C) Release 1, at 0.13 defects per story
- D) Release 2, at 0.15 defects per story

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Release 1: 6 ÷ 40 = 0.15; Release 2: 7 ÷ 55 ≈ 0.127. Release 2 shipped more defects absolutely but at a lower rate. This tests the absolute-versus-rate distinction, a common source of misleading delivery commentary — a governance slide reporting defect counts without shipment volume invites the wrong conclusion.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change in cycle time**

Median cycle time fell from 8 days to 5 days after adopting WIP limits. What is the percentage reduction?

- A) 30%
- B) 37.5%
- C) 40%
- D) 60%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (8 − 5) ÷ 8 = 37.5%. Option D divides by the new value (5) instead of the original. This tests always dividing by the starting value, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting that "median" signals a skewed distribution being described fairly rather than dragged by outliers.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Weighted cost of a blended team**

A team has 3 civil servants at £400/day and 2 contractors at £700/day. What is the team's total cost for a 10-day sprint, and what percentage of that cost is contractor cost?

- A) £26,000 total; 54% contractor
- B) £26,000 total; 50% contractor
- C) £25,000 total; 56% contractor
- D) £24,000 total; 58% contractor

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Daily cost £2,600; sprint total £26,000; contractor share = 14,000 ÷ 26,000 ≈ 54%. This tests weighted cost calculation and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting the gap between headcount share (40%) and cost share (54%) as the opening line of a conversation about team shape and value.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a burnup with scope change**

A burnup chart: total scope started at 100 points; at week 4, completed work is 40 points and total scope has risen to 120 points. The team completes 10 points per week. Assuming scope stays at 120, how many more weeks to completion, and what did the scope change add to the timeline?

- A) 8 more weeks; the scope change added 2 weeks
- B) 8 more weeks; the scope change added 4 weeks
- C) 6 more weeks; the scope change added 2 weeks
- D) 12 more weeks; the scope change added 2 weeks

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Remaining work = 80 points ÷ 10/week = 8 weeks; scope grew by 20 points, adding 2 weeks compared to the original scope. This tests separating a schedule change's causes — throughput on plan, timeline moved because scope grew — which keeps a conversation on scope decisions rather than misdiagnosing team performance.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Supplier variation cost check**

A supplier contract runs 60 days at £600/day. A proposed variation extends by 15 days at a revised £650/day applying to the extension days only. What is the variation's cost and the new contract total?

- A) Variation £9,000; new total £45,000
- B) Variation £9,750; new total £45,750
- C) Variation £9,750; new total £48,750
- D) Variation £10,000; new total £46,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Original £36,000; variation = 15 × £650 = £9,750; new total £45,750. Option C wrongly applies the new rate to all 75 days — the most common error when rates change mid-contract, worth double-checking in real variation paperwork since it can occasionally appear in a supplier's own favour.

**Question 10 (hard) — Forecasting a queue drain**

A support queue holds 180 tickets. New tickets arrive at 40/week; the team resolves 55/week. Assuming stable rates, when will the queue clear, and what backlog remains after 6 weeks?

- A) Cleared in about 12 weeks; 90 tickets remain at 6 weeks
- B) Cleared in about 12 weeks; 60 tickets remain at 6 weeks
- C) Cleared in about 4.5 weeks; 0 tickets remain at 6 weeks
- D) Cleared in about 3.3 weeks; 0 tickets remain at 6 weeks

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Net drain rate = 55 − 40 = 15/week; time to clear = 180 ÷ 15 = 12 weeks; backlog at 6 weeks = 180 − 90 = 90. Option D ignores arrivals entirely. This tests draining a queue at the net rate, not the resolution rate — and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting the forecast's sensitivity to small changes in the arrival rate.

**Question 11 (hard) — Budget variance with committed spend**

At month 4 of a 12-month £900,000 budget: actual spend £310,000, plus purchase orders committed but not invoiced totalling £85,000. What is (a) the uncommitted budget remaining and (b) the average monthly spend available for the remaining 8 months if all commitments are honoured?

- A) £590,000 remaining; £73,750 per month
- B) £505,000 remaining; £63,125 per month
- C) £505,000 remaining; £73,750 per month
- D) £590,000 remaining; £63,125 per month

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total accounted for = £395,000; uncommitted remaining = £505,000; monthly available = £63,125. This tests deducting committed-but-uninvoiced spend before declaring headroom — teams tracking only invoiced spend routinely "discover" overcommitment months late — and comparing this to the actual burn rate so far (£77,500/month) as an escalation trigger.

**Question 12 (hard) — Choosing the representative metric**

Ten completed items had cycle times (days): 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 25. A stakeholder asks: "How long does an item typically take, and can you commit that a new item will be done within 10 days?" Which answer is most defensible?

- A) "The average is 6 days, so 10 days is guaranteed."
- B) "Typically about 4 days (median); 9 of the last 10 items finished within 6 days, so 10 days is a reasonable expectation, though roughly 1 in 10 recent items has run far longer — we should understand that outlier before promising."
- C) "The average is 6 days, so most items take about 6 days."
- D) "We cannot say anything; the data is too variable."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Mean = 6 (pulled up by one outlier); median = 4, closer to the typical experience; 9 of 10 items finish within 6 days. This tests choosing the right measure (median over a skewed mean), framing a commitment with percentiles rather than a point estimate, and flagging the outlier for investigation rather than either converting an average into a guarantee or discarding usable data.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate identifies and uses the correct denominator** in a rate or percentage question.
- **Watch for whether a candidate forecasts with the net rate** (arrivals minus completions) rather than the gross completion rate.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes committed-but-uninvoiced spend from available budget.**
- **Note whether a candidate checks a skewed distribution's median against its mean** before calling a figure "typical".
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that ignores the denominator** — absolute counts (defects, spend, tickets) mislead without their base.
- **Crediting an answer that applies a revised rate to a whole contract** instead of only the varied portion.
- **Missing when a candidate treats committed-but-uninvoiced money as available.**
- **Crediting an answer that quotes the mean of a skewed distribution as "typical".**
- **Rewarding an answer that turns an estimate into a guarantee** rather than a supported expectation or range.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely a candidate extracts meaning, tests claims, and draws inferences from written material — the document types a delivery manager actually reads: Service Standard guidance, governance papers, supplier correspondence, assessment reports, policy extracts, and internal emails. A passage is presented; questions probe what it states, implies, and does not settle.

The dominant format is True / False / Cannot say. Supporting formats include best-summary selection, strongest-inference identification, purpose-of-passage questions, and clear-writing judgement. These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions.

This kind of assessment matters for a delivery manager because the role is a communications switchboard with accountability attached: listening to and interpreting the needs of technical and non-technical stakeholders begins with reading their words exactly, and every relay — guidance to the team, supplier commitments to commercial colleagues, team status to governance forums — is an opportunity to add distortion or remove it. The cardinal rule: answer only from the passage. "Cannot say" is correct whenever a passage does not settle a statement, even when professional experience suggests an answer.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: interpreting stakeholder needs and managing expectations starts with extracting exactly what was written, including conditions, exclusions, and hedges.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: addressing risks, issues and dependencies whose ownership sits outside the team means the facts relayed must be facts.
- **Inference** maps to **Life cycle management**: service standards and phase guidance state principles; deciding whether a service should move to the next phase requires sound inference without over-reading.
- **Reading conditions and exceptions** maps to **Commercial management** and **Financial management**: contracts, spend controls, and variation clauses are structured as conditions.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to the accountability for reporting and escalation: providing delivery confidence in writing rewards the plain, specific, actionable sentence over defensive fog.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–4)*

"Teams must carry out user research throughout the service's development, not only at the start. During public beta, the service must be available to all users who need it, though teams may use a phased rollout to manage risk, provided the phasing criteria are published. A service may not charge users a fee during beta unless the equivalent non-digital channel already charges one. Before a service can move from beta to live, it must meet the performance targets agreed at its beta assessment, and the team must show that the service can operate sustainably without the continued presence of its original developers."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "User research is only required at the start of a service's development."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True during beta only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage's opening sentence states the exact opposite. This tests reading polarity words like "not only" as carefully as content words, since a skim-reader can lose the meaning while matching individual phrases.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "A team may roll out its public beta in phases if it publishes the phasing criteria."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) Only with ministerial approval

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage permits phasing "provided the phasing criteria are published" — the statement restates the permission with its condition accurately. Option D imports an unstated approval step. This tests keeping a permission-with-condition structure welded together when relaying a rule.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service whose non-digital channel is free may not charge users during beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because beta services may always charge

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The exception applies only where the non-digital channel "already charges" — since it is free, the exception does not apply and the general prohibition stands. This tests the two-step reasoning of identifying a general rule and then checking whether a stated case falls inside its exception.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strongest inference**

Which of the following is best supported by the passage?

- A) Services with high staff turnover during beta may struggle to pass the live assessment.
- B) The performance targets for live are set by the live assessment panel.
- C) Phased rollout is mandatory during public beta.
- D) Beta services must be free for all users.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage requires demonstrating sustainable operation "without the continued presence of its original developers" — heavy turnover plausibly makes that harder to show. B misattributes when targets are agreed; C converts a permission into a mandate; D ignores the stated exception. This tests calibrated inference: the smallest supported step beyond the text, neither refusing all inference nor speculating beyond it.

*Passage 2 (for Questions 5–7)*

"Further to our meeting of 3 March, we confirm the revised delivery approach. The data migration tooling will be delivered in two tranches: tranche 1 (extract and validation modules) by 30 April, and tranche 2 (load and reconciliation modules) within six weeks of tranche 1's acceptance. Please note that acceptance testing of each tranche is your responsibility and must complete within 10 working days of delivery; delays in acceptance will move tranche 2's delivery date accordingly. The revised approach does not change the contract value. Should your department require the optional archival module discussed at the meeting, this would be subject to a separate change request and quotation."

**Question 5 (easy) — Fact identification**

According to the letter, which of the following is the supplier's responsibility?

- A) Acceptance testing of each tranche
- B) Delivering the load and reconciliation modules in tranche 2
- C) Raising the change request for the archival module
- D) Completing acceptance within 10 working days

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The supplier delivers both tranches; the letter explicitly assigns acceptance testing to the department ("is your responsibility"). Option C is undetermined — the letter never says who raises the change request. This tests sorting a commercial letter's obligations by owner, since every unnoticed obligation on one's own side is a hidden schedule risk.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "If the department takes 15 working days to accept tranche 1, tranche 2 will be delivered later than it otherwise would have been."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because the contract value does not change

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Tranche 2's clock starts at acceptance, and the letter states delays in acceptance move tranche 2's date accordingly; 15 days exceeds the 10-day window. Option D confuses money with time. This tests chaining a letter's clauses to a stated consequence, with the practical implication that the reader's own testing capacity is now on the critical path.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The archival module will cost more than the current contract value."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because change requests always add cost

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** No price or comparison is stated for the archival module. This tests resisting a plausible commercial instinct ("separate quotes usually cost more") in favour of what the text actually establishes — the honest relay to a colleague is "unpriced, needs a change request and quote."

*Passage 3 (for Questions 8–10)*

"The programme board considered the service's request to extend private beta by eight weeks. The board noted that the team's velocity had stabilised and that the outstanding accessibility issues, while reduced from eleven to four, included two assessed as high severity. The board agreed the extension in principle, subject to two conditions: first, that the two high-severity accessibility issues are resolved before any extension period begins; second, that the team presents a revised benefits profile at the next board. The board declined the associated request for two additional developers, citing the funding envelope, but invited a re-submission alongside the revised benefits profile."

**Question 8 (easy)**

Statement: "The board approved the eight-week extension unconditionally."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True in principle

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The board agreed "in principle, subject to two conditions" — the direct negation of "unconditionally." Option D is a half-reading trap: "in principle" appears in the text, but the statement's operative word ("unconditionally") is contradicted. This tests catching how governance qualifiers change a decision's real force.

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: "The team will present a revised benefits profile at the next board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the board ordered it

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The revised profile is a condition of the extension, not a prediction of fact — whether the team pursues the extension at all is unstated. This tests distinguishing a condition attached to a choice from an unconditional command, a distinction contract clauses and assessment conditions share throughout.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Purpose and accurate summary**

Which sentence best summarises the extract for a team's channel?

- A) "Board says no to everything — extension refused and no new developers."
- B) "Extension agreed in principle with two gates: fix the two high-severity accessibility issues before it starts, and bring a revised benefits profile to next board. Developer request declined for now but can be re-submitted with the benefits profile."
- C) "Great news — extension approved! Also we should probably look at accessibility at some point."
- D) "The board discussed our beta. More details to follow."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B captures every decision with its qualifiers; A inverts the main outcome; C inflates the outcome and demotes a hard precondition; D relays nothing. This tests the pattern for summarising governance outcomes — every decision, every condition, every door left open, with no editorial optimism or pessimism.

**Question 11 (hard) — Condition stacking**

Passage: "Spend approval is delegated to service teams for individual items under £10,000, provided the cumulative delegated spend in the quarter remains under £40,000. Items between £10,000 and £50,000 require director approval. Any item connected to a live commercial dispute requires legal review regardless of value. Delegated approvals must be logged within five working days." A team wants to purchase £8,500 of load-testing services, having already used £34,000 of delegated spend this quarter, from a supplier in a live commercial dispute with the department over an unrelated contract. What does the purchase require?

- A) Nothing beyond logging — it is under £10,000.
- B) Director approval only, because cumulative spend would exceed £40,000.
- C) Legal review, and director-level (non-delegated) approval because the cumulative delegated cap would be breached — plus logging if any delegated route is used.
- D) Legal review only; the item value is within delegation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The value test passes for delegation, but the cumulative test fails (£34,000 + £8,500 = £42,500, breaching £40,000), pushing to director-level approval, and the dispute test independently requires legal review "regardless of value". This tests stacking every applicable condition rather than stopping at the first one satisfied or checked.

**Question 12 (hard) — Evaluating an argument in a report**

A report argues: "We should extend the contract with Supplier P. Their delivery this year has been strong: all six milestones were met. Switching suppliers would incur procurement costs and a three-month onboarding delay. Supplier Q, the likely alternative, has no experience with our department. Therefore extending with P is the only responsible option." Which is the most accurate critical assessment?

- A) The argument is sound; six met milestones settle the matter.
- B) The argument offers relevant evidence for extension but overstates its conclusion: switching costs and P's record support extension, yet "only responsible option" ignores unexamined factors — P's pricing for the extension, market comparison, and whether Q's departmental inexperience actually matters for this work.
- C) The argument is worthless because it comes from someone who prefers P.
- D) The argument fails because milestone delivery is irrelevant to contract decisions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is genuinely relevant, but "the only responsible option" claims a necessity the argument never examines. Option C commits the ad hominem error; D dismisses plainly relevant data. This tests separating relevant evidence from an overstated conclusion, and naming what remains unexamined before any decision is signed off.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate treats "cannot say" as a genuine, confident answer** rather than avoiding it.
- **Watch for whether a candidate distinguishes a condition attached to a choice from an unconditional command.**
- **Note whether a candidate checks every applicable condition in a multi-gate rule**, especially clauses marked "regardless of."
- **Note whether a candidate separates relevant evidence from an overstated conclusion** in an argument-evaluation item.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and expect a candidate to read a full passage before answering its statements.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer drawn from professional instinct** ("separate quotes always cost more") rather than the passage.
- **Missing when a candidate sheds a condition in a summary or relay** — "agreed in principle, subject to..." read as "approved".
- **Crediting an answer that stops at the first satisfied gate** in a multi-condition rule.
- **Crediting an answer that converts a permission into a mandate**, or a condition into an order.
- **Missing when a candidate judges an argument by its source or fluency** rather than the gap between its evidence and its conclusion.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic scenarios from the role and asks a candidate to evaluate possible responses — most effective, least effective, best-and-worst pairings, or effectiveness ratings. Responses are scored against the consensus of experienced practitioners, anchored to professional behaviours: user focus, honesty and transparency, team empowerment, early and proportionate escalation, inclusion, and sustainable delivery.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 20 to 35 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed because the construct is judgement, not speed. Unlike cognitive items, several options may be defensible; scoring rewards recognising which is most effective, which is actively harmful, and which are merely mediocre.

This kind of assessment matters for a delivery manager because judgement is the role's irreducible core — accountable for team performance, but delivering through influence, facilitation, and coaching rather than authority over the work itself. At this level, expect scenarios about independent delivery and coordination: running the team's world largely without supervision, owning interfaces to product, stakeholders, suppliers, and governance, and coaching others in Agile and Lean practice.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Team leadership scenarios** map to **Team dynamics and collaboration**: building motivated teams, recognising and dealing with issues, creating the environment for delivery, and getting the team makeup right for the situation.
- **Coaching scenarios** map to the accountability to coach and mentor team members and others in Agile and Lean tools — the SJT tests whether a candidate builds capability or creates dependence.
- **Escalation and blocker scenarios** map to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: addressing risks, issues and dependencies including where ownership exists outside the team, and choosing the escalation rung that resolves without inflaming.
- **Stakeholder and reporting scenarios** map to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical** and **Financial management**: managing expectations, hosting difficult discussions, reporting delivery honestly.
- **Process and improvement scenarios** map to **Making a process work** and **Agile and Lean practices**: challenging unnecessarily complicated processes, guiding teams through change, and encouraging experimentation.
- **Life cycle scenarios** map to **Life cycle management**: judging when a product should move phase, and ensuring development matches the standards each phase demands.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — The failing new starter**

A developer who joined six weeks ago is visibly struggling: their work is regularly reworked, and two teammates have separately mentioned it. The developer seems unaware of the concern. What is the most effective response?

- A) Wait until the end of probation; the formal process will handle it.
- B) Have a prompt, private, specific conversation about what is being seen, understand their view, and agree support — pairing, clearer expectations, or training — with a follow-up date.
- C) Quietly route all complex work away from them so the problem stops surfacing.
- D) Raise it in the team retro so everyone can give them feedback together.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B addresses the issue while small, privately, pairing candour with support and a follow-up date. A wastes weeks by outsourcing a leadership conversation to a bureaucratic deadline; C hides the problem and caps growth; D ambushes a struggling person in a group setting. This tests early, direct, kind intervention with genuine curiosity before conclusion.

**Question 2 (easy) — The optimistic status demand**

A programme manager, preparing for a board presentation, asks for a milestone to show green this week, though it is genuinely amber — a dependency has slipped and recovery is possible but not assured. What is the most effective response?

- A) Report green; recovery is possible, so green is arguably true.
- B) Report amber with a crisp recovery narrative: what slipped, the recovery plan, the decision point at which it becomes red, and what help would accelerate it — and offer to brief the programme manager before the review.
- C) Report red to create pressure for help.
- D) Ask the product owner to make the call so it is not the candidate's name on it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Amber with a credible, actionable plan is respectable, and briefing the programme manager first converts a compliance tussle into an alliance. Option A starts a debt that compounds if recovery fails; C corrupts the signal in the other direction; D abdicates a squarely-held accountability. This tests providing delivery confidence honestly, including uncomfortable confidence.

**Question 3 (easy) — Coaching versus rescuing**

A newest ceremony facilitator, running their first retrospective, is delivering a flat, awkward session with low energy and a dominant voice steering every topic; they glance over for help. What is the most effective response in the moment?

- A) Take over the facilitation smoothly to save the session.
- B) Support without seizing: model participation from a seat in the room, put a laptop away pointedly, contribute, and offer one light assist ("Could we hear from folks who haven't spoken?") — then debrief generously afterwards, helping the facilitator design their next session.
- C) Let it fail completely so the lesson lands.
- D) Announce that retros will return to the candidate until further notice.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Light-touch assists from a participant's chair keep the session serviceable while leaving the facilitator visibly in charge, with the developmental payload delivered afterwards. A saves the session at the cost of the facilitator's growth; D converts one wobbly session into a permanent regression. This tests building capability rather than substituting for it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The supplier's slipping signal**

A supplier delivering a critical integration has given three increasingly vague weekly updates, and their lead engineer has stopped attending calls; the contractual milestone is five weeks away, and a commercial colleague is on leave for a fortnight. What is the most effective response?

- A) Wait for the milestone; the contract will speak for itself if they miss.
- B) Request a working-level demonstration of current progress this week, name the observations factually to the supplier's manager ("three vague updates, lead engineer absent — help me understand where things stand"), brief the commercial colleague's cover, and record the pattern in the risk log.
- C) Email the supplier's account director threatening contract termination.
- D) Ask developers to build a contingency version in parallel, without telling the supplier.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A working demonstration cannot be vague, and factual framing invites explanation without defensiveness; briefing the cover and logging the risk builds the record. This tests proportionate pressure through evidence, following an escalation ladder — evidence, conversation, record, then commercial machinery — rather than waiting out five weeks or deploying the heaviest response first.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Two strong engineers at war**

Two senior engineers have entrenched positions on an architectural choice; debate has curdled into sarcasm, the rest of the team has gone quiet, and work touching the disputed area is slowing. What is the most effective response?

- A) Decide the architecture personally to end the argument.
- B) Separate the dispute's technical and human layers: get the two to agree the decision criteria and a decision mechanism with a date, facilitate that process — and privately address the sarcasm and its effect on the team with each of them as a behaviour issue, whatever the technical outcome.
- C) Let them fight it out; conflict between experts is healthy.
- D) Move one of them to another team to dissolve the tension.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Agreeing criteria and a decision process converts an identity contest into an evaluable question; the behavioural layer needs a separate, private conversation regardless of who is technically right. A overrides the team's technical ownership; C mistakes curdled conflict (a silenced team) for creative conflict; D dissolves capability instead of resolving the pattern. This tests separating a legitimate technical disagreement from the damaging behaviour surrounding it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The process a candidate is told to add**

A central PMO instructs a team to complete a new 14-field weekly reporting template, most of which duplicates data already on the team's board. The team is irritated; the PMO analyst insists everyone must comply. What is the most effective response?

- A) Refuse: the team's time is too valuable for duplicate bureaucracy.
- B) Comply silently and absorb the cost forever.
- C) Comply initially while engaging the PMO constructively: understand what decisions the template feeds, demonstrate where existing data already answers them, and propose a lighter automated alternative — escalating the duplication cost through the management line if the constructive route stalls.
- D) Delegate the template to the newest team member and move on.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Comply first to avoid a governance fight that would be lost, then challenge the process on its own terms — showing existing data already serves the decisions the template feeds is the argument that actually kills duplicate templates. A wins a morning and loses the relationship; B teaches the organisation that compliance is free. This tests challenging an unnecessarily complicated process as engagement, not defiance.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Mid-sprint scope pressure from the product owner**

A product owner, under pressure from their director, wants to inject a significant new feature into the current sprint for the third consecutive sprint, displacing committed half-finished work; morale and metrics are visibly degrading. What is the most effective response?

- A) Refuse all mid-sprint changes on principle; the sprint is sacred.
- B) Accept the change; the product owner owns priority.
- C) Handle the instance and the pattern separately: facilitate a quick trade-off decision on this feature with the team, then address the recurring pattern with the product owner directly — showing the three-sprint data on abandoned work and degraded flow, and jointly redesigning intake so genuine urgency has a route that does not shred every sprint.
- D) Tell the team to absorb the feature on top of existing commitments; morale will recover.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Today's decision is made with costs visible; the pattern is addressed separately, with data ("31 points abandoned; cycle time doubled") rather than complaint. A defends a ceremony against a business need that may be real; B surrenders accountability for team performance; D is the burnout option. This tests handling an instance properly while separately fixing a damaging recurring pattern with evidence.

**Question 8 (moderate) — An unwelcome health check**

An anonymous team health check shows low scores on "psychological safety" and "learning", with a comment: "Disagreeing with the DM's process ideas doesn't feel worth the hassle." What is the most effective response?

- A) Treat the result as noise; anonymous surveys attract grumbling.
- B) Identify who wrote the comment so it can be resolved with them directly.
- C) Share the results with the team transparently, including the uncomfortable ones; name a personal part in it without defensiveness ("this says pushing back on me feels costly — I want to change that"); invite concrete suggestions through channels that do not require confronting the delivery manager directly; and change something visible, then re-measure.
- D) Run the health check again next month, hoping for better numbers.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Transparency about uncomfortable data models the safety being requested; owning the specific critique shows it landed without triggering defence; low-confrontation channels respect that the deficit cannot be fixed by demanding bravery. A demonstrates the very unsafety it reports; B converts anonymity into a trap and would end honest feedback permanently. This tests metabolising criticism into visible change rather than dismissal or defensiveness dressed as diligence.

**Question 9 (hard) — The phase-gate temptation**

A beta assessment is three weeks away. A team lead privately discloses that a load-testing shortcut means the performance figures in the evidence pack reflect a smaller dataset than the pack implies — not fabricated, but flattering. Honest testing would take two weeks and might produce worse numbers, risking the assessment date. The product owner says: "The pack is technically accurate. Let's not volunteer confusion." What is the most effective response?

- A) Proceed with the current pack; it is technically accurate and the date matters.
- B) Insist the evidence be corrected or clearly caveated, run the honest load tests now, and if the results or timing put the assessment at risk, tell the assessment team early and reschedule if needed — treating the standard as the point, not the gate.
- C) Proceed with the pack but mention the caveat verbally if an assessor happens to ask.
- D) Report the product owner to the SRO for attempted deception.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Technically accurate" that requires the adjective is the tell that a pack is designed to mislead. Correcting or caveating the evidence, generating honest data, and communicating early with the assessment team — who deal with rescheduling routinely — holds the professional line proportionately. A buys three weeks at the cost of operational credibility; D leaps past every intermediate step for what is currently an argument, not an act. This tests ensuring a service is developed in line with the appropriate standards for its phase, since an assessment exists to check reality for users' benefit.

**Question 10 (hard) — The finance discrepancy**

A monthly finance report shows a supplier line at £48,000 against a purchase order believed to be £36,000; investigation reveals a second, unfamiliar £12,000 PO on the team's cost centre, approved by a programme-level manager, for "delivery support services" that cannot be identified. What is the most effective response?

- A) Assume it is legitimate — programme managers can raise POs — and move on.
- B) Adjust the report to exclude the £12,000 so the team's figures look right.
- C) Query it factually and promptly: ask the approving manager what the PO covers, tell the finance partner it is being verified, and document the exchange — escalating through the management line if the explanation does not resolve it.
- D) Raise a fraud allegation with internal audit immediately.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** An unexplained discrepancy has a spectrum of explanations, most innocent, and a factual query resolves the majority in one email while documentation and an escalation clause cover the remainder. A abandons cost monitoring; B falsifies the report to route around a discrepancy; D deploys the gravest allegation before asking a single question. This tests monitoring cost and budget and escalating proportionately, starting with verification.

**Question 11 (hard) — The improvement that isn't landing**

WIP limits and cycle-time tracking introduced three months ago have genuinely cut cycle time by 30%, but grumbling has grown — two developers call it "micromanagement by dashboard," and this week the team ignored the limit twice. What is the most effective response?

- A) Enforce the limits more firmly; the data proves they work.
- B) Drop the limits; a practice the team rejects is not worth the friction.
- C) Bring the tension to the team openly: show the cycle-time improvement, acknowledge the felt cost honestly, and ask the team to decide — keep, adapt, or replace the practice — while being clear about the outcome (flow) that any replacement must serve.
- D) Keep the limits but stop publishing the metrics so the dashboard resentment fades.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A practice held in place against a team's will is already failing on a longer clock — the ignored limits are the leading indicator. C holds both truths (data and discontent) and puts the mechanism's design, not the outcome, into the team's hands. A converts passive resistance into standing conflict; B abandons a demonstrated improvement at the first friction. This tests recognising, reflecting, and adapting when something does not work while keeping the outcome non-negotiable.

**Question 12 (hard) — Choosing least effective: the failing dependency**

A platform team outside the candidate's control owns an API needed in four weeks; their delivery manager informally says they are likely to be six weeks late, threatening the candidate's milestone and two other teams'. Which response is **least effective**?

- A) Confirm the facts with the platform team, then promptly inform the product owner and affected teams, and convene a joint conversation about re-sequencing, interim options, and what help might accelerate the platform work.
- B) Update the plan and risk log, and re-plan the next sprints around work that does not depend on the API.
- C) Keep the information private for now — it was informal, it may improve, and alarming stakeholders early could create pressure the platform team resents.
- D) Escalate the cross-team impact through the programme's governance route with the facts and options, so the priority call is made by people who own the trade-off.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Informal early warnings are the most valuable signal in dependency management precisely because they arrive while options exist; the correct response to informality is verification, not suppression. Silence burns response time for three teams and, if it surfaces anyway, reveals concealment that spends trust. A, B, and D form the healthy triad of verify-and-communicate, adapt what is controlled, and route the trade-off to its legitimate owners. This tests actively addressing a dependency whose ownership sits outside the team rather than sitting on early information.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate addresses both a technical/process layer and a human layer** when a scenario entangles them.
- **Watch for whether a candidate follows an escalation ladder** — direct conversation, evidence and record, natural owner, management line, formal governance — rather than skipping rungs in either direction.
- **Note whether a candidate discards an option whose main benefit is protecting appearances** — the team's, the report's, or their own.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes "most effective" from "least effective" items correctly** — the least-effective format often plants three good options as distractors.
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding decisive-looking drama** — terminations threatened, decisions seized, formal allegations fired first — over proportionate action.
- **Rewarding conflict-avoidant patience** ("wait and see") when a scenario contains a live signal.
- **Crediting an answer that solves a people problem with process, or a process problem with pressure**, rather than matching the remedy to the layer.
- **Rewarding rescue over coaching** — an option where the candidate takes over facilitation, decisions, or the work itself trades the team's growth for their own comfort.
- **Missing when a candidate sits on bad news to manage others' feelings** rather than communicating it early and structured.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a delivery manager: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested reading flow data for bottleneck signatures, cross-checking statuses against their own evidence, applying multi-constraint rules exhaustively, separating measurement changes from performance changes, and prioritising by cost of delay rather than seniority or noise. The numeric section tested run rates, net drain rates, weighted team costs, committed-versus-actual budgets, and percentile-based commitments, with every step written out. The verbal section tested holding the line between what a document states, implies, and leaves unsettled — conditions welded to permissions, governance qualifiers carried intact, arguments credited for their evidence and challenged for their overreach. The situational judgement section tested the real weight of the role: honest ambers with recovery narratives, coaching instead of rescuing, pattern conversations held with data, evidence packs kept honest, and information moved promptly toward the people who can act on it.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. On the situational judgement items in particular, score for the quality of reasoning and the recognition of what is actively harmful, since several items deliberately have more than one defensible option. A debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can think carefully with delivery artefacts, work accurately with the numbers that shape a team's credibility, read precisely, and exercise sound judgement while running a team's world largely without supervision — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
