# Head of Agile Delivery Management - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of (Agile) delivery management, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At this level a candidate is an experienced practitioner who exemplifies what good looks like across the delivery roles: representing and championing the role across government and industry, leading the community of practice, being responsible for recruitment of the right people to the right teams, and being credible and influential across departments. The material is job-specific: it uses the artefacts a head of profession genuinely handles — workforce plans, capability strategies, cross-departmental governance papers, organisational design proposals, community health data, escalated commercial disputes, and budget envelopes spanning portfolios — rather than abstract puzzles.

This document covers four types of assessment, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — organisational pattern recognition, multi-constraint reasoning over structures and strategies, error checking of workforce and governance artefacts, prioritisation at head-of-role altitude, and problem solving where the system itself is the problem.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — workforce planning arithmetic, capability and recruitment funnels, portfolio budgets, community metrics, and organisational trend analysis.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — exact comprehension of policy, machinery-of-government text, cross-departmental agreements, and community communications.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — the dilemmas of organisational leadership: community, recruitment, cross-departmental influence, standards versus relationships, and stewardship of a profession.

Each section explains what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, maps its dimensions to the named skills in the role, gives a substantial set of practice questions from easy to challenging with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, then gives administration tips and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Offer any unscored practice questions the platform provides. At this level a candidate's reasoning, numbers, and judgement are treated as authoritative by an entire community, so several situational judgement items deliberately test second- and third-order thinking — what a response teaches the community watching, not only whether it resolves the immediate scenario. Score for that quality of reasoning, not only a single "correct" letter. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures reasoning with the actual material of the role. At head-of-profession level, the material is organisational: community capability data, workforce structures, cross-departmental dependency systems, governance architectures, and strategy documents. The cognitive operations remain pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving — but the objects they operate on are systems of teams and organisations rather than teams themselves.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, objectively scored against a norm group of comparable seniority, frequently adaptive, with accuracy and speed profiled separately. At this level the discriminating items are engineered around organisational reasoning traps: aggregation effects that reverse at different levels of analysis, structural explanations masquerading as individual ones, plausible strategies that fail a single quantitative check, and rules whose interactions produce conclusions no single rule states.

This kind of assessment matters at head-of level because the role's distinctive value is seeing what position-holders inside the system cannot: the organisational design flaw producing twelve teams' identical pattern, the funnel stage actually breaking a recruitment round, the constraint interaction that makes a cross-government proposal undeliverable as drafted. The assessment also measures restraint — knowing what data cannot support is tested as rigorously as knowing what it can, because conclusions at this level are repeated across departments with the candidate's authority attached.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to leading the community of practice and building capability and excellence: seeing systemic patterns across many teams' data is how a head of role knows what the community needs before individual teams do.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Team dynamics and collaboration** at head-of grade ("lead on strategy for an entire organisation") and **Planning** ("lead a continual planning process in a very complex environment"): strategy is sustained inference under organisational constraints.
- **Error checking** maps to **Financial management** and **Commercial management** at escalation grade: business cases, workforce plans, and large commercial positions reach this level to be checked by the person whose sign-off carries the profession's credibility.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the accountability for making and justifying decisions characterised by high levels of risk, impact and complexity: at head-of level, prioritisation is the allocation of finite attention and authority across an entire organisation's demands.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Making a process work** and changing organisational structures to fixable and sustainable designs: the problems that reach this level are ones where the system, not any actor within it, is the defect.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in community capability data**

An annual capability review covers 24 delivery managers across four directorates: A 9 of 10 "working at or above level"; B 5 of 6; C 2 of 5; D 2 of 3. Directorate C's programmes are also the department's most legacy-constrained, and C is the only directorate whose delivery managers have no senior delivery manager above them. What is the soundest first reading?

- A) Directorate C recruits weaker delivery managers; prioritise replacing them.
- B) C's out-of-line result (40% versus 80–90% elsewhere) coincides with two structural differences — hardest context and no senior support layer — so investigate structure before capability; the data cannot yet distinguish "weaker people" from "harder context, less support."
- C) The variation is random noise across small samples; do nothing.
- D) Directorates A and B are marking too generously.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The 40%-versus-80–90% gap coincides with two confounds any causal reading must pass through: the hardest delivery context and the only directorate lacking a coaching and calibration layer. This tests reading evidence at its actual strength — the signal warrants investigation while the confounds forbid attribution — rather than locating the problem in people (A, D) when it may be structural, or dismissing a gap this size as noise (C).

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an organisational design rule**

An operating model states: (i) every multidisciplinary product team must include a dedicated delivery manager; (ii) a senior delivery manager may cover at most two teams, and only where both teams are in the same service area; (iii) heads of role do not count as team delivery managers. Casework runs five product teams: three with dedicated DMs, one senior delivery manager covering two Casework teams. A reorganisation splits one covered team into two teams in different service areas. After the split, what is the minimum additional delivery management resource required?

- A) None; the senior delivery manager can stretch across three teams temporarily.
- B) One delivery manager: the split team's new out-of-area half needs its own cover, which the senior delivery manager cannot provide across service areas.
- C) Two delivery managers, one per post-split team.
- D) The model must be suspended during reorganisation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The SDM's coverage of the out-of-area half fails rule (ii)'s same-service-area condition, requiring one new DM there, while the Casework-resident half can stay legitimately covered (still two Casework teams). This tests running coverage rules against an org-chart change precisely — option A violates two rules and calls it pragmatism, and C overstates the gap that remains.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a workforce plan**

A draft workforce plan states: "Current establishment: 30 delivery management posts, 26 filled. Attrition assumption: 15% annually (4 leavers). Growth: 6 new posts funded from Q3. Recruitment target for the year: 8 hires, restoring full establishment by year end." What is wrong with the recruitment target?

- A) Nothing; 4 vacancies plus 4 leavers equals 8.
- B) The target ignores the 6 new posts: full establishment by year end is 36 posts, requiring 4 (current vacancies) + 4 (attrition) + 6 (growth) = 14 hires, not 8.
- C) The attrition assumption is too high for the civil service.
- D) The target double-counts leavers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Year-end establishment is 36 posts, and the target of 8 covers only the first two demand sources while silently omitting the growth the same paragraph announces. This tests reconciling a composite planning document's internal logic — the highest-yield error class in such documents, since each individual sentence is separately true.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation at head-of-role altitude**

Monday morning presents four demands: (1) a permanent secretary's office invites a capability strategy presentation to the executive committee in three weeks; (2) two senior delivery managers are in open, escalating conflict over ownership of a shared platform team, with their teams starting to take sides; (3) a cross-government working group needs review of a draft delivery standard by Friday, representing the profession's position; (4) a journalist has contacted the press office about an FOI-released document mentioning programme delays, needing a factual briefing by 15:00 today. Which allocation of the week is most defensible?

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4 — the executive committee opportunity outranks all noise.
- B) 4 today (bounded, deadlined, factual); begin 2 this week personally (escalating conflict between seniors is culturally corrosive and only the head of role can mediate it); protect real time for 3 by Friday; schedule dedicated preparation for 1 across the three weeks.
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4 — internal conflict always comes first.
- D) 4, 3, 1, 2 — external deadlines strictly before internal matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 4 is bounded and deadlined; item 2 has no deadline but a steepening cost curve since teams taking sides means the dispute is already propagating into the wider culture; item 3 carries representational weight beyond any one meeting; item 1 is the largest opportunity but the least urgent this week. This tests allocating attention by deadline reality and cost-curve steepness rather than by category (C, D) or dismissing organisational health and cross-government credibility as "noise" (A).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Aggregation reversal in delivery data**

Division X: last year 90 of 100 on time (90%); this year 28 of 30 (93.3%). Division Y: last year 10 of 20 (50%); this year 54 of 100 (54%). Both divisions improved. What happened to the organisation's overall on-time rate, and what is the lesson?

- A) It improved, since both divisions improved — component improvement guarantees aggregate improvement.
- B) It stayed flat at about 83%.
- C) It fell from 83.3% (100 of 120) to 63.1% (82 of 130): both divisions improved, yet the volume mix shifted heavily toward the lower-performing division, dragging the aggregate down — so aggregate and component trends can point in opposite directions, and an honest report states both levels.
- D) The comparison is invalid because volumes changed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both components rose, but the aggregate fell twenty points because volume share shifted heavily toward the weaker (though improving) division — a Simpson's paradox driven by mix. This tests recognising that component improvement does not guarantee aggregate improvement, and that a changed volume mix is the explanation to report, not a nuisance to exclude (D).

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction across funding and structure rules**

Rules for a proposed delivery hub: (i) at least 8 professionals must transfer at formation; (ii) no more than 2 may transfer from any single programme; (iii) programmes in their final quarter are exempt from rule (ii); (iv) formation must complete before the new financial year. Five programmes: P1 and P2 each willing to release 2; P3 willing to release 3 (capped at 2); P4, final quarter, willing to release 3; P5 willing to release 1. Can the hub form, and with what margin?

- A) No — only 7 can transfer.
- B) Yes — exactly 8: 2+2+2+1+1.
- C) Yes — 10 can transfer (2 from each of P1, P2, P3; 3 from P4; 1 from P5), a margin of 2 over the minimum.
- D) Cannot be determined without knowing the financial year date.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** P1, P2, P3 each contribute 2 (P3 capped); P4, exempt as final-quarter, contributes its full 3; P5 contributes 1: total 10. This tests applying caps and exemptions programme by programme rather than forgetting an exemption (A) or undercounting (B) — margin matters, since forming at exactly 8 leaves the hub one resignation from breaching its own founding condition.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits narrative at portfolio level**

A portfolio summary claims: "Our delivery profession's improvement programme has driven a 30% reduction in average time-to-live across the portfolio (from 40 weeks to 28), saving an estimated £2.4m annually. Additionally, the three programmes that adopted the new delivery playbook averaged 26 weeks, versus 31 weeks for non-adopters — evidence the playbook works. We recommend mandating the playbook portfolio-wide." Which is the most important analytical weakness?

- A) The 30% arithmetic is wrong.
- B) Playbook adoption was voluntary, so adopters are self-selected — likely the teams already most capable or least constrained — and the 26-versus-31 comparison cannot distinguish the playbook's effect from the adopters' pre-existing characteristics; the mandate recommendation rests on the weakest link.
- C) The £2.4m saving should be net of the improvement programme's costs.
- D) Time-to-live is the wrong metric for a delivery profession.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The 30% arithmetic is correct, and options C and D are fair but minor notes; the recommendation rests entirely on a self-selected comparison that cannot separate the playbook's effect from adopters' pre-existing characteristics. This tests spotting a self-selection confound in a recommendation that will otherwise carry the profession's credibility, especially where the analyst is invested in the finding.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Problem solving: the process nobody owns**

Every programme complains about a three-stage approvals pipeline (business case → spend control → commercial sign-off) averaging 9 weeks, of which each stage takes about 2 weeks of actual work — the other 3 weeks are hand-off queues, because the three approving teams report to three different directors, batch intakes monthly, and each requires the previous stage fully complete before opening a file. Individual escalations routinely compress single cases to 4 weeks, but the pipeline always reverts. What is the structurally correct intervention?

- A) Establish a permanent expedite route so important programmes can always escalate.
- B) Address the design, not the cases: propose to the three directors a joint redesign — aligned intake cadences (or continuous intake), parallel provisional review of near-final material, and a shared service-level target owned across the three teams — using the escalation data (4 weeks is achievable) as proof of feasible turnaround.
- C) Instruct programmes to submit everything earlier to absorb the 9 weeks.
- D) Publish a league table of the three teams' turnaround times to create pressure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Escalations succeed per case and always revert because they remove a case from the queue without changing the queue-generating design (monthly batching, serial completion, fragmented ownership). This tests intervening on each design choice through consensus among the owning directors, rather than institutionalising queue-jumping (A), hiding the cost in every plan (C), or applying pressure without redesign (D), which teams optimise around rather than fix.

**Question 9 (hard) — The strategy that fails its own arithmetic**

A draft profession strategy proposes reducing contractor reliance from 40% of 60 delivery posts to 10% within two years by converting posts. Permanent recruitment in this specialism runs at about 6 successful hires per year; permanent attrition is 10% annually. Setting aside the savings figure, is the headcount plan feasible on its own stated assumptions?

- A) Yes: 18 conversions over two years is well within reach.
- B) No: reaching 10% contractors requires the permanent base to grow from 36 to 54 (+18), while attrition costs roughly 4 permanents per year (about 8 over two years) — so total permanent hiring needed is about 26 against a stated capacity of 12; the plan exceeds its own recruitment assumption by more than double.
- C) Yes, if contractors convert directly without open competition.
- D) The plan is infeasible because attrition makes any growth impossible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Required permanent growth (18) plus attrition replacement (≈8) totals about 26 hires against a stated capacity of 12 over two years — the strategy fails its own arithmetic by roughly a factor of two. This tests testing a strategy's target against its own stated capacity rather than treating recruitment as unconstrained (A) or growth as impossible outright (D).

**Question 10 (hard) — Reading a cross-departmental commitment matrix**

Four departments sign a memorandum for a shared delivery capability. Commitments: A contributes 4 FTE, unconditional; B contributes 3 FTE, contingent on its spending review settlement; C contributes 2 FTE plus the hosting platform, but its FTE are committed elsewhere for the first two quarters; D contributes 3 FTE, unconditional. The capability needs 9 FTE from launch to be viable, launching next quarter. What is the true launch position?

- A) 12 FTE committed — comfortably viable.
- B) Launch-quarter firm commitment is 7 FTE (A's 4 + D's 3); B's 3 are conditional on an external event and C's 2 are unavailable for two quarters — the capability is 2 FTE short of viability at launch on firm commitments, and the memorandum's headline total conceals this.
- C) 9 FTE — exactly viable.
- D) The capability cannot launch for two quarters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Only A's and D's contributions are firm and available at launch (7 total); B is genuinely conditional and C is explicitly unavailable for two quarters. This tests distinguishing committed from firm-at-launch — the distinction memoranda are built to blur — and naming a solvable gap rather than reading the headline total at face value (A) or overcorrecting to impossibility (D).

**Question 11 (hard) — The reorganisation with a hidden single point of failure**

A reorganisation consolidates six product teams under three senior delivery managers (two teams each), distributes release-management duties from a dedicated function to the six teams, and creates one new platform team on which all six teams will depend for deployment infrastructure. Capacity modelling passes. What is the most important structural observation?

- A) The design is sound: capacity balances and the hierarchy is clean.
- B) Two changes interact dangerously: distributing release management removes the organisation's concentrated release expertise at exactly the moment every team's deployments come to depend on a single new platform team — creating a shared single point of failure operated by six teams that each hold only a fragment of the released function's knowledge. The design needs an explicit release-capability plan before implementation.
- C) Three senior delivery managers cannot manage six teams.
- D) Platform teams are an anti-pattern and the dependency should be removed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Each change passes a capacity check alone, but their interaction concentrates release-path dependency exactly as it scatters release expertise. This tests auditing the coupling between two individually defensible changes rather than each in isolation (A) — the signature failure of capacity-led reorganisation review — and supplying a repairable design rather than a slogan (D).

**Question 12 (hard) — Deciding with incomplete, asymmetric evidence**

A recommendation is due this week on whether to join a cross-government shared delivery-assurance service, replacing an internal function. Evidence: the shared service's two pilots report 20% cost reductions, self-reported and unpublished; the internal function's costs are precisely known and quality well regarded but unbenchmarked; joining is contractually reversible after two years, but rebuilding the internal function is estimated at 18 months; declining means the next joining window is in two years. What is the soundest reasoning?

- A) Join: a 20% saving across two pilots is compelling evidence.
- B) Decline: never trade a known-good function for unverified claims.
- C) The decision's structure matters more than either headline: the evidence is asymmetric (self-reported savings; unbenchmarked quality — neither case is proven) and reversibility is asymmetric too (contractual exit exists, but the 18-month rebuild means the practical option value of exit is far lower than the contract implies). The soundest recommendation buys information before surrendering irreversibility: negotiate a verified transition benchmark or partial-scope entry; failing that, decline and benchmark internally before the next window.
- D) Delay the recommendation until the shared service publishes its methodology.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both headline options select on unproven claims, and the pivotal asymmetry is that a disbanded function's practical reversibility is far lower than the contract's nominal exit clause suggests. This tests converting an underdetermined decision into an option-preserving structure — retained function through verified transition, or partial scope — rather than betting on someone else's press release (A), elevating incumbency to principle (B), or missing the stated deadline in the name of diligence (D).

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate diagnoses structure before people** when a performance pattern coincides with a known structural difference.
- **Watch for whether a candidate distinguishes committed from firm-at-launch** in a cross-organisational commitment matrix.
- **Note whether a candidate audits the coupling between two individually sound changes**, not just each in isolation.
- **Note whether a candidate names a solvable gap or fragility explicitly** rather than reading a headline total or a saturated plan at face value.
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that locates a problem in people** when the candidate owns and could fix the underlying structure.
- **Crediting an answer that accepts a headline total across organisations** without checking conditionality and timing.
- **Missing when a candidate audits components and misses their coupling** in an organisational design review.
- **Crediting an answer that lets advocacy-grade evidence carry decision-grade weight**, including the candidate's own side's claims.
- **Missing when a candidate treats nominal contractual reversibility as real** practical reversibility.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures fluency with the numbers this role genuinely handles — at head-of-profession level, numbers that describe organisations: workforce plans and recruitment funnels, pay and contractor cost structures, portfolio budgets and their variances, community capability metrics, and the trend arithmetic that underwrites strategy. The operations remain percentages, ratios, rates, weighted averages, and compounding, but the stimuli are organisational datasets and the traps are structural — wrong denominators across mixed populations, funnel stages confused, compounding mistaken for linearity, and headline figures that dissolve under decomposition.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 30 minutes, with 15 to 25 questions, calculator usually permitted. Expect multi-step items where selecting and structuring the right figures is the test and the arithmetic is merely the audit trail.

This kind of assessment matters at head-of level because these numbers allocate other people's futures: recruitment funnel arithmetic that is wrong under-hires for a year, a sloppy budget decomposition that loses a negotiation with finance, a misread compounding trend that writes a capability strategy for the wrong future.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Workforce and recruitment arithmetic** maps to the accountability for the recruitment of the right people to the right teams: establishment maths, attrition drag, funnel conversion rates, and time-to-fill are the quantitative core of building a community.
- **Budget and cost-structure calculations** map to **Financial management**: decomposing envelopes, projecting run rates, and pricing workforce mixes are negotiation instruments.
- **Commercial arithmetic at escalation grade** maps to **Commercial management**: the disputes that reach this level turn on numbers, and on teaching others to check them.
- **Community and capability metrics** map to leading the community of practice and supporting its professional development: participation rates, capability distributions, and progression data show whether the community is actually being built.
- **Trend and distribution analysis** maps to **Team dynamics and collaboration** at head-of grade and **Planning**: strategies stand on trends read correctly.
- **Presenting organisational numbers** maps to being credible and influential across departments: a profession's case is only as strong as its most checkable figure.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Community participation rate**

A community of practice has 85 members. Last month's three sessions drew attendances of 42, 38, and 47 (members may attend multiple sessions); unique attendees across the three sessions numbered 61. What percentage of the community attended at least one session?

- A) 49.8%
- B) 61%
- C) 71.8%
- D) 84.7%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 61 ÷ 85 ≈ 71.8%. This tests using the unique-attendee count rather than summing attendances (D) or misapplying the average attendance figure (A) — the distinction between average session reach and true community coverage changes the improvement conversation entirely.

**Question 2 (easy) — Establishment gap after growth**

Establishment grows from 48 to 60 posts next year; 44 posts are currently filled; expected attrition is 12.5% of current staff. How many hires are needed to reach full establishment by year end?

- A) 16
- B) 21 or 22 (16 for vacancies and growth, plus 5–6 attrition replacements)
- C) 12
- D) 27

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Vacancies (4) + growth (12) + attrition replacement (≈5.5) ≈ 21–22. This tests summing all three demand sources — options A and C each omit one — with a decomposed figure ("22 hires: 4 backfill, 12 growth, 6 attrition") surviving finance scrutiny far better than a bare total.

**Question 3 (easy) — Contractor cost differential**

10 contractors average £720/day (220 paid days/year); converting 6 to permanent posts at £82,000/year fully loaded. What is the gross annual saving from the 6 conversions?

- A) £158,400
- B) £458,400
- C) £492,000
- D) £950,400

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Six contractors cost £950,400/year; six permanents cost £492,000/year; gross saving £458,400. This tests carrying a multi-step calculation to its final comparison rather than stopping at an intermediate figure — and, for a strong candidate's answer, presenting gross, deductions, and net rather than a bare headline.

**Question 4 (easy) — Budget share under a cut**

A directorate's £8.4m budget faces a 7.5% proportional reduction; the delivery profession's current share is £1.6m. What is the profession's budget after the cut?

- A) £1.48m
- B) £1.52m
- C) £1.54m
- D) £1.6m — professions are usually protected

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £1.6m × 0.925 = £1.48m. Option D substitutes assumption for arithmetic. This tests computing the proportional baseline first — the figure to negotiate deviations from with a case, rather than assuming protection.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Recruitment funnel diagnosis**

A campaign: 240 applications → 60 passed sift (25%) → 36 attended interview (60% of sifted) → 9 passed interview (25% of interviewed) → 6 accepted offers (67% of passes). Sector benchmarks: sift 30%, attendance 85%, interview pass 45%, acceptance 80%. Which stage most deviates from benchmark, and what does it suggest?

- A) The sift (25% vs 30%) — advertise more.
- B) Interview attendance (60% vs 85%) — a quarter of sifted candidates are lost before interview, suggesting slow scheduling or better competing offers; fix speed and candidate contact.
- C) Interview pass (25% vs 45%) — either interviewing is miscalibrated or the sift passes weak candidates; investigate jointly with attendance.
- D) Offer acceptance (67% vs 80%) — improve the offer.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Computing each stage as a ratio of its benchmark (0.83, 0.71, 0.556, 0.84) shows interview pass rate as the largest shortfall, with attendance second and linked to it. This tests computing every stage's deviation before choosing rather than accepting the most persuasively framed distractor (B), which is genuinely worth fixing but is not the largest deviation.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Weighted capability score**

Capability review results: 3 scored 4; 10 scored 3; 8 scored 2; 3 scored 1. What is the mean capability score, and what share of the community scores 3 or above?

- A) Mean 2.5; 50% at 3+
- B) Mean 2.54; 54.2% at 3+
- C) Mean 2.63; 54.2% at 3+
- D) Mean 2.54; 62.5% at 3+

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weighted sum 61 ÷ 24 ≈ 2.54; 13 of 24 at 3+ ≈ 54.2%. This tests verifying both components of a paired figure independently, since a familiar-looking correct number in one part can mask an error in the other — and the pair together (46% below level 3) points development investment toward the largest movable cohort.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Portfolio budget variance triage**

Four programmes: Alpha budget £2.0m, forecast £2.3m; Bravo £3.5m, forecast £3.4m; Charlie £1.2m, forecast £1.5m; Delta £4.8m, forecast £4.9m. Which programme has the largest percentage overspend, which the largest absolute overspend, and what is the portfolio's net position?

- A) Charlie largest by percentage (+25%); Alpha and Charlie tie in absolute (+£0.3m each); portfolio net +£0.6m over.
- B) Alpha largest by percentage (+15%); Charlie largest absolute; portfolio net +£0.5m over.
- C) Delta largest on both measures; portfolio net +£0.7m over.
- D) Charlie largest by percentage (+25%); Delta largest absolute (+£0.1m); portfolio balanced.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Charlie's +25% is the largest percentage variance; Alpha and Charlie tie at +£0.3m absolute; portfolio net (accounting for Bravo's underspend) is +£0.6m. This tests computing variance both ways, since percentage variance finds the weakest control and absolute variance finds where the money is — different findings requiring different management actions per programme.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Time-to-fill and its cost**

Average time-to-fill is 14 weeks; each vacant week costs £1,900 in contractor cover premium. A recruitment improvement costing £30,000/year is projected to cut time-to-fill to 9 weeks, with 8 vacancies arising per year. Does the investment pay back, and by how much?

- A) No: it costs £30,000 and saves £15,200.
- B) Yes: saving = 5 weeks × £1,900 × 8 vacancies = £76,000 per year, against £30,000 — net benefit £46,000, a 2.5:1 return.
- C) Yes: saving = £1,900 × 9 × 8 = £136,800 net.
- D) Break-even, roughly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 5 weeks saved × £1,900 × 8 vacancies = £76,000; minus £30,000 cost = £46,000 net. This tests valuing the reduction (5 weeks), not the remainder (9 weeks, option C's error), and, for a strong candidate's answer, stating the break-even sensitivity to strengthen the investment case.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Compounding attrition of community knowledge**

A 40-member profession has 15% annual attrition, each leaver replaced. Roughly what fraction of today's members remain in three years, and what does this imply?

- A) 55% remain (100% − 3×15%); knowledge is safe in people's heads.
- B) About 61% remain (0.85³ = 0.614); roughly 4 in 10 of today's members — and their tacit knowledge — will be gone within three years, so community knowledge must be institutionalised (playbooks, communities, documented practice) rather than held in individuals.
- C) 85% remain; attrition only applies once.
- D) 45% remain; attrition compounds upward each year.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Retention compounds multiplicatively: 0.85³ ≈ 61%. This tests recognising that attrition compounds rather than subtracting linearly (A), and using the result as a quantitative case for institutionalising knowledge rather than trusting it to survive in individuals.

**Question 10 (hard) — Decomposing a pay-gap headline**

A headline states: "Contractors earn 2.1 times the daily cost of permanent staff." Contractors average £760/day. Permanent daily cost was computed as £52,000 ÷ 260 working days = £200/day, then footnoted "£360/day fully loaded" using a 1.8 loading factor. Which is the defensible comparison, and what is the true ratio?

- A) 2.1:1 as headlined — £760 versus £360 is close enough.
- B) £760 ÷ £360 = 2.11, so the headline is right and defensible.
- C) Neither: the comparison also needs alignment of the day bases (contractors are paid per worked day, typically ~220/year; permanents' £52,000 spreads across ~220 productive days too, not 260 calendar working days) — on aligned bases, permanent fully loaded ≈ £52,000 × 1.8 ÷ 220 ≈ £425/day, giving a true ratio of about 1.8:1. Still material, but the headline overstates by inflating the permanent denominator with non-productive days.
- D) The ratio cannot be computed without knowing contractor pension arrangements.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Aligning both sides to the same productive-day basis (≈220 days) gives a true ratio of about 1.8:1, not 2.1:1. This tests auditing both numerator and denominator for basis alignment before comparing per-unit figures — a headline that overstates its own valid core discredits the whole case once challenged.

**Question 11 (hard) — The savings target and the double count**

An efficiency programme requests £900k of savings over two years. A draft return lists: (1) contractor conversions, £458k/year from year 1; (2) recruitment improvement net benefit, £46k/year; (3) reduced time-to-fill "saving £76k/year in contractor cover" (the same £76k already inside item 2's net figure); (4) shared assurance service joining saving, £120k/year from year 2, contingent on an unapproved business case. What is the defensible two-year savings total?

- A) £1,404k — sum everything across two years.
- B) £1,008k: items 1 and 2 across two years (916 + 92), excluding item 3 (already inside item 2's net figure) and item 4 (contingent on an unapproved case) — with item 4 listed separately as pipeline, not banked savings.
- C) £1,160k — include item 4 because the business case will probably pass.
- D) £916k — only item 1 is real.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 3 double-counts item 2's gross component; item 4 is contingent and unbanked. This tests auditing a savings list for double-counting and contingency before summing — a defensible £1,008k that exceeds the £900k target with disclosed pipeline in reserve is more valuable, and more honest, than an inflated £1,404k.

**Question 12 (hard) — Choosing the honest trend story**

Applications over five campaigns: 310, 265, 228, 196, 170. A draft states: "Applications have fallen by 28 per campaign on average; at this rate we will have ~86 applications in three more campaigns and zero within six." What is the soundest analytical response?

- A) Endorse it: the average decline is (310−170) ÷ 5 = 28 per campaign, and linear projection is standard practice.
- B) The decline is better described as multiplicative — each campaign draws about 86% of the previous — so the honest projection is decay toward a floor (~108 after three more campaigns), not a march to zero; and either way the projection is secondary to diagnosing the cause of a five-campaign 45% decline in the profession's applicant pool.
- C) Both descriptions are equally valid; choose whichever supports the funding case better.
- D) Four data points of decline are too few to say anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Successive ratios are nearly constant (≈0.86), the signature of multiplicative decay, while successive differences shrink — a pattern linear decline cannot produce, and the draft's own average is miscalculated (dividing by 5 intervals when there are 4). This tests choosing the model the data actually proposes and treating diagnosis of the underlying cause as more important than the extrapolation method, rather than endorsing a refuted model (A) or selecting a model by advocacy (C).

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate decomposes a headline figure into its components** before repeating it.
- **Watch for whether a candidate sums all demand sources** in workforce arithmetic — vacancies, growth, and attrition together.
- **Note whether a candidate computes both absolute and percentage variance**, recognising they identify different problems.
- **Note whether a candidate tests a trend as both differences and ratios** before choosing a projection model.
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer built on mismatched bases** in a comparison — per-day, per-head, or per-campaign figures computed on different denominators.
- **Crediting a linear projection of a multiplicative process**, or its cousin, linearly subtracting compound attrition.
- **Missing when a candidate chooses the persuasively framed stage over the most-deviated one** in a funnel or variance diagnosis.
- **Crediting an answer that banks contingent money** or double-counts a gross figure already inside a net one.
- **Missing when a candidate selects an analytical model by advocacy** rather than by what the data actually supports.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures exactness in extracting, testing, and weighing written meaning — using the text classes a head of profession actually confronts: policy and spend-control instruments, machinery-of-government correspondence, cross-departmental memoranda, workforce and pay guidance, assessment and audit reports, and community communications. Passages are presented; questions probe what the text states, implies, permits, requires, and cannot settle.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions, with senior variants distinguished by layered conditions, deliberately hedged drafting, and institutional voice.

This kind of assessment matters at head-of level because reading at this altitude is terminal: what is concluded from a policy instrument or an audit finding becomes the profession's operating interpretation — repeated in the candidate's voice at community events and relied on by every delivery manager who assumes the head of role read the source.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Exact comprehension of instruments** maps to credibility and influence across departments: the head of role who reads policy, spend controls, and workforce frameworks exactly is the one other departments consult rather than correct.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to representing and championing the role across government and industry: public statements about what a standard or policy requires must be verifiable against the source.
- **Inference calibration** maps to **Team dynamics and collaboration** at head-of grade: high-stakes decisions rest on documents that state less than the decision needs.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to **Financial management** and leading strategy: cases for funding, reorganisation, or joining shared services are arguments whose evidence-to-conclusion gaps this level is the last position to catch.
- **Summary fidelity and clear writing** map to leading the community of practice and communicating the value of the role: compression without distortion is a daily instrument.
- **Reading advocacy fairly** maps to building consensus between organisations and highly independent stakeholders: consensus is built by a reader who states each party's position at its actual strength.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–4)*

"Digital and technology spend proposals exceeding £1m must receive central approval before commitment. Proposals between £100,000 and £1m may be approved departmentally, provided the department holds a current delegated approval agreement and the proposal is recorded in the quarterly return. Delegated approval agreements are granted for twelve months and may be revoked at any time where the centre judges departmental controls inadequate. Contract extensions are treated as new proposals at the value of the extension, except where the original approval explicitly covered the extension option, in which case no further approval is required. Nothing in this instrument removes a department's obligations under procurement law."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "A £750,000 proposal in a department with a current delegated approval agreement requires central approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because all digital spend needs central approval

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** £750,000 sits in the delegated band, and the department holds a current agreement, so central approval is not required. This tests locating the governing threshold band precisely rather than applying a blanket rule (D) the instrument's structure exists to replace.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: "A £400,000 contract extension always requires a new approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because extensions are treated as new proposals

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An explicit exception exists where the original approval covered the extension option — the statement's "always" collides with the instrument's own "except." This tests catching a universal quantifier defeated by a stated exception, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting why well-drafted original approvals name their extension options.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A department whose delegated approval agreement is revoked loses its ability to approve proposals under £100,000."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because revocation removes all delegation

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The instrument governs the over-£1m and £100,000–£1m bands only; it says nothing about sub-£100,000 spend. This tests reading regulatory silence as silence — neither permission nor prohibition — rather than extending a rule's effect into a domain it never addressed.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strongest inference**

Which is best supported by the instrument?

- A) The centre distrusts departments.
- B) The instrument is designed to make delegation conditional and reversible: time-limited agreements, revocation on judged inadequacy, and mandatory recording all keep departmental approval under continuing central oversight rather than granting it outright.
- C) Most departments have inadequate controls.
- D) Procurement law takes precedence over this instrument.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Twelve-month grants, at-any-time revocation, and quarterly recording together form the architecture of conditional, reversible delegation. This tests design-level inference — reading what a text's machinery accomplishes — over unsupported motive (A), an unsupported factual distribution (C), or a misread non-interference clause as a hierarchy claim (D).

*Passage 2 (for Questions 5–8)*

"This memorandum records the agreement between the four participating departments to establish a joint delivery profession curriculum. Each department will nominate two members to the curriculum board by 30 September. The board will agree the core syllabus by December; departments may supplement the core with local modules, provided local modules do not duplicate or contradict core content. Funding of £160,000 for the first year will be met equally by the participating departments, subject to each department's internal approvals. Should any department withdraw before the first cohort completes, its funding share becomes payable in full unless withdrawal results from a machinery of government change. The curriculum will be reviewed after the first cohort, and continuation requires the agreement of at least three departments."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Each participating department will contribute £40,000 in the first year, subject to its internal approvals."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, and the contribution is unconditional

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £160,000 ÷ 4 departments = £40,000, and the statement correctly carries the text's own qualifier. Option D strips the qualifier the memorandum makes explicit. This tests keeping a financial commitment's condition attached when relaying it — the qualifier is part of the number.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "A department that withdraws after the first cohort completes must still pay its full funding share."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because withdrawal is not permitted after the first cohort

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The full-payment consequence attaches only to withdrawal before cohort completion; the memorandum is silent about withdrawal afterward. This tests reading a clause's exact temporal scope and refusing to extend it by inference, and recognising that a penalty for early action does not imply a prohibition on later action.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Applying a compound condition**

A local module proposal covers ground the core syllabus addresses in an introductory module, but at substantially greater depth and with different case material. Is the local module permitted?

- A) Yes, clearly — greater depth is not duplication.
- B) No, clearly — the topic already exists in the core.
- C) The memorandum's test is duplication or contradiction, and an advanced treatment of a core topic sits in the test's unresolved middle ground: it plausibly neither duplicates (different depth, different material) nor contradicts, but "duplicate" is undefined — so the defensible route is a board determination, which the curriculum board exists to give.
- D) Yes, because departments may supplement the core without any restriction.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Whether "duplicate" means same topic or same content is genuinely ambiguous on the text. This tests identifying a test the passage states, applying it as far as it goes, naming the residual ambiguity honestly, and routing the determination to the body constituted to resolve it — rather than resolving genuine ambiguity by fiat in either direction (A, B).

**Question 8 (moderate) — Summary for a community**

Which summary of the memorandum for a community is most faithful and most useful?

- A) "We've joined a cross-government curriculum — details to follow."
- B) "Four departments are jointly building a delivery curriculum: core syllabus agreed by a joint board (our two nominees by 30 September), local additions allowed if they don't duplicate or contradict core, £40k first-year contribution pending internal approval, early withdrawal carries full funding liability, and continuation after cohort one needs three of four departments. What this means for you: nominations open now; module suggestions to the board via me."
- C) "We're locked in for £40k with penalties if we leave, and a committee will decide what we teach."
- D) "A new curriculum is coming which will replace all local training."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B carries every operative element, the money's qualifier, and the reader's next step. Option C is factually recoverable but framed as grievance; option D asserts a replacement the memorandum expressly contradicts. This tests fidelity of both content and tone when compressing a document for a community, since the community meets the outcome with the stance the summary models.

*Passage 3 (for Questions 9–10)*

"The audit examined delivery governance across the department's twelve major programmes. Ten programmes evidenced regular, minuted governance with appropriate senior attendance. In two programmes, governance boards had not met for over a quarter, and decisions traceable in correspondence had been taken outside any minuted forum. The audit notes that both programmes had recently lost their senior responsible owners and that interim arrangements were unclear. The audit rates delivery governance as 'moderate' overall and makes two recommendations: first, that interim SRO arrangements be documented within ten working days of any SRO departure; second, that the head of delivery management profession review whether delivery managers in the two affected programmes escalated the governance lapses appropriately."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: "The audit found that delivery managers in the two affected programmes failed to escalate the governance lapses."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, since the audit ordered a review of their escalation

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A commissioned review of whether escalation happened appropriately is not a finding that it failed. This tests distinguishing a commissioned question from a concluded finding — relaying "the audit found they failed" pre-convicts colleagues before the review the candidate is asked to conduct even begins.

**Question 10 (hard) — Weighing the finding fairly**

Which reading of the audit extract best reflects its evidential weight?

- A) Governance is failing across the department: the audit rated it only "moderate."
- B) Ten of twelve programmes evidence sound governance; the two lapses cluster with a specific, named structural trigger (SRO departure with unclear interim arrangements), which the first recommendation directly addresses — so the finding primarily indicts the succession gap, and secondarily asks (without concluding) whether the profession's escalation practice caught the lapses as it should.
- C) The audit exonerates delivery managers, since the lapses were caused by SRO departures.
- D) The "moderate" rating shows the audit was lenient; the true position is worse.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Ten of twelve programmes are sound, and the two lapses cluster with a named structural cause the first recommendation addresses directly. This tests weighing a majority finding fairly against its exceptions, matching remedy to mechanism, and holding a genuinely open question open — rather than generalising from exceptions (A), converting a structural cause into exoneration (C), or second-guessing a rating with no evidence (D).

**Question 11 (hard) — Argument evaluation: the consolidation case**

A paper argues: "Five departments each run separate delivery management professions with separate standards, curricula, and capability frameworks. This quintuplication wastes effort and confuses suppliers. A single cross-government delivery profession, centrally governed, would eliminate duplication, give suppliers one interface, and raise standards to the level of the best. We estimate £600k annual savings. The five heads of profession have been consulted and none produced evidence that departmental differences add value. We recommend the board approve consolidation." Which is the most incisive critical assessment?

- A) The argument is sound: duplication is waste, and no evidence for local value was produced.
- B) The savings estimate needs an auditable basis, and beyond it the argument has two structural gaps: it assumes uniformity raises standards to the best (centralisation can equally lock in the average, and the mechanism selecting "the best" is unspecified), and it treats "no evidence produced that differences add value" as evidence of no value — an absence-of-evidence move, weighty only if a genuine search for such evidence was conducted, which the paper does not describe. The recommendation may still be right; the case as written does not yet establish it.
- C) The argument fails because cross-government initiatives never work.
- D) The argument fails because the heads of profession are conflicted and their consultation should be discounted.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The savings figure is unaudited, the standards claim assumes an unspecified selection mechanism, and the consultation claim shifts a burden of proof without describing any search that would carry it. This tests grading each of an argument's load-bearing joints separately and distinguishing "the case is not yet made" from "the conclusion is wrong" — rather than accepting a burden-shift at face value (A), using a slogan (C), or discounting interested testimony by interest alone (D).

**Question 12 (hard) — True, false, or cannot say across instrument and practice**

Text 1 (policy): "Fixed-term appointments may be extended once, for up to twelve months, where a business case is approved by the departmental workforce board. Second extensions require Cabinet Office consent." Text 2 (email): "Re your delivery manager on the fixed-term contract: good news, the workforce board approved the business case, so we can extend her again — this is the second extension, but board approval covers it since the first extension was only six months." Statement: "The proposed extension complies with the policy as stated."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the workforce board approved the business case

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The policy's structure is ordinal, not cumulative — a second extension requires Cabinet Office consent regardless of how much of the first extension's twelve months was used. This tests refusing an email's invented mitigation and reading the instrument's rule precisely — the email's confident misreading from a domain professional is exactly the kind a head of role must correct with the text, not defer to on authority.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads quantifiers and boundaries as load-bearing** — always/never, before/after, first/second, once.
- **Watch for whether a candidate distinguishes text that answers a question, text that is silent, and text that is ambiguous**, applying the correct of the three responses to each.
- **Note whether a candidate separates a commissioned question from a concluded finding.**
- **Note whether a candidate grades an argument's individual claims separately** rather than accepting or rejecting it as a whole.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that extends a clause beyond its stated scope** — temporal, population, or value boundaries.
- **Missing when a candidate resolves textual silence by instinct** rather than naming it as silence.
- **Crediting an answer that treats a commissioned review as an adverse finding.**
- **Rewarding an answer that accepts authority over the text** when a colleague's confident reading conflicts with the instrument.
- **Missing when a candidate discounts interested testimony wholesale**, or accepts a burden-shift in an argument uncritically.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic role scenarios and asks a candidate to identify the most effective response, the least effective, best-and-worst pairs, or to rate every option. Scoring reflects expert consensus on effective professional behaviour, calibrated to the role's seniority — and at head-of-profession level, the scenarios are about stewarding a profession: recruitment and succession, community health, standards versus relationships, cross-departmental influence, and the exercise of authority where every choice is also a precedent.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 20 to 35 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, generously timed. Expect dilemmas with no clean option, senior counterparties, and second- and third-order consequences that outweigh the first-order ones.

This kind of assessment matters at head-of level because a candidate at this level exemplifies what good looks like across the delivery roles — responses are the community's reference implementations. The recurring test beneath the tests is whether a candidate can hold two goods simultaneously — standards and relationships, innovation and guardrails, care for individuals and duty to the community — without sacrificing either.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Community stewardship scenarios** map to leading the community of practice, building capability and excellence, and supporting professional development: whether responses grow the community or merely manage it.
- **Recruitment and succession scenarios** map to the responsibility for the recruitment of the right people to the right teams.
- **Standards-versus-relationships scenarios** map to exemplifying what good looks like and championing the role across government and industry: what is tolerated becomes the standard.
- **Cross-organisational scenarios** map to **Team dynamics and collaboration** at head-of grade: building consensus between organisations or highly independent stakeholders.
- **Commercial escalation scenarios** map to **Commercial management**: acting as the escalation point for large or high-risk commercial issues, and coaching others.
- **Conflict and mediation scenarios** map to understanding team psychology, strong mediation skills, and coaching an organisation on conflict resolution.
- **Organisational design scenarios** map to changing organisational structures to fixable and sustainable designs.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — The community star and the community standard**

A superb, popular senior delivery manager has for months been dismissive in community sessions toward less experienced members — interrupting, correcting with visible impatience, once describing a junior's question as "the kind of thing the framework exists so we don't have to discuss." Attendance by early-career members is declining. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept it as the cost of brilliance; the community benefits from their expertise overall.
- B) Address it privately, directly, and specifically — the observed behaviours, the attendance data, the standard expected of senior members in the community — while affirming their value; make clear the visibility that makes them valuable is exactly what makes the behaviour costly; and follow through with structural moves (junior-friendly formats, rotating speaking roles) that rebalance the room regardless.
- C) Stop inviting them to community events until the behaviour improves.
- D) Raise it openly at the next community session so everyone hears the standard.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A community whose juniors stop attending has stopped building capability — the accountability the role exists for. Option B pairs a direct, evidence-borne private conversation with structural moves that fix the room regardless of the outcome with one individual. Option A prices brilliance wrongly; C punishes without conversation; D humiliates publicly. This tests correcting a visible senior person the way that models how leaders should be corrected: privately, specifically, with the standard stated.

**Question 2 (easy) — The recruitment shortcut**

A director urgently needs a delivery manager for a politically sensitive programme and asks for their preferred candidate — a capable contractor already on the programme — to be appointed directly into the vacant permanent post, "given the urgency, we can regularise it later." Fair-and-open competition rules apply. What is the most effective response?

- A) Appoint as asked; urgency is real and the candidate is genuinely capable.
- B) Refuse and report the director's request to HR as an attempted breach.
- C) Hold the line on the rules while solving the director's actual problem: the post must be filled by fair and open competition (in which the contractor may well compete and win), and separately offer legitimate speed — an expedited campaign, interim cover arrangements, or the contractor continuing in role via the proper commercial route until the competition concludes.
- D) Appoint the contractor on a fixed-term basis to avoid the rules applying.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The director's need (continuity and speed) is legitimate; the proposed means (bypassing fair-and-open competition) is not. Option C refuses the means and serves the need, usually dissolving the pressure entirely since the preferred candidate can compete and often wins fairly. This tests separating what is being asked for from how it is being asked, and finding a route where "the right way" and "the fast way" are the same way.

**Question 3 (easy) — The failing delivery manager nobody warned**

A delivery manager rated "partially meets" this year had received "exceeds" ratings for two prior years, while performance concerns were well known and never documented or raised with the individual, who now feels ambushed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Stand by the accurate rating; the truth had to land eventually.
- B) Soften the rating back toward "meets" to be fair about the ambush.
- C) Keep the honest rating but own the institutional failure that surrounds it: acknowledge explicitly to the individual that the profession failed to give them the feedback they were owed, build a properly supported development plan with realistic time to respond, and separately address the line management failure — the manager who rated "exceeds" over known concerns — as its own performance and coaching issue.
- D) Quietly move the delivery manager to a less demanding team.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rating is accurate and must stand, but the ambush was the system's failure, not the individual's. Option C holds both truths and addresses the line manager whose kind ratings over known concerns manufactured the case. Option B re-falsifies the record; A delivers truth without justice; D relocates the problem unexplained. This tests treating feedback-culture failures as seriously as performance failures.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The cross-government working group stalemate**

Co-chairing a group drafting a shared delivery standard, four departments support the draft; the largest — whose adoption matters most — objects through its head of delivery, arguing the assurance cadence is unworkable at their scale, and has stopped attending, sending a junior observer instead. Publication is six weeks away. What is the most effective response?

- A) Publish on schedule with four supporters; the fifth will follow eventually.
- B) Go direct to the counterpart, one-to-one: understand the objection's substance (scale concerns from the largest department are plausibly informative, not merely obstructive), explore whether a tiered or scale-adjusted cadence preserves the standard's intent while resolving their constraint, and re-engage them as co-author of the amendment rather than dissenter from the draft — using the deadline as shared pressure, moving it only if a genuinely better standard needs the time.
- C) Escalate to the counterpart's director general, noting the disengagement.
- D) Water the assurance cadence down to whatever the objecting department will accept; adoption is everything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Disengagement signals a conclusion that the forum will not move; direct, substantive engagement reopens it. Separating the standard's intent from its mechanism and offering co-authorship is the consensus-builder's core move. Option A publishes a "shared" standard the most consequential department has not agreed to; C escalates over a peer's head before the merits conversation has failed; D purchases adoption with the standard's substance. This tests building consensus with a highly independent stakeholder through substance first, mechanism flexible, authorship shared.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The commercial escalation with a personal edge**

A £3m supplier dispute has escalated: the supplier claims scope creep and demands £400k; a highly rated senior delivery manager who managed the contract insists the work was in scope and is visibly invested in "winning." The record is genuinely mixed — informal change-handling, some extra work plausibly ordered by emails, poor documentation both sides. Commercial colleagues advise a negotiated settlement around £150–200k is achievable and defensible. What is the most effective response?

- A) Back the senior delivery manager fully; conceding anything undermines them and invites future claims.
- B) Settle in the advised range, and handle the senior delivery manager's part in it separately and honestly: walk the record with them, distinguish loyalty to them from endorsement of a weak position, frame the informal change-handling as the real lesson — and follow through with contract-management coaching for them and the wider community, since the failure pattern is rarely unique to one contract.
- C) Fight the claim to demonstrate the department cannot be pushed around, whatever the record shows.
- D) Settle quietly and tell the senior delivery manager it was purely a commercial decision with no reflection on their management.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The record, not morale, should decide the settlement; the separate honest conversation preserves the relationship while converting an expensive dispute into a curriculum lesson for the wider community. A spends relationship and money protecting a colleague from needed feedback; C litigates a weak file for reputation; D lies about the settlement's rationale, learning nothing. This tests resolving a large commercial issue on the merits while coaching from the case rather than concealing it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The reorganisation inherited mid-flight**

Arriving as head of delivery management, a predecessor's half-implemented reorganisation — moving delivery managers from stable team assignments into a central pool allocated quarterly — shows discouraging evidence three moves in: two strong delivery managers have resigned citing churn, programme leaders complain of lost continuity, and promised flexibility benefits have not appeared. The design has visible executive sponsorship. What is the most effective response?

- A) Complete the implementation as designed; reversing course three moves in looks chaotic and disloyal.
- B) Reverse everything immediately and publicly restore the old model.
- C) Pause further moves on the evidence, evaluate honestly — what problem was the pool solving, is that problem real, has the design's cost (attrition, continuity loss) exceeded its benefit — and take a revised recommendation to the executive sponsor: amend, complete, or unwind, with the evidence attached; protect the people mid-move either way.
- D) Continue the moves but slow the schedule, letting the design quietly fade without a decision.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** An evidence-triggered pause, honest evaluation of the underlying problem, and a recommendation with options to the sponsor honours the sponsor's intent while fixing the mechanism. A implements loyalty to a plan over its purpose; B reverses without evaluation; D leaves an undecided organisation indefinitely. This tests sustainable decision-making about organisational design, not just sustainable design itself.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The conference invitation and the vendor's shadow**

A major commercial conference invites a keynote on government delivery transformation; the conference's headline sponsor is a supplier currently shortlisted in a live procurement, and the sponsor's marketing team asks for a "fireside chat" on their stand and a quote in post-event materials. What is the most effective response?

- A) Decline the keynote entirely; the risk is not worth it.
- B) Keynote as invited — championing the role in industry is the job — but decline the sponsor's stand appearance and quotation outright, notify the commercial team of the contact given the live procurement, and have the talk's content reviewed against the procurement's boundaries so nothing said could be read as signalling.
- C) Do the keynote and the fireside chat; refusing the sponsor would be rude, and everything is public anyway.
- D) Ask the sponsor to make a written commitment that the appearance will not influence the procurement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The keynote is the role performed in public; the sponsor's asks are marketing material with procurement implications that would hand implied favour to a shortlisted bidder for zero professional benefit. Option A abandons a genuine duty; C's "everything is public anyway" is precisely why the endorsement is dangerous; D asks the beneficiary of an impropriety to certify its absence. This tests distinguishing visibility, which is the job, from capture, which is the risk.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The community survey a candidate commissioned**

A first community survey shows strong scores on pride and mission, poor scores on progression and workload sustainability in two directorates, and "leadership listens" at 38% favourable, with many raw written comments. A people team suggests presenting positives prominently and handling the rest through line management. What is the most effective response?

- A) Follow the people team's advice; morale needs protecting and line management owns the issues.
- B) Publish the results to the community in full candour — strengths and failures, including the 38% — commit to two or three specific, visible actions with dates (a progression framework; a workload review in the two named directorates), explicitly not promising what cannot be delivered, and report back on those actions on schedule, making the survey-to-action loop the community's new expectation.
- C) Re-run the survey with better-designed questions before acting; the raw comments suggest methodological noise.
- D) Publish everything and invite the community to self-organise solutions; imposed fixes would contradict the listening message.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A community doubting leadership listens is watching exactly how this survey is handled. Full candour, selective and dated commitments, and a scheduled report-back converts "listening" from sentiment into mechanism. A performs the exact behaviour the 38% predicted; C defers findings under cover of methodology; D abdicates ownership of problems only leadership can fix. This tests closing the survey-to-action loop visibly rather than managing perception of the results.

**Question 9 (hard) — The head-of-peer whose team is failing the candidate's people**

Delivery managers report a pattern from a fellow head of profession's (product) community: exclusion from planning decisions, mid-sprint re-scoping without consultation, and two documented cases of delivery managers' concerns being misrepresented to programme boards. The peer is well regarded, defensive about their community, and sits with the candidate on the departmental digital leadership team. What is the most effective response?

- A) Raise it peer-to-peer with the head of product, evidence in hand, framed as a joint professional-practice problem — "our two communities' operating pattern is failing in these specific ways; let's fix the interface together" — proposing joint interventions and agreeing how the two documented misrepresentation cases will be addressed; escalate to the digital leadership team only if the peer route fails.
- B) Coach the delivery managers to assert themselves; fighting their battles infantilises them.
- C) Raise the pattern at the digital leadership team first, so it is on the record before any private conversation.
- D) Issue guidance to the community documenting how to escalate product-manager overreach, creating a paper trail.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Framing the issue as a jointly owned interface failure gives a defensive peer room to engage without conceding their community is bad, while the two documented misrepresentation cases still get explicit handling as conduct, not culture. Option B evades a structural pattern individuals cannot coach their way out of; C escalates before conversing; D institutionalises conflict as a first resort. This tests solving an interdepartmental issue at the highest level through the sequence: evidence, peer conversation, joint fix, escalation last.

**Question 10 (hard) — The minister's office and the delivery truth**

A flagship service is six weeks from a publicly committed launch date; the honest assessment based on delivery teams' evidence is a high probability of visible service failure at launch, while a six-week delay would allow fixes. The programme's SRO, under pressure from the minister's office which has briefed the date to media, asks the candidate to "get the teams to find a way," hinting that a head of profession who cannot deliver a date might be the story instead. What is the most effective response?

- A) Get the teams to find a way; ministerial commitments outrank delivery preferences, and the role depends on being seen to deliver.
- B) Give the SRO the professional assessment in writing — the specific failure probabilities, what "finding a way" would actually require and cost, and the launch-shaping options that genuinely exist (phased launch, reduced-scope launch, soft launch with controlled volumes, or six weeks) — and make clear that whichever informed choice the accountable owners make will be supported, while declining to alter the assessment itself; treat the personal hint as noise, not input.
- C) Go around the SRO to the permanent secretary with concerns about political interference.
- D) Publicly align with the launch date while privately telling the delivery managers to document everything for the inevitable inquiry.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The launch decision belongs to accountable owners; the assessment of its risk belongs to the profession, and altering it under pressure destroys its value to everyone, including the minister. Written, options-rich delivery of the assessment, with support for whichever informed choice follows, protects both boundaries. A corrupts the assessment function into an amplifier of pressure; C escalates before the SRO has actually overridden anything; D is public assent with private dissent and an inquiry trail prepared for a failure being simultaneously endorsed. This tests knowing precisely which decisions belong to the candidate and which do not.

**Question 11 (hard) — The succession nobody wants to need**

Confidential information suggests being asked to lead a two-year cross-government programme within six months, leaving the head-of post. Neither of the two most senior delivery managers is ready — one avoids the political layer, the other has never run the community or recruitment machinery — and no succession plan exists on paper, with institutional knowledge living substantially in the candidate. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing until the move is confirmed; premature succession talk destabilises, and the move may not happen.
- B) Start succession work now, framed as institutional resilience rather than departure planning (which is simply true — heads of profession should always be one bus away from continuity): document the role's machinery, deliberately develop both candidates against their specific gaps, create structures that hold knowledge institutionally (deputies, documented processes, a community leadership group), and brief the candidate's own director on the resilience work without disclosing the unconfirmed move.
- C) Pick a preferred successor now and begin transferring the role to them quietly.
- D) Recommend the department recruit an external successor, since neither internal candidate is ready.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A profession dependent on one person's continued presence is an organisational design flaw whether or not that person leaves; the confidentiality constraint limits disclosure of the move, not resilience work generally. Deliberately developing both candidates against their specific gaps preserves fair competition and strengthens the profession regardless of outcome. C converts succession into patronage, pre-empting fair competition; D outsources the answer before attempting development. This tests building institutional resilience as a legitimate initiative in its own right, treating succession as a capability to build rather than an event to time.

**Question 12 (hard) — Choosing least effective: the profession under pressure**

A departmental efficiency review proposes cutting central profession functions (community programme, capability development, recruitment support) by 60%, arguing "delivery managers deliver in programmes; the centre is overhead." Four weeks remain to respond. Which response is **least effective**?

- A) Build the evidence case: quantify what the central functions produce (time-to-fill and its contractor-premium cost, capability progression, attrition against benchmark, the knowledge-retention arithmetic of a 15%-attrition profession) and present the 60% cut's specific consequences in the review's own financial language.
- B) Mobilise legitimate allies: brief the programme directors who consume the profession's output on what the cut means for their delivery, so the review hears from the centre's customers, not only its owner.
- C) Accept the framing privately and concentrate on protecting a personal position and team assignments in the restructured organisation, since reviews of this kind are usually decided in advance.
- D) Engage the review's premise directly and constructively: concede what is genuinely true in it (any centre accumulates activities worth pruning), offer a self-designed 20–25% reshaping that cuts the weakest activities and protects the highest-value ones, and contest the remaining 35–40% with the evidence.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A, B, and D form a complete defence — evidence in the review's own currency, third-party validation from genuinely affected customers, and a strategic concession that buys credibility for defending the rest. Option C mistakes fatalism for realism, converts the head of profession into a private negotiator for their own position while the community's capability is dismantled unresisted, and teaches the community that leadership defends itself rather than them. This tests representing and championing the role, especially when the role itself is under threat, in the open and on the record rather than privately.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate judges an option at three orders** — does it solve the scenario, what does it teach the community watching, and what norm does it set for cases nobody will see.
- **Watch for whether a candidate holds two goods at once** — standards and relationship, honesty and institutional ownership — rather than sacrificing one.
- **Note whether a candidate sequences correctly**: peer conversation before leadership-table escalation, evidence before settlement, conversation before public escalation.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes the decision that belongs to someone else from the assessment or process that belongs to them.**
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that protects an individual at the community's expense, or the reverse**, rather than serving both.
- **Crediting an answer that escalates past a direct conversation** that could have resolved the matter.
- **Rewarding an answer that purchases adoption, calm, or approval with substance** — a watered standard, a softened rating, an altered assessment.
- **Missing when a candidate makes a quiet, private move** in a role defined by visible ones — an unadvertised appointment, a fading reorganisation, undisclosed self-protection.
- **Crediting an answer that treats a structural failure as an individual one**, rather than fixing the structure the candidate owns before developing the people within it.
