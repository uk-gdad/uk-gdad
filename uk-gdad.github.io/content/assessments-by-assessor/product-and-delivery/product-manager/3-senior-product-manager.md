# Senior Product Manager - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for senior product manager. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are standardised exercises measuring the cognitive capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict performance in a role. At this level, the assessments worth administering are not abstract puzzles — they are job-specific instruments built from the materials the role actually handles: multi-team roadmaps, business cases, portfolio-level KPI sets, life cycle decision papers, stakeholder communications strategies — and the decisions it actually makes: whether a team should continue, change direction, or stop; how to plan prioritised work across multiple teams while minimising dependencies and risk; when to challenge a strategy with evidence; and how to develop the product managers reporting in.

The role level is defined by complexity and consequence. A senior product manager works on a group of products sharing a value proposition, or on a single product that is complex, high risk, or sensitive. Reasoning errors no longer cost a sprint; they cost programmes. Reading errors no longer confuse a stand-up; they misinform boards. And judgement is exercised increasingly through others — the product managers and associates managed, coached, and recruited — which multiplies both the value of getting it right and the cost of getting it wrong.

Each of the four sections follows the same pattern: an overview of what it measures and why employers use it at this level; how its dimensions map to the role's named skills, from Agile and Lean practices to Strategic ownership; graduated practice questions with complete worked explanations tying the technique back to senior product work; then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Senior-level items are deliberately engineered around genuine ambiguity, competing goods, and organisational politics — several situational and verbal items reward strong reasoning over a single "correct" letter, particularly where a distractor is a response that would have been right one level down but is a failure of delegation at this level. Score for whether the candidate's answer routes decisions through the right structures and people, not merely whether it resolves the immediate scenario. Use each section's per-item timing as a guide for planning a session, and debrief against the explanations below.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes this role demands, using realistic workplace material rather than abstract items: multi-team roadmaps with interlocking dependencies, portfolio KPI sets whose signals conflict, life cycle decision papers, benefit models, and governance artefacts where a single overlooked condition changes the correct decision.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions spanning pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group — at senior levels, often managerial or professional — and adaptive platforms escalate difficulty with performance.

At this level, relevance lies less in raw processing than in disciplined reasoning under load: planning prioritised work across multiple teams while minimising dependencies and risks (a constraint-propagation problem), using evidence to decide whether a team should continue, change direction, or stop (a deduction problem with asymmetric costs of error), and leading teams to create effective measurement methods (an error-checking problem, since badly defined measures corrupt every decision downstream) — all while stakeholders supply confident, plausible, sometimes contradictory claims that must be tested rather than absorbed.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Agile and Lean practices**: reading portfolio-level data and distinguishing structural signal from local noise.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Life cycle management** and **Strategic ownership**: using evidence to decide whether teams continue, change direction, or stop, and to challenge a strategy when needed.
- **Error checking** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and the duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: catching definitional flaws, inconsistent baselines, and gamed metrics before they reach a board.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Product management** and **Creating value for money**: planning across multiple teams and helping others make value-based decisions under interacting constraints.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Product leadership** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: decomposing a politically loaded, evidence-poor situation into testable parts, then sequencing action.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a product group**

Four related products' quarterly completion rates over three quarters: Product A 81%, 82%, 81%. Product B 78%, 74%, 69%. Product C 85%, 84%, 86%. Product D 62%, 71%, 79%. Ask which single sentence best characterises the portfolio pattern.

- A) All four products are performing comparably.
- B) A and C are stable, D is improving strongly, and B is in sustained decline — B needs diagnostic attention first.
- C) D is the weakest product because its average is lowest.
- D) B and D are both volatile and should be treated identically.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A and C oscillate within a point — stable. D has climbed 17 points — the portfolio's success story. B has fallen steadily, nine points with no sign of arrest — the pattern demanding intervention. Option C anchors on level (D's lowest average) while ignoring direction; option D collapses two opposite trends into one label. This tests reading trajectory rather than snapshot — ranking attention by momentum, not league-table position — and, in a strong answer, mining the improver for transferable causes as deliberately as triaging the decliner.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a continue/stop rule**

A governance framework states: "A product in public beta receives continued funding only if it meets at least three of its four KPI targets, or presents an approved recovery plan." A product met two of its four KPI targets this quarter. Ask which one statement must be true.

- A) The product's funding will stop.
- B) The product requires an approved recovery plan to receive continued funding.
- C) The product's team has failed.
- D) The product will meet three targets next quarter if funded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The three-of-four route is closed; the recovery-plan route is therefore now necessary — option B. Option A overreaches, since funding stops only if the second route also fails, which is not established. Options C and D leave the logic of the rule entirely. This tests the professional phrasing distinction: when a disjunctive gate loses one arm, the correct statement is "the other arm is now decisive," never a verdict the rule has not issued — a distinction that changes whether a governance conversation mobilises a team or misinforms it.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a measurement method**

Four proposed KPIs for a casework product: (A) median end-to-end case processing time, monthly. (B) number of cases closed per caseworker per week. (C) percentage of decisions overturned on review, quarterly. (D) user satisfaction among applicants, sampled monthly. Ask which measure is most likely to distort behaviour if adopted as a target.

- A) Option A
- B) Option B
- C) Option C
- D) Option D

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Applying "what is the cheapest way to make this number look good, and does that serve users?" to each: A, C and D resist easy gaming, while B's cheap route — prioritise easy cases, rush complex ones, close prematurely — is obvious and harmful, the canonical Goodhart case. This tests recognising a volume-per-person target as a distortion risk, and noting the KPI set's internal safeguard (B's distortions would eventually surface in C and D, a lagged and expensive detector) — running this test is part of leading teams to create effective measurement methods.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritising across teams, not within one**

Monday, 9am, four items across a product group: (1) a team's product manager reports their Thursday release may breach a data standard and wants a decision on delaying; (2) a request to attend another team's Wednesday sprint review; (3) a director wants a quarterly narrative "by end of week"; (4) an associate product manager has asked for urgent career advice after an approach from another department. Ask for the most defensible sequencing.

- A) 1, 3, 4, 2 — the possible standards breach first, then the director's Friday deadline, then the retention conversation, then the optional ceremony
- B) 3, 1, 2, 4 — directors first
- C) 4, 1, 3, 2 — people first, always
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — strict order of arrival

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 combines urgency, impact and role centrality — ensuring standards compliance is a named duty — and leads. Item 3 has a real deadline and follows. Item 4 is genuinely important but served fully by a same-week conversation scheduled promptly today. Item 2 is presence, not decision work. Option B ranks by seniority over impact; option C misapplies "people first" to the wrong currency. This tests impact-weighted sequencing across multiple teams, and recognising that a two-minute scheduling response is itself a prioritisation act for several of these items.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Dependency reasoning across three teams**

Three teams plan next quarter. Constraints: (i) Team Alpha's payments API must be live before Team Beta can start integration; (ii) Beta's integration takes four weeks; (iii) Team Gamma's launch requires Beta's integration complete plus two weeks of end-to-end testing; (iv) Alpha's API goes live at end of week 2; (v) Gamma's launch is publicly committed for end of week 9. Ask for the position, assuming work starts as early as dependencies allow.

- A) The plan meets the commitment exactly, with no slack.
- B) The launch lands at the end of week 8, one week early.
- C) The launch cannot happen before the end of week 8, leaving one week of slack against the commitment.
- D) The launch will miss the commitment by one week.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** API live end of week 2; Beta's integration weeks 3–6; testing weeks 7–8; earliest launch end of week 8, against a week-9 commitment — one week of slack. Option B confuses earliest possible with planned. This tests propagating a dependency chain forward correctly, and — in a strong answer — recognising that one week of slack across a three-team critical path with a public commitment is thin, prompting questions about restructuring dependencies (parallel scaffolding, overlapping testing) rather than merely reporting the number.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Error checking a business case claim**

A business case states: "The new digital grant service will save £2.4m over three years. Current processing costs £30 per application across 40,000 annual applications (£1.2m per year). The digital service will halve processing cost per application, saving £600,000 per year, which over three years with volume growth of 10% per year gives £2.4m." Ask for the most serious flaw in the arithmetic as presented.

- A) £30 × 40,000 is not £1.2m.
- B) Halving £30 does not give a £600,000 annual saving.
- C) Three years of £600,000 is £1.8m; reaching £2.4m requires the volume growth to apply, but growing volumes would also raise the counterfactual baseline — the case double-counts growth on the saving while holding the stated annual saving fixed, and presents £2.4m without showing the year-by-year arithmetic.
- D) The case fails to discount future savings to present value.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The stated components check out individually (£1.2m and £600k are both correct), but three years of £600k is £1.8m, not £2.4m — and the growth adjustment needed to close the gap is applied inconsistently and never shown year by year. This tests recomputing a headline from its stated components and, when it does not reconcile, identifying that an unsurfaced assumption is bridging the gap — a core reviewing habit for business cases the candidate is expected to write or contribute to.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Conflicting signals across data sources**

For a flagship product this quarter: digital uptake rose 64% to 71%; satisfaction rose 79% to 83%; support contact volume rose 22%; complaint themes shifted from "cannot find the service" to "cannot complete the identity step." Ask for the synthesis that best explains the pattern.

- A) The data is contradictory — satisfaction and complaints cannot both rise — so at least one source is wrong.
- B) Successful uptake growth has pulled in a larger and broader user population; most fare well (satisfaction up), but the identity step is failing a growing minority, driving contact volume — the next investigation is who is failing identity and why.
- C) The support contact rise proves the product got worse, and the satisfaction rise reflects survey bias.
- D) All metrics moving means the quarter was a success; no investigation is needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A single mechanism — a bigger, broader, mostly-happier population with a localised failure point — explains all four signals simultaneously, with the complaint-theme shift the diagnostic gem (awareness improving, identity localising as the binding constraint). Option A declares contradiction where composition change is the obvious reconciliation. This tests holding several signals at once and finding the reconciling mechanism rather than averaging into a mood, converting synthesis into a targeted next question.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritisation with explicit value reasoning**

A product group can staff exactly one of three initiatives next quarter: Initiative P relieves a legal compliance risk assessed as "likely within 12 months; high impact," no new user value. Initiative Q projects £900k over three years in verified-methodology savings, starting month 8. Initiative R is the top user-researched pain point, improving satisfaction for ~200,000 users, immediate but unquantified in money. A director says "just pick the biggest number." Ask for the strongest reasoning.

- A) Q — £900k is the biggest number, and the director has spoken.
- B) R — user needs always outrank money and risk.
- C) P — a likely-within-12-months, high-impact legal risk is an expected cost that plausibly dwarfs both alternatives and can forfeit permission to operate; then make the case to the director in exactly those value terms, while scoping whether Q or R can proceed in reduced form.
- D) Split the quarter three ways so nothing is neglected.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Converting P to expected-value terms — remediation, enforcement, litigation, loss of licence to operate — plausibly exceeds both alternatives, and unlike Q and R, its cost worsens with delay. Option A obeys an arithmetic error (only one option arrives with a number attached); option D spreads capacity for one initiative across three. This tests monetising or bounding what resists quantification before comparing it with quantified options, and communicating the recommendation in the decider's own value language rather than defiance.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving: the stalled discovery**

A discovery in week 5 of 8 is drifting: fourteen caseworkers interviewed but no articulated problem statement; the researcher and lead developer disagree about scope; the policy stakeholder presses for "the solution options paper" a discovery was never going to produce. Ask for the most effective intervention.

- A) Extend the discovery by four weeks to allow more interviews.
- B) Run a mid-discovery checkpoint: require the team to state candidate problem framings from the evidence so far, test each against the original discovery questions, reset scope explicitly with researcher and developer in the room, and re-contract with the policy stakeholder on what a discovery output is and when decisions follow.
- C) Cancel the discovery and commission an options paper directly, since that is what the stakeholder wants.
- D) Replace the researcher, since problem articulation is a research output.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Fourteen interviews without a problem statement is a synthesis and alignment failure, not an evidence shortage, so more interviews (option A) burns time treating the wrong disease. Option B forces early articulation, anchors the reset to the original discovery questions, resolves the scope dispute in the room, and re-contracts the stakeholder's misunderstanding of the life cycle phase. Option C capitulates to that misunderstanding; D personalises a structural, two-party failure. This tests diagnosing before intervening, and changing people only on evidence that the frame and contract were not the problem.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-constraint sequencing under a fixed window**

Five pieces of work over six sprints (S1–S6) for one team: security remediation (X, 2 sprints, must complete before any public-facing release); feature launch (L, 1 sprint, publicly committed no later than S4); accessibility fixes (F, 1 sprint, must complete before L); data migration (M, 2 sprints, cannot overlap X — same platform engineer); service assessment preparation (A, 1 sprint, must be S5, immediately before the S6 assessment). One piece per sprint except two-sprint items occupying consecutive sprints. Ask which schedule works.

- A) X: S1–S2, F: S3, L: S4, A: S5, M: S5–S6
- B) M: S1–S2, X: S3–S4, F: S5, L: S6
- C) X: S1–S2, F: S3, L: S4, M: impossible this window — it must move out; A: S5
- D) F: S1, L: S2, X: S3–S4, M: S5–S6

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Anchoring the rigid items (A in S5; X earliest S1–S2; F: S3; L: S4) fills S1–S5 entirely, leaving only S6 for M, which needs two sprints — M cannot fit. Option A double-books S5; B breaches L's S4 commitment; D releases L before X completes, breaching the precedence rule. This tests recognising an over-constrained schedule and stating the honest infeasibility — the correct answer to "which schedule works?" is sometimes that the constraint set does not, and that finding belongs in an open escalation, not a silently double-booked plan.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Spotting the flaw in a scaling argument**

A product manager argues: "Our assisted-digital pilot in Newcastle cut failed applications by 40%. Rolling it out nationally will therefore cut national failed applications by 40%, and since the pilot cost £50k for a city of 300,000, national rollout for 56 million people will cost about £9.3m — the business case writes itself." Ask which critique identifies the most serious flaws.

- A) The percentages are computed incorrectly.
- B) The argument assumes the pilot effect transfers uniformly (Newcastle's users, channels, and delivery conditions may be unrepresentative; pilots also enjoy novelty and hand-picked staff), and the linear cost scaling ignores both economies and diseconomies of scale — the 40% and the £9.3m are each extrapolations presented as facts.
- C) The pilot should have run longer before any conclusion.
- D) National rollout decisions are for ministers, not product managers, so the argument is out of scope.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic checks out; the flaws are inferential — effect transfer (site-specific composition, delivery conditions, novelty) and cost scaling (ignoring both sunk-cost economies and coordination diseconomies). Option C misdiagnoses duration as the flaw when representativeness is; option D confuses decision rights with analytical duty. This tests recognising that "the case writes itself" usually means the assumptions are doing the writing, and treating a single-site result as an anchor for staged testing rather than a copied point estimate.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling strategy, evidence, and a sunk commitment**

Eighteen months ago a directorate strategy committed to "a single unified portal for all business licensing services," with £1.8m spent. Since then: three rounds of research show licensees arrive via search and task-specific journeys and struggle with the unified structure; analytics show 78% of successful transactions bypass the homepage; two services have quietly built direct entry points. The board expects a "continue" recommendation for phase 3 (£600k). Ask for the strongest position to take to the board.

- A) Recommend continuing phase 3 as planned — £1.8m is spent, the strategy is agreed, and boards dislike reversals.
- B) Recommend stopping all portal work immediately and declaring the strategy failed.
- C) Present the accumulated evidence that the portal's unifying assumption no longer holds, recommend redirecting phase 3 funding toward the pattern users have chosen — strong task-based entry points with shared components (accounts, payments, common lookups) behind them — and propose the strategy be formally revised to "unified underlying services, multiple entry points," with the portal's useful components retained.
- D) Present the evidence but make no recommendation, letting the board decide unguided.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Behaviour, research and organisational revealed preference all triangulate on the same finding — users want unified plumbing, not a unified porch. Option C names the falsified assumption precisely, redirects rather than merely cancels, and formally revises the strategy so the change is governed rather than drifted into. Option A treats the sunk £1.8m as relevant to the marginal £600k decision; B overcorrects into theatre; D abdicates the recommendation a senior product manager is expected to make. This tests using evidence to challenge a strategy, including one's own, while preserving its legitimate intent.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate ranks attention by trajectory (slope, momentum) rather than snapshot level.
- Note whether the candidate phrases a disjunctive-gate conclusion correctly — "the other route is now decisive," not a verdict the rule has not issued.
- Watch for whether the candidate recomputes a headline figure from its stated components before accepting it.
- Score for recognising and stating genuine infeasibility rather than forcing a schedule to fit.
- Note whether the candidate treats sunk costs as irrelevant to a marginal decision.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a ranking by snapshot level when the question supplies trajectory.
- Missing when a candidate forces a fit on an over-constrained scheduling problem rather than flagging infeasibility.
- Accepting a headline figure a components check cannot reproduce, without probing for the bridging assumption.
- Rewarding an argument that treats sunk costs as evidence for a marginal decision.
- Missing when a candidate extrapolates a single-site pilot linearly, in effect or in cost, without naming the transfer assumption.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures accuracy and judgement with the numbers this role genuinely trades in. The mathematics remains accessible — percentages, ratios, weighted averages, rates, trends — but at senior level the data carries realistic complications: multi-product tables, business-case arithmetic with embedded assumptions, benefit models that must be audited rather than merely computed.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, calculator permitted, scored objectively and often adaptively against a professional or managerial norm group.

Numeric fluency operates in two directions at this level, and the assessment probes both. Producing: writing or contributing to business cases, determining product benefit, communicating business-value propositions. Consuming: teams, suppliers and stakeholders bring numbers engineered to persuade — pilot extrapolations, unweighted averages, totals masquerading as performance — and this role is the quality gate before those numbers reach boards.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages, rates, and funnel arithmetic** map to **Managing product outcomes**: fluency in the computations measurement methods produce, and the distortions they can hide.
- **Weighted averages and mixed-population reasoning** map to **Applying user-centred insights** and portfolio reporting.
- **Cost-benefit and unit-economics arithmetic** maps to **Creating value for money**: writing and reviewing business cases with auditable arithmetic and stated assumptions.
- **Trend reading and projection discipline** maps to **Strategic ownership** and **Life cycle management**: separating trend from noise, and projection from promise.
- **Table and chart interrogation** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Product leadership**: extracting and correcting figures in the meeting where they are presented.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio completion rates**

Product A: 24,000 starts, 18,000 completions. Product B: 6,000 starts, 5,100 completions. Ask for the individual completion rates and the group rate.

- A) A: 75%; B: 85%; group: 80%
- B) A: 75%; B: 85%; group: 77%
- C) A: 80%; B: 85%; group: 82.5%
- D) A: 75%; B: 82%; group: 77%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A: 75%; B: 85%; group: (18,000+5,100)÷(24,000+6,000)=77% — option B, not the unweighted 80% average of A. This tests aggregating raw numerators and denominators rather than averaging percentages, since unweighted blending overweights a smaller product and flatters or damages a group figure depending on which way it performs.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change with the right anchor**

Monthly support cost fell from £84,000 to £63,000. Ask for the percentage reduction and what a further 20% reduction from the new figure would be.

- A) 25% reduction; further 20% takes it to £50,400
- B) 33% reduction; further 20% takes it to £50,400
- C) 25% reduction; further 20% takes it to £42,000
- D) 21% reduction; further 20% takes it to £50,400

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** (84,000−63,000)÷84,000=25%; 63,000×0.80=£50,400 — option A. Option B anchors the reduction on the new value; C applies the further reduction to the wrong base. This tests correct anchoring and, in a strong answer, recognising that sequential percentage reductions compound (a combined 40% here) rather than add (which would misleadingly suggest 45%) — a common overstatement in chained-savings claims.

**Question 3 (easy) — Cost per outcome, not cost per activity**

Team One spent £120,000 preventing 4,000 failures. Team Two spent £45,000 preventing 1,200 failures. Ask for the correct statement.

- A) Team Two is more efficient: it spent far less.
- B) Team One is more efficient at £30 per prevented failure versus Team Two's £37.50 — though Team Two's smaller programme may face different conditions worth understanding before reallocating.
- C) Team One is more efficient because it prevented more failures.
- D) Efficiency cannot be compared across teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** £30 versus £37.50 per prevented failure — option B, with the caveat that comparability (harder cases, different regions, learning-curve stage) should be checked before reallocating on this basis alone. This tests converting activity-and-spend into cost-per-outcome rather than judging by spend or volume alone, and pairing the comparison with a comparability check.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reading a KPI table with a defined threshold**

Governance funds products meeting at least three of four KPI targets: Product X — completion 81% (≥78 ✓), satisfaction 79% (≥80 ✗), unit cost £2.95 (≤£3.20 ✓), uptake 68% (≥65 ✓). Product Y — completion 77% (≥78 ✗), satisfaction 84% (≥80 ✓), unit cost £3.18 (≤£3.20 ✓), uptake 71% (≥65 ✓). Ask which products meet the funding threshold.

- A) Both X and Y
- B) X only
- C) Y only
- D) Neither

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Both score three of four — option A. This tests correctly applying an inequality direction (unit cost is "at most," the others "at least") without letting one prominent miss colour the whole row when the rule is three-of-four, and, in a strong answer, noting that near-misses by one point deserve trend attention even when the gate passes.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted satisfaction across a product group**

Product A: 82% satisfied, 1,500 respondents. Product B: 74%, 500 respondents. Product C: 90%, 2,000 respondents. Ask for the respondent-weighted group satisfaction.

- A) 82.0%
- B) 85.0%
- C) 84.5%
- D) 86.5%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (1,500×0.82 + 500×0.74 + 2,000×0.90) ÷ 4,000 = 85% — option B, not the unweighted 82% mean. This tests weighting by respondent count correctly, and — in a strong answer — flagging that respondent counts may not match user-population weights, and that the 85% blend conceals a 16-point spread worth surfacing alongside it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Business case payback with phased benefits**

A feature costs £180,000 to build (year 0) and £20,000/yr to run. Benefits: £60,000 (year 1), £150,000 (years 2 and 3). Ask for the cumulative net position at end of year 3, and in which year cumulative net first turns positive.

- A) Net +£120,000; turns positive during year 3
- B) Net +£120,000; turns positive during year 2
- C) Net +£180,000; turns positive during year 2
- D) Net +£60,000; turns positive during year 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Year-by-year: −180,000; −140,000 (year 1); −10,000 (year 2); +120,000 (year 3, crossing zero early in the year). Option C forgets running costs entirely. This tests building the cumulative table rather than accepting a headline net figure, since the depth and length of the trough is what boards actually decide about, and phased benefits with a flat headline total is where optimism hides.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Detecting the misleading average**

A supplier reports average response time across a product group improved from 3.0 to 2.6 seconds. Own data: Service One (90% of traffic) went 2.8→2.9 seconds; Service Two (10% of traffic) went 4.8→0.5 after moving to a static page. Ask what the traffic-weighted picture shows.

- A) The supplier is right: performance improved meaningfully across the group.
- B) Traffic-weighted response went from 3.0 to 2.66 seconds, so the supplier's claim is broadly fair for the group and for users.
- C) The unweighted improvement is driven entirely by a small service's architectural change; for 90% of traffic, response worsened from 2.8 to 2.9 seconds — the supplier's average conceals a mild degradation for the overwhelming majority of users.
- D) Both datasets are wrong since they disagree.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The supplier's figures are traffic-weighted and arithmetically genuine, but decomposing by segment shows Service Two's improvement contributes more than the entire gain while Service One — 90% of users — quietly worsened. This tests decomposing an improved average by segment weight before accepting it, since averages can launder minority miracles over majority decay — the exact audit needed before endorsing a supplier's headline in a contract conversation.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Capacity arithmetic across teams**

Over 13 weeks, effective capacities: Team One 4.2 developer-weeks/week; Team Two 3.5; Team Three 5.0. Committed work: One 41; Two 52; Three 58 developer-weeks. Ask which teams are over-committed, and by how much.

- A) Only Team Two, by 6.5 developer-weeks
- B) Teams Two and Three; Two by 6.5 and Three by 7 developer-weeks
- C) Only Team Three, by 7 developer-weeks
- D) Teams One and Two; One by 13.6 and Two by 6.5 developer-weeks

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Team One: 54.6 capacity vs 41 committed — headroom 13.6. Team Two: 45.5 vs 52 — over by 6.5. Team Three: 65 vs 58 — headroom 7. Only Team Two is over — option A. Option B misreads Three's headroom as overload. This tests computing capacity from a weekly rate correctly and not judging overload by the size of the committed number alone — Team Three's largest raw commitment is in fact its healthiest position.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Benefit realisation against profile**

Profile: £30k (Q1), £60k (Q2), £90k (Q3), £120k (Q4), £300k total. Actuals: Q1 £28k, Q2 £51k, Q3 £66k. Ask for the cumulative variance at end of Q3, and — if the Q3 shortfall ratio persists into Q4 — the projected full-year total.

- A) −£35k cumulative; projected year ≈ £233k
- B) −£35k cumulative; projected year ≈ £265k
- C) −£24k cumulative; projected year ≈ £276k
- D) −£35k cumulative; projected year ≈ £240k

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Cumulative to Q3: profile £180k, actual £145k, variance −£35k. Q3 ratio: 66÷90=73.3%; applied to Q4's £120k gives £88k; projected year £233k. Option B persists the cumulative ratio instead of the specified (and worsening) Q3 ratio. This tests using the specified projection assumption precisely, and — in a strong answer — noticing the shortfall is widening (93%, 85%, 73%), the real finding for the governance conversation, rather than a static shortfall.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The denominator shift**

A team reports complaint rate improved from 4.0% to 3.2% of contacts. Underlying: last quarter 800 complaints of 20,000 contacts; this quarter 960 complaints of 30,000 contacts (a new webchat channel driving the rise). Transactions stable at 100,000/quarter. Ask for the sharper reading.

- A) The team is right: 3.2% is better than 4.0%, so complaints improved.
- B) Complaints per contact fell only because the denominator was inflated by new low-stakes webchat contacts; per 1,000 transactions — the stable base — complaints rose from 8.0 to 9.6, a 20% worsening the chosen metric conceals.
- C) The webchat channel caused the extra complaints.
- D) Both metrics are equally valid, so the team may choose either.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The team's arithmetic is honest but the metric's denominator grew 50% via a new low-complaint-propensity channel, masking a genuine 20% rise per unit of stable transaction volume. Option C over-attributes causation the data does not establish; option D's metric relativism ignores that a metric with a manipulable denominator is a self-gradeable exam. This tests normalising to the most stable base semantically connected to the outcome, before accepting an "improved" rate.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Comparing options with different risk and time profiles**

Option One: £90k cost; £200k/yr benefit from year 1; 90% confidence. Option Two: £90k cost; £520k/yr benefit from year 2 only; 50% confidence. Ask, using expected value over three years (benefits × confidence − cost, no discounting), what the comparison shows and what judgement should accompany it.

- A) Option One: EV +£450k; Option Two: EV +£430k — One leads on EV and certainty, but the £20k gap is within assumption noise, so the decision should turn on confidence in the 50% estimate, appetite for a zero-benefit year, and whether Two's upside can be de-risked before full commitment.
- B) Option Two: its £520k benefit is much larger, and product managers should back ambition.
- C) Option One: EV +£540k; Option Two: EV +£520k.
- D) The options are identical since both cost £90k.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** One: (£200k×3×0.9)−£90k=£450k. Two: (£520k×2×0.5)−£90k=£430k. A £20k gap on a round 50% estimate is false precision, not a decision. This tests computing expected value correctly (including subtracting cost, which option C omits) and stating where the model is weakest — how the 50% was estimated, whether a staged approach could de-risk it — alongside the number.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Auditing a percentage story before it reaches the board**

A draft states: "Digital uptake rose 12% this year (from 60% to 67.2%). Assisted digital demand fell 25% (from 8,000 to 6,000 sessions), confirming reduced need for the assisted route. Cost per digital transaction fell 10% (from £2.00 to £1.80), so the digital estate saved £480,000 across our 2.4 million transactions." Total transactions were stable year on year. Ask for the most important audit finding.

- A) All three claims are arithmetically consistent and the paper can proceed.
- B) The uptake claim confuses percentage points with percent (60 to 67.2 is 7.2 points, a 12% relative rise — the wording "rose 12%" will be misread); the savings figure applies the 20p unit saving to all 2.4m transactions instead of the ~1.61m digital ones (true saving ≈ £322k, overstated by ~£158k); and falling assisted sessions amid rising uptake does not by itself confirm reduced need — need may be unmet rather than absent.
- C) The assisted digital claim is wrong because demand never falls.
- D) The cost figure is wrong because £2.00 minus 10% is £1.90.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three separate audit findings: points-versus-percent ambiguity, applying a per-transaction saving to the wrong (total rather than digital) volume, and treating falling usage as confirmed reduced need when it might instead reflect degraded access. Option D miscomputes; C asserts a fact rather than auditing the inference. This tests a triple audit — units, denominators, inference-from-absence — that protects both the board and the users a metric like "reduced need" might be quietly failing.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate aggregates raw numerators and denominators rather than averaging percentages.
- Note whether the candidate builds a year-by-year cumulative table for cost-benefit questions rather than relying on a headline net figure.
- Watch for whether the candidate decomposes an improved average by segment weight before trusting it.
- Score for whether the candidate normalises a rate to its most stable, semantically appropriate denominator.
- Note whether the candidate states model weaknesses alongside an expected-value calculation, rather than treating the number as the whole answer.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an average of percentages across unequal groups, or a blend reported without its spread.
- Missing confusion between percentage points and percent, in either direction.
- Rewarding a business case that nets benefits against build cost only, forgetting running costs.
- Accepting a favourable metric with a self-inflating denominator as genuine improvement.
- Missing when a candidate lets arithmetic settle a question that is really about an unstated assumption.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures the precision with which a candidate reads, interprets and draws inference from written material of consequence at this level: strategy documents, governance minutes, legal and policy extracts, supplier contracts and correspondence, research syntheses, and board papers.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes: short passages followed by true / false / cannot say questions, plus best-summary selection, assumption identification, inference, and clear-writing judgement. The discipline is absolute: answer from the passage alone — senior items in particular weaponise professional plausibility as a distractor.

Verbal precision at this level is a control function, not a support skill: this role is frequently the last reader before words acquire force — the board paper that commits a programme, the supplier letter that concedes or preserves a contractual position, the strategy sentence teams will quote at each other for two years.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Comprehension of policy, standards, and governance text** maps to the duties to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards** and to work through **Life cycle management** gates.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Applying user-centred insights**: stating exactly what evidence supports, no more, no less.
- **Assumption and inference analysis** maps to **Creating value for money** and **Strategic ownership**: locating the load-bearing unstated premise in business cases and strategies.
- **Reading correspondence for commitment strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: hearing exactly what was promised, conditioned, or merely hoped.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Product leadership** and **Product management**: prose calibrated to its audience — specific, honest, and quotable without damage.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 6, the candidate decides whether the statement is True, False, or Cannot say. A fourth option is included where relevant.

**Question 1 (easy) — A spend control extract**

Passage: "Digital spending proposals above £100,000 require spend approval before contracts are signed. Proposals below this threshold do not require approval but must be recorded in the spend register within 30 days of contract signature. Approval decisions are normally issued within 15 working days."

Statement: "A £90,000 digital spending proposal must be recorded in the spend register within 30 days of contract signature."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, and it also requires approval

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £90,000 falls below the threshold, and below-threshold proposals carry exactly the registration obligation the statement states: True. Option D contradicts the express exemption. This tests placing a case correctly on the right side of a threshold before applying its attached obligations, and recognising "normally issued within 15 days" as a norm rather than a commitment for any follow-up statement.

**Question 2 (easy) — Governance minutes and decision status**

Passage: "The board considered the product group's proposal to consolidate three case-handling tools. The board endorsed the direction of travel, requested a full business case including workforce impacts by the November meeting, and delegated approval of discovery spend up to £75,000 to the SRO. The SRO indicated support."

Statement: "The board has approved the consolidation of the three case-handling tools."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, up to £75,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Every governance verb — considered, endorsed the direction, requested, delegated (discovery spend only), indicated support — stops short of approving consolidation, and the decision is explicitly deferred to November: False. Option D confuses a bounded discovery-spend delegation with partial programme approval. This tests tracking governance verbs precisely, since briefing "the board approved consolidation" from these minutes would misinform and be publicly correctable.

**Question 3 (easy) — Research synthesis and claim scope**

Passage: "Across two rounds of research (26 participants), small-business users consistently described the licensing renewal reminder as arriving too late to act on. Participants who employed accountants reported that reminders were typically forwarded to the accountant unread. No sole traders in the sample reported using an accountant for renewals."

Statement: "Small-business users would prefer reminders to be sent directly to their accountants."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for users who employ accountants

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Forwarding behaviour is not a stated preference, and rival hypotheses fit the same evidence equally well: Cannot say. Option D's narrower scope still lacks a preference statement. This tests distinguishing behaviour from preference — research syntheses that quietly convert one into the other are how roadmaps acquire unrequested features — with the legitimate next step being a design hypothesis to test, not a conclusion to act on.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Supplier correspondence under contract**

Passage: "We acknowledge the service credits triggered by October's availability breach and will apply them to the December invoice. Regarding the November performance figures, we note the measurement methodology remains under joint review, and we therefore reserve our position on any credits for that period. We remain committed to the remediation plan agreed on 4 November and expect completion by 31 January."

Statement: "The supplier has accepted liability for service credits in respect of November."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the remediation plan covers November

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "We reserve our position" is an express non-acceptance for November, contradicting the statement: False. Option D conflates an operational remediation commitment with financial liability acceptance — suppliers routinely commit to fixing problems while disputing the figures that would monetise them. This tests reading a reservation as a reservation and never paraphrasing it as agreement in reporting upward, since the paraphrase, once minuted, can become the concession.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A policy exception with stacked conditions**

Passage: "Services must not collect information the department already holds, unless re-collection is necessary to verify identity, or the existing information is more than two years old and the user confirms it is no longer accurate. Where re-collection occurs under either exception, the service must record the justification and review it at each service assessment."

Statement: "A service that re-collects a user's address because the held record is three years old has complied with the policy, provided it records the justification."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because three years exceeds the two-year threshold

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The exception requires two conjoined conditions — age over two years AND user confirmation of inaccuracy — and only the first is established: Cannot say, since compliance depends on an unstated fact. Option D isolates the age condition alone, the exact misreading the "and" prevents. This tests circling every conjunction in a stacked condition and demanding a fact for each side, and recognising that recording a justification cannot cure a substantively unmet exception.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Strategy text and what it actually mandates**

Passage: "By 2027 we expect the majority of our transactional services to be built on shared platform components. New services should adopt shared components by default; teams proposing bespoke builds must document why shared components cannot meet their needs. Existing services will be assessed for migration opportunistically, prioritising those approaching major re-procurement."

Statement: "The strategy requires all existing transactional services to migrate to shared components by 2027."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for services approaching re-procurement

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage carries three graduated postures — expectation about most services, a rebuttable default for new builds, and opportunistic prioritisation for existing services — none of which support a universal mandate: False. Option D upgrades "prioritising" into "requiring." This tests mapping mandate gradients (expect/should/must, majority/default/all) precisely, since both over-reading and under-reading a strategy misuse it in real disputes.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Best summary of a decision paper**

Passage: "We assessed three options for the failing appointments module: remediate in place (£250k, retains known architecture, high confidence), replace with the cross-government booking component (£180k integration, resolves accessibility backlog, medium confidence pending a technical spike), and full bespoke rebuild (£600k, maximum flexibility, low confidence in the timeline). We recommend option two, subject to a two-week technical spike confirming API compatibility; if the spike fails, we recommend option one as fallback. Option three is not recommended at current funding levels."

Ask which sentence best summarises the paper.

- A) The paper recommends replacing the module with the cross-government component, conditional on a two-week compatibility spike, with in-place remediation as the agreed fallback and the bespoke rebuild ruled out at current funding.
- B) The paper recommends the cheapest option because budgets are constrained.
- C) The paper recommends remediating in place, with replacement as a future aspiration.
- D) The paper finds all three options viable and defers the decision pending a spike.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option A preserves the primary recommendation, its condition, the contingent fallback, and the bounded rejection at correct strength. Option B misattributes the reasoning to cost alone; D misreads a conditional recommendation as no decision. This tests fidelity to strength — every recommendation, condition, fallback and bounded rejection preserved — the compression skill needed to discuss product strategy at all levels.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The assumption a business case stands on**

Passage: "Consolidating our two licensing front-ends into one will reduce user confusion, because applicants currently start in the wrong service 18% of the time, and each misdirected start costs an average of 11 minutes before users find the correct route. A single front-end eliminates wrong starts, saving applicants approximately 40,000 hours annually."

Ask which unstated assumption is most critical to the claimed saving.

- A) That users prefer single front-ends aesthetically.
- B) That the consolidated front-end's internal routing will not simply relocate the confusion — users who today pick the wrong service must, in the new design, still be routed to the correct journey, and the case assumes that routing costs them approximately zero time.
- C) That the two licensing services have similar transaction volumes.
- D) That 11 minutes is measured accurately.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Eliminates" is the load-bearing word: the underlying routing task does not vanish with consolidation, it relocates inside the single front-end, and the case books the entire saving as if the new routing cost nothing. This tests skeletonising an argument to find where confusion, contact or failure demand is assumed to disappear rather than relocate — a pattern recurring across many benefit cases, where the honest version estimates the residual cost at the new location.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Two passages, one apparent contradiction**

Passage 1 (performance report, May): "Median processing time for standard applications fell from 9 days to 6 days following the March automation release." Passage 2 (caseworker forum notes, May): "Caseworkers report that applications now take longer to process than before March, with complex cases requiring extensive manual rework of automated pre-checks."

Statement: "The two passages cannot both be accurate."

- A) True
- B) False — both can be accurate if automation accelerated standard applications (moving the median) while adding rework to complex cases, which the median for standard applications would not register.
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because caseworker perception is subjective

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both claims are scoped differently — a median over standard applications versus caseworker experience of complex cases — and a reconciling world exists where automation helps one population and burdens the other invisibly to the first metric: False. Option D dismisses frontline testimony as subjective rather than data about a population the headline metric cannot see. This tests reading the population, statistic and unit of each claim before declaring conflict, and treating an apparent contradiction as the start of inquiry, not its end.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement: the escalation paragraph**

Ask which paragraph best serves an escalation to a director with five minutes and no context, about a supplier performance issue.

- A) "Further to ongoing dialogue with our delivery partner regarding performance variances against contractual baselines, a range of remediation levers are being explored in the context of the broader relationship, and we will revert with a fuller position in due course."
- B) "Our booking supplier missed the 99.5% availability target for the third consecutive month (October: 97.1%), causing an estimated 4,200 failed citizen bookings. Service credits are applied, but the remediation plan has now slipped twice. I recommend we trigger the contract's formal improvement notice this week; if November also breaches, we begin exit planning. I need your agreement to issue the notice."
- C) "The supplier situation has deteriorated significantly and urgent senior intervention is now required as a matter of priority."
- D) "October availability was 97.1% against a 99.5% target; service credits were applied per clause 14.2; the remediation plan baseline was re-agreed on 4 November; a further review is scheduled for 30 November; the joint service board meets quarterly; minutes are attached."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B delivers fact pattern, user consequence, recommendation and a specific decision request, all sized for a five-minute read. Option A conceals via vagueness; C carries urgency without content; D is fact-complete but judgement-free, outsourcing the actual decision to the reader. This tests writing an escalation that selects its facts around the decision being requested — a paragraph with all the facts and no ask is unfinished, not neutral.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference from an evaluation report**

Passage: "The 12-week proactive-contact trial called applicants whose applications stalled for 10+ days. Called applicants completed at 64%, against 41% for stalled applicants in the comparison group. However, the trial required callers to attempt contact within two working days of the stall, a standard met in 71% of cases; completion among applicants actually reached within two days was 78%. Trial staffing used experienced caseworkers volunteering from other duties, and the evaluation notes that stalled applications rose 8% service-wide during the trial period for unrelated seasonal reasons."

Statement: "If the proactive-contact approach is rolled out with the same two-day contact standard met at the same rate, completion among called applicants of around 64% is a better-supported expectation than 78%."

- A) True
- B) False — 78% is the demonstrated effect of the intervention working properly
- C) Cannot say
- D) False — the seasonal rise invalidates all the trial's figures

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 64% is the intention-to-treat figure, matching a rollout that holds the operational contact rate constant as stated; 78% is a per-protocol figure inflated by excluding operational failure and possible selection: True. Option B is the optimism error that inflates business cases by quoting the per-protocol number for an at-scale decision. This tests distinguishing which number answers which question, and — in a strong answer — shading even 64% down further given the trial's non-replicable volunteer staffing.

**Question 12 (challenging) — What the strategy sentence commits the candidate to**

Passage (a strategy quoted back at the candidate): "We will prioritise reducing failure demand across the licensing estate, and will not commence major new feature programmes until the top five drivers of avoidable contact have been addressed." A programme director emails: "Your own strategy says no new features until failure demand is fixed. The renewals redesign my team wants is therefore blocked, and so is your API programme. Please confirm both are paused."

Statement: "The strategy text, as quoted, blocks the API programme only if the API programme is a major new feature programme and the top five avoidable-contact drivers have not been addressed."

- A) True
- B) False — the strategy blocks all new work until failure demand is fixed
- C) Cannot say
- D) False — strategies cannot block programmes

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The prohibition applies to a defined class under a defined condition — exactly the biconditional the statement states: True. The director's email silently expands "major new feature programmes" into "all new work" and "top five drivers addressed" into "failure demand fixed" — a higher bar than the strategy set. This tests returning to a strategy's exact drafted text when it is quoted back, since load-bearing terms like "major," "top five" and "addressed" are litigated in emails exactly like this one.

### Administration tips

- Score for the three-way test applied mechanically — could the statement be false while the passage stays true?
- Watch for whether the candidate reads governance and correspondence for verb weight (considered/noted/endorsed/approved; acknowledge/reserve/commit).
- Note whether the candidate circles every conjunction in a stacked condition and every modal gradient in a mandate.
- For evaluation-report items, score on whether the candidate distinguishes the population and delivery conditions behind each figure (intention-to-treat versus per-protocol).
- For clear-writing items, score on the four-part escalation test: facts, consequence, recommendation, ask.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting professional plausibility as if it were textual support.
- Missing when a candidate flattens a mandate gradient — reading "endorsed the direction" as approval, or "prioritising" as requiring.
- Rewarding an inference that converts behaviour into preference, usage into need, or a remediation commitment into liability acceptance.
- Accepting one satisfied limb of a conjoined condition as compliance.
- Missing when a candidate quotes a per-protocol figure for an intention-to-treat, at-scale decision.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to evaluate the effectiveness of possible responses. Scoring keys derive from experienced practitioners' consensus and the profession's behavioural frameworks. At senior level, distractors are engineered with particular care: responses correct one level down (personally absorbing work that should now be routed through others), responses that pattern-match to leadership clichés (decisive unilateralism, unconditional team backing), and responses whose flaw is visible only at organisational timescale.

Typical format: an online exercise of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, usually untimed. This is the closest of the four instruments to the actual job: the role manages and coaches product managers and associates, recruits, protects teams from distraction and direction churn, represents product management with senior stakeholders, and uses evidence to decide whether teams continue, change direction, or stop — challenging strategy when the evidence demands it, including strategies authored personally.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **People management scenarios** map to the duties to **manage associate product managers and product managers** and be involved in **recruiting**.
- **Team protection scenarios** map to **Product leadership**: absorbing organisational turbulence without becoming a filter that leaves teams naive or stakeholders blind.
- **Evidence and governance scenarios** map to **Life cycle management** and **Managing product outcomes**: continue/pivot/stop recommendations and honest metrics.
- **Strategy and challenge scenarios** map to **Strategic ownership**: using evidence to challenge vision and strategy, including strategies authored and sponsors respected.
- **Stakeholder and mediation scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Creating value for money**.
- **Standards and integrity scenarios** map to the duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: accessibility, data protection and evidential honesty as non-negotiables.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A product manager's first big mistake**

A product manager managed by the candidate misread a governance condition and told their team to begin a public beta launch sequence without the required assessment report cleared. Caught a day in; two days of lost work and some stakeholder embarrassment would unwind it. The product manager is capable, mortified, and this is their first serious error. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Unwind the launch quietly and personally and tell the product manager it has been handled — no need to dwell.
- B) Have the product manager lead the unwinding — correcting the sequence, informing their stakeholders, and briefing the governance team — while supporting from behind, then run a blame-free debrief on how the condition was misread and what check would have caught it.
- C) Take over the situation, inform stakeholders personally, and note the error formally for the product manager's next performance review.
- D) Let the launch continue since unwinding causes embarrassment; retrofit the assessment clearance as fast as possible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B corrects the error properly and visibly while building the capability the error revealed missing, since the person leading their own recovery, visibly backed, preserves standing and signals that mistakes surface safely. Option D converts an honest mistake into a knowing breach; A teaches that errors vanish upward; C formalises blame and teaches concealment. This tests whether the candidate develops a person through a first honest error rather than absorbing or punishing it.

**Question 2 (easy) — The direction change request, third time this quarter**

A senior policy deputy director asks for a team to be redirected for the third time this quarter, toward a ministerial interest needing "something visible in six weeks." The previous two redirections produced work demonstrated once and shelved. The team is mid-way through a committed outcome improvement. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Redirect the team again — ministerial interest is the weather; teams adapt.
- B) Refuse on the team's behalf and inform the deputy director that the roadmap is fixed for the quarter.
- C) Meet the deputy director: acknowledge the ministerial need, surface the pattern (two prior redirections, both shelved, at a stated cost to committed outcomes), propose meeting the visibility need without derailing the team — an existing demo, a lightweight prototype from spare discovery capacity, or a genuinely scoped six-week slice if the need is real — and agree a decision rule for future redirections.
- D) Accept the redirection but have the team quietly continue the committed work at reduced pace, showing the deputy director only the ministerial workstream.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C acknowledges the legitimate need, surfaces the pattern as a value-for-money argument, offers graded cheaper alternatives, and converts a recurring ambush into governed process. Option A treats churn as weather when the record shows waste; B spends the relationship without an alternative; D is a hidden double-book that, once discovered, becomes a trust breach. This tests protecting a team through negotiation with evidence, not through blanket refusal or deception.

**Question 3 (easy) — Recruitment shortcut pressure**

Recruiting two product managers, after a strong first sift, a director under staffing pressure suggests skipping second interviews and taking the top two on paper, with clearance offered to bypass process. One paper-leader interviewed unevenly at sift; a third candidate interviewed exceptionally but scores lower on written criteria. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Take the director's route — speed matters, clearance is offered, and performance management exists for a reason.
- B) Run the full process as designed: explain to the director that the second interview exists precisely to resolve cases like the uneven paper-leader and the exceptional lower-scorer, offer to compress the timeline (days, not weeks) rather than delete the stage, and hold the panel to the published criteria.
- C) Skip the interviews but substitute personal judgement of the candidates for the missing stage.
- D) Escalate the director's suggestion to HR as an attempted breach of recruitment rules.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B holds the fairness obligations of merit-based recruitment while solving the director's actual problem (speed) through compression rather than deleting the exact stage where a contradictory evidence set needs resolving. Option A predictably mis-hires; C substitutes unstructured instinct for the process fair recruitment exists to discipline; D treats a poor suggestion, offered with clearance, as misconduct rather than declining it with reasons. This tests protecting recruitment fairness while still serving a legitimate underlying pressure.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two teams claim the same engineer**

The only engineer with deep knowledge of a legacy integration layer is claimed by two teams for the same six-week window: one for a security remediation with a hard external deadline, one for a data migration underpinning a ministerial commitment two quarters out. Both product managers have escalated, each convinced their need is absolute. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Give the engineer to Team One — security deadlines are hard, and decide the rest later.
- B) Split the engineer 50/50 across both teams for the six weeks.
- C) Convene both product managers with the engineer: sequence rather than split (security remediation's hard external deadline first), identify what migration preparation genuinely requires the engineer versus what others can advance meanwhile, put knowledge transfer into the plan so the single point of dependency shrinks, and reset Team Two's schedule openly with the stakeholders of the ministerial commitment.
- D) Tell the two product managers to resolve it between themselves — they were hired to make decisions.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Sequencing beats splitting for scarce-expertise work, where context-switching costs bite hardest; option C also decomposes the claimed need, schedules knowledge transfer to prevent the next collision, and resets the second team's schedule openly rather than absorbing the slip silently. Option D abdicates a decision that structurally cannot be resolved a level down — each product manager is correctly advocating their product's need, and only the portfolio owner holds the trade-off frame. This tests system-level resource allocation, not just fair division.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The metric a candidate's own dashboard contradicts**

A flagship KPI (renewal completion) shows 84%, green, on a definition changed two quarters ago (abandoned renewals restarted within 7 days now count as single successful journeys); on the old definition the figure would be 76%, below the 80% amber threshold. The change was properly approved but the dashboard does not annotate it, and nobody has asked. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Report 84% — the definition was properly approved, and reopening it invites confusion.
- B) Report 76% instead, since it reflects the "real" performance.
- C) Report 84% with the definition change annotated, show the old-basis 76% as a comparator with the trend on both bases, and propose the dashboard permanently footnotes definitional breaks — separately assessing whether the new definition is actually the better measure of user success.
- D) Quietly ask the analytics team whether the definition could be revisited next year.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C reports the official figure while surfacing the definitional break and both trends, and proposes a systemic fix (footnoting breaks by default) rather than treating this as a one-off. Option A is procedurally defensible but substantively misleading; B unilaterally reverts governance in the other direction; D defers indefinitely while the convenient number stands. This tests transparency about a metric even when — especially when — it flatters the candidate's own reporting.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The star performer with a pattern**

The strongest product manager delivers exceptional outcomes, but three separate informal channels over two months report abrasive cross-team behaviour: interrupting a junior researcher, dismissing a delivery manager's risk flag with visible contempt, being "someone people prepare for." No formal complaint exists. Their products carry the group's hardest deliverables. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Wait for a formal complaint — informal reports are hearsay, and the delivery record speaks for itself.
- B) Raise it directly and specifically with them now: name the reported behaviours and their impact, listen genuinely for context and pressures, make the expectation unambiguous, agree observable changes, and follow up — while separately watching the settings where it occurs and making clear to the group, through personal behaviour, what the standard is.
- C) Restructure meetings so the product manager attends fewer cross-team settings.
- D) Mention it lightly at their next scheduled review in two months, sandwiched between praise for delivery.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three independent channels over two months is a pattern, and the absence of formal complaint reflects reporters' safety, not the behaviour's acceptability. Option B is specific, immediate, genuinely two-directional, and structured for follow-through. Option A prices the delivery record as if it did not already include the discretionary effort of people who have stopped raising risks; C treats exposure rather than behaviour; D dilutes the message and delays it for two months. This tests holding the strongest performer to the same standard as anyone else, especially because a group calibrates norms on what its leader tolerates from its best people.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Continue, pivot, or stop, with the sponsor in the room**

A two-year-old product, sponsored personally by a director who champions the wider portfolio, shows flat usage at a third of business case projection, unit costs double target, two failed pivots, and user research showing the underlying need is real but better met through an adjacent channel. The quarterly review, chaired by the director, is next week. The candidate's evidence-based view is stop-and-fold. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Recommend another pivot — stopping a director's sponsored product at their own review risks the portfolio relationship.
- B) Brief the director before the review: walk through the evidence and the stop-and-fold recommendation, acknowledge what the product taught, give them room to test the reasoning and shape the narrative, then take the recommendation to the review with the analysis in full.
- C) Take the stop recommendation straight to the review without warning — surprise prevents lobbying.
- D) Present the evidence neutrally at the review and let the director draw their own conclusion.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence clearly supports stop; a third pivot (option A) is escalation of commitment with the added corruption of using public money as social lubricant. Option B's pre-brief is sound governance practice with sponsors, giving time to test the reasoning, credit for what the product taught, and agency in the narrative — without changing the recommendation itself. Option C converts an evidence conversation into a status confrontation; D abdicates the recommendation the role is accountable for making. This tests managing the choreography of a difficult recommendation without softening its conclusion.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The associate's flawed analysis, already circulating**

An associate product manager's benefits analysis for a stakeholder working group is discovered, after circulating, to compute benefits against total users rather than affected users, overstating the headline roughly threefold. The working group meets in four days; one stakeholder has already quoted the headline upward. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct the document personally, reissue it quietly, and tell the associate at their next one-to-one.
- B) Tell the associate the error privately, then work through the correction with them and have them reissue it with a clear note before the meeting — including direct notice to the stakeholder who quoted it — and debrief afterwards on both the method and the experience of correcting in public.
- C) Let the meeting proceed on the circulated figures — the error favours the candidate's programme, and the methodology is arguable.
- D) Email the working group flagging the error and noting the analysis was produced by a junior colleague and should have been checked.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B corrects the error at the speed it travelled, works through the correction with (not around) the associate, notifies the stakeholder who already quoted it, and debriefs on both method and the experience of a public correction. Option C compounds a favourable error into a credibility debt; D names a junior colleague's fault to the group it circulated to; A corrects competently but removes the associate from their own recovery. This tests correcting errors that reflect well on the candidate's own programme with the same urgency as ones that do not, and developing rather than sidelining the person responsible.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the accessibility non-negotiable at programme scale**

A programme board, needing a flagship service live before a legislative deadline eight weeks out, is told meeting the date requires deferring remaining accessibility fixes (screen reader failures on two of seven journey steps) into a post-launch backlog "with a committed fix-by date." The chair asks for the product view. Ask for a ranking:

1. State that launching with known screen reader failures excludes disabled users from a statutory service and carries legal risk under accessibility regulations; propose the scope alternative — launch on the deadline with the five compliant steps plus an assisted route covering the two failing steps while they are fixed — and if the board still prefers full launch, require the risk to be formally accepted at board level with legal advice minuted.
2. Support the deferral with the committed fix-by date — legislative deadlines outrank all other considerations, and a backlog commitment is a real plan.
3. Refuse: state that launch will not be allowed until every accessibility fix is complete, whatever the legislative consequence.
4. Suggest the board seek further analysis of the options and return to the question at its next monthly meeting.

- A) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 1; least effective: 2
- C) Most effective: 3; least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 3; least effective: 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 names the stakes accurately, engineers a genuine third option (compliant steps plus assisted route) meeting both the date and the access obligation, and forces formal, minuted ownership of any deviation. Response 4, despite sounding cautious, forecloses the good options by burning the weeks the assisted-route build needs, deciding by default under the guise of diligence. Response 3 exceeds the candidate's authority but is at least legible and honest; response 2 knowingly endorses excluding disabled users on an unenforceable backlog promise. This tests recognising that at board level, a deferral that forecloses good options can be more damaging than an honestly wrong answer given openly.

**Question 10 (challenging) — A candidate's strategy, their evidence**

A channel strategy authored two years ago — consolidating five entry routes into two — is 70% complete with measurable gains. A junior product manager, visibly nervous, brings analysis suggesting the final phase will harm 60,000 annual users of the remaining specialist route: completion rates 30 points higher there than on the consolidated route, support contacts tripling post-migration in the pilot cohort. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Thank them, but proceed as planned — the strategy's overall gains are proven, the final phase completes the vision, and exceptions erode strategic coherence.
- B) Examine their analysis seriously and visibly: test the data with them (cohort comparability, pilot representativeness), and if it holds, adapt the strategy — retain or redesign the specialist route for that population within the two-route architecture — crediting their analysis in the change, and telling the group why the strategy moved.
- C) Hand the analysis to an own-team analyst to quietly re-derive before responding at all.
- D) Ask the product manager to present it to the programme board and let governance decide.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B tests the analysis rigorously with its author, adapts the strategy's mechanism while preserving its intent if the evidence holds, and credits the challenger publicly. Option A treats coherence that requires harming 60,000 identifiable users as aesthetics rather than strategy, and treats sunk gains from earlier phases as justifying the marginal decision; C sidelines the junior colleague's work; D outsources both analytical and political risk to the person with least cover. This tests whether a candidate can amend their own strategy in public on evidence, with credit flowing to whoever brought it.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The confidential heads-up**

A trusted peer in finance informally and in confidence indicates next month's spending review will likely cut the product group's budget by around 25%, with decisions fluid for roughly two weeks. Nothing is announced. One team is about to sign a 12-month contractor extension that would consume much of the flexibility a cut would demand. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell the product managers everything now — they deserve the truth, and planning needs lead time.
- B) Do nothing differently — unannounced decisions are not actionable, and acting on leaks rewards leaking.
- C) Act on the substance without breaching the confidence: pause or shorten the contractor extension on defensible portfolio grounds, quietly stress-test the group's plans against a 25% scenario, prepare the value evidence that could inform the still-fluid decision through legitimate channels, and say nothing that identifies the source or the specifics until announcement.
- D) Use the two-week window to lobby the finance director directly, citing what was heard.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C holds three duties simultaneously — to the confidence, to the teams, and to the still-forming organisational decision — pausing the flexibility-destroying contractor extension on grounds statable openly, and routing evidence through legitimate channels. Option A spreads unactionable anxiety and risks identifying the source; B falsely treats foreknowledge as unusable; D burns the source, the channel, and the candidate's own standing. This tests metabolising privileged information into defensible action rather than into quotable claims.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The supplier demo that doesn't add up**

At a market-engagement day, a supplier demonstrates an AI triage tool claiming "94% accuracy, deployed with three government departments." The director, enthusiastic, asks for a pilot to start this quarter. Observations: the demo ran on curated data; "accuracy" was undefined; the three departments are unnamed; the candidate's own casework has an asymmetric error cost (a false "routine" label on a safeguarding-relevant case is far costlier than the reverse). Procurement rules apply; teams are already at capacity. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Decline to engage — AI triage vendors overclaim routinely, and the observations confirm this one is not credible.
- B) Channel the director's enthusiasm into a structured evaluation: define success measures first (accuracy by error type on this service's own data, with the safeguarding asymmetry weighted; baseline against current triage), require the reference-department evidence, run any pilot through proper procurement as a time-boxed trial against those pre-registered measures, and give the director a realistic quarter-shaped first step — the evaluation design, not the deployment.
- C) Start the pilot as asked — the director's instruction is clear, procurement can be handled as a small-value trial, and concerns can be tested inside the pilot itself.
- D) Ask the supplier to re-run the demo on a sample of this service's own data before deciding anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B defines success measures — weighted for the safeguarding asymmetry a single accuracy figure would miss entirely — before any deployment, routes any pilot through proper procurement, and gives the director a genuine quarter-shaped deliverable rather than refusing outright. Option A converts plausible scepticism into a closed door; C risks improper disaggregation and undefined success criteria; D hands the supplier the candidate's data with no framework or protection. This tests meeting enthusiasm with structure and claims with the candidate's own definitions, especially where an asymmetric error cost changes what "accurate" should even mean.

### Administration tips

- Score for the recurring senior fingerprint: acting through structures, engineering third options before accepting binaries, and pre-briefing choreography while never negotiating conclusions.
- Watch for whether a candidate's response is right one level down but a delegation failure at this level — personally absorbing recovery work, doing the fix rather than developing someone through it.
- For most/least formats, score the least-effective response as the one that decides by default (a deferral that forecloses options), not merely the most timid one.
- Note whether the candidate holds non-negotiables (user safety, accessibility, evidential honesty, fair process, data protection, procurement propriety) even under senior or sponsor pressure.
- Watch for scenarios probing authorship or sponsor bias — a candidate's own strategy, framework, or championed product — and score for whether evidence still wins.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a star-performer or sponsor exemption — different standards for people whose delivery or patronage the candidate depends on.
- Missing "kindness that stunts" — quiet fixes, absorbed recoveries, deferred feedback, praise-sandwiched corrections.
- Rewarding managed truth rather than truth itself — filtered dashboards, unannotated definition changes, evidence presented without a recommendation.
- Accepting escalation of commitment dressed as strategic coherence — a third pivot, a completing phase, harm to identifiable users justified by sunk gains.
- Missing a binary framing (full launch versus missed deadline, this team versus that team) where the strongest response engineers a third option.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through portfolio-level pattern reading, dependency chains propagated to their honest conclusions (including infeasibility), and strategic recommendations that redirect rather than merely defend or destroy; the auditing arithmetic of this level — group rates built from raw numbers, chained savings compounded correctly, cumulative benefit tables, decomposed averages, and expected values delivered with their error bars; mandate gradients, governance verbs, reservation language, stacked conditions, and the escalation paragraph that lets a director decide in five minutes; and the senior fingerprints in situational judgement — recoveries given to the people who need the growth, sponsors pre-briefed but never appeased, non-negotiables held through engineered third options, confidences metabolised into defensible action, and strategies amended in public when the evidence arrives.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items at this level reward the quality of reasoning and the symmetry of a candidate's standards as much as the final choice. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
