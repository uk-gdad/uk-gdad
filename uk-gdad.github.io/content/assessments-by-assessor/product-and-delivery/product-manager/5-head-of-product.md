# Head of Product - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of product. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are standardised exercises measuring the cognitive capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict performance in a role. At head-of-product level, the job-specific versions are built from material this role actually governs: organisational product strategy, profession-wide workforce and pay decisions, community health data, business cases owned through their life cycles, cross-government relationships, and standards set and raised across product management in an organisation. The decisions they simulate: prioritising across multiple complex portfolios with data, influencing the priorities of senior stakeholders, managing recruitment, development, moves and exits across an entire community, line managing senior and lead product managers, and representing product community interests to organisational management — including when those interests are inconvenient to the room.

This matters at the top of the profession's ladder for three reasons. Errors are systemic: a misread trend or an imprecisely read policy at this level miscalibrates an organisation's product strategy, a community's pay case, or a profession's standards. This role is the profession's assessor: overseeing every community member's role, assignment, development, pay and performance, contributing to service assessments, and setting the standards others are measured against. And this role is the exemplar: the reflective practice this guide demands is the practice asked of a whole community.

Each of the four sections follows the same pattern: what it measures and why it matters at head level; how its dimensions map to the role's named skills; practice questions progressing from easy to challenging with complete worked explanations tying each technique to organisational product leadership; then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score for conclusions no stronger than the evidence, unknowns named, and the confidence to say "this cannot be concluded yet" when that is true — at this level, calibrated uncertainty is itself a deliverable. Use each section's per-item timing as a guide for planning a session, and debrief against the explanations below.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes this role demands using realistic material. At head-of-product level that material is organisational: strategy documents whose internal consistency this role owns, community workforce data, cross-portfolio outcome sets, business cases developed and iterated, governance structures designed, and the reasoning of senior people whose arguments must be evaluated, strengthened, or challenged in rooms where the stakes are organisational direction.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions across pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving, scored objectively against a senior professional or executive norm group.

At this level the assessment simulates three distinctive cognitive loads: governing at one remove (almost everything about a community's products arrives mediated through dashboards and reports, so reasoning must include reasoning about the reporting layer itself); deciding across incommensurables (organisational prioritisation trades products against people against standards against relationships); and reasoning in public (inferences become organisational positions the moment they are voiced).

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Life cycle management** at organisational scale: distinguishing structural causes from noise and from instrument effects.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic ownership** and the duty to **set and raise standards**: reasoning validly about what rule systems require, permit, and imply.
- **Error checking** maps to **Creating value for money** and **Managing product outcomes**: audit disciplines applied to a candidate's own artefacts first.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Product management**: organisational sequencing with trade-offs made explicit and defensible.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Product leadership** and **Agile and Lean practices**: decomposing organisation-scale, politically loaded problems into parts evidence can settle and structures can fix.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading community-level signals**

A product community's quarterly indicators: voluntary turnover 8% (stable for two years); internal moves between portfolios up from 3 to 11 this quarter; applications per advertised product post down from 40 to 16 over three campaigns; the annual community survey shows "I can see a career path here" down 12 points. Ask for the synthesis that best serves a head of product.

- A) Turnover is stable, so the community is healthy; the other signals are noise.
- B) The signals cohere as an early-stage retention risk: people are not yet leaving (turnover stable) but are repositioning internally (moves up), external attractiveness is falling (applications down), and the survey names the mechanism — career-path visibility; act now on progression clarity, while the leaving hasn't started.
- C) Applications falling means the employment market has improved; nothing internal is indicated.
- D) The survey drop is the only real signal; commission a deeper survey.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Turnover is a lagging indicator — the last thing to move; internal repositioning, falling applications, and declining career-path sentiment are leading indicators of the same variable, with the survey naming the mechanism. Option A anchors on the reassuring lagging indicator; D responds to information by commissioning more information, a head-level procrastination pattern. This tests reading leading indicators before lagging ones, and acting in the window where action (progression frameworks, honest career conversations) is still cheap.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a standard the candidate set**

A standard states: "Every product must have a named product manager. A product manager may be named on at most two products, unless the head of product grants an exception, which must be time-limited." An audit shows one product manager is named on three products, with no exception recorded. Ask which conclusion follows necessarily.

- A) The product manager is at fault and should be spoken to.
- B) The standard has been breached.
- C) Either an exception was granted but not recorded, or the standard has been breached — and since the standard's control is the record, the compliance position is failed either way until resolved.
- D) The standard is too strict and should be revised.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The facts are consistent with two worlds — genuine breach, or granted-but-unrecorded exception — so option B overconcludes. Option C's second clause is the head-level content: in a governance system, the record is the control, so an unrecorded exception is indistinguishable from a breach and the compliance position fails either way. This tests recognising that an audit ambiguity is a defect in the control design as much as in the behaviour, before assigning blame or revising the standard.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a strategy document's internal logic**

A draft strategy states: (1) reduce the portfolio from 40 to 25 products over two years, retiring low-value products; (2) every business unit will retain at least its current product coverage; (3) no retirement proceeds without a transition path for users; (4) headcount stays flat, with released capacity reinvested in priority products. Ask which pair of statements is in direct tension.

- A) Statements 1 and 3
- B) Statements 1 and 2
- C) Statements 3 and 4
- D) Statements 2 and 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reducing 40 products to 25 removes 15; unless every removed product comes from units with duplicate coverage (unestablished), some unit loses coverage it currently has, contradicting statement 2. The other pairs are compatible. This tests adversarially pairing every commitment in a strategy against every other before publication — a document negotiated with different audiences for different sentences will contain exactly this kind of unstated trade-off.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation at head level**

Monday morning: (1) an executive committee paper on next year's product strategy needs final revisions before Thursday (~half a day); (2) a lead product manager asks urgently to discuss "something sensitive" about a team member, wanting 30 minutes; (3) a director in another business unit disputes the community's involvement in their programme, copying the candidate's director; (4) a cross-government community session needs a theme decision this week. Ask for the most defensible sequencing.

- A) 2 (today, 30 minutes), 1 (protected time before Thursday), 3 (a measured reply within a day or two, after brief thought), 4 (any day this week)
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — the executive paper and the director dispute both outrank a team-level issue
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4 — reputational challenges copied to the candidate's director always come first
- D) 1, 2, 4, 3 — ignore the director's email; it will resolve itself

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 2 goes first not because it certainly outranks the strategy paper, but because its expected cost of delay is highest and its cost of hearing is lowest. Item 3 gets a measured reply after brief thought rather than a same-hour reaction, since heat-of-the-moment replies to copied-in disputes escalate inter-unit conflicts. This tests sequencing by expected cost of delay for people-sensitive items and by quality of response for reputational ones, with quick triage (booking the 30 minutes, protecting the half-day, acknowledging the email) happening in the first hour regardless.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The instrument or the world**

Six of fifteen products show completion-rate declines of 2–5 points this quarter; all six use a central analytics platform that changed its session-tracking methodology mid-quarter; the nine products on other instruments show no decline. Each of the six teams has drafted a plausible local explanation. Ask for the strongest reading.

- A) Six independent product problems, each with a plausible local explanation; let each team pursue its own fix.
- B) The partition of decline-by-instrument (all six decliners on the changed platform; all nine non-decliners elsewhere) points to a measurement artefact, not six coincident product failures; validate by re-basing one or two products' figures on the old methodology, pause the six local fix-workstreams pending that check, and note how readily teams generated plausible causal stories for an artefact.
- C) The declines are real and the platform change is coincidence, since teams found explanations.
- D) Switch all products off the central platform.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Decline correlates perfectly with instrument, not product type or user base — the probability of six independent causes distributing exactly along instrument lines is negligible. This tests recognising an instrument change as the prior hypothesis to test before six teams spend a quarter fixing noise, and the observation that plausible causal stories are generated on demand for any number placed in front of people — explanation-availability is not evidence.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Auditing the survival of a candidate's own business case**

Three years ago a business case funded a product operating model at £2.1m annually, justified by £3.5m projected savings. Honest stocktake: contractor spend is down £1.4m (verified); duplication savings (£1.2m projected) were never baselined and cannot now be verified; delivery-speed benefits (£0.9m projected) rest on a real 34% cycle-time improvement never converted into cashable terms. Ask for the strongest refresh position.

- A) Restate the £3.5m as achieved, since the model is clearly working.
- B) Report verified benefits of £1.4m against £2.1m cost, state plainly that £2.1m of projected benefits were never baselined or converted and are therefore unverifiable as money, present the cycle-time gain in its native units with a defensible conversion range, commit to baselining the previously unmeasured benefits from now, and argue continuation on the honest position — including its implication that the case currently verifies at less than its cost.
- C) Quietly rebase the case around new future benefits and avoid mentioning the original projections.
- D) Report £1.4m verified plus the original £2.1m projections as "on track", since absence of measurement is not evidence of absence.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B states the case currently verifies below cost and argues continuation on the full honest picture, including a real gain shown in native units with a range rather than a retro-fitted point estimate. Option D is true and irrelevant — the question is what can be asserted, and "on track" asserts achievement; C works once, until anyone reads the original case. This tests owning a business case's own measurement failures rather than claiming, obscuring, or quietly rebasing around them.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The cross-government reuse decision**

Another department offers a mature casework-triage platform at marginal cost under a cross-government agreement. It fits roughly 70% of requirements; the missing 30% includes two essential workflows, one covered by the offering department's roadmap within a year, the other "open to co-development." A bespoke build costs £2.8m; adoption plus co-development costs £0.9m over two years. Ask for the strongest position.

- A) Decline: 70% fit means 30% failure, and caseworkers' essential workflows are non-negotiable.
- B) Adopt fully now and require caseworkers to adapt their workflows to the platform.
- C) Pursue adoption structurally: validate the 70% and the two gaps with actual users (not just requirement lists — observed workflows), negotiate the co-development of the second workflow into the cross-government agreement with governance and exit terms, sequence migration so the first products to move are those the current fit serves, and treat the £1.9m differential as buying membership of a multi-department platform whose maintenance is shared — with the risks (their roadmap, their priorities) explicitly priced and governed.
- D) Wait a year to see whether their roadmap delivers the missing workflow.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option A treats a requirements-list claim of "essential" as a veto rather than something to validate by observation; B burns caseworker trust by fiat; D buys the same information later at the cost of a year of ageing products and no influence over the roadmap. Option C validates by observation, secures the gap through governed co-development, and sequences migration to let the fit lead. This tests treating cross-government reuse as a negotiated, governed, sequenced relationship rather than either a download or a refusal.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The pattern in assessment results**

Over two years, 14 of an organisation's service assessments took place: 11 passed. The three "not met" results share features: all three teams had product managers in their first year, and all three failed on evidencing how user research changed the product. Preparation support is a written guide and an optional dry-run. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Note that 11 of 14 is a strong pass rate and continue as is.
- B) Treat the pattern as a targeted capability gap with a targeted fix: first-year product managers fail specifically on evidencing research-to-product causation, so build that specific competence into first-year development — research-traceability practice as routine work, not assessment prep — make the dry-run default rather than optional for first-year product managers, and check whether the guide even addresses evidence trails; then watch the next cycle.
- C) Require every team to be assessed only when their product manager has over a year's tenure.
- D) Challenge the assessment standard itself, since it disproportionately fails new product managers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is a capability-system signal, not a performance verdict or standards complaint: evidencing research-to-product causation is a craft habit experienced product managers do automatically. Option C games the exposure by delaying assessment rather than fixing the gap; D escalates against a standard that is plainly finding something real. This tests distinguishing a fixable development-system gap from a standards problem, and building the fix into routine practice rather than assessment-specific cramming.

**Question 9 (moderate) — The two dashboards that disagree**

A transformation office dashboard shows milestone attainment falling (82%→71%); a community dashboard shows outcome (KPI) attainment rising (60%→74%) over the same window; both are accurate. The transformation office's executive paper leads with the decline. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The transformation office's figure is wrong and should be withdrawn.
- B) Both figures are right and they measure different things: milestone attainment tracks conformance to plans, outcome attainment tracks delivery of value — and the divergence is informative, not contradictory: teams are increasingly re-planning around evidence (missing milestones set quarters ago) while hitting outcomes more often; the executive paper should present both series with this reading, and the deeper question — which measure should govern — is a strategy conversation to be led by the candidate, not a dashboard dispute.
- C) Accept the milestone framing and instruct teams to hit more milestones.
- D) Present the outcome figure to the executive as the true one and dismiss milestone tracking as legacy thinking.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Milestones falling while outcomes rise is the statistical signature of a community re-planning around evidence rather than a delivery problem — if both series fell, that would differ. Options A and D fight the other instrument; C surrenders to it. This tests reading two disagreeing but accurate instruments jointly for what their divergence reveals, and elevating "which measure should govern" to a strategy conversation rather than a dashboard contest.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Sequencing an organisational transformation**

Four organisational changes: (T) migrate all teams to a new target operating model; (S) launch revised product standards, which the model assumes; (C) capability uplift (training/coaching to those standards); (P) a new progression framework mapped to the standards. Constraints: S must finalise before C's curriculum and before P; T assumes teams already operate under S (migrating then changing standards forces rework); the board wants T started immediately, in parallel with everything else; C takes two quarters after curriculum build (one quarter); P finalises one quarter after S, and staff consultation on P needs one further quarter before anything affecting roles (including T) can conclude for affected staff. Ask for the soundest position to take to the board.

- A) Agree to start T immediately in parallel — board support is precious and re-work risk is speculative.
- B) Sequence S first (it is the load-bearing dependency for C, P, and T alike), run C's curriculum build and P's finalisation in parallel immediately after S, start T's preparation now but hold its conclusion for affected staff until P's consultation completes — and show the board the dependency arithmetic: starting T "immediately" in full would deliver visible restructuring one quarter earlier at the price of re-work against unfinalised standards and a consultation-sequencing breach that could void the process for affected staff.
- C) Deliver T first as the board wishes, then retrofit S, C, and P to the migrated organisation.
- D) Decline to sequence and deliver all four workstreams simultaneously with a programme office managing the interactions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** S is upstream of C, P, and T's stability; P's consultation must complete before T concludes for affected staff. Option B lets T's preparation start now (genuine board visibility) while holding conclusion until consultation completes, and shows the board the price of full-immediate-T rather than either obeying or refusing outright. Option C inverts the entire logic of a standards-based model; D pretends simultaneity dissolves dependencies rather than converting them into daily collision management. This tests decomposing a politically demanded item into the part that can start now and the part the constraints genuinely govern.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The flaw in the benchmarking argument**

A commissioned review argues: "Comparable organisations operate at one product manager per 2.3 delivery teams; yours operates at one per 1.6. Moving to the benchmark ratio would release 9 posts (£810k) with no delivery impact, since the comparators deliver successfully at that ratio." Ask for the strongest critique.

- A) The arithmetic is wrong.
- B) The benchmark transfers a ratio without transferring its conditions: "comparable organisations" is unevidenced comparability (their product complexity, team seniority, governance load, and delivery models are unexamined); the comparators' success at 2.3 is survivorship-filtered (organisations failing at thin ratios don't appear as benchmarks); the causal claim "no delivery impact" is asserted from cross-sectional correlation, not from any organisation having moved from 1.6 to 2.3 and measured the consequence; and the review nowhere costs what product managers at 1.6 currently produce that 2.3 would drop — the correct response is to require the comparability evidence and the transition-effect evidence, while offering this organisation's own data on what the 0.7 difference in coverage actually does.
- C) Consultancy benchmarks are always wrong and the review should be dismissed.
- D) Accept the finding — external benchmarks are more objective than internal advocacy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The failures are inferential: unevidenced comparability, survivorship-filtered comparators, a causal leap from cross-sectional correlation, and an uncosted counterfactual for the current coverage. Option C's blanket dismissal loses a winnable evidence contest by seeming defensive; D mistakes external for objective, when externality removes one bias and imports others. This tests catching all four inferential failures in a ratio-transfer argument and responding with counter-evidence rather than either accepting or dismissing wholesale — a symmetric rule that applies equally when a ratio would favour the candidate's own community.

**Question 12 (challenging) — When a candidate's two hats disagree**

Facing a 15% cost reduction, analysis trusted by the candidate shows the least-damaging organisational option includes reducing the product community by six posts (vacancy freezes, two compulsory-adjacent exits); an alternative favoured by other directorates protects headcount but cuts discovery and research budgets, which the same analysis says destroys more value less visibly. The leadership team decides next week. Ask for the strongest position.

- A) Fight for zero product-post reductions — the community trusts the candidate to protect them, and that trust is the foundation of the role.
- B) Advocate the budget-cut option since it preserves the candidate's own people, and people are harder to rebuild than budgets.
- C) Argue the evidence as an organisational leader: present the value-destruction comparison honestly — including that it points at the candidate's own community's posts — negotiate the implementation to minimise harm (vacancy-freeze first, redeployment and cross-government moves before exits, development support for those affected), and then tell the community the truth about the position taken and why, owning it personally rather than attributing it to the leadership team.
- D) Abstain from the discussion, declaring the conflict of interest, and let the other directors decide.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The organisational hat governs the decision (arguing against one's own trusted analysis because it is inconvenient would be the same interest-driven reasoning being critiqued in others); the champion hat governs implementation (freeze-first, redeployment, genuine support) and the telling (personal ownership, not hiding behind "the leadership team decided"). Option D removes the only product-literate voice from the room at the exact moment it decides product's future. This tests whether a candidate's authority with the organisation (arguing evidence against self-interest) and authority with the community (owning hard decisions personally) both survive a genuine conflict, rather than either surrendering one hat to the other or abstaining.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies leading indicators as more actionable than lagging ones in a community-health scenario.
- Note whether the candidate treats an audit ambiguity as a control-design defect rather than immediately assigning individual blame.
- Watch for whether the candidate adversarially pairs a strategy document's commitments before accepting it as coherent.
- Score for whether the candidate tests an instrument-change hypothesis before accepting six independent causal stories.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes evidenced comparability from an unevidenced ratio-transfer in a benchmarking argument.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that anchors on a lagging indicator (turnover, pass rate) as reassurance while ignoring leading signals.
- Missing when a candidate explains a number before validating that it reflects the world rather than the instrument.
- Rewarding a strategy review that fails to test whether every commitment can jointly hold.
- Accepting a benchmark or ratio transferred without its underlying conditions, especially when it flatters the candidate's own position.
- Missing when a candidate lets one hat (organisational leader, community champion) govern a decision that belongs to the other.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures accuracy and judgement with the numbers this role genuinely trades in. At head-of-product level those numbers are organisational: community workforce and pay data, profession-wide budgets, portfolio-of-portfolios outcome sets, business cases developed and owned, benchmarking submissions, and derived figures whose construction must be both performed flawlessly and audited relentlessly in the work of others.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, calculator permitted, scored objectively against a senior professional or executive norm group.

This role is simultaneously the profession's chief producer and chief consumer of consequential arithmetic — producing business cases and pay cases whose credibility is exactly as strong as their weakest computation, and consuming every case, dashboard, benchmark, and benefit claim that passes upward as the final product-literate audit before figures acquire executive force.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Workforce and pay arithmetic** maps to the duties to **oversee role, assignments, development, pay and performance** and **manage recruitment, development, moves and exits**.
- **Business case construction and audit** maps to **Creating value for money**: developing and owning cases through the life cycle.
- **Cross-portfolio aggregation and prioritisation** maps to **Managing product outcomes**: aggregation done honestly and decomposition done instinctively.
- **Benchmark and comparator arithmetic** maps to **Product leadership** and **representing product community interests**: reading external comparisons for their conditions, samples, and causal claims.
- **Projection and scenario discipline** maps to **Strategic ownership**: projections with stated assumptions, ranges, supplying pools, and break-points.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Community headcount and coverage**

A community has 42 product professionals covering 60 delivery teams. The strategy adds 12 teams next year; 5 professionals are expected to leave to normal turnover. Ask how many people must be recruited to keep the current coverage ratio.

- A) 5
- B) 8
- C) 13
- D) 17

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Current ratio 0.7 per team; future need for 72 teams ≈ 50.4; future supply without recruitment 37; requirement ≈ 13.4, rounding to 13 — option C. Option B computes growth need only, forgetting replacement of the 5 leavers. This tests recognising that recruitment need is growth plus replacement, with replacement typically the larger, steadier term at community scale.

**Question 2 (easy) — Turnover cost, computed honestly**

A community of 42 loses 6 to external moves. Assumptions: recruitment cost £8,000/hire; average vacancy 4 months, half contractor-filled at a £300/day premium (21 working days/month); productivity ramp costing 2 months' output valued at £9,000/month. Ask for the estimated annual cost of this turnover.

- A) £48,000
- B) £234,000
- C) £156,000
- D) £310,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Recruitment: £48,000. Contractor premium: 3 vacancies × 4 months × 21 days × £300 = £75,600. Ramp: 6 × 2 × £9,000 = £108,000. Total ≈ £231,600 ≈ £234,000 — option B. This tests costing each component correctly and, in a strong answer, stating the model's own perimeter honestly (it excludes unfilled-vacancy output loss and knowledge loss, so the figure is a floor) — the honesty that makes the figure usable in a retention business case.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pay distribution and the median**

Product manager pay: mean £52,400; median £48,200; 10th percentile £41,000; 90th percentile £68,500. A staff representative says "half our product managers earn less than £48,200"; a finance colleague says "the average product manager earns £52,400." Ask which statement about the two claims is correct.

- A) The representative is wrong; the mean is the better summary.
- B) Both claims are accurate as stated, and the £4,200 gap between mean and median indicates the distribution is skewed upward by higher salaries — the top of the distribution pulls the mean above the experience of the typical person, so "the average product manager earns £52,400" is arithmetically true and experientially misleading.
- C) The two figures contradict each other; one dataset must be wrong.
- D) The percentiles show most salaries are near £52,400.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both statistics are correctly stated and coexist without contradiction; the mean-median gap itself is a skew indicator. This tests recognising that mean and median answer different questions, and holding both — median for fairness/experience, mean for budget, the gap as a standing skew indicator — rather than treating one as simply "the" correct summary.

**Question 4 (easy) — Portfolio outcome aggregation**

Three portfolios report KPI attainment: Portfolio One 14 of 20 (70%); Portfolio Two 6 of 8 (75%); Portfolio Three 22 of 32 (68.75%). A draft states "Community KPI attainment: 71.25% (average of the three portfolios)." Ask for the correct community figure and whether the draft's method matters.

- A) 71.25% — the draft is right, and the method is fine.
- B) 70% — computed from totals (42 of 60 KPIs met); the draft's unweighted average happens to land close this time because the portfolios' sizes are not wildly different, but the method is wrong and will mislead badly whenever a small portfolio outperforms or underperforms.
- C) 68.75% — the largest portfolio's figure should stand for the community.
- D) 75% — the best portfolio shows what the community can achieve.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 42÷60=70%, versus the draft's unweighted 71.25% mean. This tests the standing rule — aggregate raw numerators and denominators, never percentages — and recognising that a near-miss this quarter teaches the wrong lesson ("the shortcut is roughly right") until a quarter when a small portfolio's swing produces a materially wrong headline.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The recruitment campaign's diversity arithmetic**

A product manager campaign: 360 applications, 40% from ethnic minority backgrounds (144). Sift passed 90 (30 minority, 60 other). Interviews appointed 12 (3 minority, 9 other). Ask what the stage-by-stage rates show.

- A) The process is fair: 25% of appointments were diverse candidates.
- B) Ethnic minority candidates passed sift at 20.8% versus 33.3% for other candidates, and converted interview at 10% versus 15% — the funnel narrows representation at both stages (40% of applications → 33.3% of sift passes → 25% of appointments), with the sift the larger drop; investigate both stages, starting with sift criteria and scoring.
- C) The final 25% exceeds the workforce benchmark, so no investigation is needed.
- D) The numbers are too small to compute anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sift pass rates: 30÷144=20.8% versus 60÷216=27.8%; interview conversion: 3÷30=10% versus 9÷60=15%; representation narrows at every stage. This tests computing group rates at each funnel stage rather than reading only the endpoint, and recognising that fairness is measured against the applicant pool the campaign attracted, not the incumbent workforce.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The pay case's cost, precisely**

Building a pay case: 42 staff, current pay bill £2.226m. Proposal: £3,000 targeted uplift for 15 hard-to-fill specialists, plus a 2% general uplift for all 42 (applied to current salaries, before the targeted uplift). On-costs add 28% to any salary increase. Ask for the total annual cost.

- A) £89,520
- B) £114,586
- C) £101,232
- D) £57,600

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Targeted: £45,000. General: £2.226m×0.02=£44,520. Subtotal £89,520; on-costs £89,520×0.28≈£25,066; total ≈£114,586 — option B. Option A omits on-costs, the single most common error in draft pay cases. This tests including on-costs on the full salary increase and applying uplifts in the sequence specified, since ambiguity about ordering invites both cost disputes and staff grievances.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Strategy targets and the base effect**

Two-year strategy targets: digital transactions +50%, cost per transaction −20%, total processing cost "flat or falling". Year-two actuals: transactions 5.0m→7.8m; unit cost £3.20→£2.48; total cost £16.0m→£19.3m. A director calls the strategy "a failure on its own cost test." Ask for the correct assessment of the three targets.

- A) All three targets were missed.
- B) Transactions +56% (met), unit cost −22.5% (met), but total cost rose 20.6% because volume growth outran unit-cost reduction (1.56 × 0.775 = 1.209) — the third target was arithmetically incoherent with the first two: any volume growth above ~29% at the achieved unit cost makes "flat total cost" impossible, so the strategy's real failure was setting three targets that could not jointly hold, and the honest account says so rather than defending or conceding "failure".
- C) The director is wrong: total cost is irrelevant when unit cost falls.
- D) Transactions met, unit cost missed, total cost met.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 7.8÷5.0=+56%; 2.48÷3.20=−22.5%; 19.3÷16.0=+20.6%, and the identity 1.56×0.775=1.209 confirms the total-cost rise follows necessarily from the other two achievements. This tests recognising that the three targets were jointly infeasible from the day they were signed — a target-setting failure to own and correct, not a delivery failure to defend or concede, and multiplying target identities together before signing any multi-metric target set in future.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The consolidation case's double count**

Case One (consolidating three casework products) claims £600k annual savings, £220k from "decommissioning legacy Alpha." Case Two (a lead's data-platform case) claims £480k, £220k from "retiring Alpha's data store, enabled by the migration." Alpha's total annual running cost is £220k. Ask for the correct position.

- A) Combined savings £1.08m; both cases proceed as drafted.
- B) The same £220k of Alpha running cost appears in both cases; it can be booked once — the combined true saving is £860k, one case must release the claim (attribution decided by which programme actually causes and carries the decommissioning), and the sign-off process should ask how a single system's full running cost came to be claimed twice within one month.
- C) Split the £220k evenly, £110k to each case.
- D) Neither case may claim it, since decommissioning is business-as-usual.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Alpha's cost can only be released once regardless of how many programmes enable retirement; true combined saving is £860k. Option C's even split makes both cases wrong to make neither team unhappy. This tests recognising that savings belong to budget lines, booked once, with attribution following causation and accountability — and, in a strong answer, proposing a shared register of claimed savings by budget line as the structural fix.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reading the executive dashboard's percentage story**

A dashboard reports: "Benefits realised: 45% of target. Time elapsed: 60%. Conclusion: benefits realisation is 15 points behind schedule." The approved benefits profile is back-loaded: 10% by the first third, 30% by the 60% point, 100% at completion. Ask for the correct reading.

- A) The dashboard is right: 45% realised at 60% elapsed is behind.
- B) Against the approved profile, 45% realised at the 60% point is 15 points ahead of the 30% profiled for now — the dashboard's linear-time comparison assumes benefits accrue evenly, which this programme's approved profile explicitly does not; correct the dashboard's method to profile-versus-actual, and note that linear benefit assumptions will systematically misreport every back-loaded programme in both directions: behind early, ahead late.
- C) Both readings are equally valid interpretations.
- D) Benefits percentages cannot be compared to time percentages under any method.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Against the approved profile (30% by the 60% point), 45% realised is 15 points ahead, not behind — the dashboard compared against the wrong baseline. This tests recognising that a variance is only meaningful against its approved baseline, and generalising that linear-time dashboards systematically misreport non-linear (typically back-loaded) transformation programmes.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The workforce model's compounding**

Modelling a community three years out: starting 42 staff; 12% annual attrition; recruitment capped at 8/year (hires arrive year-end). Strategy requires 55 staff in three years. Ask for the position at the end of year three, applying the constraints year by year.

- A) 55 — the target is met exactly.
- B) About 53 — two short of target, so the target is nearly met and no change is needed.
- C) About 50 — five short of the target; the arithmetic shows the ceiling: with 12% attrition, recruiting the maximum 8 every year converges the community toward a steady state of about 67 (where 12% of 67 ≈ 8), and reaching 55 by year three specifically requires either lifting the recruitment ceiling early (the year-one hire cohort compounds) or cutting attrition — a combination of both is needed within the same hiring ceiling.
- D) About 46 — the target is unreachable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Year 1: 42×0.88+8=44.96. Year 2: 44.96×0.88+8=47.56. Year 3: 47.56×0.88+8≈49.86 — about 50, five short, not the "nearly met" reading of option B. This tests running the compounding year-by-year rather than trusting the intuitive "3 years × 8 hires = +24" estimate, and recognising that early hires compound in value (worth more by year three than late hires), pointing toward the real remedies: front-loaded extra hiring, reduced attrition, or both.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The benchmarking submission awaiting a candidate's signature**

A cross-government submission drafts "cost per product professional" as £3.9m ÷ 42 = £92,857. Checking: the £3.9m includes £610k of contractor spend (6 contractor FTEs) and £240k of community-wide costs; the 42 counts only civil servants. Last year's submission used the same method. Other departments' guidance suggests most include contractor FTEs in headcount. Ask what should be submitted.

- A) £92,857 — consistency with last year's submission matters most.
- B) Recompute on the benchmark's evident intent and disclose: include contractor FTEs in both numerator and denominator — £3.9m ÷ 48 = £81,250 — state the method and the change from last year in the submission's notes, and flag to the benchmark's owners that method variance across departments (contractors in or out) likely swamps the differences the benchmark reports; internally, restate last year on the new method so the trend is read consistently.
- C) £78,095 — exclude the £240k of community costs as well, since other departments may not count them.
- D) Submit both figures and let the benchmark's users choose.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The original draft divides a numerator that includes contractor cost by a denominator that excludes contractor people — an inconsistent ratio. Option B corrects it (£81,250), discloses the method and change, and flags cross-department method variance as swamping the benchmark's reported differences. This tests fixing an inconsistent ratio and disclosing the correction even when it changes a favourable-looking figure, and flagging a systemic measurement problem beyond one's own submission.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The investment committee's three-way choice**

One of three £400k investments can be funded. Option One: modernise the highest-volume service, £280k/yr modelled benefit, 85% confidence, from year one. Option Two: shared design system, £190k/yr modelled direct savings (80% confidence, year one) plus unmodelled structural benefits. Option Three: data platform enabling cross-service analytics, no direct cashable benefit modelled, argued qualitatively. Three-year EVs on modelled benefits: One +£314k; Two +£56k; Three unmodellable as drafted. Ask for the strongest recommendation.

- A) Option One — it has the highest modelled expected value, and modelled value is the only defensible basis.
- B) Option Three — enabling infrastructure always beats point solutions in the long run.
- C) Recommend Option One on the modelled arithmetic while stating its boundary honestly: the comparison is only as complete as the modelling, and Options Two and Three carry real value the models cannot yet see — then convert that observation into structure: require Option Two's case to model its per-service reuse benefit (it is modellable — services × build-hours saved), require Option Three's case to name the decisions it would change and their value, and commit to re-running the comparison next cycle with all three on comparable footings; fund One now, and say exactly why the others lost and what would make them win.
- D) Split the £400k three ways to avoid choosing.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option One's EV wins decisively on the modelled evidence, and the committee needs a recommendation from the evidence that exists. Option C both funds One and improves the modelling standard for future cycles, rather than hardening "modelled value is the only defensible basis" into doctrine (option A) or asserting infrastructure superiority where the case declined to do the work (option B). This tests recommending on the evidence that exists while owning the modelling standard that decides how every future comparison gets argued — the recommendation and the standard are one deliverable.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate aggregates raw numerators and denominators across a portfolio, rather than averaging percentages.
- Note whether the candidate includes on-costs and correct sequencing in workforce and pay arithmetic.
- Watch for whether the candidate compares a variance against its approved baseline rather than a default linear assumption.
- Score for whether the candidate runs a compounding calculation (workforce, benefit realisation) year by year rather than trusting an intuitive estimate.
- Note whether the candidate discloses a correction to a figure even when the correction changes a favourable-looking result.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an averaged percentage across portfolios, or a near-miss used to justify the shortcut method.
- Missing an inconsistent ratio — mismatched populations in numerator and denominator.
- Accepting pay or workforce arithmetic without on-costs or year-by-year compounding.
- Rewarding a linear baseline applied to a non-linear reality (benefit accrual, headcount growth, cost-versus-volume targets).
- Missing a double-counted benefit claim across two business cases sharing an underlying budget line.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment tests whether a candidate can determine exactly what a piece of written material establishes. For a head of product the material is business cases, spending approvals, governance frameworks, employment and recruitment guidance, cross-government agreements, and the research and performance reporting a community produces.

The commonest format presents a passage followed by statements to be marked True, False or Cannot Say. True means the passage establishes it, False means it contradicts it, and Cannot Say means it settles neither — regardless of what the candidate happens to know.

A candidate's own experience is the main hazard: knowing how funding approvals or business cases usually work, while the passage describes this one specifically.

Three specifics matter particularly at this level. First, funding and approval documents attach conditions with precise scope, and a condition attached to one tranche, product or stage does not travel to another. Second, governance and employment guidance allocate authority in language that looks interchangeable and is not — approves, endorses, is consulted, may direct, must consult — and acting outside remit on people's development, pay, moves and exits is a serious matter, not a technicality. Third, business cases and strategies are written persuasively, and separating what is asserted from what is established is a routine job requirement, often performed by the most senior person able to do it.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading funding and approval conditions** maps to **Creating value for money** and **Strategic ownership**.
- **Reading governance** maps to **Product leadership** and ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards.
- **Reading employment and recruitment guidance** maps to managing recruitment, development, moves, and exits.
- **Reading business cases** maps to **Managing product outcomes**.
- **Reading research and performance reporting** maps to **Applying user-centred insights**.
- **Reading cross-government agreements** maps to representing product community interests.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Funding for the programme is approved in two tranches. The first tranche is released on approval of the outline business case. The second tranche is released on approval of the full business case, and is conditional on the programme demonstrating that the benefits identified in the outline case remain achievable. Where the full business case identifies benefits lower than those in the outline case, the second tranche may be reduced proportionately at the discretion of the investment committee. Funding not drawn within the financial year in which it is released lapses and must be re-approved. The investment committee meets quarterly and may, in exceptional circumstances, approve funding out of cycle."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: The first tranche is released on approval of the outline business case.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, with no further condition attached to the first tranche — the benefits-remain-achievable test applies to the second. This tests whether the candidate keeps straight which condition attaches to which tranche, the hinge the next three questions turn on.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: If the full business case identifies lower benefits, the second tranche will be reduced.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the tranche "may be reduced... at the discretion of the investment committee" — two separate discretion markers in one clause. The statement's "will" asserts automaticity the passage contradicts. This tests distinguishing discretionary from automatic consequences in a funding condition.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: Funding released in one financial year and left unspent must be re-approved before use.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, with the trigger being the year of release rather than approval. This tests whether the candidate catches which event (release, not approval) starts the clock — a detail that decides real spending plans.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: The programme cannot receive funding between quarterly investment committee meetings.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The committee "may, in exceptional circumstances, approve funding out of cycle," refuting the absolute "cannot." This tests recognising that a discretionary power defeats an absolute claim in either direction — "will" (Question 2) or "cannot" (here).

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"Where a post has been advertised twice without a suitable appointment, the recruiting manager may propose a revised specification, an increased pay band subject to approval by the pay committee, or engagement of an interim resource for a period not exceeding twelve months. Interim engagements beyond twelve months require the approval of the director of resources. An interim engagement should not be used where the underlying need is permanent, unless a plan to establish permanent capability accompanies the request. Recruiting managers should consult the head of the relevant profession before advertising a post requiring specialist skills."

**Question 5 (moderate)**

Statement: A recruiting manager can increase the pay band for a post advertised twice without success.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The manager "may propose" an increased band "subject to approval by the pay committee" — proposing is not deciding. This tests distinguishing a proposal power from a decision power within the same sentence that grants a genuinely unilateral option elsewhere.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: An interim engagement can never be used where the underlying need is permanent.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The exception ("unless a plan... accompanies the request") provides a route, and the base obligation is "should not" rather than "must not" — either reason defeats "never." This tests spotting the "unless" clause that supplies an exception route.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: A recruiting manager must consult the head of profession before advertising a specialist post.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says "should consult," not "must," a deliberate distinction in a passage that elsewhere uses "require the approval of." This tests reading "should" against "must" within one passage, since the head of profession being consulted needs to know whether a manager who skipped it breached something or simply didn't follow good practice.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: An eighteen-month interim engagement would require both the director of resources' approval and a plan to establish permanent capability.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The first limb is established (18 months exceeds 12); the second is not, since a capability plan is required only where the underlying need is permanent, which the statement does not establish. This tests checking each limb of a compound statement separately — one unestablished conjunct makes the whole Cannot Say.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"The product's quarterly report states that user satisfaction rose from 71% to 78% following the redesign. The sample comprised 340 responses to a survey offered at the end of the transaction. Completion rate over the same period rose from 62% to 64%. The report notes that a concurrent change reduced the number of questions in the transaction from 24 to 17. The report recommends applying the redesign to two further products. It does not report satisfaction among users who abandoned the transaction."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: User satisfaction rose following the redesign.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, and "following" is a temporal claim, not a causal one. This tests recognising how little "following" actually claims, ahead of the causal question in Question 10.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: The redesign caused the increase in satisfaction.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A concurrent change (questions reduced 24 to 17) is an entirely sufficient rival explanation, confounded with the redesign. This tests noticing a confound the report's own recommendation glosses over — a recommendation to roll out the redesign may in fact be a recommendation to ask fewer questions.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: The satisfaction figures represent all users of the transaction.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The survey was offered only at the end of the transaction and excludes abandoners — with completion at 64%, roughly a third of users. This tests recognising an excluded population, and naming the direction of the resulting bias: the excluded group is likeliest to be dissatisfied.

**Question 12 (harder)**

Statement: The completion rate improvement was small relative to the satisfaction improvement.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Satisfaction rose 7 points, completion 2 points — small on any reading, and both figures are supplied directly. This tests recognising the most useful reading in the passage: a large satisfaction gain among completers alongside a small completion gain is consistent with the redesign helping people who were already going to finish — a considerably weaker result than the report's recommendation implies.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate tracks which condition attaches to which tranche or stage.
- Note whether the candidate reads "may" as defeating both "will" and "cannot" claims.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes proposing from deciding, and "should" from "must," within one passage.
- Score for whether the candidate checks each limb of a compound statement separately.
- Note whether the candidate looks for a concurrent change before accepting a causal claim, and asks who a sample excludes.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a discretionary reduction or power read as automatic.
- Missing when a candidate treats a recommendation's implied cause as established fact.
- Rewarding "should consult" read as a hard requirement.
- Accepting a compound statement marked True on the strength of one established limb.
- Missing when a candidate accepts end-of-transaction survey data as representing all users.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment presents realistic scenarios and asks the candidate to identify effective and ineffective responses. For a head of product the scenarios involve the people in a community — their development, performance, moves and exits — alongside product strategy, senior stakeholders, and the standards set for a profession this role leads but does not deliver.

The commonest formats ask for the most and least effective response, or for ratings, scored against professional consensus at comparable levels.

Three patterns run through strong answers at this level. First, this role is responsible for people whose work it no longer does — judgements are formed from second-hand, heavily confounded evidence, and strong responses include a step to check the picture before acting on the version that arrived. Second, this role sets standards paid for by every product manager on every product for as long as they stand — whether the benefit exceeds the distributed cost is the question that decides a standard, not merely whether it describes good practice. Third, a community watches how the hardest cases are handled — defending a defensible call that went wrong, being honest about failure, holding a standard against senior pressure — and that determines whether anyone brings a problem forward early.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **People leadership** maps to overseeing every member of the community, including development, pay, and performance.
- **Recruitment and mobility** maps to managing recruitment, development, moves, and exits.
- **Coaching** maps to coaching others in product management and **Agile and Lean practices**.
- **Standards** maps to setting and raising standards for product management.
- **Senior stakeholder work** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Strategic ownership**.
- **Evidence judgement** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Managing product outcomes**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A product manager described as difficult**

A delivery director tells the candidate that one of their product managers is "blocking progress" on a programme. Ask for the most effective first step.

- A) Speak to the product manager about unblocking it.
- B) Get their account before doing anything, because "blocking" is what it looks like from outside when a product manager is declining to commit to something they believe is wrong for users — and if that is what is happening, the useful response is the opposite of unblocking them.
- C) Ask the director what specifically is blocked.
- D) Observe the next programme board.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** "Blocking" describes an effect, and its two commonest causes — an unreasonable product manager, or one rightly holding a line — point in opposite directions. Option C gets the same account from the same source; A accepts the framing and instructs accordingly before knowing which cause applies. This tests gathering the missing account before acting on a characterisation that could describe either a problem or good practice.

**Question 2 (easy) — Coaching a lead product manager**

A lead product manager presents a prioritisation the candidate thinks is wrong, but the reasoning is coherent. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Ask what they weighed and what would change their mind, then offer the concern with reasons — because they may hold context not yet known, and if they do not, the reasoning is what transfers rather than the conclusion.
- B) Tell them to reprioritise.
- C) Accept it; prioritisation is their job.
- D) Ask them to take it to their stakeholders and see what they say.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** "Coherent but different from mine" is the normal, desirable result of delegating prioritisation, and option A tests it without overriding it, locating exactly where the disagreement lies. Option B instructs on preference alone, teaching a lead their judgement is decorative. This tests coaching a disagreement rather than overruling or abdicating it.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Setting a standard**

The candidate is considering requiring every product to publish a quarterly benefits report. Ask for the most effective analysis.

- A) Require it; benefits tracking is good practice.
- B) Price it across the community — twenty products, four reports a year, a day or two each is forty to eighty product-manager days annually — and ask whether the same benefit is available more cheaply, such as reporting only where a benefits claim was used to secure funding.
- C) Require it for major products only.
- D) Recommend rather than require it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The premise in A is true and does not settle whether the distributed cost is worth it; option B computes the cost and asks where it earns its keep. A recommended report is produced by those who least need it — option D gets the cost without the coverage. This tests pricing a proposed standard across its full population before setting it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A performance concern reported second-hand**

Two stakeholders separately say a product manager is not performing; their product is behind. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Begin a performance conversation with the product manager.
- B) Establish what is actually happening before characterising it, because a product behind schedule with unhappy stakeholders is equally consistent with a product manager holding a difficult line, an under-resourced team, or a genuine performance problem — and these need entirely different responses.
- C) Ask their line manager for an assessment.
- D) Wait for the next performance cycle.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The three causes look identical from the outside and diverge completely in what should happen next — a product manager saying no to a stakeholder generates exactly this pattern. Deferring to a cycle boundary (D) lets a genuine problem worsen and leaves a wrongly accused person under a cloud for months. This tests distinguishing a difficult line from a performance problem before acting.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A strong product manager wants to leave**

An excellent senior product manager privately says they are considering leaving because there is no progression route. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Offer a promotion if one is available.
- B) Be honest about what is and is not available, and work the routes that exist — stretching scope, a role elsewhere in the organisation, a cross-government move — because keeping them in government is a far better outcome than losing them, and a head who helps people move internally hears about approaches early.
- C) Explain that progression takes time.
- D) Accept it; people move on.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Honesty, including unwelcome honesty, keeps the channel open longest. Option C is the standard deflection that confirms exactly what they suspect and converts a conversation into a decision. This tests widening the frame beyond one's own establishment when a strong person signals they are considering leaving.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A director wants a standard waived**

A director asks the candidate to waive the discovery phase for a product they want delivered quickly. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Refuse; discovery is a standard.
- B) Establish what they are actually short of — usually time to a visible outcome — and offer what meets that within the standard, such as a shortened discovery with a defined question, or an early alpha on the part of the problem that is already understood.
- C) Waive it; the director is accountable for the outcome.
- D) Waive it and record the risk.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The pressure is nearly always about time to visibility, not discovery itself; a two-week discovery with one clear question meets that need without abandoning the practice. Option D gets the worst of both — the product proceeds without evidence while the record changes nothing. This tests finding what a director is actually short of before waiving a standard.

**Question 7 (harder) — A product that should be stopped**

A product is consuming significant resource with evidence it will not achieve its outcomes; its senior sponsor is committed to it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Set out what the evidence shows, what continuing costs and what the alternatives for that resource are, and put the decision to whoever is accountable for the investment — because stopping is a legitimate outcome and the decision needs to be made explicitly rather than by drift.
- B) Recommend stopping it.
- C) Raise it with the sponsor privately and leave it with them.
- D) Continue; the sponsor is committed.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Products that should stop and do not, through drift, are among the most expensive things in a portfolio. Option A gives the accountable decision-maker evidence, cost, and alternatives, framing stopping as normal rather than an accusation. This tests treating a product's retirement as a normal life-cycle outcome requiring an explicit decision, not a default of continuation.

**Question 8 (harder) — Evidence against a decision the candidate supported**

A product direction the candidate backed a year ago now shows worse outcomes than the approach it replaced. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Raise it personally, including that the candidate supported it, and propose how to establish whether the direction caused the difference.
- B) Examine the data carefully first; correlation is not causation.
- C) Investigate quietly and act if it holds up.
- D) Note it alongside the other benefits of the direction.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Option B is methodologically correct but is also precisely what a defensive position would say — rigour and defensiveness look identical until it is clear whether the data is raised openly or withheld until challenged. Option D changes the subject to offsetting benefits, discounting everything said afterwards once noticed. This tests raising contrary evidence about a self-backed decision openly, before being shown it.

**Question 9 (harder) — Recruiting into a thin market**

The candidate must recruit a senior product manager into a specialism where the market is thin and the pay band is below market rate. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say plainly that the role as specified is unlikely to fill, and offer the alternatives — reshaping it for a strong generalist who can grow into it, an interim arrangement while capability is built, or a case for the band.
- B) Run the campaign and see.
- C) Make the case for a higher band.
- D) Recommend a contractor.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** A role unlikely to fill stays open for months with no cost recorded anywhere. Option A names it immediately and offers three routes, of which reshaping for growth is the most overlooked and often best. This tests naming a foreseeable recruitment failure early rather than spending a campaign to learn what is already known.

**Question 10 (harder) — A standard the candidate set is not being followed**

Eighteen months after setting a performance-reporting standard, most teams are not doing it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Enforce it through governance.
- B) Establish why — whether teams do not know it, do not find it applicable, or find compliance more expensive than the alternative — because a standard widely ignored by capable people is more often unusable than defied, and each cause has a different remedy.
- C) Rewrite the standard to match what teams do.
- D) Escalate the non-compliance to their directors.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Widespread non-compliance by capable people is evidence about the standard, not just about behaviour. Escalating to line directors (D) damages the community relationship and treats a systemic signal as a discipline matter. This tests diagnosing why a standard is ignored — unknown, inapplicable, or expensive to follow — before enforcing, escalating, or abandoning it.

**Question 11 (harder) — Representing the community**

The candidate is asked to speak at a cross-government event about the organisation's product practice, where the reality is mixed — strong work and one significant failure. Ask for the most effective approach.

- A) Present all of it, and be specific about what went wrong in the failure and why, because that is the part the audience can learn from and the candidate's own community will hear whether the organisation they work in was described accurately.
- B) Present the strong work.
- C) Present the successes and refer generally to challenges.
- D) Decline until the picture improves.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Two audiences are listening — the external one wants transferable material, and the internal one hears whether their head of profession told the truth. Vague reference to "challenges" (C) reads as evasion to practitioners and costs more credibility than the failure itself. This tests presenting a specific, honest account of failure rather than omission or vague deflection.

**Question 12 (harder) — A defensible decision that went wrong**

A product manager made a well-reasoned call that turned out badly, and stakeholders are looking for accountability. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Back the decision publicly on the reasoning available at the time, be clear about what has been learned, and handle any genuine development need privately — because a community that watches a defensible decision punished will stop making decisions.
- B) Support them privately and let the process take its course.
- C) Acknowledge the error and commit to preventing recurrence.
- D) Review whether the decision was in fact defensible.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** With the reasoning stipulated as sound, the question is what the community learns. Option A separates decision quality (judged on what was knowable) from outcome (which was not), backing publicly while handling development privately. Private support with public silence (B) reads as abandonment and most reliably teaches people to stop deciding. This tests backing a defensible decision visibly while keeping any genuine development conversation private.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate gathers the missing account before acting on a second-hand characterisation.
- Note whether the candidate prices a proposed standard's distributed cost across the whole community before setting it.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes a defensible-but-unwelcome position (a product manager holding a line, a director wanting a standard waived) from an actual performance or compliance problem.
- Score for whether the candidate treats stopping a product, or raising evidence against their own past decision, as a normal professional act rather than an admission of failure.
- Note whether public and private handling are separated correctly — backing decisions and difficult truths publicly, handling individual development privately.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an instruction to reprioritise, waive a standard, or act on a characterisation before establishing what is actually happening.
- Missing when a candidate lets a failing product continue because its sponsor remains committed.
- Rewarding escalation of a community's systemic non-compliance to individual line directors, rather than diagnosis.
- Accepting private support paired with public silence as sufficient backing for a defensible decision.
- Missing vague reference to "challenges" or failure in a public account, in place of specifics.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through four assessments pitched at head of product, and the threads are worth drawing together.

The cognitive section was about systems rather than instances — reading a pattern across products for the cause that generates it, weighing decisions by reversibility rather than size, and asking what a request is actually for before pricing the thing that was asked for.

The numeric section was the arithmetic that decides portfolio questions: benefits normalised and stripped of what would have arrived anyway, costs multiplied across the community that bears them, and the difference between a metric moving and an outcome changing.

The verbal section was about the documents that govern money and people. A discretionary "may" defeats both "will" and "cannot". Proposing is not deciding. "Should consult" is not "must". And a satisfaction figure gathered at the end of a transaction excludes precisely the people most likely to be dissatisfied.

The situational judgement section circled what makes this role distinctive: accountable for a profession no longer practised day to day, judged through second-hand evidence, with almost all effect running through other people's willingness. The strong answers checked the picture before acting on it, distinguished a product manager holding a difficult line from one performing badly, treated stopping a product as a normal outcome, and backed a defensible decision in public while handling development in private.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and the situational judgement section's paired most/least format rewards discriminating between plausible options as much as identifying the best one. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
