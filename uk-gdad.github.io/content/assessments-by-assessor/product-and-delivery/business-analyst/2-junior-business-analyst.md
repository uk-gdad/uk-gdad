## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes the role actually uses — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving — through practical scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of number sequences and rotated shapes, a job-specific version presents a swimlane diagram with a flaw in it, a set of business rules to reason from, or a backlog with dependencies to untangle.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, with roughly 15 to 30 multiple-choice questions. Many platforms are adaptive, so a test that feels increasingly difficult can indicate strong performance. Scoring is typically norm-referenced, and employers often see accuracy and speed measured separately. Most platforms offer unscored practice questions before the timer starts; encourage the candidate to take them.

This kind of assessment matters for a junior business analyst because the core output at this level is structured thinking made visible. Supporting the representation of a business situation within a defined task draws on pattern recognition and logical modelling; contributing to evaluating a process draws on error checking; juggling specific responsibilities against direction received draws on prioritisation.

### How this assessment maps to the role

Each cognitive dimension connects to a named skill in the role:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business modelling** and **Business process improvement**: visualising business processes and supporting the representation of business situations means noticing recurring structures and spotting where a model breaks its own conventions.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: contributing to option assessment under supervision, and explaining how an approach changes between scrum and kanban, both require reasoning validly from rules and facts to conclusions.
- **Error checking** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements** and **Testing (business analysis)**: supporting the capture and validation of requirements, and helping trace requirements through to tested functionality, is systematic error detection.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the role level: being responsible for the output of specific tasks while receiving direction means constantly sequencing competing demands sensibly, and knowing when a clash needs a conversation rather than a guess.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Digital and data systems analysis** and **User experience analysis**: supporting the specification of system requirements, and supporting the analysis and prioritisation of user experience needs, both mean decomposing a fuzzy situation into parts that can be examined one at a time.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded. Items progress from easy to hard.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a process model**

A team's process modelling convention says: every process step is named with a verb followed by a noun ("Check application", "Send letter"), and every decision point is phrased as a yes/no question ending in a question mark ("Application complete?"). Reviewing a draft model, the candidate sees these five labels: (1) Check application; (2) Application complete?; (3) Sending of the letter; (4) Record decision; (5) Escalation needed?. Which label breaks the convention?

- A) Label 2
- B) Label 3
- C) Label 4
- D) Label 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Labels 1 and 4 are verb-plus-noun, correct for steps. Labels 2 and 5 are yes/no questions with question marks, correct for decisions. Label 3, "Sending of the letter", is a noun phrase, not verb-plus-noun; the convention requires "Send letter". This tests checking against a stated convention exactly, useful because consistent labelling keeps models scannable and lets colleagues search and compare them reliably.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a business rule**

A benefits service has this rule: "An application is fast-tracked if the applicant is terminally ill or the application is a resubmission following an administrative error." A candidate is told that a named applicant's application was not fast-tracked. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) The application contained an error.
- B) The applicant is not terminally ill and the application is not a resubmission following an administrative error.
- C) The application will be rejected.
- D) The applicant did not meet the financial eligibility criteria.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule states that either condition is sufficient for fast-tracking. Since the application was not fast-tracked, neither sufficient condition can have applied — the reasoning pattern known as denying the consequent: from "if A or B, then C" and "not C", one may validly conclude "not A and not B". Options C and D introduce topics the rule never mentions. This tests reasoning about what a business rule does and does not imply, directly relevant to validating requirements against policy constraints.

**Question 3 (easy) — Prioritising a task list**

A candidate is responsible for four pieces of work this week: (1) a process model needed by Wednesday for a stakeholder workshop; (2) reviewing a colleague's user stories, requested "by Friday, ideally sooner"; (3) a monthly timesheet, due Friday, taking ten minutes; (4) drafting survey questions for user research starting next Monday. It is Monday morning. Which order of focus is most defensible?

- A) 3, 2, 1, 4 — clear the quick task, then help others, then own work
- B) 1, 2, 4, 3 — workshop-critical model first, then the review, then the survey draft, with the timesheet slotted in before Friday
- C) 4, 1, 2, 3 — next week's work first because research is important
- D) 2, 1, 3, 4 — helping colleagues always comes first

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The process model has the earliest deadline and feeds a stakeholder workshop, so it anchors the week. The story review is due Friday but a colleague is waiting, so it comes next. The survey draft must be ready for Monday and needs meaningful time, while the ten-minute timesheet can be slotted into any gap before Friday. This tests sequencing by deadline and impact together rather than promoting a trivial task, mismatching effort against dependency, or applying a rigid rule such as "helping colleagues always comes first".

**Question 4 (easy) — Error checking a swimlane handoff**

A swimlane diagram shows a grant application process across three lanes: Applicant, Caseworker, Finance team. The written procedure says: "The caseworker approves the application and passes it to the finance team, who make the payment and notify the applicant." The diagram shows: Applicant submits → Caseworker approves → Caseworker makes payment → Caseworker notifies applicant. What is wrong with the diagram?

- A) The notification step is missing.
- B) The payment and notification steps are both in the wrong lane — the procedure assigns them to the finance team.
- C) The approval step should be in the Finance lane.
- D) Nothing — the diagram matches the procedure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Submission and approval match the procedure. The procedure's second half assigns payment and notification to the finance team ("who make the payment and notify the applicant"), but the diagram keeps both in the Caseworker lane. Option A is false — notification is present, just misplaced, a distinction worth being precise about since the fixes differ. This tests reading each step alongside its lane, since swimlane errors in financial processes misstate a segregation-of-duties control rather than being merely cosmetic.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction about iteration**

A team is deciding how to deliver a new reporting feature. Two facts are agreed: (i) the users' actual reporting needs are poorly understood; (ii) the underlying data is well understood and stable. A senior colleague says: "This is exactly the situation where iteration earns its keep." Which statement best explains why iteration is especially valuable here?

- A) Iteration is always the fastest way to deliver any feature.
- B) Because user needs are uncertain, releasing small versions and learning from real use will correct misunderstandings early, before much has been built on them.
- C) Because the data is stable, the team can build the whole feature in one pass without user feedback.
- D) Iteration lets the team skip requirements work entirely.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The risk here is concentrated in fact (i): user needs are poorly understood, so a big up-front design is likely to be wrong in unpredictable ways. Iteration attacks that risk directly, correcting misunderstandings while they are still cheap. Option C draws the wrong conclusion from fact (ii), which reduces a different risk. This tests explaining why iteration fits a specific situation rather than reciting that iteration is generally good.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in user research findings**

Five participants attempted the same three tasks in a usability test (✓ = completed, ✗ = failed): P1: ✓✗✓; P2: ✓✗✓; P3: ✓✓✗; P4: ✓✗✓; P5: ✓✗✓ (Task 1: Find form; Task 2: Complete form; Task 3: Check status). What is the most useful pattern to report to the team?

- A) P3 is an unusually skilled participant.
- B) Task 2 failed for four of five participants — the form completion step has a systematic problem worth investigating; Task 3's single failure is worth a note.
- C) The service works well because Task 1 always succeeded.
- D) The sample is too small to say anything at all.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reading by columns rather than rows is the right approach in usability data, since tasks are usually the unit of interest. Task 2's one-of-five completion rate is a striking, consistent failure across four different people — the signature of a systematic design problem, not individual error. This tests reading a small usability sample for the pattern that points at an action, since four-of-five failing the same task is a strong qualitative signal even though the sample cannot support percentages.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking requirements against a policy constraint**

A policy constraint states: "Applicants must be able to complete the entire process without providing an email address." Draft requirements: (1) the system must send an application receipt to the applicant's email address; (2) the system must display an on-screen reference number when an application is submitted; (3) the system must allow status checks using the reference number and the applicant's date of birth; (4) the system must offer email notifications to applicants who choose to provide an email address. Which requirement conflicts with the policy constraint?

- A) Requirement 1
- B) Requirement 2
- C) Requirement 3
- D) Requirement 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Requirement 1 is phrased unconditionally, presuming every applicant provides an email address, which breaks the constraint. Requirements 2 and 3 need no email. Requirement 4 makes email explicitly optional, exactly how requirement 1 should have been phrased. This tests spotting unconditional "must" phrasing that smuggles in an assumption, a core requirements-validation skill, particularly consequential for digital-inclusion constraints in government services.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with WIP and blockers**

A kanban team's columns: To do / Analysis (WIP limit 2) / Build (WIP limit 3) / Test (WIP limit 2) / Done. Current state: Analysis holds items A and B; Build holds C, D, E; Test holds F and G. Item F is blocked awaiting a security review taking three more days; G finishes testing today. Analysis of item A has just finished. What happens to the flow, and what is the most useful action?

- A) A moves to Build immediately — Build's limit is not reached.
- B) A cannot move to Build (full at 3), so raise it at stand-up; when G completes today, one Build item can move to Test, then A can enter Build. Meanwhile, F's three-day blockage deserves attention since it holds a Test slot.
- C) A moves straight to Test, skipping Build, because Test will have a free slot today.
- D) Nothing can move until F is unblocked.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build already holds three items against its limit of 3, so A cannot enter yet. When G completes, Test drops to one item, freeing a Build slot once one of C, D or E advances. F sitting blocked in Test for three more days throttles the whole downstream flow — the kind of systemic issue WIP limits are designed to make visible, and escalating the security review may be worth more than any local shuffling. This tests reading a board as a system of constraints rather than a simple to-do list.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Spotting the flaw in a process improvement claim**

A team lead argues: "We piloted the new triage checklist in the Croydon office last month. Croydon's error rate fell from 12% to 9%. The other offices did not use the checklist and their average error rate stayed at 12%. So the checklist works, and rolling it out nationally will cut the national error rate by a quarter." What is the strongest criticism of the final conclusion?

- A) The error rates are probably measured wrongly.
- B) Croydon's fall shows nothing because error rates never change for real reasons.
- C) The comparison is encouraging, but one office over one month is a small basis — Croydon may differ from other offices, other local changes may explain the fall, and a national rollout may not reproduce pilot conditions.
- D) A fall from 12% to 9% is a fifth, not a quarter.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The argument has a real strength (a comparison group holding steady at 12%), but the leap to a confident national forecast from one office in one month ignores possible differences between Croydon and other offices, other local changes, and the tendency of pilots to outperform rollouts. Option D's arithmetic complaint is itself wrong: 3 ÷ 12 = 25%, genuinely a quarter. This tests welcoming evidence while sizing it honestly, and separating a promising pilot from a proven national result.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

Four analysis activities for a discovery: stakeholder interviews (I), a process observation visit (O), a data-quality review (D), and a findings workshop (W). Constraints: (i) W must come after all three other activities; (ii) O must happen on a Tuesday or Thursday, when the operations centre runs a full service; (iii) D cannot start until the data extract arrives, promised for Wednesday morning; (iv) each activity takes one full day, at most one per day. The week runs Monday to Friday. Which schedule satisfies every constraint?

- A) I Monday, O Tuesday, D Wednesday, W Thursday
- B) O Monday, I Tuesday, D Wednesday, W Friday
- C) I Monday, D Tuesday, O Thursday, W Friday
- D) I Wednesday, O Thursday, D Friday, W Friday

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option B breaks constraint (ii) — O on Monday. Option C breaks constraint (iii) — D on Tuesday, before the Wednesday extract. Option D breaks constraint (iv) — two activities share Friday. Option A satisfies all four constraints. This tests eliminating options by finding the single constraint each one breaks, rather than constructing a schedule from scratch — the same technique used to validate a real discovery plan against hard constraints.

**Question 11 (hard) — Deduction with an exception clause**

A department's data-sharing rule states: "Case data may be shared with another government department only if a data-sharing agreement is in place, unless the request is from law enforcement with a signed urgency certificate, in which case data may be shared and the agreement completed retrospectively within ten working days." Case data was shared yesterday with another department, with no data-sharing agreement in place at the time. Which conclusion must be true if the sharing complied with the rule?

- A) The rule was breached.
- B) The request came from law enforcement with a signed urgency certificate, and an agreement must be completed within ten working days of the sharing.
- C) A data-sharing agreement is not needed for this kind of request.
- D) The ten-working-day clock has already expired.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The default path requires an agreement before sharing; the exception path (law enforcement plus signed certificate) permits sharing first with retrospective completion. Given that the sharing complied with the rule despite no agreement, only the exception path is available. This tests mapping a default path and an exception path separately, then locating the given facts on the one they satisfy — precisely the reading required when assessing how policy constraints shape system requirements.

**Question 12 (hard) — Choosing the right model for the job**

A stakeholder group needs to understand a problem: applicants are abandoning a licence application partway through, and nobody agrees where or why. Four modelling approaches are available. Which is the most appropriate first step?

- A) A detailed data model of the licensing database, showing every entity and relationship.
- B) An organisation chart of the licensing directorate.
- C) A process model of the applicant's journey from start to submission, annotated with the drop-off numbers at each step.
- D) A full target operating model for the future licensing service.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The disagreement is about where and why people abandon a journey, and a process model annotated with drop-off numbers makes the "where" visible step by step, turning argument into evidence. The database's structure and the org chart model the wrong layer, and a target operating model designs a future state before the current problem is understood. This tests model selection as an analytical decision in its own right — the best model is the simplest one that makes the disputed question visible.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads the actual ask** — most, least, must be true, best first step — rather than the question they expected.
- **Watch for whether a candidate adds facts the question never gave**, particularly in deduction items.
- **Note whether a candidate reads tables by the unit of interest** — rows or columns — before diving in.
- **Note whether a candidate uses elimination on constraint questions** rather than constructing an answer from scratch.
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer built on a plausible but unstated assumption.**
- **Missing when a candidate miscounts against a stated limit** — "at least", "at most", "within", and "by" each carry an exact meaning.
- **Crediting an answer that personalises a data pattern** rather than checking whether the story is systemic.
- **Letting one hard question distort the overall impression** rather than looking at the pattern across the section.
- **Penalising a defensible answer to a question with no perfect option** — occasionally every option has a flaw, and the task is to find the least-wrong one.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and confidently a candidate handles the numbers the role genuinely produces: percentages and percentage changes, averages and weighted averages, ratios, rates, and data in tables and charts. It is not an advanced maths test — nothing beyond everyday arithmetic — but it is a precision test, checking whether the candidate selects the right numbers, applies the right operation, and interprets the result sensibly under moderate time pressure.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 30 minutes, with 15 to 20 questions, each presenting data followed by a multiple-choice question. A calculator is usually permitted; offer rough paper.

This matters for a junior business analyst because at this level numbers stop being someone else's job: contributing to evaluating a business process means computing the before-and-after; supporting the prioritisation of user experience needs means summarising research data; helping assess options means comparing costs and volumes. Every item below shows its full working so the method can be adopted directly.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and percentage change** map to **Business process improvement**: contributing to the analysis and evaluation of processes almost always means quantifying change, and knowing the difference between a percentage and a percentage-point change.
- **Averages and weighted averages** map to **User experience analysis** and **Context, problem and option analysis**: survey scores, task times, and satisfaction ratings arrive as averages, and combining groups of different sizes correctly matters.
- **Ratios and rates** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: velocity, throughput, and capacity are rates.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements** and **Testing (business analysis)**: coverage tables, defect summaries, and traceability matrices are tabular data.
- **Sense-checking and interpretation** map to **Business modelling**: noticing when a figure is implausible is often the first clue that a model or an extract is wrong.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of stories completed**

A team planned 24 user stories for a programme increment and completed 18 of them. What percentage of the planned stories were completed?

- A) 66%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75 = 75%. 18/24 simplifies to 3/4, confirming three-quarters. This tests a basic percentage calculation and simplifying a fraction before dividing when the numbers share an obvious factor.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average handling time**

Six phone enquiries, timed during a process observation, take 4, 7, 5, 9, 6, and 5 minutes. What is the mean handling time?

- A) 5 minutes
- B) 5.5 minutes
- C) 6 minutes
- D) 6.5 minutes

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total = 36 minutes; ÷6 = 6 minutes. The values range from 4 to 9, and 6 sits inside that range. This tests basic averaging, and a strong candidate's answer notes that the mean alone hides the 9-minute call, worth reporting alongside the range since long calls may indicate complex cases or system slowness.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a defect summary table**

| Severity | Raised | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | 4 | 4 |
| Major | 15 | 9 |
| Minor | 36 | 18 |

What fraction of all raised defects have been fixed?

- A) Just over a half
- B) Exactly a half
- C) Just under two-thirds
- D) Three-quarters

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total raised = 55; total fixed = 31; 31 ÷ 55 ≈ 56%, just over half. This tests reading a table for both a total and its severity breakdown — all four critical defects are fixed, majors are at 60%, minors at 50%, a pattern worth noting since fixing effort is concentrated where severity is highest.

**Question 4 (easy) — Percentage decrease in processing time**

After a process improvement, the average time to process a claim fell from 20 days to 16 days. What is the percentage decrease?

- A) 16%
- B) 20%
- C) 25%
- D) 4%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Change = 4 days; 4 ÷ 20 (the original value) = 20%. Option C comes from dividing by the new value instead — the single most common percentage-change error. This tests the direction rule: change is always measured against where a figure started.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average satisfaction score**

Summarising user satisfaction across two channels: online, 300 respondents, mean score 4.2 out of 5; phone, 100 respondents, mean score 3.4. What is the overall mean satisfaction score across all respondents?

- A) 3.8
- B) 4.0
- C) 4.1
- D) 3.9

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Averaging the two averages directly gives 3.8 (option A), the trap, since the groups differ in size. Weighting: online total = 1,260; phone total = 340; combined = 1,600 ÷ 400 respondents = 4.0. This tests recognising when a weighted average is needed, and a strong candidate's answer also notes the overall figure conceals a 0.8-point channel gap that may be the more actionable finding.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Sprint velocity and forecasting**

A scrum team's last four sprints delivered 21, 19, 24, and 20 story points. The remaining backlog for the current release is 63 points. Using the four-sprint average velocity, how many more sprints should be forecast to finish the release?

- A) 2 sprints
- B) 3 sprints
- C) 4 sprints
- D) 5 sprints

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Average velocity = 84 ÷ 4 = 21 points per sprint; 63 ÷ 21 = 3 sprints exactly. This tests a rate-and-scale calculation, with a strong candidate's answer noting that a non-exact division would round up (since the release is not done until the work is done) and that the forecast inherits the assumption that future sprints resemble past ones.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage points versus percentages**

A digital service's take-up rose from 40% of applicants to 50% of applicants after an awareness campaign. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Take-up rose by 10 percentage points, which is a 25% increase in take-up.
- B) Take-up rose by 10%, which is the same as 10 percentage points.
- C) Take-up rose by 25 percentage points.
- D) Take-up rose by 10%, so the campaign made little difference.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The percentage-point change is 50 − 40 = 10 points; the percentage change is 10 ÷ 40 = 25%. Option B conflates the two measures — a rise of 10% of 40% would be only 4 points, to 44%. This tests distinguishing percentage points from percentages, one of the most common confusions in workplace reporting.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratios in a research sample**

A user researcher plans 24 usability sessions, split between "digitally confident" and "digitally less confident" participants in the ratio 1:2 respectively. How many digitally less confident participants should be recruited?

- A) 8
- B) 12
- C) 16
- D) 18

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 1:2 divides the sample into 3 shares of 8; digitally less confident = 2 shares = 16. This tests anchoring which side of a ratio belongs to which group — the word "respectively" pins the assignment, and skimming past it reverses the recruitment.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reading a two-way table**

Applications by channel and outcome: Online — Approved 640, Rejected 80, Incomplete 80. Post — Approved 120, Rejected 40, Incomplete 40. Which statement is supported?

- A) Online applications had a higher approval rate than postal applications.
- B) Postal applications had a higher approval rate than online ones.
- C) Approval rates were identical across channels.
- D) Most rejections came from postal applications.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Online approval rate = 640 ÷ 800 = 80%; post approval rate = 120 ÷ 200 = 60%. Option D is also wrong on its own terms: 80 online rejections exceed 40 postal ones. This tests denominator discipline — computing each channel's own rate rather than comparing raw counts across groups of very different sizes.

**Question 10 (hard) — Two-step target calculation**

A service receives 3,200 applications per month, 15% currently arriving by post. A digital take-up plan aims to cut postal applications by 30% over the next year, with total volume unchanged. If the plan succeeds, how many applications per month will still arrive by post, and what percentage of all applications will that be?

- A) 336 applications; 10.5%
- B) 480 applications; 15%
- C) 144 applications; 4.5%
- D) 336 applications; 12%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Current postal volume = 15% of 3,200 = 480; reduction = 30% of 480 = 144; remaining = 336; as a share of the unchanged total = 336 ÷ 3,200 = 10.5%. This tests avoiding base drift in a multi-step problem — the 30% applies to the postal volume, not the total, and the final percentage divides by the total, not the postal figure.

**Question 11 (hard) — Cost comparison across options**

Clearing a scanning backlog of 9,000 documents: Option 1, in-house scans 150 documents per day at £1.50 per document; Option 2, external supplier, flat £11,700, completes the backlog in 30 days; Option 3, hybrid — supplier takes 6,000 documents for £8,000 over 40 days, while in-house scans the remaining 3,000 in parallel at its usual rate. Which option is cheapest, and which is fastest?

- A) Cheapest: Option 2; fastest: Option 2
- B) Cheapest: Option 3; fastest: Option 3
- C) Cheapest: Option 1; fastest: Option 2
- D) Cheapest: Option 3; fastest: Option 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option 1: cost £13,500, time 60 days. Option 2: cost £11,700, time 30 days. Option 3: cost £8,000 + £4,500 = £12,500; time is the slower parallel stream, 40 days (the supplier's portion), not the faster 20-day in-house portion. Option 2 wins on both cost and time. This tests totalling every cost component of a hybrid option and taking the maximum, not the minimum, of parallel durations — common errors in real options assessments.

**Question 12 (hard) — Interpreting a trend with a definition change**

Monthly complaint volumes: January 210, February 205, March 200, April 320, May 318, June 322. A note states: "From April, the complaints definition was widened to include informal complaints raised by phone, previously logged separately; roughly 110 informal phone complaints were logged per month under the old system." A stakeholder says: "Complaints jumped 60% in April — the service has deteriorated badly." What is the fairest assessment?

- A) The stakeholder is right: a 60% rise is a serious deterioration.
- B) Adjusting for the definition change (about 110 informal complaints per month now included), like-for-like volumes are roughly flat — around 210 before and about 210 after — so the data shows no evidence of deterioration.
- C) The service improved, because informal complaints fell.
- D) The data proves nothing because definitions can always change.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's arithmetic is correct (120 ÷ 200 = 60%), but the definition changed from April. Subtracting the newly included 110 from the later months gives roughly 210, 208, and 212 — essentially flat against January to March's 210, 205, 200. This tests checking whether a step-change in a time series reflects a measurement change before interpreting it as a real trend.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate names the base** before every percentage.
- **Watch for whether a candidate writes intermediate results down** in multi-step problems.
- **Note whether a candidate sense-checks an answer** against benchmarks such as halves, quarters, and tens.
- **Note whether a candidate checks a footnote or definition change** before interpreting a trend.
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that averages averages** across different-sized groups instead of weighting.
- **Crediting an answer that divides by the new value** in a percentage-change calculation.
- **Missing when a candidate confuses percentage points with percentages.**
- **Crediting an answer that compares raw counts where a rate was needed.**
- **Missing when a candidate rounds too early**, compounding error through intermediate steps.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely a candidate reads, understands, and draws conclusions from written material. Each item presents a short passage — for this role, typically a policy extract, a governance note, a stakeholder email, a research summary, or a service standard — followed by statements to evaluate. The dominant format asks the candidate to judge statements as **True** (supported), **False** (contradicted), or **Cannot say** (insufficient information). Supporting formats test inference and clear-writing judgement.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions, often organised as several statements per passage. The governing rule: answers rest only on the passage, not workplace knowledge or experience.

This matters for a junior business analyst because the role converts language into deliverables: capturing requirements from what stakeholders said, not what they roughly meant; reading policy constraints for what they do and do not require; drafting user stories and acceptance criteria that other people build from.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements**: capturing and validating requirements depends on extracting exactly what a source document requires, permits, and stays silent about.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis**: contributing to option assessment means separating what the evidence establishes from what is merely plausible.
- **Inference** maps to **Business modelling** and **Digital and data systems analysis**: reading a procedure or specification and working out its consequences is how a model is checked against its source.
- **Reading stakeholder language** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: this level explicitly supports stakeholder relationship management, starting with reading messages attentively, including their qualifications and hedges.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Testing (business analysis)** and **User experience analysis**: acceptance criteria and findings summaries must be unambiguous, since vague wording becomes defects downstream.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–3)*

"The department's accessibility policy requires all new internal digital tools to meet WCAG 2.2 level AA before launch. Tools launched before this policy took effect must be assessed within twelve months of the policy's start date, and any that fail assessment must have a remediation plan agreed within eight weeks of the assessment. The policy does not apply to third-party tools procured through the central framework, which are covered by separate contractual accessibility clauses."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "A new internal digital tool may launch before meeting WCAG 2.2 level AA, provided a remediation plan is agreed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule for new tools requires meeting the standard "before launch". The remediation-plan route belongs to a different population — legacy tools that fail their retrospective assessment. This tests noticing a passage with two rules for two different groups, and a statement that quietly swaps the groups — the first question to ask of any clause is which population it actually governs.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Third-party tools procured through the central framework have no accessibility obligations."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the policy "does not apply" to those tools, but immediately adds they "are covered by separate contractual accessibility clauses" — obligations exist, just elsewhere. This tests reading a sentence to its end before extracting a rule, since "X is exempt from A, but covered by B" reverses the first impression.

**Question 3 (easy)**

Statement: "Most of the department's existing internal tools will fail their accessibility assessment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes a process but says nothing about how many tools exist, have been assessed, or will pass. This tests treating "Cannot say" as a genuine answer rather than filling a gap with plausible-sounding outside expectation — a discipline directly relevant to summarising a policy from the document rather than from assumption.

*Passage 2 (for Questions 4–6)*

"From: Devi (Product Manager). Subject: Re: options for the renewals notification. Thanks for the options summary — really clear. I've discussed with the service owner and we're leaning towards option 2 (email with SMS fallback), but we haven't decided. Two asks before Thursday's session: first, we need indicative costs for options 2 and 3 only — don't spend time costing option 1, as printing costs rule it out. Second, the service owner asked a good question: does option 2 work for users who have neither email nor mobile? If the answer is no, we need to know what proportion of the user base that is before we commit. Can you dig into both? Shout if Thursday is unrealistic."

**Question 4 (easy) — Comprehension**

Which pieces of work does Devi's email ask for before Thursday?

- A) Cost all three options and confirm the decision for option 2.
- B) Provide indicative costs for options 2 and 3, and investigate whether option 2 covers users with neither email nor mobile — including what proportion of users that is, if it does not.
- C) Cost option 1 and research printing suppliers.
- D) Arrange a meeting between the service owner and users without email.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email flags exactly two asks, including the second's conditional structure ("if the answer is no"). Option A invents a decision the email explicitly withholds; option C does the one piece of work the email rules out. This tests extracting multiple signposted instructions into a checklist while preserving conditional logic, since flattening a conditional into an unconditional task wastes effort.

**Question 5 (moderate)**

Statement: "The decision to adopt option 2 has been made."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Devi writes "we're leaning towards option 2... but we haven't decided" — a direct contradiction of the statement, making the answer False rather than Cannot say. This tests recognising that "leaning towards" signals provisional status, and that precision about decision status protects stakeholder relationship management from analysts who over-report certainty.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following is the best inference from Devi's email as a whole?

- A) Cost is not a factor in this decision.
- B) The choice between options is being tested against both affordability and whether the notification reaches all users, before any commitment is made.
- C) Option 3 has already been rejected.
- D) The service owner opposes option 2.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Costs are requested, cost already eliminated option 1, and the service owner's question about universal reach is explicitly tied to "before we commit". Option D over-reads a probing question as opposition, when the service owner is part of the "we" leaning towards option 2. This tests distinguishing a due-diligence question from a hostile position — misreading challenge as resistance produces inaccurate stakeholder summaries.

*Passage 3 (for Questions 7–9)*

"Requirements sign-off procedure: A requirements baseline may be signed off only when every requirement in scope has been reviewed by the business owner and the lead developer. Requirements marked 'deferred' are excluded from the baseline and do not require review before sign-off. After sign-off, any change to a baselined requirement must go through change control; changes to deferred requirements may be made informally until they are brought into scope. Bringing a deferred requirement into scope requires review by both reviewers and re-issue of the baseline."

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "A requirements baseline can be signed off even though some requirements associated with the project have not been reviewed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The review obligation covers requirements "in scope"; deferred requirements are excluded from the baseline and do not require review before sign-off. So a project can carry unreviewed deferred requirements while validly signing off its baseline. This tests tracking scope qualifiers rather than only the headline rule — "in scope" is the boundary of every obligation in the sentence.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applying the procedure to a scenario**

After sign-off, the business owner wants two changes: (1) reword baselined requirement R-12; (2) update deferred requirement R-30, which stays deferred. According to the procedure, what is required?

- A) Both changes must go through change control.
- B) Change 1 must go through change control; change 2 may be made informally.
- C) Both changes may be made informally since the business owner requested them.
- D) Change 2 requires review by both reviewers and re-issue of the baseline.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** R-12 is baselined, so its change goes through change control; R-30 stays deferred, so its change may be made informally. Option D misapplies the bringing-into-scope clause, which triggers only when a deferred requirement enters scope. This tests classifying a case correctly before applying its governing rule, rather than defaulting to the heaviest process everywhere.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

A team wiki note summarises when informal changes are allowed. Which wording is clearest and most accurate?

- A) "Informal changes are fine for anything not yet built."
- B) "You can change deferred requirements informally, but only while they stay deferred — once one comes into scope, it needs review by the business owner and lead developer and a re-issued baseline, and changes to baselined requirements always go through change control."
- C) "Modifications of a non-baselined nature may, in certain deferred circumstances, potentially be progressed absent formal change governance, subject to applicable procedural provisions."
- D) "All changes need change control."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B preserves every load-bearing element in plain words. Option A substitutes a different test ("not yet built") for the procedure's actual test of deferred status. Option C buries the meaning under hedged officialese. Option D erases the informal route the procedure deliberately provides. This tests writing that keeps meaning while losing fog, directly relevant to drafting accurate, plain acceptance criteria.

*Passage 4 (for Questions 10–12)*

"Discovery findings summary (extract): We interviewed 14 small-business owners about the licence renewal process. Eleven said the renewal reminder arrives too close to the deadline; of these, six had paid a late fee in the past two years. Nine interviewees renewed online, three by phone, and two by post. Several online renewers described the payment step as confusing, and two abandoned their first attempt before completing it. Interviewees who renewed by post did not report payment difficulties. We did not ask about the application process for new licences, and these findings should not be read as covering it. Sample size and recruitment mean findings are indicative rather than statistically representative."

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A majority of the interviewees said the renewal reminder arrives too close to the deadline."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A majority of 14 requires at least 8; eleven exceeds that comfortably (≈79%). This tests a small embedded calculation and reporting a finding at the strength the sample actually earns — "a majority of interviewees" is supportable, but "a majority of small-business owners" would be Cannot say given the passage's own caveat about representativeness.

**Question 11 (hard)**

Statement: "The late fees paid by interviewees were caused by the reminder arriving too close to the deadline."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association — six of the eleven who found reminders late had also paid late fees — but never asserts causation, and late fees can arise for reasons unrelated to reminder timing. This tests the correlation-causation boundary, the single most common overreach in findings summaries; the defensible phrasing keeps the association visible without promising causation.

**Question 12 (hard) — Inference within stated limits**

Asked "based on this extract, what should we look at first?", which response best reflects the findings and their stated limits?

- A) "Redesign the postal renewal route — it is the least used channel."
- B) "The strongest signals are reminder timing and the online payment step: most interviewees found reminders too late, and the payment step confused several online renewers, two of whom abandoned. Both warrant investigation, though with 14 interviews these are indicative findings, not proof."
- C) "Fix the new licence application process, since renewals and applications are similar."
- D) "No action can be considered until we run a statistically representative survey."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B ranks by signal strength, proposes proportionate next steps, and carries the passage's own caveat forward without letting it paralyse action. Option C walks past the passage's explicit fence that it does not cover the application process. Option D weaponises the caveat into inaction. This tests producing a findings summary a team can steer by: strongest signals first, behavioural evidence weighted above stated opinion, limits stated, action proportionate.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate tracks scope words** — in scope, deferred, new, existing — since most hard items turn on which population a rule governs.
- **Watch for whether a candidate reads a compound sentence to its end** before extracting a rule.
- **Note whether a candidate calibrates claim strength** — "leaning towards" is not "decided"; "associated" is not "caused".
- **Note whether a candidate does embedded arithmetic on paper** rather than eyeballing a majority or a proportion.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and budget by passage rather than by statement when planning the session.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding a "True" answer given because a statement is plausible** rather than stated in the passage.
- **Confusing False with Cannot say** — False requires contradiction; silence is never contradiction.
- **Missing when a candidate swaps populations** between two rules the passage sets out for two different groups.
- **Missing when a candidate over-reads a probing question as a stated position.**
- **Missing when a candidate lets a caveat swallow a finding, or lets a finding escape its caveat.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks which response is most or least effective. The material is human rather than technical: a stakeholder pushes for scope, a deadline collides with quality, a discrepancy surfaces in a colleague's figures, a colleague quietly struggles. The candidate is usually asked to choose the most effective response, choose the least effective, rank responses from best to worst, or rate each independently.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed because they measure judgement rather than speed. Responses are scored against a profile agreed by experienced practitioners — senior business analysts and others familiar with how UK government digital teams work. The scoring rewards user focus, open collaboration, ownership of outputs, honesty about problems and mistakes, proportionate escalation, and inclusion.

This matters for a junior business analyst because the level is defined by a particular balance: receiving direction from senior colleagues while being responsible for the output of specific tasks, and supporting stakeholder relationship management. That balance generates daily judgement calls — when to resolve something directly versus when to raise it, how to be helpful to a stakeholder without committing the team, what to do when process and deadline pull in opposite directions.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Judging when to act and when to raise** maps to the role level: being responsible for the output of specific tasks while receiving direction from more senior business analysts means constantly locating the line between one's own call and a supervisor's call.
- **Handling stakeholder pressure** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: this level explicitly supports stakeholder relationship management, and scenarios test being responsive and respectful without conceding scope or misrepresenting decisions.
- **Protecting evidence and honesty in analysis** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements**, **User experience analysis** and **Testing (business analysis)**: captured requirements, research findings, and test records are valuable only while truthful.
- **Team behaviours in delivery** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: ceremonies, WIP limits, and iteration only work when people behave as the method assumes.
- **Inclusion and user focus** map to **Business process improvement** and the profession's purpose: services and processes must work for everyone.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded. All options are things a real person might plausibly do — the differences lie in their consequences.

**Question 1 (easy) — The scope-expanding stakeholder**

A candidate is responsible for capturing requirements for a small improvement to a grants system. In a review session, a stakeholder from the finance team says: "While you're at it, add a requirement for a full audit-trail export — it's basically the same work." The candidate suspects it is substantial extra work, not in the agreed scope. What is the most effective response?

- A) Add the requirement — keeping finance stakeholders happy matters most.
- B) Tell the stakeholder that scope is fixed and the request cannot be considered.
- C) Capture the request accurately as a candidate requirement, note it as outside the current agreed scope, and take it to the senior business analyst and the prioritisation discussion — telling the stakeholder that is what is happening.
- D) Quietly investigate the export requirement for a few days before mentioning it to anyone.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C captures the need faithfully, makes its scope status explicit, routes it to the people who own prioritisation, and tells the stakeholder the route. Option A converts a hallway remark into committed scope; option B protects scope but loses the request and bruises the relationship; option D spends unprioritised time while hiding a live request from the team. This tests capturing everything, committing nothing beyond one's authority, and routing visibly.

**Question 2 (easy) — A model challenged in public**

A candidate produced a process model for a workshop. Mid-session, an operations team leader says, in front of everyone: "This is wrong — step four hasn't worked like that since last year." The candidate modelled it from the current procedure document. What is the most effective response?

- A) Defend the model firmly — the documented procedure was followed, so the model is correct.
- B) Thank them, say the model reflects the documented procedure, and suggest capturing how step four works in practice right now — since the gap between document and reality is itself a valuable finding.
- C) Apologise, agree the model is wrong, and offer to redo it after the workshop.
- D) Move the workshop on quickly to avoid conflict.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B reframes the moment as discovery: if procedure and reality have diverged, surfacing that is more valuable than defending the model's correctness. Option A defends correctness at the cost of the finding; option C concedes error with no evidence of one; option D loses the finding and discourages future challenges. This tests treating a model as a claim about reality that a source can get wrong, with loyalty to accuracy rather than to the artefact.

**Question 3 (easy) — A mistake discovered after distribution**

Two days after circulating a requirements summary, a candidate realises one requirement's priority is wrong — "must have" typed where the workshop agreed "could have". Development planning happens tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Correct the document today, reissue it to everyone with a short note saying what changed and why, and tell the senior business analyst.
- B) Say nothing — one priority among many is unlikely to matter.
- C) Mention it verbally at tomorrow's planning session if the requirement comes up.
- D) Correct the master document quietly so future readers see the right priority.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A "could have" recorded as "must have" directly distorts tomorrow's planning. Option A corrects the artefact, informs every recipient before they rely on the wrong version, and keeps the supervisor informed. Option D fixes the future while abandoning everyone who already has the wrong version. This tests handling an error fast, openly, and completely rather than gambling on it going unnoticed.

**Question 4 (easy) — The struggling ceremony**

A team's sprint retrospectives have become a formality: the same two people talk, actions are rarely recorded, and nothing changes. The candidate — not the facilitator — finds them unhelpful and suspects others do too. What is the most effective response?

- A) Stop attending; retrospectives are optional value, and this one is clearly zero.
- B) Tell the delivery manager privately that retrospectives are being run badly.
- C) At the next retrospective, raise the pattern itself as an improvement item — for example, suggesting rotating facilitation, a different format, and actually tracking actions — and offer to help.
- D) Wait until someone more senior notices; process quality is not a junior analyst's concern.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C uses the ceremony to repair the ceremony, converts private frustration into a shared, actionable observation, and offers help rather than only criticism. Option B routes around the team as a first move; option A abandons a team commitment; option D misreads a named skill — understanding how multidisciplinary teams and their methodologies work applies to ways of working, not just products. This tests raising a team problem constructively in the room, available to any team member regardless of seniority.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Research findings under pressure**

Usability testing findings are mixed: users completed the new journey faster, but four of six participants failed the eligibility-check step. The product manager, preparing a show-and-tell for senior leaders, says: "Let's lead with the speed improvement and leave the eligibility thing for another day — it'll muddy the story." The candidate drafted the findings summary. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — the product manager owns the narrative, and the eligibility finding will keep.
- B) Say the summary must reflect both findings; suggest framing the eligibility failure as a discovered risk with a plan ("journey is faster; eligibility step needs redesign — here's what we're doing"), and if the product manager still insists on dropping it, involve the senior business analyst before the summary is finalised.
- C) Present the full findings to the senior leaders directly at the show-and-tell to make sure the truth comes out.
- D) Keep the eligibility finding out of the summary but mention it quietly to a developer so someone knows.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four of six participants failing a step is a material finding, and a summary omitting it misinforms downstream decisions. Option B defends truthfulness while helping the product manager — "risk with a plan" is both honest and a stronger leadership story — and ends with a correctly scaled backstop. Option C hijacks a leadership meeting before trying a collaborative route; option D substitutes a private whisper for the record. This tests defending the truthfulness of an artefact meant to inform decisions.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Two seniors, two instructions**

A senior business analyst asked a candidate to spend the week detailing requirements for feature A. Today the product manager — who does not manage the candidate but leads the product — asks them to switch immediately to analysing a data question about feature B, "because the board meeting moved to Thursday". The senior business analyst is in workshops until tomorrow but reads messages at lunchtime. What is the most effective response?

- A) Switch to feature B — the product manager leads the product and the board meeting is a serious deadline.
- B) Continue with feature A — the senior business analyst's instruction predates the product manager's request.
- C) Tell the product manager about the current commitment, message the senior business analyst summarising the conflict and proposing a plan (for example, starting the feature B analysis now given the board deadline, resuming feature A on Friday), and proceed on the proposed plan if no objection comes by early afternoon.
- D) Split every day this week evenly between the two features without telling anyone.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C makes the conflict visible to both parties, proposes a concrete resolution, uses the known communication window, and includes a sensible default so a hard deadline is not hostage to silence. Options A and B both resolve mechanically and leave someone important in the dark; option D halves progress on both fronts while telling nobody. This tests engineering a situation so the right people can adjudicate quickly, rather than adjudicating unilaterally or concealing the conflict.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The uncomfortable data point**

Preparing materials for a process improvement workshop, a candidate finds that the "average approval time: 3 days" figure the improvement case has been built on excludes cases referred for extra checks — a fifth of all cases, averaging 15 days. Including them, the true average is around 5.4 days. The improvement case was written by the head of the operations team, sponsoring the workshop in three days. What is the most effective response?

- A) Use the 3-day figure — it is the sponsor's own number, and the workshop is about improvement anyway.
- B) Verify the calculation, then take it to the senior business analyst promptly, proposing how to raise it with the sponsor — for example, presenting both figures as "3 days for standard cases; 5.4 days overall including referred cases", which is arguably a stronger case for improvement.
- C) Present the corrected figure at the workshop without warning anyone, so the data speaks for itself.
- D) Email the sponsor's director about the misleading figure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verifying first respects that the exclusion might be a documented, defensible definition rather than an error. Routing through the senior business analyst, who knows the sponsor and history, with a proposal rather than a bare problem — including a framing that makes the correction easier to welcome — resolves it constructively. Option C ambushes the sponsor publicly; option D escalates an unverified discrepancy over two heads. This tests never suppressing a data problem, and never weaponising one either.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Accessibility versus the deadline**

Testing a new document-upload feature the afternoon before sprint review, a candidate finds it cannot be operated by keyboard alone — the upload button only responds to mouse clicks, excluding users who cannot use a mouse. The acceptance criteria do not mention keyboard access, though the organisation's standards require WCAG compliance. Developers say: "Log it as a nice-to-have for later." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree it is a nice-to-have, since the acceptance criteria passed.
- B) Record the criteria as passed, but log the keyboard failure as a defect against the organisation's accessibility standard — not a nice-to-have — flag the gap in the acceptance criteria to the senior business analyst, and suggest the demo mention the known issue.
- C) Insist the demo is cancelled until keyboard access works.
- D) Say nothing now and raise keyboard access at the retrospective.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The acceptance criteria, as written, genuinely passed, so the test record stays honest — but severity is set by the applicable standard and the users excluded, not by whether someone wrote a criterion for it, so "nice-to-have" mislabels a compliance defect. Flagging the criteria gap closes the loop that caused the miss. Option C overshoots a normal, disclosed work-in-progress demo; option D parks a compliance defect for the ceremony calendar. This tests passing the letter, logging the truth, and fixing the gap.

**Question 9 (moderate) — The colleague who asks to bend a record**

A developer asks a candidate, who maintains the traceability matrix, to mark two requirements as "validated with business owner". "She's on leave until Monday, but she'll definitely agree — and if those rows aren't green, the release review on Friday will make us re-plan for nothing." What is the most effective response?

- A) Mark them validated — the business owner almost certainly will agree, and blocking the release over paperwork helps nobody.
- B) Refuse and report the developer to the delivery manager for attempting to falsify records.
- C) Decline to mark them validated, but engage the real problem: check whether validation can genuinely happen before Friday (a deputy, a documented email approval, a brief call), and if not, tell the senior business analyst so the review hears an honest status with a plan rather than a red surprise.
- D) Mark them "validated" but keep a private note that they are not really.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C declines to record something that has not happened while taking the underlying problem seriously, checking for a legitimate fast path and, failing that, routing an honest status with a plan through the senior business analyst. Option A trades the matrix's integrity for schedule comfort; option D lets the official record lie while a private note preserves nothing the team can use. This tests that records exist for the team and are only worth maintaining truthfully.

**Question 10 (hard) — Least effective: the slipping deadline**

A candidate is responsible for delivering a business process analysis by Friday to feed a procurement decision. On Tuesday they realise a needed data source is unavailable until Thursday, making Friday extremely tight. Which response would be **least effective**?

- A) Tell the senior business analyst today about the data delay, with options: a Friday version flagged as provisional pending Thursday's data, a Monday delivery, or descoping one process area.
- B) Ask whether anyone knows an alternative source or extract for the same data that is available sooner.
- C) Work quietly and intensely, compress the analysis into Thursday night, and deliver something on Friday without mentioning the problem to anyone.
- D) Check with the procurement lead whether Friday is a hard gate or whether Monday with complete data would serve the decision better.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A, B and D are the standard toolkit for a slipping deadline — early escalation with options, attacking the constraint, and testing whether the deadline is truly fixed. Option C conceals a known risk from everyone who could help or adjust, stakes a procurement decision on a single compressed overnight analysis, and forecloses every option the others would have opened. This tests the principle that a visible deadline risk stops being private information the moment it is known.

**Question 11 (hard) — Ranking responses: the workshop that excludes**

Co-facilitating a requirements workshop for a service used heavily by people with limited digital access, a candidate notices the day before that every invited attendee is a policy or digital professional — no operational staff, no user representation. The senior business analyst set the list. Rank these responses from most to least effective: (1) raise it with the senior business analyst today, proposing a quick addition or an explicit plan to validate outputs with operational staff and user research afterwards; (2) say nothing before the workshop, but during it, repeatedly challenge attendees' assumptions by asking "how do we know users want this?"; (3) add two operational staff to the invitation without consulting anyone; (4) run the workshop as planned and never revisit the gap.

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4
- D) 1, 2, 4, 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 surfaces the gap to its owner in time, with proportionate fixes offered. Response 4 delivers requirements built entirely on professionals' assumptions, permanently unvalidated — worse than any relational harm. Response 3 pursues the right value by overriding the senior colleague's decision without a word, a recoverable but real trust breach. Response 2 is a weak half-measure that keeps the gap visible without fixing it. This tests ranking a representation gap as an analysis defect, not merely a fairness concern, and following the hierarchy of raise-and-propose, then keep-visible, then unilateral action, then silence.

**Question 12 (hard) — Competing goods: mentoring versus delivery**

A trainee business analyst who joined three weeks ago brings their first solo process model, visibly proud, due to be presented to the operations manager tomorrow morning. It has real problems — inconsistent notation, a missing decision point, two steps in the wrong order. The candidate has a requirements deadline of their own tomorrow, and was asked by the senior business analyst to "keep an eye on" the trainee. What is the most effective response?

- A) Fix the model directly tonight and send it back — fastest for everyone, and the presentation will go well.
- B) Say it needs work and list the problems by email, then return to the deadline.
- C) Spend a focused half-hour with the trainee now: walk through one or two problems together so they learn the checking method, let them fix the rest themselves this afternoon, offer a quick end-of-day review, and mention to the senior business analyst that this is being balanced against the deadline.
- D) Say it is fine as it is — confidence matters most at trainee level, and the operations manager will forgive a rough first model.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C spends the scarce resource — time — where it teaches most: transferring the checking method rather than just correcting the diagram, letting the trainee fix the remainder, protecting tomorrow's presentation with a quick check, and keeping the workload trade-off visible to the senior colleague who delegated it. Option A produces a good model and no growth, leaving the trainee unable to defend it under questioning; option B is cold and poorly timed; option D protects today's feelings at tomorrow's expense. This tests treating developing others as part of the job at this level, not a distraction from it.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate engages the underlying problem** rather than only refusing a bad request or complying with it.
- **Watch for whether a candidate locates the authority line** in a scenario — whose call this actually is — before choosing.
- **Note whether a "least effective" answer identifies concealment** — silent fixes, quiet workarounds, unmentioned risks — as the most damaging pattern.
- **In ranking items, note whether a candidate anchors the extremes first**, then compares the middle options by which harms are recoverable.
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that resolves a conflict by seniority alone** rather than by making information visible to the right decision-maker.
- **Missing when a candidate treats loyalty to a colleague as justification for a false status or a bent record.**
- **Rewarding over-escalation** — jumping to directors or public forums before direct conversation and normal line management.
- **Missing under-escalation** — sitting on a known risk because raising it feels awkward.
- **Rewarding a heroic fix that hides a problem from the people who own it**, or that solves someone's problem for them rather than building their capability.
