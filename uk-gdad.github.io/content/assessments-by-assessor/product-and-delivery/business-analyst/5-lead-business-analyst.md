# Lead Business Analyst - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for lead business analyst, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At this level a candidate works in the enterprise arena, in highly complex problem spaces typically spanning multiple products or services, as a leader in the business analysis community across government and externally, with functional or people management accountabilities and ownership of the main stakeholder relationships. The material is job-specific: it uses the artefacts a lead business analyst genuinely handles — conceptual models supporting strategic planning, cross-cutting options appraisals, portfolio-level requirement sets, operating model designs, stakeholder strategies — rather than abstract puzzles.

This document covers four types of assessment, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — percentages, ratios, weighted averages, rates, and interpreting tables and charts.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — precise comprehension, True/False/Cannot-say discipline, inference, and clear-writing judgement.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — realistic dilemmas involving strategic influence, evidence integrity at scale, building capability in others, and methodology leadership.

Each section explains what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, maps its dimensions to the named skills in the role, gives a substantial set of practice questions with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, then gives administration tips and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Offer any unscored practice questions the platform provides. At this level, errors that matter are systemic — a candidate's advice reaches directors and boards who rarely check the underlying reasoning — so the situational judgement items in particular reward responses that fix both the immediate instance and the underlying mechanism; score for that combination rather than a single "correct" action. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. A candidate is presented with the materials of enterprise-level analysis — conceptual models, cross-programme dependency maps, operating model comparisons, portfolio prioritisation frameworks, governance papers — and asked to reason quickly and accurately about them.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is typically norm-referenced and often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately. Most platforms offer unscored practice questions before the timer starts; encourage the candidate to take them.

This kind of assessment matters for a lead business analyst because the role is defined by complexity that exceeds any single artefact: determining scope and methodology for representing complex programmes across an organisation, framing problems so people who will never see the underlying detail can act on them, spotting when two programmes' models quietly contradict each other, and troubleshooting situations that arrive tangled, political and half-described. The people this candidate would advise rarely check the reasoning behind that advice; they act on it.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in the role profile:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business modelling** and **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: determining the scope and methodology for representing complex programmes, and helping define capabilities and operating models, both mean recognising recurring structures across multiple products and services at once.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Consultancy**: advising on options analysis, feasibility and impact assessment, and providing recommendations from specialist knowledge, requires valid reasoning from constraints and evidence to consequences — and the discipline to deduce nothing more.
- **Error checking** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements** and **Testing (business analysis)**: directing the approach to requirements management and reviewing test plans and outcome reports means detecting inconsistencies between artefacts produced by many hands across many teams.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: negotiating delivery priorities, directing stakeholder strategy, and choosing where scarce attention goes across multiple products are all ranking problems under competing pressures.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Business process improvement** and **Digital and data systems analysis**: leading the identification of large-scale improvement opportunities, and bringing business perspectives and technical concepts together into recommendations, both mean decomposing tangled enterprise problems into parts that can be reasoned about, tested and recombined.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across programme models**

Three programmes have each modelled their case-handling process for a cross-government shared capability proposal. Programme A: Receive → Validate → Assess → Decide → Notify. Programme B: Receive → Validate → Assess → Decide → Notify → Archive. Programme C: Receive → Assess → Validate → Decide → Notify. Which observation is most significant for a shared capability design?

- A) Programme B has one more step than the others.
- B) Programme C validates after assessment rather than before, which may reflect a genuinely different control philosophy — or an error — and must be resolved before the processes can share a capability.
- C) All three programmes use five or six steps, so they are essentially identical.
- D) Programme A lacks an Archive step, so it must be non-compliant with records requirements.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Programmes A and B share an identical core sequence; Programme C inverts validation and assessment, which a shared capability cannot serve without configuration. Option C confuses superficial similarity with structural equivalence; D leaps from an absent step in a summary model to a compliance verdict. This tests distinguishing structural differences between models from cosmetic ones — the core pattern skill of determining scope and methodology for representing complex programmes across an organisation.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an assurance rule**

A portfolio assurance rule states: "A programme may proceed past its alpha gate only if its business analysis products have been peer-reviewed by an analyst from outside the programme." Programme X proceeded past its alpha gate last month. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) Programme X's analysis products were of high quality.
- B) Programme X's analysis products were peer-reviewed by an external-to-programme analyst, assuming the rule was followed.
- C) Programme X's analysis products were peer-reviewed by an external-to-programme analyst.
- D) The peer review found no defects.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule makes external peer review a necessary condition for passing the gate; if the rule was followed, the review happened — but nothing confirms the rule was followed, so the deduction must carry that assumption explicitly, which B does and C omits. A and D confuse the occurrence of review with its outcome. This tests surfacing a hidden assumption explicitly, since governance rules are exactly the things that sometimes get waived under pressure, and advice that relies on an unstated assumption is advice that fails audit.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a conceptual model**

A conceptual model commissioned for strategic planning states: "Each service is owned by exactly one directorate. Each directorate owns at least one service. The Licensing service is owned by both the Regulation directorate and the Commercial directorate." What is the correct characterisation?

- A) The model is internally consistent.
- B) The third statement contradicts the first; the model cannot be baselined until the ownership of Licensing is resolved to a single directorate or the cardinality rule is deliberately changed.
- C) The second statement contradicts the first.
- D) The model is consistent provided the two directorates cooperate.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The first statement asserts one-to-one ownership; the third asserts dual ownership of Licensing; they cannot both be true. Option D mistakes an organisational workaround for logical consistency — cooperation does not make "exactly one" compatible with "both". This tests recognising a cardinality contradiction in a conceptual model, and that the resolution is a decision (a single named owner, or a model-wide cardinality change with wider consequences), not a redrawing.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across an enterprise portfolio**

Monday morning presents: (1) an investment committee paper due Thursday, 80% complete, deciding between two operating model options; (2) a request to speak at a cross-government community event in three weeks, agenda deadline Friday; (3) a supplier's requirements interpretation that will, if built, breach a data-sharing constraint, with build starting Monday next; (4) a quarterly capability review of eight managed analysts, due in two weeks, not yet drafted. Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4 — committee paper, then the constraint breach, then the agenda, then the reviews
- B) 3, 1, 2, 4 — the constraint breach first because breaches are always first, then the paper, then the rest
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — strict deadline order
- D) 3, 1, 4, 2 — breach, paper, reviews, community

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The committee paper is 80% complete, feeds a Thursday decision, and is scarce, non-delegable work. The constraint breach is urgent but its first response is cheap and immediate — a same-day instruction to pause and a delegation to document the gap, with review midweek — so it does not need to displace the paper. Option B's "breaches are always first" is a slogan that, once the cheap first response is understood, is unnecessary; D buries a community commitment that needs only a short reply by Friday. This tests choosing what to delegate with direction versus what to do personally, not just sequencing by rule.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction across dependency chains**

A portfolio dependency map: (i) Service P's re-platforming requires the shared payments capability; (ii) the shared payments capability go-live requires Programme Q's API gateway; (iii) Programme Q's API gateway has slipped by two quarters; (iv) Service R's re-platforming requires no shared capabilities. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) Service P's re-platforming will slip by two quarters.
- B) Service P's re-platforming cannot complete earlier than the delayed payments capability allows; Service R's re-platforming is unaffected by the slip, so far as these dependencies show.
- C) Service R will now re-platform before Service P.
- D) The payments capability should be descoped from Service P.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The slip constrains P's earliest possible date, but whether P slips by exactly two quarters depends on unstated float, so A overstates. R has no mapped dependency on the slip, though B's qualifier "so far as these dependencies show" matters, since unmapped dependencies may exist. C predicts a race the facts do not state; D is an unmotivated recommendation, not a deduction. This tests propagating constraints exactly — earliest dates, not certain slips — and flagging the limits of the map being reasoned from.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in demand data**

Across twelve services, requirements churn (baselined requirements changed within three months): nine services sit between 8% and 14%; three services show 31%, 29% and 34%, and all three take their requirements from the same policy directorate. What is the strongest analytical move?

- A) Conclude the three teams' analysts are weak and arrange training.
- B) Conclude policy-driven churn is inevitable and exclude the three services from churn reporting.
- C) Treat the clustering as a signal of a shared upstream cause — investigate how that directorate's policy positions are communicated and stabilised, and whether analysis is engaging with policy early enough — before attributing anything to the teams.
- D) Average all twelve services and report 16% churn as within tolerance.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three outliers sharing a common upstream source shifts the probable cause from the teams to the shared input. Option A punishes the visible node rather than the causal one; B removes the very signal needed to improve the interface; D launders a bimodal distribution into a meaningless mean. This tests locating a system-level pattern's cause at the right level of the system — the opportunity here is the policy-to-delivery interface, not any one team.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking an options appraisal's logic**

An options appraisal argues: "Option 1 (extend the legacy system) is rejected because the legacy platform exits support in 2027. Option 2 (single new build) is rejected because no single supplier can meet all requirements. Option 3 (modular build on the shared platform) is therefore the right choice." What is the principal logical weakness?

- A) The appraisal does not state Option 3's costs.
- B) The argument eliminates alternatives without ever positively assessing Option 3 against the requirements, risks and constraints — survival by elimination is not evidence of adequacy, and the option set itself may be incomplete.
- C) Option 1's rejection is invalid because support contracts can be extended.
- D) Three options is too few for a compliant appraisal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Even if both rejections are sound, nothing shown demonstrates Option 3 is feasible, affordable or acceptable — it merely remains standing, and the option set's exhaustiveness is asserted nowhere. Option A names a symptom of this deeper gap; C challenges a premise on outside knowledge the appraisal may address elsewhere, but is not the principal weakness; D invents a rule that does not exist. This tests recognising the recommended option that was never actually appraised, only left over — a common defect in options analysis quality assurance.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving: framing an ambiguous request**

A director general says: "Casework is too slow, morale is poor, and the board wants it fixed by spring. Have a look at it." Casework spans three services, two case management systems, and 400 staff. What is the strongest first analytical move?

- A) Commission a full current-state process map of all three services to a uniform level of detail.
- B) Define and agree the problem frame first: what "too slow" means measurably and to whom, how slowness and morale might be related, what "fixed" would look like by spring, and which slices of the casework system the evidence says to examine first — then scope analysis to that frame.
- C) Procure a casework software replacement, since two systems for one function is the obvious root cause.
- D) Survey all 400 staff about morale before any process analysis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The request is an executive symptom report, not a problem definition. Option B forces the questions that make spring achievable — benchmark, case types, causal relationship between morale and slowness, what "fixed" means — then lets evidence select where deep analysis pays. Option A spends the whole runway on uniform detail across a system whose problems are almost certainly non-uniform; C decides the answer before analysis; D elevates one strand to a prerequisite it need not be. This tests framing a problem, named directly in the consultancy skill, before committing scarce analytical attention.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint design of a review programme**

Four programmes — Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta — need deep-dive reviews across four consecutive weeks, one per week. Constraints: (i) Alpha's review must precede Charlie's; (ii) Bravo's review cannot be in week 1; (iii) Delta must be reviewed the week immediately after Bravo; (iv) Charlie cannot be reviewed in week 4. Which schedule satisfies all constraints?

- A) Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta
- B) Alpha, Bravo, Delta, Charlie
- C) Bravo, Delta, Alpha, Charlie
- D) Alpha, Charlie, Bravo, Delta

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** From (iii), Bravo and Delta occupy consecutive weeks; from (ii), Bravo is not week 1. If Bravo–Delta take weeks 2–3, Alpha and Charlie take weeks 1 and 4 — but Charlie cannot be week 4, a contradiction. So Bravo–Delta take weeks 3–4, leaving weeks 1–2 for Alpha then Charlie. That is option D. Option A breaks (iii); B breaks (iv); C breaks (ii). This tests deriving forced placements from the tightest constraints first, rather than testing candidates by feel — the method behind real multi-programme scheduling.

**Question 10 (hard) — Deduction about operating model consequences**

A target operating model states: (i) all initial customer contact moves to a single national contact centre; (ii) regional offices retain casework but lose local contact teams; (iii) cases requiring local knowledge are flagged by the contact centre and routed to the relevant regional office. Today roughly 30% of contacts are resolved on the spot by local teams using local knowledge, without ever becoming cases. If the model is implemented as stated, which consequence must follow?

- A) The 30% of contacts previously resolved locally on the spot must either be resolved by the national centre — without the local knowledge that resolved them — or be flagged and routed as cases to regional offices, increasing regional casework.
- B) Customer satisfaction will fall by 30%.
- C) Regional offices will refuse to accept the routed cases.
- D) The national contact centre will need 30% more staff than planned.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** With local contact teams removed and initial contact centralised, the 30% must land at the national centre, which lacks the local knowledge; the model's only mechanism for such contacts is flagging and routing them as cases — swelling regional casework with work that previously never became casework at all. That disjunction is forced by the model's own statements. B invents a quantified prediction; C and D predict behaviour and staffing the facts do not give. This tests deriving the workload and knowledge-flow consequences an operating model's structures entail, and surfacing a forced disjunction before implementation makes it a discovery.

**Question 11 (hard) — Prioritising analytical investment under uncertainty**

Two of four proposed discovery investigations can be staffed this quarter: (1) a minister-favoured new digital service, high political enthusiasm, but a comparable service in another department failed user research testing badly last year; (2) consolidation of three overlapping internal case systems, unglamorous, strong prima facie benefits case, low uncertainty; (3) a data-sharing initiative two directorates each assume the other is analysing, unowned, with a statutory deadline eight months out; (4) a proof of concept for an AI triage tool a supplier has offered "free of charge" using live case data. Which pair represents the strongest analytical investment?

- A) 1 and 4 — highest political and innovation value
- B) 2 and 3 — the strong benefits case plus the orphaned statutory deadline
- C) 1 and 2 — balance political demand with solid benefits
- D) 3 and 4 — the two nobody else is doing

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Investigation 3 is the highest-risk item precisely because everyone assumes it is covered, with a short statutory deadline. Investigation 2 is a reliable, low-uncertainty return. Investigation 1's comparable failure suggests a short, cheap evidence review first rather than a full discovery slot; investigation 4's "free" proof of concept carries data protection costs that need resolving before, not after, a supplier touches live data. This tests risk-adjusted prioritisation at portfolio altitude — guiding tactical decision making and influencing strategic plans — over responding to the loudest signal (political enthusiasm) or the two orphaned items superficially.

**Question 12 (hard) — Applied problem solving: reconciling contradictory evidence**

The performance dashboard shows average end-to-end processing time falling steadily for six months. Frontline staff and stakeholder complaints both insist the service is getting slower. Both sources are confirmed accurate as measured. Which hypothesis best reconciles the contradiction and should be tested first?

- A) Staff are resistant to change and their perception is simply wrong.
- B) The dashboard's average is being pulled down by compositional change — for example, a growing share of simple, fast cases — while complex cases are actually getting slower; test by segmenting processing time by case type over the six months.
- C) The dashboard has a calculation bug.
- D) Complainants are a vocal minority and should be discounted.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** When two accurate measurements contradict, the resolution is almost always that they measure different things — an overall average can fall while every subgroup worsens if the mix shifts toward fast cases. Hypothesis B explains both observations and is cheaply testable by segmentation. Options A and D discard an evidence source the stem states is accurate; C contradicts the stem. This tests recognising a compositional (Simpson's paradox-style) mechanism that reconciles two accurate but conflicting sources, rather than discarding one of them.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate names hidden assumptions explicitly** — "assuming the rule was followed", "so far as the map shows" — rather than deducing a stronger conclusion than the facts support.
- **Watch for whether a candidate attributes a systemic pattern to the shared input rather than the visible individuals or teams.**
- **Note whether a candidate insists on positive assessment** of a surviving option rather than accepting elimination as adequacy.
- **Note whether a candidate frames an ambiguous request before committing analytical resource to it.**
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that converts a constraint into a certain prediction** rather than an earliest-possible bound.
- **Crediting an answer that attributes a systemic pattern to individuals.**
- **Missing when a candidate accepts "the others were rejected" as an appraisal of what remains.**
- **Missing when a candidate analyses before framing** an ambiguous executive request.
- **Rewarding an answer that discards one of two accurate but conflicting evidence sources** rather than finding the reconciling mechanism.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures a candidate's ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from quantitative information of the kind this role genuinely handles. The arithmetic stays within percentages, ratios, averages, rates, and projections; what is really under test is judgement — extracting the right figures from tables and charts, choosing the right operation under time pressure, and recognising what a number does and does not establish.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 40 minutes, with 15 to 25 questions, each anchored to a table, chart, or short data-rich passage. Calculators and rough paper are normally permitted.

This matters for a lead business analyst because the numbers at this level are usually someone else's numbers: suppliers present benefits models, programmes present velocity and churn statistics, finance presents unit-cost comparisons, directors quote figures in committees. This role's quality assurance is the gate quantitative claims pass through before reaching investment committees, and the ones that mislead tend to mislead structurally — wrong denominators, unweighted averages, mix effects, annualisation games, survivorship in samples.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and percentage change** map to **Business process improvement**: leading the identification of large-scale improvement opportunities means comparing baselines, targets and actuals across services without conflating points, percentages and bases.
- **Ratios, unit costs and weighted comparisons** map to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Consultancy**: feasibility and impact assessments turn on cost per case, benefit-cost ratios and like-for-like normalisation across options of different scales.
- **Averages, distributions and mix effects** map to **Business modelling** and **Digital and data systems analysis**: conceptual and quantitative models that support strategic planning must survive compositional questions or they will misdirect the decisions built on them.
- **Rates, capacity and forecasting** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: negotiating delivery priorities and shaping operating models both require converting demand, capacity and productivity assumptions into feasible plans.
- **Reading tables and charts under scrutiny** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders means verifying the figures on which agreement is about to be reached, and reviewing test outcome reports means re-deriving their aggregates.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Share of a portfolio**

A portfolio contains 45 active analysis engagements: 18 in citizen-facing services, 12 in internal operations, 9 in data and reporting, and the rest in policy support. What percentage of engagements are in policy support?

- A) 6%
- B) 13.3%
- C) 15%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Policy support = 6; 6 ÷ 45 ≈ 13.3%. Option A reports the count as if it were the percentage. This tests basic proportion calculation and a sense-check (6 of 45 is a bit more than 1 in 8) — portfolio composition figures like these open most function-level reports.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change with the correct base**

Average cost per case fell from £48 to £42 over a year. A director claims "a 14% efficiency gain". Is the claim accurate?

- A) Yes — the reduction is 14.3%, which rounds to 14%.
- B) No — the reduction is 12.5%; the director has divided by the new cost instead of the original.
- C) No — the reduction is 6%.
- D) No — the reduction is 87.5%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reduction = £6; 6 ÷ 48 (the original) = 12.5%. The director's 14% comes from dividing by the new value (6 ÷ 42). This tests re-deriving a percentage-change claim from its correct base before it reaches a committee — a check that catches an error that flatters improvements and therefore survives unchallenged for a long time.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a two-way table**

Requirements produced and defects found in review: Team North, 120 requirements, 18 defects. Team South, 200 requirements, 24 defects. Which team has the higher defect rate per requirement, and what is it?

- A) South — 24 defects is more than 18
- B) North — 15% versus South's 12%
- C) North — 18% versus South's 15%
- D) They are equal at 12%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** North: 18 ÷ 120 = 15%; South: 24 ÷ 200 = 12%. North's rate is higher despite South's larger count. This tests computing rates rather than comparing raw counts, since raw defect counts punish the most productive teams — a small act of measurement governance with large cultural effects.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Weighted average across services**

Consolidating user satisfaction: Service A, 82% satisfied from 1,500 respondents; Service B, 68% satisfied from 500 respondents. A draft paper reports "average satisfaction 75%". What is the correct combined satisfaction rate?

- A) 75.0%
- B) 76.5%
- C) 78.5%
- D) 80.0%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Weighted: (1,500×0.82 + 500×0.68) ÷ 2,000 = 1,570 ÷ 2,000 = 78.5%. The draft's 75% is the unweighted mean, understating the true figure by 3.5 points. This tests re-deriving an average of averages from counts before it reaches a committee under the candidate's name, and, for a strong candidate's answer, noting that unweighted means can inflate as easily as deflate depending on which side has the smaller sample.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Benefit-cost ratio comparison**

Proposal 1: costs £600,000, delivers £1,500,000 over five years. Proposal 2: costs £250,000, delivers £750,000. Available budget £700,000; proposals are independent. What does benefit-cost analysis support?

- A) Fund Proposal 1 only — it has the largest total benefit.
- B) Fund Proposal 2 only — it has the higher benefit-cost ratio (3.0 versus 2.5).
- C) Fund neither until ratios exceed 3.0.
- D) Fund Proposal 2 first on ratio, but note that the £700,000 budget cannot cover both (£850,000); the residual £450,000 cannot fund Proposal 1, so the committee must either fund 2 alone, fund 1 alone, or seek £150,000 more to capture both.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Ratios: Proposal 1, 2.5; Proposal 2, 3.0. Ratio ranking favours 2, but the combined cost (£850,000) exceeds the budget, and 1 alone delivers double the benefit of 2 alone within budget — a mechanical "fund the higher ratio" rule would leave £450,000 idle and £750,000 of benefit unclaimed. This tests laying out affordable combinations with totals rather than applying a single decision rule silently when a budget constraint binds.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capacity modelling for a function**

A function of 10 analysts has 44 productive weeks each per year. Demand: programme work 320 analyst-weeks, discovery 60, assurance reviews 40. 5% of gross capacity is held in reserve. How many analyst-weeks short (or spare) is the function?

- A) 2 weeks short
- B) 20 weeks spare
- C) 2 weeks spare
- D) 42 weeks short

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Gross capacity = 440; reserve = 22, leaving 418 plannable; demand = 420; balance = −2. Option B forgets the reserve; C flips the sign; D double-counts it. This tests presenting a full capacity plan — gross, reserve, net, demand, balance — since the credibility of every subsequent prioritisation conversation rests on a truthful capacity number with no headroom hidden or invented.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Trend versus seasonality**

Eight quarters of application volumes (Q1–Q8, in thousands): 10.2, 8.1, 9.0, 12.3, 11.0, 8.9, 9.8, 13.4. A colleague's paper states: "Volumes fell 19% between Q4 and Q6, indicating declining demand." What is the sounder reading?

- A) The colleague is right: 12.3k to 8.9k is a material decline.
- B) Compare like quarters across years: Q1→Q5 rose 7.8%, Q2→Q6 rose 9.9%, Q3→Q7 rose 8.9%, Q4→Q8 rose 8.9% — demand is growing roughly 8–10% year on year with a seasonal peak in Q4-equivalents; the "fall" is seasonality.
- C) Demand is flat because the mean of all eight quarters is stable.
- D) The data is too noisy to interpret.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The colleague compares a seasonal peak with an off-peak quarter, manufacturing a decline from the seasonal cycle itself. Comparing each quarter with its counterpart one year earlier shows consistent 8–10% growth. This tests using year-on-year comparison rather than adjacent-period comparison in seasonal data, since mistaking seasonality for decline (or growth) is among the most expensive simple errors an organisation can make.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Unit cost normalisation across options**

Outsourced document processing: £2.10 per document, minimum annual charge £630,000. In-house: £400,000 fixed plus £0.80 per document. Low scenario 250,000 documents; high scenario 500,000. What does the cost comparison show?

- A) Outsourced is cheaper in both scenarios.
- B) In-house is cheaper in both scenarios: £600k versus £630k at low volume, and £800k versus £1,050k at high volume.
- C) In-house is cheaper at high volume only.
- D) Outsourced is cheaper at low volume only.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Low volume: outsourced £525,000 lifted to the £630,000 minimum; in-house £600,000. High volume: outsourced £1,050,000; in-house £800,000. In-house wins both, and the gap widens with volume. This tests reading fixed/variable structures and contractual floors into a like-for-like comparison — without the minimum-charge clause, outsourced would win the low scenario, which is exactly why such clauses exist.

**Question 9 (hard) — Time-phased benefits with attrition**

Automation releases 12,000 staff-hours per year at £28 per hour, from go-live at the start of Q3 of year 1. Realisation ramps: 40% in the first live quarter, 70% in the second, 100% thereafter. From the start of year 3, the annual figure erodes by 10% per year (of the original full-year value). What benefit does the forecast give for year 1 and year 3 respectively?

- A) Year 1: £92,400; Year 3: £302,400
- B) Year 1: £168,000; Year 3: £336,000
- C) Year 1: £92,400; Year 3: £336,000
- D) Year 1: £336,000; Year 3: £302,400

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Full annual value £336,000, quarterly £84,000. Year 1: Q1–Q2 £0; Q3 (40%) £33,600; Q4 (70%) £58,800; total £92,400. Year 3: full value £336,000 less 10% decay (£33,600) = £302,400. This tests building a quarter-by-quarter timeline before checking totals — the two honesty adjustments (ramp-up and decay) that separate credible benefits models from optimistic ones.

**Question 10 (hard) — Sample composition and survivorship**

A supplier reports: "92% of users who completed our post-transaction survey rated the new journey good or excellent (n = 1,204)." Operational data: 48,000 users attempted the journey, 31,200 completed it; the survey was offered only on the completion page. Which statement best characterises what the 92% establishes?

- A) 92% of all users rate the journey good or excellent.
- B) The journey is performing well; 92% is an excellent score on a large sample.
- C) Among completers who chose to answer (1,204 of 31,200, a 3.9% response rate), 92% rated the journey positively; the figure says nothing about the 16,800 users (35% of attempts) who never completed — the group most likely to have had a poor experience — so it cannot support claims about users overall.
- D) The survey is worthless and should be ignored.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 16,800 attempters (35%) never completed and were never surveyed; of completers, only 3.9% responded. The 92% is doubly filtered by survivorship and self-selection, useful as evidence about completers' sentiment but not generalisable to all users. This tests asking "who could not appear in this number?" — the numeric core of reviewing evidence before it reaches decision makers.

**Question 11 (hard) — Mix effect in a performance comparison**

Year 1: simple cases 40,000 at 5 days; complex cases 10,000 at 25 days. Year 2: simple cases 55,000 at 6 days; complex cases 5,000 at 30 days. A report claims: "Average processing time improved from 9 days to 8 days." Verify the averages and choose the best characterisation.

- A) The claim is arithmetically wrong.
- B) The averages are right (Year 1: 9 days; Year 2: 8 days), but both case types got slower (5→6 days, +20%; 25→30 days, +20%); the "improvement" is entirely mix shift toward simple cases. Performance worsened for every case a user actually experiences.
- C) The averages are right and performance genuinely improved.
- D) Complex cases should be excluded as outliers, showing simple-case performance of 6 days.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic checks out (9.0 and 8.0 days), but every case type deteriorated 20%; the average improved only because the caseload mix shifted toward simple cases. This tests recognising this compositional pattern (a form of Simpson's paradox) on sight, and the corrective habit of reporting segment-level trends alongside any aggregate.

**Question 12 (hard) — Reconciling finance and operational counts**

Finance reports £8.40 cost per transaction (total cost £4,200,000). Operations reports 560,000 transactions. Finance's count includes only completed transactions; the dashboard counts attempts; 12% of attempts fail before completion, costing on average half as much. Using the dashboard's attempt count, what is the approximate cost per completed transaction implied by consistent accounting?

- A) £7.50
- B) £8.04
- C) £8.40 — finance's figure is already on that basis
- D) £8.93

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Finance's £8.40 implies 500,000 completed transactions (4,200,000 ÷ 8.40); the dashboard's attempts less the 12% failure rate give 492,800 completions — close enough to indicate the two sources broadly agree once counting bases align, and finance's figure already represents cost per completed transaction. This tests establishing precisely what each source counts before performing an adjustment the question does not actually call for.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate re-derives a figure before relying on it**, rather than passing on an unverified headline.
- **Watch for whether a candidate draws a timeline** for any time-phased calculation before multiplying.
- **Note whether a candidate interrogates a sample's denominator** — who is counted, excluded, or never had the chance to appear.
- **Note whether a candidate segments an aggregate** to check for a mix effect before accepting a headline trend.
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that divides by the new value** in a percentage-change calculation.
- **Crediting an answer that averages averages** across differently sized groups without weighting.
- **Missing when a candidate trusts an aggregate across a mix shift.**
- **Crediting an answer that ranks options by benefit-cost ratio alone** when a budget constraint binds.
- **Missing when a candidate performs an adjustment a reconciliation question does not call for**, rather than first establishing what each figure already represents.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate extracts meaning from written material of the kind this role handles daily — policy papers, legislative summaries, governance terms of reference, supplier contracts, ministerial correspondence, standards and strategies — and how soundly they reason from what a text actually says rather than what experience predicts it will say.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes. Each short passage carries a cluster of questions: True / False / Cannot say; best-supported inference; and clear-writing judgement. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, setting aside outside knowledge — harder for experts, since expertise supplies confident assumptions the passage may not share.

This matters for a lead business analyst because verbal precision operates at organisational stakes: the wording conventions this level sets for requirements management propagate through every team influenced. Senior correspondence must be read for exactly what it commits and withholds, and written advice from this level is often quoted, sometimes selectively, in papers the candidate never sees.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: translating business drivers, goals and constraints into objectives starts with reading source documents — strategies, legislation, mandates — with exactness, including their qualifiers, definitions and scope limits.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis**: separating what evidence states from what it suggests, and holding "not established" as a legitimate finding, is the verbal form of sound options and impact analysis.
- **Inference judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Consultancy**: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders requires reading correspondence for its commitments, hedges and deliberate silences.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Business modelling** and this level's coaching accountabilities: conceptual models and advisory papers that support strategic planning must be unambiguous to readers who cannot query the author.
- **Reading standards and contracts** maps to **Testing (business analysis)** and **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: acceptance obligations, quality gates and methodology standards bind only what their words actually bind.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–5)*

"Departments may adopt the shared casework platform on either the standard or the enhanced tier. The standard tier includes hosting, maintenance and quarterly releases. The enhanced tier additionally includes a dedicated integration team and monthly releases. Departments adopting either tier must complete a data protection impact assessment before migration and must nominate a senior responsible owner. Requests for functionality changes are considered by the cross-government design board, which meets monthly; the board prioritises requests that benefit multiple departments. A department may exit the platform with six months' notice, provided all data extraction obligations in Schedule 4 are met. Nothing in this agreement transfers statutory accountability for casework decisions from the adopting department to the platform provider."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "A department on the standard tier receives releases every month."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage assigns quarterly releases to the standard tier and reserves monthly releases as an enhanced-tier addition — a direct contradiction. This tests reading tier and scope boundaries precisely, since two-tier structures generate distractors that quietly transfer a feature between tiers, exactly as happens in real commercial conversations.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "A department must nominate a senior responsible owner before adopting either tier."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage's timing condition ("before migration") attaches only to the DPIA, not to the SRO nomination, which is mandatory but carries no stated timing. The statement adds a timing requirement the passage neither guarantees nor contradicts. This tests noticing how naturally a nearby qualifier can be distributed across a whole sentence — the mechanism by which requirements acquire unintended constraints.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "If a department exits the platform without meeting Schedule 4's data extraction obligations, its six months' notice is invalid."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Schedule 4 compliance is a condition of the exit right, but the passage never spells out the consequence of failing it — invalid notice is one possibility among several. This tests the difference between knowing a condition exists and knowing what happens when it fails.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following is best supported by the passage?

- A) The design board will reject a functionality request that benefits only one department.
- B) The framework's authors anticipated that departments might be held responsible for casework decisions made using the platform, and wished to prevent any doubt that this responsibility stays with departments.
- C) The enhanced tier is better value for money than the standard tier.
- D) Schedule 4 requires data to be extracted within six months.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A defensive clause of this kind ("nothing in this agreement transfers... accountability") is included precisely because the transfer might otherwise be argued or assumed. Option A over-reads a priority rule as a rejection rule; D fuses two unrelated details (six months' notice, Schedule 4) into fabricated content. This tests distinguishing what a text is doing (foreclosing an argument) from what it merely mentions.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

A department asks for a board summary of the exit provision. Which summary is most accurate and clear?

- A) "We can leave whenever we like with six months' notice."
- B) "We can exit on six months' notice, but only if we meet the data extraction obligations in Schedule 4 — so exit planning must include Schedule 4 compliance from the start."
- C) "Exit is essentially impossible; the agreement locks us in via Schedule 4."
- D) "Exit requires six months' notice and design board approval."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B reproduces both elements of the provision and adds one implication that follows directly from the conditional structure. Option A drops the proviso; C editorialises it into an impossibility; D imports the design board from an unrelated clause. This tests the standard boards act on without reading the underlying agreement: every element of the source, nothing not in the source, plus only implications that follow necessarily.

*Passage 2 (for Questions 6–8)*

"Thank you for the discovery report on casework consolidation. I have read the summary and recommendations. I am minded to accept the recommendation to consolidate onto a single platform, subject to two conditions. First, the transition must not reduce service levels for vulnerable users at any point; if your implementation analysis cannot evidence this, I would rather phase more slowly. Second, the trade union consultation must begin before any public announcement. I have not yet discussed this with ministers, and until I do, please treat the direction of travel as provisional. I would like your implementation analysis by the 20th. My office will arrange time with you and the service owners in the first week of next month."

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comprehension**

Which statement most accurately captures the director general's position?

- A) The consolidation recommendation is approved and can be announced once union consultation begins.
- B) The DG provisionally supports consolidation, conditional on evidence protecting vulnerable users' service levels throughout the transition and on union consultation preceding any announcement — with the whole direction remaining provisional pending ministerial discussion.
- C) The DG has rejected the recommendation until ministers decide.
- D) The DG requires the implementation analysis before forming any view.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The letter's architecture is hedged acceptance, two named conditions, and an explicit provisionality marker; option B reproduces all three layers. A converts "minded to accept... provisional" into approval; C mistakes provisionality for rejection; D reverses the letter's order. This tests mapping a senior stakeholder's position with three-layer precision — lean, conditions, status — the discipline behind briefing accurately rather than propagating a stronger mandate than exists.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inference**

What can most safely be inferred about the DG's priorities?

- A) The DG regards protection of vulnerable users as a constraint worth trading delivery speed for.
- B) The DG considers union agreement more important than ministerial approval.
- C) The DG doubts the quality of the discovery report.
- D) The DG intends to announce the consolidation next month.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The letter states an explicit trade-off — "I would rather phase more slowly" — with speed volunteered as the variable to sacrifice. B compares two things the letter never ranks against each other; C has no textual basis; D fabricates a decision from a diary note about a meeting. This tests restating a trade-off the author has already made, in the author's own structure, rather than predicting beyond it.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

Which briefing line to an analysis team is best?

- A) "The DG has approved consolidation — full speed on implementation planning."
- B) "Nothing is decided; carry on as before."
- C) "The DG provisionally backs consolidation. Our implementation analysis, due the 20th, must evidence that service levels for vulnerable users hold throughout transition — if we can't evidence that, we design a slower phasing. Nothing is public, and nothing is final until ministers are consulted; union consultation must precede any announcement."
- D) "The DG wants the analysis by the 20th; details are in the letter if anyone wants them."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C translates each element of the DG's position into its operational consequence for the team, neither inflating the mandate (A) nor deflating it (B). D delegates comprehension to a document most of the team will skim. This tests the translation duty at this level — turning senior intent into team-actionable meaning without loss or gain.

*Passage 3 (for Questions 9–12)*

"All analysis products that inform investment decisions above £1 million must undergo independent quality review before submission. A review is independent if the reviewer had no role in producing the product and does not report to the product's author. Reviewers must be accredited under the departmental scheme; accreditation lapses after two years unless renewed. Where no accredited independent reviewer is available within the department, an accredited reviewer may be sought from another department, or the product may be submitted with an explicit statement that independent review was not performed, together with the reasons and the author's own quality declaration. Products informing decisions below the threshold are encouraged, but not required, to follow the same process. This standard does not apply to analysis produced under emergency response protocols."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: "An analysis product informing a £800,000 investment decision that skips independent review breaches the standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mandatory requirement applies above £1 million; £800,000 sits below the threshold, where the standard makes the process "encouraged, but not required". This tests checking scope before concluding — applying an above-threshold duty to a below-threshold case is the mirror image of the more notorious error (waiving a duty above the line), and both misread scope.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A reviewer whose accreditation lapsed last month, but who had no role in the product and does not report to its author, can provide the independent review the standard requires."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The standard imposes two separable requirements — independence and accreditation. This reviewer satisfies independence but fails accreditation, which is mandatory, so the review cannot satisfy the standard. This tests noticing when a distractor front-loads satisfied conditions and slips a failed one past inside a subordinate clause.

**Question 11 (hard) — Applying the standard to a scenario**

A £3 million investment decision needs an analysis product submitted next week. The department's only two accredited reviewers both worked on early drafts of the product. Under the standard, which course is compliant?

- A) Have the less-involved accredited reviewer perform the review, noting their limited role.
- B) Seek an accredited reviewer from another department; if none can be found, submit with an explicit statement that independent review was not performed, the reasons, and the author's quality declaration.
- C) Submit without review and without any statement, since no independent reviewer exists.
- D) Have the author's line manager, who is not accredited, perform the review instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both internal reviewers had a role in producing the product, so neither is independent — the standard's definition admits no degrees, eliminating A. The standard provides exactly two fallbacks in order, which B follows precisely. C takes the fallback's outcome while discarding its conditions; D substitutes an unaccredited reviewer. This tests sequential application of a multi-clause standard without skipping a node.

**Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement on drafting a standard**

A new clause covering conflicts of interest is needed. Which draft is best?

- A) "Reviewers should avoid conflicts of interest where possible."
- B) "A reviewer must declare any personal, financial or professional interest in the product's outcome before the review begins. Where a declared interest could reasonably be seen to affect the reviewer's judgement, the review must be reassigned; where no alternative reviewer is available, the fallback process in paragraph 4 applies, and the declared interest must be stated in the submission."
- C) "Conflicts of interest are unacceptable and will result in disciplinary action."
- D) "Reviewers must be conflict-free."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B defines the obligation, its trigger, its consequence, and its edge case, integrating with the standard's existing fallback machinery. A creates no testable obligation; C specifies a sanction without defining the offence or mechanism; D states an undefined, unachievable absolute. This tests the same testability, traceability and edge-case discipline demanded of system requirements, applied to drafting a standard.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate distinguishes False from Cannot say precisely**, including when a scope or timing qualifier is at stake.
- **Watch for whether a candidate reads senior correspondence for its layered structure** — lean, conditions, provisionality — rather than its headline tone.
- **Note whether a candidate fuses unrelated details** into fabricated content, or keeps them separate.
- **Note whether a candidate takes a fallback's outcome without its stated conditions** when applying a standard to a scenario.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and expect a candidate to build a brief mental map of a dense governance passage before answering.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer built from domain expertise** rather than the passage's actual words.
- **Missing when a candidate reads "prioritises X" as "rejects not-X".**
- **Crediting an answer that treats a hedged position ("minded to accept", "in principle", "provisional") as a decision.**
- **Missing when a candidate takes a fallback's permission without its attached transparency conditions.**
- **Rewarding drafting with untestable words** — "should where possible", "conflict-free", "appropriate" — in a clear-writing item.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks a candidate to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. There is rarely a single provably correct action; responses are scored against a behavioural key built with experienced practitioners — for this role, behaviours such as strategic influence, integrity under pressure, developing capability in others, proportionate escalation, inclusion, and stewardship of the profession itself.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 25 to 45 minutes with 8 to 15 scenarios. Response formats include choosing the most effective response, the most and least effective, ranking all responses, or rating each independently. For UK government digital roles, the scoring key is strongly informed by the Service Standard's user-centred ethos and Civil Service behaviours.

This matters for a lead business analyst because the SJT is the assessment closest to the actual substance of the job. This level is defined by judgement exercised through others and upward: directing stakeholder strategy rather than just managing stakeholders, enabling other analysts to select methods rather than selecting for them, and carrying professional authority across an organisation and a community that extends beyond it.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Strategic influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Consultancy**: scenarios test whether a candidate influences and negotiates with senior stakeholders to resolve issues and enable progress — holding ground on evidence while leaving room to move.
- **Evidence integrity at scale** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Business process improvement**: scenarios probe whether the analysis a function produces stays honest when programmes, suppliers or sponsors need it to say something else.
- **Building capability, not dependency** maps to this level's coaching accountabilities and **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements**: enabling others to select and apply appropriate methods is tested through struggling-team and talent-management scenarios where doing it directly is the seductive wrong answer.
- **Methodology leadership** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Business modelling**: scenarios test whether a candidate sets and defends proportionate standards — and changes them when evidence says to — rather than enforcing or abandoning them under pressure.
- **User focus and inclusion at enterprise scale** map to **User experience analysis** and **Digital and data systems analysis**: scenarios include constituencies without a voice in governance — excluded users, frontline staff, future maintainers — and reward responses that represent them.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — A director shopping for a different answer**

A team's options analysis recommends the modest Option B over the ambitious Option C, on cost and deliverability evidence. The director asks for "a more experienced analyst take another look, someone who understands the strategic picture". What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree to reassign the analysis to a more senior analyst with instructions to reconsider.
- B) Meet the director to understand what strategic factors they believe the analysis missed; if there are genuinely new considerations, incorporate them transparently and re-run the appraisal; if not, stand behind the team's work and explain the evidence personally.
- C) Politely refuse and refer the director to the head of profession.
- D) Ask the team to soften the recommendation to "no clear preference" so the director can choose freely.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The request may be answer-shopping — or the director may hold context the analysis never received; option B distinguishes the two cases. Option A concedes that recommendations are negotiable by reviewer selection; C escalates before attempting resolution; D corrupts the analysis to avoid conflict. This tests protecting the evidence while genuinely engaging the concern behind a challenge to it.

**Question 2 (easy) — Analyst's flawed work is already with stakeholders**

An analyst circulated an impact assessment to programme stakeholders yesterday. Today they report a significant error — a doubled benefits figure — that materially strengthens the case. No decisions have been taken yet. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to correct it quietly in the next scheduled version; drawing attention now would damage their credibility.
- B) Support them to issue a prompt correction to all recipients, briefly and without drama, before any decision relies on the figure; help them check the rest of the paper; and afterwards, review together how the error escaped review — treating their coming forward as exactly the behaviour worth encouraging.
- C) Take over: issue the correction personally under the candidate's own name so it carries authority.
- D) Report the error to the programme board as a quality failure in the analyst's work.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Correction cost rises steeply once a wrong figure is embedded in decisions. Option B corrects fast and completely, checks the rest of the paper, and treats disclosure as behaviour to reinforce rather than punish. Option A gambles decision integrity on comfort; C teaches that errors mean confiscation; D punishes self-disclosure publicly. This tests protecting psychological safety around error-reporting as an operational asset, not just a value.

**Question 3 (easy) — Community leadership versus employer convenience**

A department under budget pressure tells a co-chair of a cross-government community to stop "spending time on other departments' problems" and step back for six months. The community's annual event, convened by this candidate, is in eight weeks. What is the most effective response?

- A) Comply immediately; the employer sets priorities.
- B) Ignore the instruction; cross-government community leadership is part of the role profile.
- C) Make the case to management that community leadership is an explicit accountability of the role and returns value to the department — recruitment pipeline, shared practice, cross-government influence — and propose a proportionate arrangement: an orderly handover of event convening to the co-chair, with time commitment reduced and made visible, reviewed after six months.
- D) Escalate to the head of the government business analysis profession, asking them to overrule the department.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both pressures are legitimate; option C negotiates rather than picking a side, making the community's value explicit and offering a proportionate, reviewable reduction that keeps the event's continuity intact. Option A abandons a made commitment unexamined; B insubordinately ignores a management instruction; D invites external authority before attempting resolution. This tests treating competing legitimate claims as a design problem rather than a loyalty test.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assurance gaming**

A programme director whose case will be reviewed next month invites informal "pre-review of the pack so there are no surprises at the gate", adding that a previously failed gate "caused six months' delay, which nobody wants repeated". What is the most effective response?

- A) Decline all contact before the gate to preserve independence.
- B) Accept the informal pre-review and, if the pack is weak, quietly help fix it so the gate goes smoothly.
- C) Offer early engagement openly and on standard terms: publish the assurance criteria, run a documented readiness session available to every programme, and be explicit that early feedback helps them fix defects but the gate review itself remains independent and its outcome unconstrained — while noting the delay remark changes nothing.
- D) Report the director's remark to the SRO as attempted intimidation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C separates a legitimate request (early clarity improves packs) from illegitimate pressure (the delay remark), making early engagement transparent and universal while stating gate independence is untouched. Option A protects independence at the cost of assurance's improvement value; B is capture — a reviewer who privately fixed the pack cannot independently review it; D treats a deniable remark as a formal offence on first occurrence. This tests neutralising pressure on the record while preserving the legitimate value in a request.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Talent outgrowing the function**

A strongest senior analyst confidentially discloses considering an external role, feeling ready for lead-level work with no vacancy available. Losing them would seriously hurt two programmes. What is the most effective response?

- A) Persuade them to stay by emphasising loyalty and the disruption their departure would cause.
- B) Explore what "lead-level work" means to them; identify genuine stretch that can be offered now — deputising on a programme, leading a community workstream, directing cross-cutting analysis — be honest about the vacancy position and timeline, actively sponsor them for lead opportunities inside and beyond the function, and plan resilience for the two programmes regardless of their decision.
- C) Immediately begin succession planning and reduce their programme exposure so departure hurts less.
- D) Offer to match the external role's salary through a retention payment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B offers real stretch now, honesty about what cannot be promised, genuine sponsorship including external options, and resilience planning that de-dramatises the decision. Option A leverages guilt; C punishes disclosure with marginalisation; D answers a development question with money that eventually expires. This tests behaving as a steward of a person's career and the wider profession rather than as an owner of a resource.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Standards versus a delivery emergency**

A programme in crisis, supported by its own senior analyst, asks to skip the requirements standard for one release to hit a legally significant date: "the standard is right normally, but this release is small and the date is statutory." What is the most effective response?

- A) Refuse: standards that flex under pressure are not standards.
- B) Grant a blanket exemption for the release; statutory dates outrank internal standards.
- C) Work with the programme's senior analyst to apply the standard proportionately rather than binarily: identify the minimum set that protects the statutory outcome itself — traceability and acceptance criteria for the legally significant requirements, lighter treatment for the rest — document the exemption, its rationale and its expiry, and schedule the deferred work as named debt.
- D) Escalate the conflict to the programme's SRO to decide.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The trap both A and B accept is a binary framing; C re-derives the standard's purpose under constraint, applying full rigour where legal risk concentrates and documenting a time-boxed exemption with its debt named. Option A positions the function as delivery's obstacle; B abandons exactly the requirements whose failure would be most expensive; D outsources a methodology judgement that belongs to this role. This tests proportionality and co-designing an application of a standard with the team affected by it.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inclusion challenge at programme scale**

A programme board is about to approve a channel strategy built on the candidate's function's analysis: digital-first, with the assisted digital route reduced to a phone line operating 10am–2pm. Late data shows 22% of current users use the assisted route, concentrated among older and disabled users, with 60% of their contacts falling outside 10am–2pm. The paper is on Thursday's agenda; the director wants no late changes. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let the paper proceed; the data arrived after the analysis was complete, and beta will reveal any problems.
- B) Tell the programme director the analysis must be withdrawn and rewritten, missing Thursday's board.
- C) Brief the programme director immediately with the specific numbers, recommend the paper proceed with an amended assisted-digital provision (or an explicit board-level decision on the risk, with the data tabled), and make clear that as the analysis's owner the board cannot be allowed to decide on figures now known to understate excluded users' needs.
- D) Raise the data informally with a sympathetic board member so it surfaces in discussion without attribution.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C gets the correction into the decision through the front door and at speed, preserving Thursday where possible while stating the integrity line plainly. Option A delegates an equity decision to beta, where the cost lands on excluded users first; B may be where events end up but as an opening move forecloses a targeted amendment; D is influence without accountability. This tests ownership of analysis under the candidate's name, and courage exercised with respect for the relationship.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Supplier relationship deteriorating**

A strategic supplier's requirements interpretations keep drifting from agreed intent — each drift small, plausibly deniable, and toward reduced supplier effort — while the account director remains charming and cooperative in meetings; commercial colleagues say the contract is "not being breached as such". What is the most effective response?

- A) Instruct analysts to accept the interpretations; without breach, there is no case.
- B) Establish a joint requirements interpretation log with the supplier: every ambiguity resolved in writing, agreed by both sides, with drift made visible as a documented pattern — and share the accumulating record with commercial colleagues so the pattern is available at the next contractual moment, while raising the trend directly and professionally with the account director.
- C) Confront the account director in the next programme meeting with accusations of bad faith.
- D) Route all future requirements through lawyers before the supplier sees them.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The problem is structural — ambiguity harvested one deniable instance at a time — and B changes the structure by ending deniability, aggregating drift into visible pattern, arming commercial colleagues, and raising the trend directly. Option A surrenders the requirements baseline permanently; C makes an unevidenced accusation; D poisons collaboration and slows delivery. This tests building an evidenced record while keeping the relationship functional.

**Question 9 (hard) — Most and least effective: two departments, one truth**

Analysis for a shared service used by two departments genuinely shows adequate overall provision with severe peak-time bottlenecks. Department A wants the analysis to show under-provision (supporting a funding bid); Department B wants it to show over-provision (supporting a cost-reduction case). Both departments' directors will see the report. Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses.

- A) Publish one report to both departments: adequate aggregate provision, severe peak bottlenecks, with the peak-time evidence broken out — and brief both directors together on what the finding means for each of their cases.
- B) Produce a single report but let each department write its own executive summary.
- C) Delay publication until the two departments align their positions.
- D) Emphasise the bottleneck findings to Department A and the aggregate adequacy to Department B, since both are true.

**Correct answer: Most effective A; least effective D**

**Explanation:** Option A publishes one truth once to everyone, and a joint briefing prevents each department metabolising the report into its preferred half. Option D is a sophisticated form of dishonesty — each briefing survives fact-checking while the combination is designed to mislead, and once the directors compare notes, the function's independence is spent. B licenses spin at the summary layer; C makes truth wait for politics. This tests independence and transparency when a finding is genuinely two-sided and two audiences want opposite halves of it.

**Question 10 (hard) — A methodology change a candidate disagrees with**

Government-level guidance requires discovery phases across the profession to adopt a new standardised outcomes framework. Real experience across the candidate's portfolio suggests it fits transactional services well but distorts discovery for complex casework services, and two programmes are about to start casework discoveries. As a visible community leader, the stance taken will be noticed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Adopt the framework fully and without comment; guidance is guidance.
- B) Publicly criticise the framework at the next cross-government community event and advise the programmes to ignore it.
- C) Adopt the framework in good faith across the portfolio, instrument the two casework discoveries to gather structured evidence on where it helps and distorts, feed that evidence through the profession's formal channels with proposed amendments — and share the experience honestly, framed as improving the framework, when the community asks.
- D) Quietly exempt the casework programmes from the framework while publicly endorsing it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C adopts the framework in good faith, converts opinion into structured evidence, works through formal channels with proposed amendments, and communicates honestly when asked. Option A wastes the evidence that only this vantage point can gather; B spends leadership credibility on defiance and teaches that guidance is optional when disagreed with; D is hypocrisy that, once discovered, ends authority on standards permanently. This tests dissenting through institutions with evidence rather than through defiance or concealment.

**Question 11 (hard) — The benefits a predecessor promised**

Newly arrived as lead, a candidate concludes a predecessor's signed-off business case overstated benefits by roughly a third — optimistic uptake, no ramp-up, double-counted savings. The programme is politically prominent, mid-delivery, and its quarterly benefits report is due in three weeks. What is the most effective response?

- A) Report against the approved baseline as the predecessor defined it; reopening a signed-off case is not the candidate's call.
- B) Quietly re-baseline the benefits downward in the quarterly report without drawing attention to the change.
- C) Validate the conclusion rigorously first (three weeks in — test the double-counting and uptake analysis with the workstream leads), then brief the SRO and programme director with the corrected model, the specific defects, and options: re-baseline formally with governance approval, present the quarterly report against both baselines with the discrepancy explained, and strengthen benefits assurance so the next case starts honest.
- D) Commission an independent external review of the business case and pause benefits reporting until it concludes.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Verification first (three weeks is enough to form a hypothesis but not enough to bet the programme's credibility on it), then the SRO who owns the baseline, then a dual-baseline transparency mechanism for the interim. Option A reports figures now believed false; B conceals a correction governance will eventually discover; D outsources and delays on an unvalidated hypothesis. This tests verification before escalation and respect for governance ownership when correcting an inherited baseline.

**Question 12 (hard) — Most and least effective: function under existential pressure**

A new chief operating officer questions whether the business analysis function should exist, given product managers write user stories and architects do the modelling. A restructure consultation opens in six weeks; the team is anxious and two have already asked for references. Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses.

- A) Prepare an evidence-based case for the function: quantified contribution (defect prevention rates, benefits validated, discovery outcomes), testimony from the service owners and directors the analysts serve, honest acknowledgement of where the challenge has merit, and proposed reshaping where the evidence supports it — while being straight with the team about the situation and the timetable.
- B) Lobby the directors best known to protect the function before the consultation formally opens.
- C) Have each analyst document everything they do daily, and submit the fourteen activity logs as the function's defence.
- D) Tell the team there is nothing to worry about while handling the politics quietly.

**Correct answer: Most effective A; least effective D**

**Explanation:** Option A answers an analytical question about analysis with quantified outcomes, independent testimony, honest acknowledgement of the challenge's valid core, and proposed reshaping — plus candour with the team. Option D is a promise that cannot be kept, discovered false when the consultation paper lands, accelerating exactly the departures it aimed to prevent. B substitutes relationship capital for evidence; C documents activity rather than value. This tests evidence-based advocacy combined with candour under existential pressure to a function.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate's response fixes both the immediate instance and the underlying mechanism** — the correction plus the assurance gap, the exemption plus its documentation.
- **Watch for whether a candidate separates a legitimate request from illegitimate pressure** braided together in the same ask.
- **Note whether a candidate routes corrections and dissent through the accountable owner first**, at speed and with options attached, rather than through anonymous or sympathetic intermediaries.
- **Note whether a candidate answers at the level being assessed** — personally rewriting the artefact or absorbing the work should score as one level too junior.
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding sophisticated dishonesty** — selective emphasis of true statements to different audiences, tailored summaries, silent re-baselines.
- **Rewarding purity that abandons usefulness** — refusing all pre-gate contact, enforcing standards without proportionality, halting everything pending perfect information.
- **Crediting retention by guilt or gold** over honest stretch, sponsorship and resilience planning.
- **Missing when a candidate escalates before attempting resolution, or conceals instead of escalating** — misplacing a scenario on the escalation gradient is the most common single error.
- **Rewarding an answer that defends every hectare** of a challenged function or standard, rather than acknowledging a challenge's valid core and redesigning around it.
