# Trainee Business Analyst - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for trainee business analyst, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. The material is job-specific: it uses the real materials of the role — user stories, simple process maps, requirements lists, stakeholder emails, sprint boards, survey results, and test scripts — rather than abstract puzzles about shapes or made-up words.

This document covers four types of assessment, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — thinking, logic, and error checking.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — percentages, averages, and reading tables.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — reading and understanding workplace documents.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — choosing good responses to realistic work dilemmas.

Each section explains what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, then maps it to the specific skills in the role, then gives a substantial set of items with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, followed by administration tips and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded — they form the answer key for scoring and debrief, not material for the candidate to study beforehand. Offer any unscored practice questions the platform provides so a candidate is not disadvantaged by unfamiliarity with the screen layout. Because this is a trainee-level role, expect — and allow for — a wide range of prior exposure to structured assessment: judge reasoning and care, not polish. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief regardless of outcome. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures how well a candidate thinks through everyday work problems: spotting patterns, following logical rules, finding errors, deciding what to do first, and solving small practical puzzles. Unlike a general intelligence test full of abstract shapes, a job-specific version uses materials a trainee business analyst would genuinely meet — a requirements list with a numbering system, a set of acceptance criteria, a simple process map, or a kanban board.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 30 minutes, with somewhere between 15 and 30 multiple-choice questions, each with one correct answer among four options. Many platforms are adaptive, so questions may appear to get harder as a candidate answers correctly — this is not itself a sign of struggling. Scoring is typically comparative, against a benchmark group, balancing speed and accuracy rather than a single pass mark. Most platforms offer a few unscored practice questions before the timed section begins; encourage the candidate to take them.

This kind of assessment matters for a trainee business analyst because the heart of business analysis is careful, structured thinking. Even at trainee level, and even under supervision, the job involves noticing when two documents disagree, following a numbering convention exactly, understanding which task is most urgent, and reasoning about what a rule means in practice. This assessment measures those habits of mind in miniature.

### How this assessment maps to the role

Each cognitive dimension connects to a named skill in the role:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business process improvement** and **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements**. Contributing to the analysis of business processes, or providing task-based outputs using a predetermined requirements management life cycle, means constantly noticing recurring structures: step numbering, naming conventions, repeated process shapes, and requirement categories.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Adapting to delivery methodologies**. Explaining why iteration matters, or how an approach would change between scrum and kanban, means reasoning correctly from rules to consequences.
- **Error checking** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements** and **Testing (business analysis)**. Supporting the capture and validation of requirements, and helping trace requirements through to tested functionality, is careful error checking by another name.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the role level itself: a trainee receives direction from more senior business analysts and works under supervision, so knowing which task to do first — and when to ask — is a daily skill.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Digital and data systems analysis** and **Business process improvement**: supporting the analysis of IT system capabilities and contributing to process improvements both mean breaking a fuzzy situation into parts that can be worked on, one step at a time.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded. Items start easy and get harder.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a requirements catalogue**

A team's requirements catalogue uses a simple ID pattern: the letters "REQ", a hyphen, a three-digit number, a hyphen, and a category code of exactly two capital letters (for example, REQ-014-FN for a functional requirement). While tidying the catalogue, the candidate sees these entries:

1. REQ-021-FN
2. REQ-022-NF
3. REQ-23-FN
4. REQ-024-DA

Which entry breaks the ID pattern?

- A) Entry 1
- B) Entry 2
- C) Entry 3
- D) Entry 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule requires a three-digit number. Entry 3 reads "REQ-23-FN" — "23" has only two digits, so it should be "REQ-023-FN". Entries 1, 2 and 4 all have the letters, the three-digit number, and a two-letter category code, so they follow the rule. Notice that knowing what the category codes "NF" or "DA" mean is unnecessary — the pattern only says "exactly two capital letters". This tests checking against a convention exactly rather than by assumption, useful because broken IDs break the traceability that lets requirements link to tests.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a team rule**

A scrum team has a working agreement: "A user story can only be moved to 'Done' if all of its acceptance criteria have passed testing." At the end of the sprint, a colleague says: "Story 47 is in the 'Done' column." Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Story 47 was the most important story in the sprint.
- B) All of story 47's acceptance criteria have passed testing.
- C) Story 47 had only a small number of acceptance criteria.
- D) Story 47 was tested by the developers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The working agreement is a rule of the form "Done only if all criteria passed". Since story 47 is in Done, the rule guarantees that its acceptance criteria have all passed — exactly what B says. The other options might be true, but nothing in the rule or the facts guarantees them. This tests the discipline of concluding only what the given information guarantees, without adding assumptions to what a stakeholder or a document actually said.

**Question 3 (easy) — Ordering the steps of a simple process**

A candidate is documenting how a citizen's paper form is handled, from observation notes: (i) the form is scanned before it is checked for completeness; (ii) incomplete forms are returned to the citizen straight after the completeness check; (iii) complete forms are entered into the case system after the completeness check. Which sequence correctly shows the path of a complete form?

- A) Check for completeness → Scan → Enter into case system
- B) Scan → Enter into case system → Check for completeness
- C) Scan → Check for completeness → Enter into case system
- D) Enter into case system → Scan → Check for completeness

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Note (i) fixes the first relationship: scanning comes before the completeness check. Note (iii) fixes the second: for a complete form, entry into the case system comes after the check. Together this gives Scan → Check → Enter, option C. This tests turning messy observation notes into a correct ordered sequence — the first step of process mapping, and part of the candidate's contribution to business process improvement.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation as a trainee**

It is 9:30 on Tuesday morning, and the candidate has four things on their plate: (1) a senior business analyst asked them to finish updating the requirements catalogue by midday, ready for a stakeholder meeting; (2) a developer has emailed a question about a user story — no deadline given; (3) mandatory information security training is due by the end of the month; (4) the team's shared folder is getting messy and could do with tidying. What is the most sensible order in which to tackle these?

- A) 4, 3, 2, 1
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 3, 1, 2, 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The catalogue update (item 1) has the nearest deadline, was set by the senior colleague who directs the candidate's work, and feeds a stakeholder meeting — it comes first. The developer's question (item 2) has no stated deadline, but someone is waiting on it, so it comes next. Mandatory training (item 3) matters but is due at month end. Folder tidying (item 4) is useful housekeeping with no deadline and no one waiting. This tests sequencing by deadline and impact together — work a supervisor has directed by a specific time usually anchors the day, and where two directed pieces of work clash, the expected move is to ask rather than guess.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Error checking a user story against its criteria**

A user story in a team's backlog reads: "As a caseworker, I want to search for a case by reference number, so that I can find a case quickly." Its acceptance criteria are: (1) given a valid reference number, the matching case is displayed; (2) given an invalid reference number, an error message is shown; (3) given a valid postcode, all matching cases are displayed; (4) the search completes within 3 seconds. Which acceptance criterion does not belong with this story?

- A) Criterion 1
- B) Criterion 2
- C) Criterion 3
- D) Criterion 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Criterion 1 covers the success path for a reference number search; criterion 2 covers the failure path; criterion 4 sets a performance limit on that search. Criterion 3 describes searching by postcode — a different feature entirely, which belongs in its own story. This tests spotting a criterion that has drifted outside a story's scope, part of the validation support named in defining and managing requirements, and catching it early saves rework later. A strong candidate's answer notes that the right next step is to raise it with a senior business analyst rather than delete it unilaterally.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about delivery methodologies**

Team A uses scrum: work is planned into two-week sprints, and scope is fixed once the sprint starts. Team B uses kanban: work items are pulled continuously from a prioritised queue, with no sprints. On Wednesday of the first week of Team A's sprint, a stakeholder brings an urgent new request that must start within two days. Based only on the information given, which statement is best supported?

- A) The request can be added to Team A's current sprint without affecting anything.
- B) Team B could start the request within two days if it is placed at the top of their queue and capacity allows.
- C) Neither team can start the request within two days.
- D) Team A should switch to kanban.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Team A's sprint scope is fixed once started, with more than a week left to run — adding the request would break the stated rule, ruling out A. Team B pulls work continuously from a prioritised queue, so placing the request at the top means it starts as soon as capacity frees up — plausibly within two days, supporting B (note the careful qualification "could... if capacity allows"). C is too strong, since Team B's model clearly permits a fast start. D is a large organisational change the facts do not justify. This rehearses the skill of adapting to delivery methodologies: explaining the practical difference between scrum and kanban.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in survey categories**

A candidate is sorting feedback comments into categories a senior colleague defined: "Access problems", "Speed", "Content clarity", and "Other". The last twenty comments have been categorised: Access problems 8, Speed 1, Content clarity 1, Other 10. Earlier batches from the same survey averaged: Access problems 4, Speed 5, Content clarity 6, Other 5. What is the most sensible first response to the latest batch?

- A) Users suddenly stopped caring about speed and clarity.
- B) The pattern has shifted noticeably — it is worth checking how "Other" is being used before drawing conclusions.
- C) The categories should be deleted and rebuilt from scratch.
- D) The earlier batches must have been categorised wrongly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Earlier batches spread comments fairly evenly across the four categories; the new batch piles half of everything into "Other" and almost nothing into "Speed" or "Content clarity". A jump in "Other" is a classic sign that the categoriser was unsure where things fit, whether from unclear guidance or genuinely new themes. Option B treats the changed pattern as a prompt to check — an odd pattern is a question, not an answer. This tests the analyst's habit of noticing "the data looks different — why?" and checking calmly rather than leaping to a conclusion, central to business process improvement work.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a kanban board**

A team's kanban board has four columns: "To do", "In analysis", "In development", "In testing". Working-agreement WIP limits: at most 3 items in analysis, at most 4 in development, at most 2 in testing. The board currently shows: To do 6, In analysis 3, In development 4, In testing 2. A developer finishes an item in development and it is ready to move to testing. What must happen before that item can move?

- A) Nothing — it can move to testing immediately.
- B) An item must leave the testing column first, because testing is at its WIP limit.
- C) An item must be added to the "To do" column first.
- D) The analysis column must be emptied first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The testing column holds 2 items against a WIP limit of 2 — it is full, so a third cannot enter until one of the current two finishes and leaves. This tests recognising why a board "blocks" a move, part of explaining how kanban supports delivery, and in real life a blocked move like this often prompts a helpful stand-up conversation about who can help finish the items in testing.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Error checking between two documents**

A candidate checks a draft process map against the written procedure it is based on. The procedure says: "All refund requests over £500 must be approved by a team leader before payment. Refund requests of £500 or less are paid without team leader approval." The draft map shows a decision point "Refund over £500?" with the "Yes" branch going straight to "Make payment" and the "No" branch going to "Team leader approval" then "Make payment". What is wrong with the map?

- A) Nothing — the map matches the procedure.
- B) The decision point tests the wrong amount.
- C) The Yes and No branches are the wrong way round.
- D) The map is missing the refund request step.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The procedure requires approval for refunds over £500. On the map, "Yes" (over £500) goes straight to payment with no approval — the opposite of the rule — while "No" (£500 or less) is sent for approval it does not need. The decision test itself uses the right amount, so B is wrong; the swap of branches is the defect. This tests catching one of the most common — and most dangerous — process mapping errors, one that looks complete and tidy at a glance; reading each branch out loud as a full sentence is a reliable checking technique.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-step deduction about requirement dependencies**

Four requirements have dependencies: (i) R2 cannot be built until R1 is complete; (ii) R3 cannot be built until both R1 and R2 are complete; (iii) R4 can be built at any time, but must be tested together with R3. Only one requirement can be built at a time, each taking one week; testing takes no extra time in this puzzle. What is the earliest week in which R3's build can finish?

- A) Week 2
- B) Week 3
- C) Week 4
- D) It cannot be determined.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** R1 has no dependencies, so it is built in week 1. R2 needs R1 complete, so R2 is built in week 2. R3 needs both R1 and R2, done by the end of week 2, so R3's build occupies week 3 and finishes at the end of week 3. Week 2 wrongly skips R2; week 4 wrongly assumes R4 must be built before R3, when the rule only requires them to be tested together. This tests tracing a dependency chain accurately, a routine skill in backlog planning and requirements management.

**Question 11 (hard) — Spotting the flaw in an improvement argument**

A colleague proposes: "Last month we introduced a new online form. This month, call volumes to the helpline fell by 10%. Therefore the new form caused the fall in calls, and we should now remove the helpline number from the website to push everyone online." Which is the strongest criticism of this argument?

- A) A 10% fall is too small to measure.
- B) The fall in calls might have other causes, and even if the form helped, removing the helpline number does not follow — some users may still need to call.
- C) Online forms are always better than phone lines.
- D) The colleague should not propose improvements without being asked.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two flaws hide in the argument. First, "calls fell after the form launched" does not prove the form caused the fall — the classic "after it, therefore because of it" mistake. Second, even if the form did reduce calls, "remove the helpline number" jumps far past the evidence: 90% of calls still happen, and some users cannot or prefer not to use online services. This tests the ability to separate "the data shows X" from "therefore we should do Y" — a skill central to contributing to option analysis.

**Question 12 (hard) — Combining constraints to schedule analysis work**

A candidate supports two pieces of analysis: a process observation (needs one full day, must happen on a day the operations team is in the office) and a requirements workshop write-up (needs two consecutive half-days, must finish before the workshop review meeting). The operations team is in the office Tuesday and Thursday only. The review meeting is Thursday at 16:00. The candidate is only available Monday to Thursday this week, and can do at most one activity per half-day. Which plan works?

- A) Observation Tuesday; write-up Wednesday morning and Thursday morning.
- B) Observation Wednesday; write-up Monday morning and Tuesday morning.
- C) Write-up Thursday afternoon and Friday morning; observation Tuesday.
- D) Observation Thursday; write-up Thursday morning and Thursday afternoon.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Plan A: the observation is on Tuesday, an operations office day. The write-up occupies Wednesday morning and Thursday morning — two consecutive days' half-days, finishing before Thursday 16:00. Nothing clashes. Plan B fails because the observation falls on Wednesday, not an office day. Plan C fails because Friday is unavailable, and Thursday afternoon finishes too close to the 16:00 meeting. Plan D double-books Thursday, exceeding the one-activity-per-half-day rule. This tests eliminating options by finding the single constraint each one breaks, faster and more reliable than searching for the answer directly.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads the exact wording of a rule** rather than what they expected it to say.
- **Watch for whether a candidate adds unstated assumptions** to a deduction question — a correct answer sticks to what the facts guarantee.
- **Note whether a candidate uses elimination** on harder questions by finding the constraint each wrong option breaks.
- **Offer the platform's unscored practice questions** before the timed section, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.
- **Look for improvement, not perfection** — this is a trainee-level assessment, and reasoning quality matters more than a flawless score.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that rushes an easy early question** and slips on something straightforward.
- **Crediting an answer built on an assumption the question does not support.**
- **Missing when a candidate misreads exact wording** — "three digits" or "at most 2" have precise, testable meanings.
- **Letting one unfamiliar question format distort the overall impression** of the candidate's ability.
- **Treating a single wobbly response as a verdict** rather than looking at the pattern across the section.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately a candidate works with the numbers that appear in the job: percentages, averages, ratios, simple rates, and data presented in tables and charts. It is not a maths exam — no algebra, no geometry, nothing beyond school arithmetic. It tests whether a candidate can read a table carefully, pick out the right numbers, do a short calculation correctly, and interpret the result sensibly, under gentle time pressure.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 20 and 30 minutes, with 15 to 20 questions, each presenting some data — a small table, a chart, or a short paragraph with numbers — followed by a multiple-choice question. A basic calculator is usually allowed; check the platform's instructions and offer rough paper.

This matters for a trainee business analyst because analysis runs on numbers even at trainee level: comparing processing times before and after a change, tallying requirements by category, summarising survey responses. Every item below shows its arithmetic step by step, so the method can be borrowed even when the numbers change.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Business process improvement**: contributing to the evaluation of processes almost always means expressing change as a percentage, and understanding what that does and does not show.
- **Averages** map to **Context, problem and option analysis**: summarising how a business area works often starts with averages, and knowing when an average is misleading is part of understanding context.
- **Ratios and rates** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: sprint capacity, throughput per week, and items completed per person are all rates.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements** and **Testing (business analysis)**: traceability matrices, test result summaries, and coverage tables are all tabular data.
- **Sense-checking results** maps to the trainee role level: because the candidate works under supervision, the most valuable numeric habit is noticing when a figure looks wrong before it reaches a senior colleague.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; a calculator and rough paper are appropriate. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of requirements approved**

A requirements catalogue contains 40 requirements; 30 have been approved by stakeholders. What percentage of the requirements have been approved?

- A) 65%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75; × 100 = 75%. Half of 40 is 20 (50%), and three-quarters of 40 is 30, confirming 75%. This tests the basic part-over-whole percentage calculation and the habit of sense-checking against easy fractions.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average daily case volume**

Observing a casework team for a process improvement task, a candidate records: Monday 24, Tuesday 30, Wednesday 27, Thursday 33, Friday 26 cases completed. What was the average (mean) number of cases completed per day?

- A) 26
- B) 27
- C) 28
- D) 30

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Sum = 140; ÷5 = 28. The daily values range from 24 to 33, and 28 sits comfortably in the middle — an average outside the range of the data would signal an arithmetic slip. This tests basic averaging and range sense-checking, and a strong candidate's answer notes that quoting both the average and the range paints a fairer picture.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a simple table**

| Test area | Tests run | Tests passed |
|---|---|---|
| Login | 20 | 18 |
| Search | 25 | 25 |
| Payments | 40 | 32 |
| Reports | 15 | 12 |

Which test area has the lowest pass rate?

- A) Login
- B) Search
- C) Payments
- D) Reports

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Pass rate is passed ÷ run, not the raw "passed" count. Login: 90%. Search: 100%. Payments: 80%. Reports: 80%. Payments and Reports tie exactly at 80%, but Payments carries far more failed tests (8 versus 3), making it the area of greatest concern and the intended answer. This tests reading proportions rather than raw counts, and reading past a numerical tie to what the data implies practically — pass rate and failure count together tell the fuller story.

**Question 4 (easy) — Percentage change in form completions**

An online application form was completed 1,200 times in March and 1,380 times in April. By what percentage did completions increase?

- A) 12%
- B) 15%
- C) 18%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Change = 180; 180 ÷ 1,200 (the original figure) = 0.15 = 15%. A common error divides by the new figure (1,380) instead. This tests the percentage-change formula and measuring against the starting point — the basic evidence format for process improvement comparisons.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Ratio of story types in a backlog**

A backlog contains 18 new-feature stories and 12 bug-fix stories. What is the ratio of new-feature stories to bug-fix stories, in its simplest form?

- A) 18:12
- B) 6:4
- C) 3:2
- D) 2:3

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 18:12 simplifies by dividing both sides by 6, giving 3:2. Option B is only partly simplified; option D reverses the order. This tests simplifying a ratio to its lowest terms while matching the order stated in the question.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Sprint capacity rate**

A scrum team completed 48 story points over the last 3 two-week sprints. Assuming the average rate continues, how many story points would the team be expected to complete over the next 5 sprints?

- A) 60
- B) 70
- C) 80
- D) 90

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rate = 48 ÷ 3 = 16 points per sprint; × 5 = 80. This tests a simple rate-and-scale calculation. A strong candidate's answer also flags the assumption doing the work here — "assuming the average rate continues" — since real velocity varies with holidays, illness, and other disruptions, making a forecast like this a starting point for discussion rather than a promise.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Averages with a missing value**

A satisfaction survey (scale 1–5) had 4 responses on Monday averaging 4.0, and 6 responses on Tuesday averaging 3.5. What is the overall average?

- A) 3.7
- B) 3.75
- C) 3.8
- D) 3.65

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Averaging the two averages directly gives 3.75 (option B) — the trap, since the response counts differ. Weighting instead: Monday total = 4 × 4.0 = 16; Tuesday total = 6 × 3.5 = 21; combined = 37 ÷ 10 responses = 3.7. Because Tuesday had more responses, the true average sits closer to Tuesday's 3.5 than to Monday's 4.0. This tests recognising when a weighted average is needed, which happens constantly when combining groups of different sizes in survey summaries.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a chart described in words**

Helpline calls per week: week 1: 500; week 2: 450; week 3: 400; week 4: 380. A colleague says, "Calls are falling by about 10% every week." Is this a fair summary?

- A) Yes — each week falls by exactly 10%.
- B) Roughly — the falls are 10%, 11.1%, and 5%, so the trend is downward but the last week slowed.
- C) No — calls are actually rising.
- D) No — the fall is 50 calls per week, which is 20%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Week 1→2: 50 ÷ 500 = 10%. Week 2→3: 50 ÷ 450 ≈ 11.1%. Week 3→4: 20 ÷ 400 = 5%. The falls are 10%, about 11%, then 5% — downward but slowing. Option A wrongly assumes an equal absolute fall means an equal percentage fall. This tests calculating each period's percentage change individually rather than trusting an approximate verbal summary, and the combination of broad-strokes agreement with precise detail that makes a summary trustworthy.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Proportion of process time**

Timing a request-handling process: receive and log, 5 minutes; check details, 15 minutes; approve, 10 minutes; notify the requester, 10 minutes. What percentage of total process time is spent on "check details"?

- A) 25%
- B) 30%
- C) 37.5%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total = 40 minutes; 15 ÷ 40 = 0.375 = 37.5%. 15 is more than a quarter of 40 (10) and less than half (20), confirming the range. This tests calculating a proportion of a total correctly — the raw material of process improvement, since the step taking nearly 40% of the total time is a natural first place to look for improvements (though not automatically the most improvable one).

**Question 10 (hard) — Two-step percentage with a target**

A service receives 2,500 applications per month; currently 8% contain errors requiring manual correction. A form redesign is expected to reduce the number of error applications by 25%. If it works as expected, how many applications per month would still require manual correction?

- A) 150
- B) 175
- C) 200
- D) 50

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Current errors = 8% of 2,500 = 200. The 25% reduction applies to this figure, not the whole volume: reduction = 0.25 × 200 = 50; remaining = 200 − 50 = 150. Option C is the unreduced figure; option D is the reduction itself, not what remains. This tests applying a percentage to the correct base in a two-step problem — a common trap is applying the 25% to 2,500 instead of 200.

**Question 11 (hard) — Comparing two options with different measures**

Two options for clearing a backlog of 600 unprocessed applications: Option 1, temporary staff processing 25 applications per day at £300 per day; Option 2, an automation tool costing £4,500 once, processing 40 applications per day. New applications can be ignored for this comparison. Which statement is correct about clearing the whole backlog?

- A) Option 1 takes 24 days and costs £7,200; Option 2 takes 15 days and costs £4,500.
- B) Option 1 takes 20 days and costs £6,000; Option 2 takes 12 days and costs £4,500.
- C) Option 1 takes 24 days and costs £4,500; Option 2 takes 15 days and costs £7,200.
- D) Both options take the same time.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option 1: 600 ÷ 25 = 24 days; cost = 24 × £300 = £7,200. Option 2: 600 ÷ 40 = 15 days; cost = £4,500 flat. Option C swaps the costs between the options. This tests working each option through fully and matching each figure to the correct option — a strong candidate's answer also notes that the arithmetic answers only part of a real comparison, which would also weigh set-up time and accuracy.

**Question 12 (hard) — Interpreting a traceability coverage table**

| Group | Requirements | With test cases |
|---|---|---|
| Applications | 60 | 54 |
| Payments | 25 | 15 |
| Notifications | 15 | 15 |

A colleague says: "Overall coverage looks like about 90%, so we're fine." What is the overall coverage, and is the statement fair?

- A) 84% — the figure is too high, and Payments at 60% needs attention.
- B) 90% — the statement is accurate.
- C) 84% — but since it rounds to 90%, the statement is fair.
- D) 80% — the figure is far too high.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total requirements = 100; total with test cases = 84; overall coverage = 84%, not "about 90%" (84% does not round to 90%, ruling out C). The group breakdown matters more: Applications 90%, Notifications 100%, Payments only 60% — the overall average smooths over a weak spot in exactly the area, payments, where untested requirements often carry the most risk. This tests both correct arithmetic and the habit of asking for a breakdown whenever a single overall percentage is quoted, since averages hide and breakdowns reveal.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate names the base** before calculating a percentage.
- **Watch for whether a candidate averages averages** instead of weighting by group size where the groups differ.
- **Note whether a candidate sense-checks an answer** against an easy benchmark such as a half or a quarter.
- **Allow a calculator and rough paper**, and keep timing consistent across candidates for this level.
- **Look for improvement, not perfection** — reasoning and method matter more than a flawless score at trainee level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that divides by the new value** in a percentage-change calculation rather than the original.
- **Crediting an answer that reverses a ratio's stated order.**
- **Missing when a candidate applies a percentage to the wrong base** in a two-step problem.
- **Missing when a candidate compares raw counts where a rate was needed**, or the reverse.
- **Letting a large-looking number intimidate a candidate into abandoning an otherwise manageable calculation** — note this as a coaching point, not just a wrong answer.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads, understands, and draws conclusions from written material. Each item presents a short passage — for this role, a policy extract, an email from a senior colleague, a service standard, or a section of a project document — followed by statements to evaluate. The most common format asks the candidate to judge each statement as **True** (the passage supports it), **False** (the passage contradicts it), or **Cannot say** (the passage does not settle it either way). Other formats test inference and clear-writing judgement.

These assessments are usually taken online, timed between 15 and 25 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions. The governing rule, worth stating to the candidate up front: answers rest only on the passage, not on general knowledge or experience. Even where a candidate knows from work that something is usually true, if the passage does not say it, the answer is "Cannot say".

This matters for a trainee business analyst because the role is built on precise reading and precise writing: supporting the capture and validation of requirements means understanding exactly what stakeholders said, not roughly what they meant. A single misread word — "must" read as "should", "all" read as "most" — can send a whole piece of work in the wrong direction.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Comprehension of dense text** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements**: supporting the identification and validation of requirements means reading stakeholder documents and correctly extracting what is required, optional, or out of scope.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis**: assessing options under supervision requires separating what the evidence establishes from what is merely assumed.
- **Inference** maps to **Business process improvement** and **Digital and data systems analysis**: reading a process description or system specification and working out what follows is inference in action.
- **Understanding stakeholder language** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: describing what stakeholders find important, and why, starts with reading their emails and papers attentively.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Testing (business analysis)** and everyday drafting: acceptance criteria, business scenarios, and notes must be unambiguous, because ambiguous words become defects later.

### Practice questions

Present each passage and its questions in order. Instruct the candidate to answer using only what the passage says. Do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

*Passage 1 (for Questions 1–3)*

"All new digital services in the department must complete a service assessment before moving from private beta to public beta. Service assessments are booked through the standards team and require at least four weeks' notice. A service that does not pass its assessment may reapply after addressing the assessment panel's recommendations. Services classed as internal-only tools are exempt from service assessment but must still complete an accessibility review."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "A new digital service must pass a service assessment before it can move from private beta to public beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage says all new digital services "must complete a service assessment before moving from private beta to public beta", and that a service which "does not pass... may reapply" — implying that passing, not merely attending, unlocks progress. This tests reading two sentences together to confirm a conclusion, exactly how policy constraints are read in analysis work.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Internal-only tools do not need an accessibility review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states internal-only tools "are exempt from service assessment but must still complete an accessibility review" — the opposite of the statement. The trap is latching onto the word "exempt" without noticing the exemption applies to the service assessment, not the accessibility review. This tests reading past a mid-sentence "but" that reverses the direction of a sentence, a common feature of policy text.

**Question 3 (easy)**

Statement: "The standards team takes four weeks to conduct a service assessment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says assessments "require at least four weeks' notice" — about booking lead time, not the assessment's duration, which the passage never states. This tests distinguishing notice periods from durations, and treating "Cannot say" as a genuine, confident answer rather than filling a gap with a plausible guess — in requirements work, the equivalent habit is recording an unspecified point as an open question rather than an assumption.

*Passage 2 (for Questions 4–6)*

"From: Priya (Senior Business Analyst). Subject: Workshop follow-up. Thanks for your notes from yesterday's requirements workshop — they were clear and well organised. Two things before Friday. First, please draft user stories for the three payment-related needs we captured; use the standard template, and don't worry about acceptance criteria yet, as we'll write those together. Second, the operations manager mentioned a monthly reporting need near the end — I don't think we captured it properly, so please add it to the open questions log rather than drafting a story for it. If anything is unclear, ask me before Friday rather than guessing."

**Question 4 (easy) — Comprehension**

According to the email, what should be done about the monthly reporting need?

- A) Draft a user story for it using the standard template.
- B) Add it to the open questions log.
- C) Write acceptance criteria for it with Priya.
- D) Ask the operations manager to attend another workshop.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Priya is explicit: the reporting need was not captured properly, so it should be added "to the open questions log rather than drafting a story for it." Option A misapplies the instruction meant for the three payment needs to the wrong item — a classic error of blending two instructions that sit near each other in a message. This tests separating multiple instructions in a single email into distinct, correctly attributed actions.

**Question 5 (moderate)**

Statement: "Priya thinks the workshop notes missed the monthly reporting need entirely."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Priya says "I don't think we captured it properly" — imperfect capture, not necessarily a complete miss. The statement makes a stronger claim than the email supports, and the email does not contradict it either. This tests calibrating the strength of a claim to the strength of the evidence, since "not captured properly" and "missed entirely" would lead to different follow-up actions in real requirements work.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following is the best inference from Priya's email, taken as a whole?

- A) Priya was disappointed with the workshop notes.
- B) Priya prefers to be asked rather than have assumptions made when instructions are unclear.
- C) The payment-related stories are less important than the reporting need.
- D) Acceptance criteria are not needed for payment stories.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email closes with "If anything is unclear, ask me before Friday rather than guessing" — a direct statement of the preference in option B. Option A contradicts the passage, which praises the notes. Option D overreaches: acceptance criteria are deferred ("yet"), not dropped. This tests inference from a small word doing decisive work — "yet" signals more is coming, a common feature of requirements documents.

*Passage 3 (for Questions 7–9)*

"The department's change advisory process applies to all changes to live services. Standard changes — those with a documented, pre-approved procedure — may be implemented without further approval, provided the procedure is followed exactly. All other changes require a change request. Emergency changes may be implemented before a change request is raised, but a retrospective change request must be submitted within two working days. The change advisory board meets each Wednesday and will not consider incomplete requests."

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "A change with a documented, pre-approved procedure never requires any approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says standard changes "may be implemented without further approval, provided the procedure is followed exactly" — "further" acknowledges an approval already exists (the pre-approved procedure), and the permission is conditional. The statement's "never" sweeps both details away, so the passage contradicts it. This tests treating absolute words — never, always, all, none — as a signal to check for conditions and exceptions in the surrounding text.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applying the passage to a scenario**

A production incident on Friday afternoon forces an immediate change to a live service, before a change request is raised. According to the passage, what must happen next?

- A) Nothing — emergency changes are exempt from the change process.
- B) A retrospective change request must be submitted by the end of Tuesday.
- C) The change must be reversed until the Wednesday board meeting.
- D) A retrospective change request must be submitted within two calendar days, so by Sunday.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage requires "a retrospective change request... within two working days." The change happened Friday; the next two working days are Monday and Tuesday, giving a deadline of end of Tuesday. Option D miscounts using calendar days rather than working days. This tests translating a rule plus a date into a concrete deadline — "working days versus calendar days" is a common place real projects trip up.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

A candidate is drafting a note summarising the standard-change rule for a team wiki. Which wording is clearest and most accurate?

- A) "Standard changes are basically fine to just do."
- B) "Standard changes (which have a documented, pre-approved procedure) can be implemented without further approval, as long as the procedure is followed exactly."
- C) "In the event of a change being categorised as standard in nature, implementation may potentially proceed absent additional approval formalities, subject to procedural conformance."
- D) "Changes can be implemented without approval."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B keeps every load-bearing element of the rule — what a standard change is, the permission, and the condition — in plain, actionable words. Option C preserves the meaning but buries it in padded, formal phrasing. Option D drops the word "standard" and the condition, turning a narrow permission into a blanket one. This tests writing that keeps meaning while losing fog, plain-language judgement directly relevant to drafting user stories and acceptance criteria.

*Passage 4 (for Questions 10–12)*

"The benefits team processed 12,400 applications in the last quarter. Of these, 9,300 were submitted online and the remainder by post. Postal applications took on average nine days to process, compared with three days for online applications. The team's target is to process 90% of all applications within five days. The head of service has stated that increasing online uptake is the single most effective way to meet the target, although she acknowledged that a minority of applicants will always need a postal route, and that any change must not disadvantage them."

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "More than three-quarters of last quarter's applications were submitted online."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three-quarters of 12,400 is 9,300 exactly (12,400 × 0.75 = 9,300), and the passage states exactly 9,300 online applications — precisely three-quarters, not more. Because the passage pins the figure precisely, it contradicts the claim rather than merely failing to support it. This tests a hybrid verbal-numeric item: performing the arithmetic before judging the statement, and trusting checked arithmetic over a confident-sounding claim.

**Question 11 (hard)**

Statement: "The team met its five-day processing target last quarter."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** It is tempting to reason from the averages (online three days, postal nine days) to a conclusion about the target. But averages do not reveal distributions: an average of three days could include applications taking six or seven days, and the target is about the percentage processed within five days, which the passage never gives. This tests recognising that means and percentiles answer different questions, and that service targets are almost always percentile-style — a genuinely hard discrimination.

**Question 12 (hard) — Inference from a stakeholder's stated position**

Which of the following best reflects the head of service's position, as stated in the passage?

- A) The postal route should be closed as soon as possible to meet the target.
- B) Online uptake should increase, while protecting the minority of applicants who need the postal route.
- C) The five-day target should be relaxed because postal applications will always be slow.
- D) Online applications should be prioritised over postal applications in the processing queue.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The head of service makes three connected points: increasing online uptake is the most effective lever; a minority "will always need a postal route"; and "any change must not disadvantage them." Option A directly contradicts the second and third points. Option D confuses channel uptake with queue priority, which the passage never mentions. This tests representing a stakeholder's position faithfully, including its qualifications and protections, not just its headline — foundational to stakeholder relationship management.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate treats "Cannot say" as a genuine answer** rather than avoiding it.
- **Watch for whether a candidate notices reversal words** — "but", "however" — that pivot a sentence's meaning.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes notice periods, durations, and deadlines.**
- **Note whether a candidate answers from the passage alone**, not from outside knowledge of how the process usually works.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and budget by passage rather than by statement when planning the session.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding a "True" answer given because a statement is plausible** rather than stated in the passage — this single pitfall accounts for more mismarking than any other.
- **Missing when a candidate ignores a reversal after "but" or "however".**
- **Crediting an answer that treats "not properly" as "not at all", or "may" as "must".**
- **Missing when a candidate confuses a notice period with a duration.**
- **Crediting an answer drawn from general knowledge or experience** rather than the passage itself.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment (often called an SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks which response is most or least effective. Unlike the other assessments, there is no arithmetic and no passage to parse — the material is human: a stakeholder pushes for something, a deadline slips, a mistake is spotted, a colleague needs help. The candidate is usually asked to pick the most effective response, the least effective response, rank all responses from best to worst, or rate each response's effectiveness.

These assessments are usually taken online, over 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed because they measure judgement rather than speed. There is rarely a single "perfect" option; responses are scored against a profile agreed by experienced practitioners — senior business analysts and people familiar with the UK government's ways of working. The scoring rewards user focus, open collaboration, ownership of one's own work, escalating at the right moment, acting inclusively, and honesty when things go wrong.

This matters for a trainee business analyst because at this level judgement matters more than knowledge. Working under supervision means the most important daily decisions are not "what is the right analysis technique?" but "should I ask now or keep trying?", "who needs to know about this?", and "what do I do when two instructions conflict?". Sound judgement — asking good questions at good moments, owning mistakes, treating stakeholders and colleagues with care — is what builds trust quickly at this level.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Knowing when to ask versus act** maps directly to the role level: a trainee receives direction from more senior business analysts and works with others or under supervision. Many scenarios test whether a candidate can distinguish "figure it out yourself" tasks from "check first" tasks.
- **Handling stakeholder requests** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: describing who stakeholders are and what they find important — and responding helpfully without accidentally committing the team to things it cannot promise.
- **Raising problems and errors** maps to **Defining and managing business needs, user needs and requirements** and **Testing (business analysis)**: validation work only protects the team if findings are raised promptly, accurately, and kindly, including findings about one's own work.
- **Team behaviours in delivery** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: scrum and kanban are social systems as much as boards and ceremonies.
- **Care for users and inclusion** maps to **Business process improvement** and the profession's core purpose: government services must work for everyone.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded. Where an item asks for the most effective response, all four options are things a real person might do — the differences lie in their consequences.

**Question 1 (easy) — Unclear instructions**

A senior business analyst asked a trainee to "tidy up the requirements catalogue before Thursday". Halfway through, the trainee realises they are not sure whether "tidy up" includes deleting requirements marked "rejected", or just reformatting them. The senior colleague is in meetings for the next hour but reachable by message; Thursday is two days away. What is the most effective response?

- A) Delete the rejected requirements — a tidy catalogue should not contain rejected items.
- B) Send a short message asking whether to delete or keep the rejected items, and carry on with the parts that are clear while waiting.
- C) Stop all work on the catalogue until speaking to the colleague face to face.
- D) Leave the rejected requirements untouched and do not mention them.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B combines the two behaviours that define effective trainee work: seeking clarification on the genuinely ambiguous, hard-to-undo part, and keeping productive momentum on the unambiguous parts. Option A guesses on the riskiest interpretation; option D hides the ambiguity until it resurfaces with less time to fix it; option C over-escalates a small question into a full stop. This tests the general pattern of asking early and specifically while keeping moving on what is clear — widely described as the behaviour that makes a trainee easy to supervise.

**Question 2 (easy) — A stakeholder asks for a requirement to be added**

Taking notes in a requirements workshop, a trainee is approached privately afterwards by an operations manager: "Add a requirement for an export-to-spreadsheet button — it doesn't need discussion, everyone wants it." Requirement decisions in this team go through the senior business analyst and product manager. What is the most effective response?

- A) Add the requirement — the manager knows the business better than the trainee does.
- B) Refuse, and explain that stakeholders are not allowed to raise requirements outside workshops.
- C) Thank them, record the request as a candidate requirement, and say it will be passed to the senior business analyst and product manager for prioritisation.
- D) Suggest they email the product manager directly and record nothing.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C treats the stakeholder with respect, is honest about the process (prioritisation is not the trainee's to grant), and takes ownership of the handover. Option A bypasses the team's decision process and commits the team to unprioritised work, beyond a trainee's authority. Option B is wrong on process and damages the relationship. Option D drops the request into a gap. This tests combining helpfulness with honesty about one's own authority in stakeholder relationship management.

**Question 3 (easy) — Finding a mistake in one's own work**

A trainee updated a process map last week and circulated it to the team. Today, rereading it, they notice a decision branch drawn the wrong way round — the map sends high-value refunds straight to payment without approval. Two colleagues have already used the map in discussions. What is the most effective response?

- A) Quietly fix the map and re-upload it without telling anyone, to avoid embarrassment.
- B) Tell the senior business analyst promptly, fix the map, and let the two colleagues know the corrected version is available and what changed.
- C) Say nothing — someone will probably notice eventually, and the map is only documentation.
- D) Mention it at the retrospective in two weeks' time.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is prompt, honest, and complete: the supervisor is not surprised later, the artefact is corrected, and the people who consumed the wrong version are told what changed — the step most often skipped. Option A leaves two colleagues carrying wrong information forward, and a discovered silent fix damages trust more than the original mistake would. Option C underestimates the consequence, since this error concerns a payment approval control. This tests whether a candidate owns and surfaces an error quickly rather than hiding or delaying it — what supervisors watch for is not whether mistakes happen, but how they are handled.

**Question 4 (easy) — Stand-up is drifting**

A scrum team's daily stand-up, meant to last 15 minutes, has run over 30 minutes for a week because two developers debate technical solutions in detail while everyone else waits. The candidate is the newest and most junior person on the team. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — as a trainee it is not their place to comment on team ceremonies.
- B) Interrupt the developers mid-debate and tell them they are breaking the rules of scrum.
- C) Raise the pattern at the retrospective, or mention it to the delivery manager or senior business analyst, suggesting long discussions move to after stand-up.
- D) Stop attending stand-up since it wastes time.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C routes the observation through the channels designed for exactly this — retrospectives exist so anyone can raise how the team works. Option A misunderstands that process observations are everyone's business in a multidisciplinary team. Option B has the right diagnosis but a confrontational delivery unlikely to land well from the newest member. Option D unilaterally abandons a team commitment. This tests raising a team problem constructively through the right forum rather than fixing it directly or ignoring it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Conflicting instructions**

A senior business analyst asked a trainee to spend today preparing requirement summaries for tomorrow's show-and-tell. Mid-morning, the product manager messages: "Please drop everything and check the acceptance criteria on these five stories — the developers are blocked." The senior colleague is on leave and unreachable. What is the most effective response?

- A) Follow the product manager's instruction — they are more senior, so their request automatically wins.
- B) Explain what has been asked for tomorrow, ask how urgent the blockage is, and agree an order — for example, unblocking the developers first if genuinely blocking, then returning to the summaries.
- C) Ignore the product manager's message because the senior colleague's instruction came first.
- D) Attempt both tasks simultaneously, switching every few minutes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B makes the competing demands visible, gathers the missing information about urgency, and agrees a sequence based on impact rather than rank or silence. Option A abandons a commitment without a word; option C ignores urgency entirely; option D reliably delivers both tasks late and badly. This tests transparent prioritisation under conflicting instructions, with a strong candidate's answer also noting the value of leaving the absent supervisor a note explaining the decision.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pressure to skip validation**

A trainee has been asked to capture requirements from a workshop into user stories. With a deadline close, a developer says: "Don't bother getting the operations team to check your stories — we all heard the workshop, just write them up and we'll start building." The team's process includes validating captured requirements with the people who expressed them. What is the most effective response?

- A) Skip validation — the developer was in the workshop, so the risk is low.
- B) Insist on the full validation process and say nothing about the deadline pressure to anyone.
- C) Tell the senior business analyst that the process is being ignored and let them handle it.
- D) Explain why validation protects the team, propose a lightweight check — such as a same-day review of the drafted stories with the operations lead — and flag the tension to the senior business analyst if pressure continues.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Option D holds the professional line while engaging with the real constraint: a lightweight same-day check preserves the safeguard at minimal cost. Option A trades a known safeguard for schedule comfort; when a misheard requirement surfaces in testing, the "saved" time is repaid with interest. Option C escalates before attempting any resolution. This tests protecting the purpose of a process while flexing its weight, keeping the supervisor informed, rather than either rigid insistence or silent compliance.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A colleague is struggling**

A fellow trainee on a neighbouring team confides that they are overwhelmed: they do not understand the requirements tool everyone uses, are afraid to ask their busy senior colleague, and are staying late to hide their slow progress. The candidate figured out the same tool last month. What is the most effective response?

- A) Offer to walk them through the tool, and gently encourage them to tell their senior colleague how they are finding things — sharing how asking for help worked out fine for the candidate.
- B) Tell their senior colleague immediately that the fellow trainee is struggling and hiding it.
- C) Sympathise, but explain that helping them is not the candidate's responsibility.
- D) Do their tool-based tasks for them for a few weeks until they catch up.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option A addresses both the practical gap (peer teaching is fast and confidence-building) and the underlying fear of asking, using the candidate's own experience as evidence that asking is safe. Option B breaks confidence and removes the colleague's chance to raise it themselves. Option D hides the problem and prevents genuine learning. This tests inclusive, collaborative behaviour that addresses a cause rather than only a symptom.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Spotting an accessibility gap**

Helping to test a new internal form against its acceptance criteria, all criteria pass. But the candidate notices the form's error messages appear only as a colour change (red outline) with no text, which may be a problem for colour-blind users. Accessibility is not mentioned in the acceptance criteria. What is the most effective response?

- A) Pass the stories — the job was to test against the acceptance criteria, and they all pass.
- B) Fail all the stories on accessibility grounds, even though the criteria passed.
- C) Pass the criteria as written, and separately raise the colour-only error messages with the senior business analyst as a possible accessibility issue and a gap in the acceptance criteria.
- D) Rewrite the acceptance criteria to include accessibility, then fail the stories against the new criteria.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C keeps the test record honest (the stories genuinely pass the criteria as written) while raising the real problem — a likely accessibility barrier and the gap that let it through — with the person who can act on it. Option A follows the letter and abandons the user; option B breaks the test record's integrity and exceeds decision authority at this level; option D goes further, unilaterally changing agreed criteria. This tests capturing faithfully, acting within one's authority, and routing the judgement call to the right person with the observation attached.

**Question 9 (moderate) — An awkward moment with data**

Preparing a summary, a trainee notices a completion-rate figure the operations manager has been quoting in meetings (92%) does not match what the raw report shows for the same period (84%). The operations manager is senior, well liked, and has used 92% in a slide deck for weeks. What is the most effective response?

- A) Use 92% in the summary — the operations manager must have a reason, and it avoids awkwardness.
- B) Use 84% in the summary without comment and let people notice the difference themselves.
- C) Check the reading of the report first, then show the discrepancy to the senior business analyst, asking how best to raise it, with the raw report to hand.
- D) Email the operations manager's director pointing out the error.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C verifies first (the discrepancy might be a definitional difference or a misreading), then raises it with a supervisor who knows the relationships and history and can judge how best to proceed. Option A knowingly propagates a figure believed to be wrong. Option D escalates a possibly unverified issue over the manager's head, maximal relationship damage for minimal certainty. This tests never suppressing a data discrepancy while never weaponising one either — verify, then raise it through the person who supervises the work.

**Question 10 (hard) — Least effective: deadline versus quality**

A team must submit a requirements pack to a governance board by Friday. On Wednesday, a trainee realises one section — covering how the service will handle users who cannot use digital channels — is thin, based on a single conversation, with the assisted-digital team never consulted. The senior business analyst is aware the section is weak and says: "We'll submit Friday and improve it later." Which response would be **least effective**?

- A) Accept the decision, and offer to start arranging the assisted-digital conversation now so the improvement is ready soon after submission.
- B) Ask whether the board should be told the section is provisional, so the weakness is transparent.
- C) Contact the governance board secretariat directly on Thursday to say the pack should not be trusted.
- D) Note the concern to the senior colleague in writing, then support the Friday submission as directed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C bypasses the supervisor after a considered decision, presents a personal view to a governance body as if it were the team's, and frames a known, managed weakness as untrustworthiness — damaging the team's credibility and the supervisory relationship. The other three are all reasonable trainee behaviours, which is what makes this a hard item: option A adds constructive ownership, option B improves the decision without opposing it, and option D preserves the concern honestly while committing to the team's course. This tests distinguishing legitimate escalation, which goes through or alongside the management line, from undermining, which goes around it.

**Question 11 (hard) — Ranking responses: the overheard shortcut**

A trainee overhears two developers agreeing to mark several user stories as "tested" without running the full test scripts, because "the scripts are outdated anyway and we're behind." The team's test records feed into a go-live decision two weeks away. Rank these responses from most to least effective: (1) speak to the developers directly, say what was overheard, ask about the outdated scripts, and suggest raising the script problem with the test lead rather than skipping testing; (2) tell the senior business analyst what was overheard, factually, and let them decide next steps; (3) update the test scripts over the weekend so the developers have no excuse; (4) do nothing, since developers are responsible for their own practices and the conversation was not meant to be overheard.

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4
- B) 2, 1, 4, 3
- C) 1, 2, 4, 3
- D) 3, 1, 2, 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Response 1 edges out response 2 for first place because it addresses the people concerned directly, assumes good faith about the outdated-scripts complaint, and routes the underlying problem to its proper owner — direct, non-accusatory conversation is usually the best first move. Response 2 is a close second and becomes the right move immediately if the conversation fails, since false test records feeding a go-live decision are serious. Response 4 does no new harm but ignores an integrity issue everyone shares responsibility for. Response 3 actively creates harm while looking heroic: unrequested weekend work, outside remit, that addresses the excuse rather than the behaviour. This tests ranking by anchoring the clearly best and clearly worst first, then comparing the middle pair by consequences.

**Question 12 (hard) — Competing goods: the user in the room**

A team runs a workshop with operations staff to map the current complaints-handling process. One participant — a part-time staff member who handles the most complex complaints — keeps being talked over by two confident senior managers, and has gone quiet. The candidate is co-facilitating by managing the process map on screen while the senior business analyst facilitates the discussion. Twenty minutes remain and the workshop is behind schedule. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — the on-screen map is the candidate's job, facilitation belongs to the senior colleague, and time is short.
- B) At the next natural pause, say something like: "Before we close this section, could we hear from Sam? You handle the complex complaints — does this map match what you see?"
- C) Interrupt the senior managers and tell them they are dominating the discussion and excluding a colleague.
- D) After the workshop, email Sam privately to apologise for the managers' behaviour.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B invites the quiet expert in by name, at a natural pause, with a concrete and flattering question — fully within a co-facilitator's role, and analytically valuable since Sam holds exactly the exceptions and edge cases that make a process map true rather than tidy. Option A hides behind role boundaries when inclusion in a co-run workshop is everyone's business. Option C pursues the right value confrontationally. Option D is too late to help the artefact. This tests recognising that inclusive behaviour and good analysis are often the same move — the people most easily talked over frequently know the most about how work really happens.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate combines honesty with helpfulness** — the strongest responses usually address the immediate need and keep the right people informed.
- **Watch for the "capture, act within authority, route upward" pattern** that recurs across several scenarios at this level.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes escalating (through or alongside a supervisor) from bypassing (around one).**
- **In ranking items, note whether a candidate anchors the extremes first**, then compares the middle options by consequence.
- **Since these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an aggressive "strong leadership" response** — publicly confronting colleagues or forcing a view rarely reflects good judgement at trainee level.
- **Rewarding a passive option that avoids all conflict** — "say nothing" or "not my responsibility" options are usually weak, since constructive engagement through the right channel almost always beats silence.
- **Confusing deference with judgement** — "they are senior, so do what they say" fails whenever the senior person lacks context or the request bypasses agreed process.
- **Rewarding a heroic fix that hides the problem from the people who own it** — secret weekend fixes and quiet workarounds tend to score lower than they first appear to deserve.
- **Missing when a candidate's answer overlooks the user** — accessibility, assisted-digital users, or a quiet expert voice in a scenario should weigh heavily in scoring.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a trainee business analyst: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested structured thinking at the heart of the craft: checking IDs against conventions, reasoning from team rules to their consequences, ordering process steps, spotting swapped branches, and tracing dependency chains. The numeric section tested ordinary arithmetic done accurately: percentages, percentage change, averages, weighted averages, rates, and the habit of sense-checking every figure. The verbal section tested reading exactly what is on the page — True, False, or the underrated Cannot say. The situational judgement section came back repeatedly to the judgement calls that define trainee life: when to ask, how to own a mistake, how to hold a process line kindly, and how to bring a quiet expert into the conversation.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score, and particularly valuable at trainee level, where the goal is as much development as selection.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can think carefully, work accurately with numbers, read precisely, and exercise sound judgement under supervision — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
