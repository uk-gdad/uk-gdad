# Principal Developer (Management) - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for principal developer on the management track. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A principal developer on the management track leads and plans development across large or multiple teams, is an expert across many technical areas or a deep specialist in one, and uses that expertise alongside related disciplines to keep multiple teams working effectively. The role summary also names developing capability through internal mobility, career paths and recruitment, and identifying, testing and championing emerging technologies.

The management track adds responsibilities that change the centre of the work: setting the strategy and managing resource allocation for solution development programmes, and working with client functions to establish business requirements, alongside leading and developing teams of experts, analysing and optimising processes across the organisation, and setting policy and standards.

That combination is unusual and it is what makes the role difficult to test for. This level is expected to hold deep technical judgement while spending most of its time on things that are not technical — where the people go, what the programme commits to, what the business actually needs as distinct from what it has asked for, and which of several defensible strategies the organisation will follow. The technical depth is not decoration: a resource allocation made by someone who cannot judge technical difficulty is guesswork, and a requirement negotiated by someone who cannot see the implementation cost is a promise the teams will have to break.

Three features of the role shape what the assessment is built to test. First, resource allocation is zero-sum, where every yes is an unstated no, and the discipline is making the no explicit. Second, business requirements as stated are usually solutions in disguise, and the useful work is recovering the need underneath. Third, strategy at this level is mostly about what will not be done, the part organisations resist.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate recovers the real need behind a stated request, prices the opportunity cost of an allocation, and checks the internal consistency of someone else's numbers rather than accepting them at face value. Verbal items reward precise reading of funding conditions, governance and employment language against the passage alone. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a response that makes reasoning visible to a person who is disappointed by the outcome, protects the honesty of information flowing upward from client functions and programmes, and names the candidate's own contribution to a past failure rather than leaving it unattributed.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through role-realistic material. On the management track at principal level that means programme strategy, resource allocation, requirements analysis, organisational process design, and capability planning.

The format is typically fifteen to thirty minutes online, covering deduction, pattern recognition, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving, scored against a norm group of comparable seniority.

Three things distinguish this section at this level. First, stated requirements are usually solutions — "we need a dashboard" is an answer somebody has already reached, and the question it answers, usually about a decision they need to make faster, often has a cheaper answer. Second, allocation decisions are comparative: there is no good allocation in isolation, only the best available use of a person or a period, and evaluating one option without its alternatives is not evaluation. Third, organisational effects lag and confound — a change made now shows up eighteen months later, mixed in with several other changes, which means reasoning about mechanisms matters more than waiting for outcomes.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Requirements analysis** maps to working with client functions to establish business requirements.
- **Allocation and prioritisation** map to managing resource allocation for solution development programmes.
- **Strategy reasoning** maps to setting the strategy for those programmes.
- **Process diagnosis** maps to **Development process optimisation**.
- **Capability reasoning** maps to developing capability through mobility, career paths, and recruitment.
- **Risk and security judgement** map to **Information security** and **Systems design**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Deduction from an allocation rule**

Policy: "A team may be allocated additional developers only where its backlog exceeds two sprints of capacity, unless the allocation is to cover planned absence." A team received additional developers. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) Its backlog exceeded two sprints.
- B) The allocation covered planned absence.
- C) Either its backlog exceeded two sprints, or the allocation covered planned absence, or the policy was not followed.
- D) The allocation was justified.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A rule with an exception, plus a fact silent on both branches, licenses only the disjunction — and the third limb, the policy not being followed, must be included, since the facts do not establish that the policy was applied at all. This tests deducing the full disjunction rather than picking a branch (A, B) or equating compliance with justification, which are different claims (D).

**Question 2 (easy) — A requirement that is a solution**

A client function states they need a real-time dashboard showing case volumes. Ask for the strongest first step.

- A) Scope the dashboard and estimate it.
- B) Ask what decision they would make differently with the information and how quickly — because "real-time dashboard" is a solution, and if the underlying need is a weekly capacity decision then a daily emailed figure meets it at a fraction of the cost.
- C) Explain that real-time is expensive.
- D) Offer a daily report instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Real-time" is among the most expensive words in requirements and is almost never actually needed — the two-part question in option B recovers the actual need, since a decision made weekly cannot benefit from real-time data no matter how much it would be liked. This tests recovering the underlying need before proposing a solution — option D reaches a plausible answer without establishing it, which may be wrong and also teaches the client that their request gets substituted rather than understood, while C leads with cost, which sounds obstructive, and A prices the solution rather than meeting the need.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Allocation with an unstated cost**

A programme director asks to move two developers onto a high-profile project for six weeks. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Move them; the director has asked and the project is high-profile.
- B) State what the move costs — specifically what those two people would otherwise have delivered and what slips as a result — and let the director decide with both sides visible, because a resource request considered without its opportunity cost is always granted.
- C) Decline; the existing work is committed.
- D) Move one developer as a compromise.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Every allocation is a comparison, and the request as posed presents only one side of it; option B supplies the other and leaves the decision where it belongs, since the director may well decide the high-profile project is worth the slip once it is visible. This tests surfacing the opportunity cost of a resource request rather than granting it by default (A) or splitting the difference without evaluating either side (D, which delivers two half-served pieces of work), or making the decision unilaterally (C).

**Question 4 (moderate) — Diagnosing a programme in trouble**

A programme is six months behind; the programme manager attributes it to unclear requirements, teams attribute it to changing priorities. Ask for the strongest reading.

- A) Requirements are unclear; the programme manager has the fullest view.
- B) The two accounts are likely the same phenomenon seen from different positions — priorities changing and requirements being unclear are both what it looks like when the business need has not been settled — so the useful question is who is empowered to decide the scope and whether they have.
- C) The teams are closer to the work and more likely correct.
- D) Both are symptoms of poor programme management.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** When nobody has authority to settle scope, teams experience it as priorities changing and a programme manager experiences it as requirements never becoming clear. This tests recognising two apparently contradictory accounts as one underlying situation and identifying the single answerable, actionable question (who decides, and have they) — rather than adjudicating between the accounts on the basis of position (A, C) or naming a category without a mechanism (D).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Strategy as exclusion**

Asked to set a technical strategy for solution development across the organisation. Ask what makes it useful.

- A) A clear statement of the technologies and approaches the organisation will use.
- B) A clear statement of what the organisation will not do — which platforms will not be supported, which patterns are out of scope, which needs will not be met centrally — because a strategy that only says yes provides no basis for declining anything and therefore constrains nothing.
- C) Alignment with the wider government technology strategy.
- D) Endorsement from senior leadership.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A strategy is a set of decisions made in advance so individual cases do not have to be argued, and it can only do that if it excludes — a document listing approved technologies without saying what happens to the unapproved ones generates the same argument every time. This tests understanding that exclusion is what makes a strategy useful — options C and D are genuinely necessary conditions for a strategy to have effect, not what makes it useful, and A is the form most strategies take and the reason most have no effect.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A process improvement with a distributed cost**

Considering mandating a design review step before any significant build starts. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) Adopt it; design review catches expensive mistakes early.
- B) Price the delay across the volume — if forty significant builds a year each wait an average of five days for a review slot, that is 200 team-days of delay annually, which the review must beat in avoided rework — and design the mechanism so the waiting time is small, since the value is in the review and the cost is nearly all in the queue.
- C) Adopt it for high-risk builds only.
- D) Make it advisory rather than mandatory.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Design review is usually worthwhile, and the reason such steps fail is almost always the queue rather than the review itself. This tests quantifying the delay across annual volume and identifying the design constraint (review slot availability) that actually determines whether it works, rather than adopting a control without pricing it (A) or avoiding the mandate and thereby the benefit (D, since advisory reviews are sought least by the teams that need them most).

**Question 7 (harder) — Competing bids for the same capacity**

Three programmes each need the same specialist team next quarter, with stated business-case benefits of £2m, £1.4m and £900k. Ask for the strongest basis for deciding.

- A) The £2m programme; it has the largest claimed benefit.
- B) Interrogate the claims before comparing them — benefits in business cases are self-reported by the party requesting resource, are stated on different bases and over different periods, and frequently include benefits that would arrive anyway — and then decide on comparable figures, weighted by what is lost if each waits a quarter.
- C) Split the team across all three.
- D) Decide on strategic alignment rather than benefits.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Self-reported benefits from parties competing for a resource are among the least reliable numbers in an organisation, and comparing them as stated rewards optimistic authorship. This tests normalising the claims and adding the criterion the business cases omit — what a quarter's delay actually costs each — rather than treating the largest claim as the largest benefit (A), the fairness instinct that serves all three badly (C), or substituting one soft criterion for another (D).

**Question 8 (harder) — A capability decision**

Two teams each have an expert in a critical technology; one expert is leaving. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Recruit a replacement.
- B) Treat this as revealing a single point of failure that existed before the resignation — one expert per team is not resilience — so the response is to spread the knowledge deliberately, through pairing, documentation and rotation, and recruitment is one input to that rather than the whole answer.
- C) Move the remaining expert to cover both teams.
- D) Reduce dependence on the technology.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The resignation is the trigger, not the problem — two teams with one expert each was already fragile, and replacing the leaver restores the fragile state rather than fixing it. This tests asking what a triggering event revealed rather than only what it caused, addressing the structural position rather than a single point of failure across both teams (C, strictly worse) or a partial, slower remedy alone (D).

**Question 9 (harder) — Reading a request from a client function**

A client function asks for a system replicating their current manual process exactly, including several steps they acknowledge are workarounds for a limitation in an old system being retired. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Build what they asked for; they know their process.
- B) Point out specifically which steps exist only because of the system being retired, and propose the process without them — because automating a workaround makes it permanent, and the point at which the old system goes is the only realistic opportunity to remove those steps.
- C) Build it as specified and improve it later.
- D) Ask them to redesign their process first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The client function has already identified the workarounds as workarounds, making the conversation available now — automating them encodes a retired system's limitations into a new one indefinitely, and "improve it later" (C) reliably does not happen once people are trained on the process. This tests naming specifically which steps and why, making the response a proposal rather than a criticism, rather than deferring the whole piece of work to them (D) or building precisely what was asked while ignoring what they have already flagged (A).

**Question 10 (harder) — An organisational pattern**

Over two years, four separate programmes have each built their own notification capability, each aware of the others. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The teams should collaborate more.
- B) Four teams independently choosing to build rather than reuse, while knowing about each other, indicates that reuse was more expensive than building — most likely because the existing capabilities were not discoverable, not usable without their owning team's help, or came with a dependency on that team's roadmap — so the fix is in what makes shared capabilities usable, not in encouraging collaboration.
- C) A central notification service should be mandated.
- D) The programmes were under time pressure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four informed teams making the same choice is a rational response to incentives, not a failure of cooperation, and the realistic mechanisms each imply a different remedy. This tests identifying the incentive behind a repeated rational choice rather than prescribing a behaviour without addressing its absence (A), mandating reuse without making it cheaper (C, which produces compliance theatre), or accepting a plausible but non-explanatory cause (D, which does not explain a repeated pattern).

**Question 11 (harder) — A strategy that is being ignored**

Eighteen months after a technical strategy was set, most teams' choices are inconsistent with it. Ask for the strongest first step.

- A) Enforce it through the design authority.
- B) Establish why — whether teams do not know it, do not find it applicable to their situation, or find compliance more expensive than the alternative — because those have entirely different remedies, and a strategy widely ignored by capable people is more often unusable than defied.
- C) Rewrite the strategy to match what teams are doing.
- D) Escalate the non-compliance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Widespread non-compliance by competent teams is evidence about the strategy, and the three realistic causes diverge sharply in their remedy. This tests diagnosing before enforcing — option A enforces before diagnosing and risks malicious compliance against a genuinely unusable strategy, C abandons the strategic intent, and D treats a systemic signal as a discipline matter.

**Question 12 (harder) — Deciding between two defensible strategies**

Two credible technical strategies are proposed for the next three years; both are defensible, and senior colleagues are split. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Choose the technically stronger one.
- B) Ask which is more recoverable if wrong, and which the organisation can actually execute given its skills and delivery capacity — because with two defensible options the differentiator is rarely technical merit, and a strategy the organisation cannot execute is worse than a slightly weaker one it can.
- C) Take the decision to leadership.
- D) Adopt elements of both.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** When two options are both defensible, the technical comparison has already failed to separate them, and continuing to argue it produces a decision by stamina rather than reasoning. This tests introducing the criteria that do separate genuinely tied options — reversibility and executability — rather than a consensus-seeking hybrid (D, which typically acquires the costs of both and the coherence of neither) or deferring a decision that is the role's to make (C).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate recovers the underlying decision or need behind a stated requirement, rather than scoping the requirement as posed.
- Note whether the candidate states the opportunity cost of a resource allocation explicitly, rather than granting or refusing a request without comparison.
- Watch for whether the candidate defines a strategy or standard by what it excludes as well as what it permits.
- Score for whether an organisational pattern (repeated non-reuse, non-compliance) is read as a rational response to incentives rather than a failure of individual judgement or collaboration.
- Note whether a triggering event (a resignation, a slipped date) is used to reveal a pre-existing structural weakness rather than treated only as the problem itself.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a scoped and estimated solution for a requirement that was never interrogated for the underlying need.
- Missing when a candidate grants a resource request without stating what it displaces.
- Rewarding a strategy or standard defined only by what it permits, with no stated exclusions.
- Accepting self-reported business-case figures compared directly without normalisation.
- Missing when a candidate treats an organisational pattern (repeated duplication, widespread non-compliance) as an individual or team failing rather than a rational response to a structural incentive.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment tests whether a candidate can reach a defensible conclusion from data at speed. On the management track at principal level the data is programme finance, resource models, benefits cases, portfolio-level delivery metrics, and capacity plans.

The format is typically fifteen to twenty-five minutes with a calculator, mixing computation with interpretation. Interpretation items dominate at this level, since the numbers dealt with are mostly produced by other people who want a particular answer.

Three habits are worth naming: at this level, a candidate is usually assessing someone else's arithmetic, and the errors are rarely in the sums — they are in the base, the period, the assumption of linearity, or a benefit that would have arrived anyway; people costs dominate software programmes, and any analysis treating them as fixed while varying the technology is measuring the smaller half; and portfolio-level figures conceal enormous variation, with a mean across programmes generally the least informative statistic available.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Programme finance** maps to setting strategy and managing resource allocation.
- **Benefits analysis** maps to working with client functions to establish business requirements.
- **Resource modelling** maps to managing resource allocation across programmes.
- **Process metrics at scale** map to **Development process optimisation**.
- **Capacity and availability** map to **Availability and capacity management**.
- **Risk quantification** maps to **Information security** and **Systems design**.

### Practice questions

*Table 1 — Four development programmes, current year*

| Programme | People | Annual cost (£000) | Features delivered | Live incidents |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atlas | 24 | 2,640 | 61 | 14 |
| Beacon | 15 | 1,725 | 48 | 4 |
| Cirrus | 32 | 3,840 | 44 | 27 |
| Dovetail | 9 | 945 | 31 | 3 |

**Question 1 (easy) — Cost per person**

Ask which programme has the highest annual cost per person.

- A) Atlas
- B) Beacon
- C) Cirrus
- D) Dovetail

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Atlas £110k; Beacon £115k; Cirrus £120k; Dovetail £105k. This tests basic per-person calculation, and in a strong candidate's answer, noting the modest spread (£105k–£120k) means cost differences between these programmes are driven almost entirely by headcount rather than by rate.

**Question 2 (easy) — Total portfolio**

Ask for the approximate total annual cost of the four programmes.

- A) £7.5m
- B) £9.2m
- C) £11.4m
- D) £8.1m

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 2,640 + 1,725 + 3,840 + 945 = £9,150k, about £9.2m across 80 people. This tests basic totalling, establishing the denominator against which every subsequent percentage or portfolio-level proposal is measured — a £200k tooling investment is 2% of the portfolio, a very different conversation from £200k in the abstract.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Cost per feature**

Ask which programme has the highest cost per feature delivered.

- A) Atlas
- B) Beacon
- C) Cirrus
- D) Dovetail

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Atlas £43k; Beacon £36k; Cirrus £87k; Dovetail £30k. This tests computing a per-unit figure, and, in a strong answer, noting "features" is not a standardised unit — a programme delivering large features scores badly on this measure while delivering more — so the metric identifies where to look rather than what is definitively true.

**Question 4 (moderate) — What the data supports**

Ask for the strongest conclusion about Cirrus from Table 1.

- A) Cirrus is inefficient and should be restructured.
- B) Cirrus is the outlier on every derived measure — highest cost per person, highest cost per feature, and by far the most incidents — which makes it the clearest candidate for investigation, though the table contains nothing about what the programmes are building and a high-complexity or high-risk programme would produce exactly this profile.
- C) Cirrus has too many people.
- D) Cirrus's incidents are caused by its size.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The consistency across three independent measures is a real signal, and the confound is decisive: the table has no column for what each programme does, and a programme replacing a critical legacy system carrying decades of business rules would show this profile regardless of how well it is run. This tests stating a finding together with its limit — option A converts a signal into a verdict about people and structure, C infers overstaffing from output, and D asserts a causal mechanism the data does not distinguish.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Incidents normalised**

Ask which programme has the highest incident rate per feature delivered.

- A) Atlas
- B) Beacon
- C) Cirrus
- D) Dovetail

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Atlas 0.23; Beacon 0.08; Cirrus 0.61; Dovetail 0.10 incidents per feature. This tests normalising by delivery volume before comparing — the gap widens rather than narrows once normalised, strengthening the case that something specific to Cirrus warrants attention.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A benefits claim**

A client function proposes a system claiming £1.8m annual savings from removing 30 full-time equivalent roles, at a fully loaded cost of £42,000 per role. Ask whether the claim is internally consistent.

- A) Yes; 30 × £42,000 = £1.26m, close enough.
- B) No — 30 roles at £42,000 is £1.26m, not £1.8m, so the claim overstates by about £540,000 or 43%, and the difference needs explaining before the case can be relied on.
- C) Yes; other savings would make up the difference.
- D) There is not enough information.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 30 × £42,000 = £1,260,000 against a claimed £1,800,000, an overstatement of £540,000. This tests checking a business case's internal arithmetic before engaging with its conclusions — option A waves through a 43% gap, and option C supplies an unstated explanation on the author's behalf rather than requiring it to be stated.

**Question 7 (harder) — Benefits that would arrive anyway**

The same case notes that 12 of the 30 roles are currently vacant and were due to be removed in a planned restructure. Ask for the strongest assessment.

- A) The saving is unaffected; the roles still go.
- B) Those 12 roles would be removed regardless, so they are not a benefit attributable to this system — the attributable saving is 18 roles at £42,000, about £756,000, which is 42% of the claim.
- C) The 12 vacancies should be excluded but the restructure roles counted.
- D) The whole case should be rejected.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A benefit that would arrive without the investment is not a benefit of the investment, and double-counting savings already committed elsewhere is a common inflation in a business case. This tests stripping out benefits attributable to something other than the proposal, arriving at 18 × £42,000 = £756,000 against the claimed £1.8m, rather than rejecting the whole case outright (D, when £756,000 may still justify the cost) or drawing the distinction in the wrong place (C).

*Table 2 — Resource plan, next four quarters*

| Quarter | Available developer-quarters | Committed | Requested (uncommitted) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 80 | 74 | 12 |
| Q2 | 80 | 68 | 22 |
| Q3 | 84 | 51 | 40 |
| Q4 | 84 | 44 | 48 |

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading the plan**

Ask in which quarter committed plus requested demand most exceeds availability.

- A) Q1
- B) Q2
- C) Q3
- D) Q4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Q1 over by 6; Q2 over by 10; Q3 over by 7; Q4 over by 8. This tests comparing against each quarter's own capacity rather than the largest total demand (Q4 at 92, the figure the eye is drawn to and the wrong one, since availability also rises in the second half of the year) — the more important observation is that every quarter is oversubscribed.

**Question 9 (harder) — What the pattern shows**

Ask for the strongest interpretation of the trend in Table 2 from Q1 to Q4.

- A) Demand is increasing.
- B) Committed work falls from 74 to 44 while requested work rises from 12 to 48, which is the normal shape of a plan where near-term work is firm and later work is aspiration — so the later quarters are not really oversubscribed yet, they are unallocated, and the decision point is when requests convert to commitments.
- C) The organisation is running out of capacity.
- D) More developers are needed in Q4.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Commitments are made close in, and distant quarters hold requests not yet decided — reading Q4's apparent oversubscription as a capacity crisis misunderstands the number, which is a queue of asks mostly to be declined, deferred or dropped. This tests distinguishing committed demand from requested demand — options A, C and D each treat unconverted requests as real demand, which is how organisations end up hiring for work that never materialises.

**Question 10 (harder) — Cost of a portfolio-wide change**

Considering a change requiring two days of training for every developer across the four programmes. Using Table 1 headcounts and a working year of 220 days, ask for the approximate cost in delivery capacity.

- A) About 0.9%
- B) About 2.2%
- C) About 4.4%
- D) About 0.4%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total headcount 80; training days 80 × 2 = 160; total annual capacity 80 × 220 = 17,600; 160 ÷ 17,600 ≈ 0.91%. This tests expressing a portfolio-wide intervention as a percentage of total capacity, converting "we can't spare two days" into a weighable proposition — just under 1%, or about £83,000 at the portfolio's £9.15m cost.

**Question 11 (harder) — A trend across a portfolio**

Portfolio-wide feature delivery per quarter over two years: 41, 44, 39, 47, 43, 46, 42, 48. Ask, for a stakeholder wondering whether delivery is improving, for the strongest answer.

- A) Yes; the trend is upward from 41 to 48.
- B) The series oscillates between 39 and 48 with a very slight upward drift — the first four average 42.75 and the last four 44.75, a difference of about 5% that sits well within the quarter-to-quarter variation — so the honest answer is that delivery is broadly flat and the data cannot support a claim of improvement.
- C) No; delivery is flat.
- D) There is insufficient data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First-to-last comparison suggests a 17% rise; comparing the two halves gives about 5%, smaller than the within-half range. This tests comparing halves of a time series rather than endpoints — option A picks the two endpoints that tell the preferred story, the single most common misuse of a time series in a management report, and option C is closer to right and still overclaims, since "flat" is itself a conclusion the noise barely supports.

**Question 12 (harder) — Marginal resourcing**

A programme with 24 people is 8 weeks behind; adding 6 people is proposed to recover the schedule. Ask for the strongest assessment.

- A) Six people is a 25% increase and should recover roughly 8 weeks over a quarter.
- B) Adding a quarter more people to a late programme will slow it in the near term — the new people need onboarding from the existing team, and coordination overhead rises — so the realistic effect is a further delay before any gain, and the recovery options that work on this timescale are scope reduction or sequencing changes rather than headcount.
- C) Adding people never helps a late programme.
- D) Add three people instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is Brooks's observation: onboarding six people costs the existing twenty-four real time, and coordination paths grow faster than headcount. This tests rejecting a linear recovery-by-headcount projection and identifying the levers that actually work on a short timescale (scope, sequencing) — option C overstates, since extra capacity does help over a longer horizon, and the point is specifically about the recovery timescale.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate checks a business case's internal arithmetic before engaging with its conclusions.
- Note whether the candidate strips out benefits attributable to something other than the proposal under consideration (a benefit that would arrive anyway).
- Watch for whether counts are normalised by volume before programmes, teams or periods are compared.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes committed demand from an unconverted queue of requests in a resource plan.
- Note whether the candidate compares halves or an appropriate window of a time series rather than its endpoints when characterising a trend.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting programme metrics read as evidence about people rather than about what is being built.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a claimed saving without dividing it by its stated unit cost.
- Rewarding a benefits case that counts savings already committed to another initiative.
- Accepting uncommitted requests treated as real, current demand.
- Missing when a candidate proposes headcount as a short-timescale recovery plan for a late programme.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment tests whether a candidate can determine exactly what written material establishes. On the management track at principal level the material is programme governance, business cases, funding conditions, employment and resourcing guidance, and agreements with client functions.

The commonest format gives a passage and asks for statements to be marked True, False or Cannot Say. True means the passage establishes it, False means it contradicts it, Cannot Say means neither. A candidate's own experience is the main hazard, since they will know how these arrangements usually work and the passage describes this specific one.

Three specifics matter at this level: funding and governance documents attach conditions, and a condition attached to one thing does not travel to another; business cases and strategies are written persuasively, making the separation of what is asserted from what is established a routine requirement rather than an academic exercise; and employment and resourcing guidance uses precise modal language, where the difference between "may", "should" and "must" determines whether discretion exists.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading governance and funding conditions** maps to managing resource allocation for programmes.
- **Reading business cases** maps to working with client functions to establish requirements.
- **Reading employment guidance** maps to leading and developing teams of experts.
- **Reading strategy** maps to setting strategy for solution development programmes.
- **Reading standards** maps to setting policy and standards.
- **Precision in written advice** maps to this role's advice to senior stakeholders.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Funding for the programme is approved in two tranches. The first tranche is released on approval of the outline business case. The second tranche is released on approval of the full business case, and is conditional on the programme demonstrating that the benefits identified in the outline case remain achievable. Where the full business case identifies benefits lower than those in the outline case, the second tranche may be reduced proportionately at the discretion of the investment committee. Funding not drawn within the financial year in which it is released lapses and must be re-approved. The investment committee meets quarterly and may, in exceptional circumstances, approve funding out of cycle."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: The second tranche of funding is released on approval of the full business case.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, with a further condition about benefits remaining achievable. This tests the baseline reading before the passage's complications.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: If the full business case identifies lower benefits, the second tranche will be reduced.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the tranche "may be reduced proportionately at the discretion of the investment committee" — two separate signals of discretion in one clause. This tests reading "may" plus "at the discretion of" as making reduction a possibility rather than an automatic consequence, which the statement's "will" wrongly asserts.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: Funding released in one financial year and unspent must be re-approved before it can be used.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests noting the trigger is the year of release rather than the year of approval — a tranche approved in March and released in April has the following twelve months, not one.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: The programme cannot receive funding between quarterly investment committee meetings.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage contradicts this: the committee "may, in exceptional circumstances, approve funding out of cycle." This tests recognising that a discretionary power refutes claims in both directions — the same "may" that defeated a "will" in Question 2 here defeats a "cannot", establishing possibility without certainty either way.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The service agreement between the digital function and the client function provides that the client function is responsible for defining business requirements and for prioritising the delivery backlog. The digital function is responsible for technical decisions, including choice of technology, architecture, and delivery approach. Where a business requirement can only be met at disproportionate technical cost, the digital function must set out the cost and propose alternatives, and the client function decides whether to proceed. Changes to the agreement require the consent of both functions. The agreement was last revised at the start of the current financial year."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: The client function prioritises the delivery backlog.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly as one of two responsibilities assigned to the client function. This tests the baseline reading, worth holding against what follows about disproportionate technical cost.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: The digital function can decline to build a requirement that would cost a disproportionate amount.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage contradicts this: the digital function "must set out the cost and propose alternatives," and "the client function decides whether to proceed." This tests distinguishing a duty to inform from a power to refuse — the decision is allocated to the client function, not withheld by the digital function.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: The client function can require a particular technology to be used.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Technical decisions "including choice of technology" are allocated to the digital function. This tests recognising that the passage assigns the decision elsewhere rather than merely omitting a client power — the mirror image of Question 6, together defining a clean split between what to build and how.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: The agreement has been revised within the last twelve months.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The agreement "was last revised at the start of the current financial year," with no indication of how far into that year the passage's "now" sits. This tests recognising a relative time reference given one end of an interval but not the other — probably true, and not established.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Where a post has been advertised twice without a suitable appointment, the recruiting manager may propose a revised specification, an increased pay band subject to approval by the pay committee, or engagement of an interim resource for a period not exceeding twelve months. Interim engagements beyond twelve months require the approval of the director of resources. An interim engagement should not be used where the underlying need is permanent, unless a plan to establish permanent capability accompanies the request. Recruiting managers should consult the capability lead before advertising a post requiring specialist skills."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: A recruiting manager can increase the pay band for a post advertised twice without success.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the manager "may propose" an increased pay band "subject to approval by the pay committee" — proposing is not deciding. This tests distinguishing a genuine unilateral option (revising the specification, in the same sentence) from one requiring approval elsewhere, which makes this statement False rather than Cannot Say.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: An interim engagement can never be used where the underlying need is permanent.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage contradicts "never": an interim engagement "should not be used where the underlying need is permanent, unless a plan to establish permanent capability accompanies the request." This tests catching two independent reasons the statement fails — the exception clause, and the base obligation being "should not" rather than "must not."

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: A recruiting manager must consult the capability lead before advertising a specialist post.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage uses "should consult", not "must" — deliberate, given the same passage uses "require the approval of" and "subject to approval by" elsewhere. This tests reading "should" against "must" within the same passage, recognising a strong expectation rather than an absolute requirement.

**Question 12 (harder)**

Statement: An interim engagement of eighteen months would require the approval of the director of resources and a plan to establish permanent capability.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The first half is established (engagements beyond twelve months require the director of resources' approval); the second is not, since the capability plan is required only where the underlying need is permanent, which the statement does not establish. This tests checking each limb of a compound statement separately — one unestablished conjunct makes the whole statement Cannot Say.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes proposing from deciding when reading who holds authority in a passage.
- Note whether the candidate reads a discretionary power ("may") as defeating both an asserted certainty ("will") and an asserted impossibility ("cannot").
- Watch for whether a duty to inform or set out costs is correctly read as distinct from a power to refuse or veto.
- Score for whether a relative time reference is checked for its anchor before being accepted as established.
- Note whether the candidate checks every limb of a compound statement separately rather than accepting it as a whole once part of it is confirmed.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a discretionary reduction or action read as automatic.
- Missing when a candidate treats an obligation to set out costs as a right of veto.
- Rewarding an assumption that a client or requesting party can direct a decision explicitly allocated elsewhere.
- Accepting a probably-true but unestablished time claim marked True.
- Missing when a candidate marks a compound statement True on the strength of only one confirmed limb.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment presents realistic scenarios and asks the candidate to identify effective and ineffective responses. On the management track at principal level the scenarios involve allocation decisions that disappoint people, relationships with client functions, disagreement with senior colleagues, and the persistent gap between what a programme has promised and what it can deliver.

The commonest formats ask for the most and least effective response, or for ratings. Scoring is against a key derived from experienced practitioners at comparable seniority.

Three patterns run through the strong answers at this level. First, this role makes decisions that disappoint capable people regularly, and how that is done determines whether they keep bringing problems forward — visible reasoning matters more than the decision going their way. Second, the relationship with client functions is the mechanism through which requirements get established honestly, and a client function that has learned to inflate its case because it is discounted has stopped giving useful information. Third, this role is far enough from delivery that its picture is second-hand, and strong responses consistently include a step to check the picture rather than acting on the version that arrived.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Resource allocation** maps to managing resource allocation for solution development programmes.
- **Client relationships** maps to working with client functions to establish business requirements.
- **Strategy leadership** maps to setting programme strategy.
- **Capability development** maps to mobility, career paths, and recruitment.
- **Process and standards** map to **Development process optimisation** and setting policy.
- **Technical judgement in a management context** maps to this role's expert-level technical skills.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Declining a request**

A programme manager makes a well-argued case for two more developers; the decision has gone against it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell them the answer is no.
- B) Tell them the answer is no, what it was weighed against, and what would change it — because a decision with visible reasoning can be planned around and argued with on the merits, while a bare no invites them to try again through a different route.
- C) Tell them there is no capacity available.
- D) Defer the decision to next quarter.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The decision is the same in all four; what differs is what the programme manager can do next, and option B's "what would change it" turns a refusal into information. This tests giving visible reasoning with a decline — option C is least effective because it is usually not quite true (capacity exists and has been allocated elsewhere, and people can tell), while D avoids the conversation and costs a quarter.

**Question 2 (easy) — A client function's stated requirement**

A client function insists a system must produce a particular report in a specific, expensive format. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Build it; the client function defines requirements.
- B) Ask what the report is used for and by whom, because expensive format requirements usually come from an existing downstream process or an external obligation — and if it is an internal habit there may be a cheaper answer, while if it is a statutory return the format is genuinely fixed and the conversation is over.
- C) Propose a cheaper alternative format.
- D) Explain the cost and ask them to reconsider.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The question distinguishes two cases that look identical from outside and have opposite answers — a statutory return is immovable, while an internal convention someone has maintained for years is often negotiable and unquestioned. This tests finding out which case applies in one question — option C proposes before understanding, occasionally proposing something illegal, and D puts the cost on the client without helping them evaluate it.

**Question 3 (moderate) — A programme that will miss its commitment**

Concluding a programme will miss a publicly committed date by around four months, while its leadership believes it can recover. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept their assessment; they are closer to it.
- B) Establish what their recovery plan depends on and test those assumptions specifically — recovery plans usually rest on one or two optimistic premises — and if the assumptions do not hold, put the revised position to whoever owns the public commitment, since the cost of a late disclosure rises steeply the closer it gets to the date.
- C) Report the assessment upward immediately.
- D) Wait a month to see whether recovery materialises.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Examining the recovery plan's assumptions rather than comparing overall judgements respects the programme's position without deferring to it indefinitely, and disclosure timing matters — a four-month slip disclosed now is a planning problem, and the same slip disclosed in three months is a crisis. This tests testing assumptions before acting — option D is least effective, spending the month in which disclosure would have been cheapest, while C goes over the programme's head before checking.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two strong internal candidates**

Two strong developers apply for the same lead role; one is appointed. Ask for the most effective response to the other.

- A) Tell them it was close and encourage them to apply again.
- B) Tell them specifically what the difference was and what would make the difference next time, and follow it with something concrete — an opportunity, a piece of scope, a route into the next vacancy — because "it was close, try again" without specifics is the message that precedes a resignation.
- C) Explain that the decision was difficult.
- D) Offer them a different role.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** An unsuccessful strong internal candidate is at their most likely to leave in the following two months, and generic encouragement convinces them there is no path. This tests giving a specific answer and a concrete next step, the two things that keep people — option C is least effective, saying nothing of substance, while A is the standard response heard as a polite refusal.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A client function that has learned to inflate**

A client function consistently overstates urgency and benefits, apparently because it expects the case to be discounted. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Continue discounting; it works.
- B) Name the dynamic with them directly and change one's own side of it — commit to taking their stated position at face value and to explaining any decision that goes against them — because the inflation is a rational response to being discounted, and only the discounting party can break the loop.
- C) Ask them to be more realistic.
- D) Apply a consistent discount factor openly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is an equilibrium each side maintains by responding to the other, and it degrades the information available to allocate on; option B is the only response that changes the incentive, requiring the discounting side to move first. This tests breaking a discounting equilibrium — option A is least effective, preserving an arrangement in which neither party knows what the other means, and D formalises the arms race rather than ending it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reallocating from a struggling programme**

A programme is behind and consuming a large share of capacity; another programme with a clearer benefit case is waiting. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Move capacity to the programme with the clearer benefit case.
- B) Establish first whether the struggling programme is behind because it is under-resourced or because it is poorly scoped — removing capacity from an under-resourced programme guarantees failure while removing it from an unscoped one changes nothing — and be clear that a decision to reduce it is a decision about what it will no longer deliver.
- C) Split the difference and move some capacity.
- D) Leave it; the struggling programme needs support.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The same observable state — behind and expensive — arises from causes with opposite implications for what should happen next; option B distinguishes them and adds the honest framing that reducing resource is always a scope decision. This tests diagnosing before reallocating — option C is least effective, the fairness reflex applied to an analytical question that answers neither cause.

**Question 7 (harder) — Disagreement with a director**

A director wants a programme structured in a way believed likely to fail, for organisational rather than technical reasons. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Set out the technical objections.
- B) Engage with the organisational reasoning on its own terms — understand what the structure is meant to achieve politically or operationally, and look for a structure that achieves the same thing without the failure mode — because a technical objection to an organisationally-motivated decision does not connect, and will be heard as not understanding the constraint.
- C) Comply; the structure is their decision.
- D) Escalate above the director.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** If a structure is chosen to give a group visible ownership or fit a funding boundary, a technical argument against it addresses nothing the director cares about. This tests engaging with the actual organisational constraint and finding a structure that satisfies both — option D is least effective, escalating a disagreement not yet properly had, while A is the natural move that fails to connect.

**Question 8 (harder) — Evidence a strategy is not working**

Two years into a technical strategy, the intended consolidation has not happened and teams are diverging further. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Strengthen enforcement.
- B) Establish why the strategy is not being followed before doing anything else, since two years of divergence by capable teams usually means the compliant path is harder than the alternative — and if that is the case the remedy is to make the compliant path easier, through platform, tooling or funded migration, rather than to enforce harder.
- C) Revise the strategy to reflect current practice.
- D) Accept that consolidation is not achievable.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two years is long enough that the explanation is structural, most commonly that consolidating costs a team real time now for a benefit accruing later — no amount of enforcement changes that arithmetic for an individual team. This tests diagnosing the mechanism before enforcing — option A is least effective, escalating a losing approach and producing nominal compliance, while D concedes before diagnosing.

**Question 9 (harder) — A capable person in the wrong role**

An excellent developer promoted into a lead role is struggling and appears unhappy. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Give them more support and time.
- B) Have the conversation directly, including that returning to a senior technical role is a legitimate option rather than a failure — because the most common reason people stay in a management role that does not suit them is that stepping back looks like defeat, and the person with authority can make it not look like that.
- C) Return them to their previous role.
- D) Arrange management training.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Both plausible causes — needing support, or being in the wrong role — are addressed by the conversation, and neither is addressed by acting without it; the second half of option B (framing stepping back as legitimate) is what makes the option usable in practice. This tests offering the conversation before deciding unilaterally — option C decides for the person without it, least effective.

**Question 10 (harder) — Pressure to commit to a scope**

A client function asks for a committed scope for a twelve-month programme, where the teams' estimates carry wide uncertainty. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Commit to the scope; a programme needs a defined scope.
- B) Commit to the outcomes and the earliest deliverables precisely, and to the later scope in ranges with the uncertainty stated — and propose a review point at which the later scope firms up — because a precise twelve-month commitment made on uncertain estimates is a commitment that will be broken, and breaking it costs more than the discomfort of saying so now.
- C) Refuse to commit until the estimates improve.
- D) Commit with a contingency allowance.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Option B gives the client function the certainty genuinely available (near-term deliverables and overall outcomes) without manufacturing certainty that is not, with a review point telling them when they will know more. This tests giving honest, structured commitment rather than a precise scope known to be unreliable — option A is least effective, spending twelve months of goodwill at once, and D is the conventional answer that fails because a hidden contingency gets consumed early.

**Question 11 (harder) — A supplier relationship**

A supplier delivering a significant component is underperforming; the programme depends on them and switching would be costly. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Escalate formally with the supplier's management.
- B) Establish specifically what is failing and whether it is capability, resourcing or a misaligned incentive in the contract — and address whichever it is, since a supplier resourcing the work with junior staff because the contract pays for time is a contract problem, not a performance problem, and escalation will not fix it.
- C) Begin planning to switch suppliers.
- D) Add own people to cover the shortfall.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The three possible causes need different remedies, and a contractual incentive misalignment is both the commonest and the one escalation cannot touch. This tests diagnosing the cause before acting — option D is least effective, the instinct that gets a programme through the quarter while quietly transferring the supplier's obligation to internal teams that continue to be paid.

**Question 12 (harder) — A decision that has gone wrong**

An allocation decision made six months ago contributed to a programme's difficulties; nobody has attributed it to the person who made it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Note it privately and allocate differently in future.
- B) Say it, specifically, in whatever review is happening — because the organisation is currently learning the wrong lesson about why the programme struggled, and an allocation error that goes unnamed will be repeated by whoever allocates next.
- C) Ensure the programme gets the resource it needs now.
- D) Raise it privately with a line manager.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A review reaching the wrong conclusion about cause will produce the wrong remedy, and allocation decisions are made repeatedly by several people, so an unnamed error is highly likely to recur. This tests naming one's own contribution to a failure while a review is still forming — option A is least effective, the comfortable choice that guarantees repetition since a private resolution to do better does not transfer to anyone else.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether a response gives visible reasoning alongside a decision that disappoints the person receiving it, rather than a bare outcome.
- Note whether the candidate protects the honesty of information flowing from client functions or programmes, rather than reinforcing a dynamic that rewards inflation or optimism.
- Watch for whether the candidate diagnoses a cause (why a programme is behind, why a supplier is underperforming, why a strategy is unfollowed) before choosing a remedy.
- Score for whether the candidate names their own contribution to a past failure proactively, particularly where nobody else has yet attributed it.
- Note whether a commitment made under uncertainty is structured honestly (near-term precision, longer-term ranges with a review point) rather than either overpromising or refusing to commit at all.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a decision delivered without its reasoning or the conditions that would change it.
- Missing when a candidate would formalise or reinforce a discounting dynamic with a client function rather than breaking it.
- Rewarding a remedy chosen (reallocating resource, escalating a supplier, enforcing a strategy) without first diagnosing the underlying cause.
- Accepting a precise twelve-month commitment made on estimates known to carry wide uncertainty.
- Missing when a candidate would leave their own past contribution to a failure unnamed while a review draws the wrong conclusion.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through the gap between what is asked for and what is needed that defines principal developer work on the management track: "we need a real-time dashboard" is a solution somebody reached before speaking to anyone; a resource request without its opportunity cost is always granted; a strategy that only says yes constrains nothing; and four teams knowingly building the same capability is a rational response to reuse being expensive, not a failure of collaboration.

The numeric section was about assessing other people's arithmetic: checking the internal consistency of a business case before engaging with its conclusions, stripping out benefits that would have arrived anyway, distinguishing committed demand from a queue of requests, comparing halves of a time series rather than endpoints, and knowing that adding a quarter more people to a late programme slows it before it helps.

The verbal section was about the documents defining this role's authority and discretion: proposing is not deciding, a duty to set out costs is not a power to refuse, "may" defeats both "will" and "cannot", and a compound statement needs every limb established before it is True.

The situational judgement section was about the fact that most decisions at this level disappoint someone capable. The strong answers made reasoning visible, distinguished causes that produce identical symptoms, engaged with organisational constraints rather than arguing past them, and named the candidate's own errors so the organisation would learn the right lesson from them.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — a great deal of what makes this role work is the quality of information reaching the person in it, and that quality is set by how they respond to it; people bring accurate estimates, early bad news and honest requests to someone who has shown accuracy is not punished. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
