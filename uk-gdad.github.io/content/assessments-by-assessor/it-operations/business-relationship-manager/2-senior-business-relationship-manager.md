# Senior Business Relationship Manager - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for senior business relationship manager, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At this level, the assessments worth taking seriously are job-specific: they draw on the artefacts the role genuinely works with — investment pipelines, shared-needs analyses, relationship maturity assessments, spending review submissions, user research findings, service strategies, and the conflicting briefings of stakeholders who each believe their version of events — and on the judgements the role genuinely makes, such as deciding when a set of similar needs across departments justifies a single shared service, how to evaluate whether a current strategy still meets the business's requirements, and how to keep complex needs understood, prioritised and communicated when the organisation's attention is scarce.

A senior business relationship manager develops relationships across the organisation to understand business ambitions and ensure digital products and services support them, leads activities that improve relationship maturity, value, user experience and efficiency, explores shared needs and encourages re-use and commoditisation, ensures strategic demands such as spending reviews are supported, and partners with others to provide strategic guidance. That work is a sustained exercise in the four capabilities these assessments measure.

This document is organised into four assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why employers use it at this level, a mapping of the assessment dimensions to the specific skills in the role summary, a substantial set of items with full worked explanations for you to use as an answer key, administration notes, and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring. The items progress from easy to challenging, and the challenging ones are genuinely senior: ambiguity, competing goods, and organisational consequences are part of the design.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and the same time allowance for every candidate you assess at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Let the candidate work through each section without interruption, and note anything unusual about the conditions so your record is honest. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes this role demands, using the materials of the job rather than abstract puzzles. At senior level, the emphasis shifts from simple accuracy toward reasoning under complexity: reconciling sources that disagree, deducing consequences from layered rules, spotting the error that matters among many candidate errors, and prioritising when every item carries a stakeholder's expectations.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group — often a managerial or senior-professional one, worth knowing since the candidate is compared with peers, not the general population. Short, ungraded warm-up items usually precede the scored test.

For a senior business relationship manager, cognitive assessment is relevant because the role is, at its core, structured sense-making on the organisation's behalf. The role leads investigative work into problems and opportunities, absorbs large amounts of conflicting information to produce solutions, explores shared needs across units whose vocabularies and data rarely line up, and evaluates whether current strategies still meet business requirements. Each of those duties is a cognitive task performed in public, with conclusions carried into governance and spending decisions.

### What it measures for the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to the duty to explore shared needs, encouraging re-use, central management, sharing and commoditisation.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic thinking**: defining strategies and policies, and evaluating whether current strategies meet business requirements, requires valid reasoning from commitments and constraints to their consequences.
- **Error checking** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)** at this level: leading the collection of information means finding inconsistencies before a board finds them.
- **Reconciling conflicting information** maps to the defining line of the business analysis skill — absorb large amounts of conflicting information and use it to produce solutions.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Ownership and topic**: taking accountability for issues and proactively searching for potential problems only works if a candidate can rank a crowded field by impact, urgency and dependency.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Service focus** and **Service management framework knowledge**: taking inputs and establishing coherent frameworks that work.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across demand submissions**

Four business units submit needs to a candidate's shared-demand review. Unit A asks for "a tool to track supplier commitments and renewal dates". Unit B asks for "a contract register with alerting before expiry". Unit C asks for "a dashboard of our software licences and when they lapse". Unit D asks for "a case management system for complaints handling". As the senior lead exploring shared needs, which grouping is most defensible?

- A) All four are one shared need for a "tracking tool".
- B) A, B and C share an underlying need — visibility and alerting on time-bound commercial agreements — while D is a distinct need.
- C) Each need is distinct and should be assessed separately.
- D) B and D share a need because both involve deadlines.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Stripping each request to its underlying structure: A, B and C all reduce to the same pattern — a register of time-bound commercial agreements with forward alerting — a strong candidate for a single shared solution. D is structurally different: complaints case management is a workflow with states, parties and resolution paths, not a register with alerts. Option A over-abstracts to a level where a shared solution would serve nobody. Option C under-abstracts, forfeiting shared-service efficiency. Option D groups on a surface feature rather than underlying structure. The senior habit this item tests: abstract to the level where genuine commonality appears, and stop there.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from strategy commitments**

An organisation's technology strategy states: (i) any new business application must be cloud-hosted unless a documented exception is approved; (ii) applications handling protected data must complete a security assessment before go-live; (iii) approved exceptions are published in the exceptions register. A unit tells a candidate their new on-premises application went live last month handling protected data, and it does not appear in the exceptions register. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The application has not completed a security assessment.
- B) The cloud-hosting rule has been breached, because no exception is registered.
- C) Either an approved exception exists that has not yet been published, or the cloud-hosting rule has been breached.
- D) The application must be migrated to the cloud.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The application's absence from the register is compatible with two states: no exception exists (a breach of rule (i)), or an exception was approved and the register has not caught up. Only the disjunction in C must be true. Option B treats the register as infallibly current, an assumption rather than a fact. Option A concerns a different rule the facts do not address. Option D states a remedy no rule requires. This item tests whether a candidate checks which thing actually failed — the paperwork or the behaviour — before telling a delivery board a unit breached strategy, since the two conversations and remedies are entirely different.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a spending review table**

A draft spending review submission includes this table of a programme's costs:

| Year | Capital (£k) | Resource (£k) | Total (£k) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 800 | 400 | 1,200 |
| Year 2 | 500 | 600 | 1,100 |
| Year 3 | 200 | 700 | 900 |
| Programme total | 1,500 | 1,700 | 3,300 |

Which figure is wrong?

- A) Year 2 total
- B) The capital programme total
- C) The resource programme total
- D) The overall programme total

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Cross-footing the table: rows check out (800+400=1,200; 500+600=1,100; 200+700=900), columns check out (capital 1,500; resource 1,700). The corner, checked two independent ways — summing year totals (1,200+1,100+900=3,200) and summing column totals (1,500+1,700=3,200) — both give 3,200, but the table prints 3,300. The overall total is wrong. This item rewards cross-footing every summary table before it is sent onward and computing the corner by two routes, since agreement of two independent paths is what turns "looks right" into "is right" — and a spending review team will find a £100k discrepancy in minutes.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a senior week**

Monday morning. Five items compete for a candidate's attention:

1. The spending review team needs the candidate's directorate's efficiency narrative by Thursday; a draft exists but two unit heads have not yet confirmed their figures.
2. A relationship maturity workshop led by the candidate with a struggling unit is scheduled for Wednesday; materials are ready, but the unit head hinted Friday that "some of my team think this is a box-ticking exercise".
3. A supplier's monthly report, received this morning, shows a service supporting casework slipped below its availability target for the second consecutive month.
4. A user researcher has shared findings suggesting a flagship internal product is being widely worked around; she asks when the candidate can discuss them.
5. The candidate's team's junior BRM asks for an hour to prepare for her first solo engagement with a director on Friday.

Which sequencing is most defensible for Monday itself?

- A) Chase the two unit heads (1), then engage the supplier issue (3), book the researcher discussion (4), coach the junior (5), and address the workshop scepticism (2) on Tuesday.
- B) Deal with the supplier issue (3) first and fully, then the spending review (1), leaving the rest to later in the week.
- C) Start with the workshop scepticism (2) since Wednesday is closest, then the spending review (1), then the rest.
- D) Coach the junior (5) first as people come first, then handle items in deadline order.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1's constraint is other people's time — unit heads need days, not hours, so the chase must launch Monday. Item 3, a second consecutive breach on a casework-supporting service, warrants proactive engagement Monday under **Ownership and topic**'s proactive clause. Item 4 needs only a booking today, but a signal about a widely worked-around flagship product deserves a fast, visible response. Item 5 needs an hour before Friday, best committed Monday to protect preparation time. Item 2's scepticism is best addressed Tuesday with a considered call. Option B lets one issue consume the day while the spending review clock runs; option C treats calendar proximity as priority; option D honours a value while ignoring the week's structure. This item rewards launching dependencies, engaging emerging risks, booking what needs booking, and protecting development time.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Reconciling conflicting information**

A candidate is investigating why adoption of a shared document platform has stalled. Three sources conflict. The platform team's telemetry shows 3,100 weekly active users, up 5% this quarter. The intranet survey (420 responses, self-selected) shows 61% of respondents "rarely or never" use the platform. Two unit heads tell the candidate separately that "everyone has gone back to email attachments". Which reading best reconciles the evidence?

- A) The telemetry is correct and the survey and unit heads are wrong; adoption is growing.
- B) The survey is correct and the telemetry must be miscounting.
- C) The sources may all be accurate about different things: telemetry counts any weekly activity, however shallow; the self-selected survey over-represents the dissatisfied; and the unit heads report their own units. The next step is to segment telemetry by unit and depth of use.
- D) With sources in conflict, no conclusion is possible until a new, independent study is commissioned.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is the defining senior business analysis move: absorbing conflicting information to produce a solution rather than crowning one source. Each source has a scope and a bias, and C names them — telemetry measures behaviour bluntly, the self-selected survey over-represents the dissatisfied, the unit heads offer vivid but local evidence. These are different instruments pointed at different parts of the same phenomenon, and C's proposed segmentation would either reconcile them or expose a real measurement fault. Options A and B each commit source-picking in opposite directions. Option D is a failure of nerve, avoiding analysis at the cost of months. The right answer usually honours all the evidence by finding the frame in which it fits together.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction through the ITIL life cycle**

A service management framework distinguishes: an *incident* (unplanned interruption or degradation), a *problem* (underlying cause of one or more incidents), and a *change* (addition, modification or removal of anything that could affect services). Rules: (i) recurring incidents with a common suspected cause must be linked to a problem record; (ii) a workaround documented on a problem record may be applied to incidents without a change request; (iii) a permanent fix that modifies a production service requires an approved change. The document platform has failed the same way five times this month; a script that restarts the failed component restores service in minutes; the vendor has issued a patch said to remove the fault entirely. Which statement is correct?

- A) The restart script and the vendor patch both require approved change requests.
- B) Five similar incidents should be linked to a problem record; the restart script can be documented as a workaround and applied without a change request; applying the vendor patch requires an approved change.
- C) Once the patch is available, the incidents can be closed without a problem record.
- D) The restart script is a permanent fix and should be recorded as a change.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Five same-signature failures require rule (i)'s problem record. The restart script restores service without modifying it — a workaround, permitted under rule (ii) without a change request, so option A over-controls and option D miscategorises. The vendor patch modifies the production service permanently, triggering rule (iii). Option C inverts the logic — the patch's availability makes the problem record more important, since it tracks the fix through change and confirms recurrence stops. This item tests **Service management framework knowledge**: routing real events through the right process so workarounds keep users working, problems get root causes, and changes get proportionate scrutiny.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits logic chain**

A unit's investment case, being quality-assured by a candidate before it reaches the board, argues: "Automating invoice matching will remove 4,000 hours of manual checking annually (Enabler). This releases two full-time posts (Intermediate benefit). Headcount reduction of two posts saves £90,000 per year (End benefit), which funds the system's £70,000 annual cost with £20,000 net saving (Financial case)." The unit's own workforce plan, attached as an annex, states that the two affected staff will be redeployed to clear the invoice dispute backlog. Which is the critical inconsistency?

- A) 4,000 hours does not equal two full-time posts.
- B) The case claims a cashable £90,000 headcount saving while the annex commits to redeploying both posts, so the saving is capacity released, not cash — and the financial case as stated collapses.
- C) £70,000 annual cost is too high for an invoice matching tool.
- D) Invoice disputes should be automated before invoice matching.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A fails as a criticism — 4,000 hours is a defensible conversion to roughly two posts. Options C and D import opinions from outside the document. The genuine defect is B: the financial case depends on £90,000 being cashable, while the annex commits both posts to redeployment, meaning the paybill is unchanged. The case must choose its story — cash the posts or redeploy them — and claiming both is a double-count spending review scrutiny exists to catch. This item tests the senior QA habit of checking links within a case against its own annexes.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Multi-constraint engagement scheduling**

A candidate must run four sessions in one working week (Monday–Friday, at most one session per day): a spending review options briefing (S) for the executive committee, a shared-needs workshop (W) with four unit heads, a supplier escalation meeting (E), and a user research playback (P). Constraints: (i) the executive committee meets only on Tuesday, and S must be presented there; (ii) the spending review options pack — pre-reading for W — is finalised the day after S, and the unit heads need one full working day with the pack before W takes place; (iii) the supplier's account director is available Monday and Thursday only; (iv) the research playback must be held on the day immediately before W, because its findings feed directly into the workshop. On which days must W and E fall?

- A) W Thursday, E Monday
- B) W Friday, E Monday
- C) W Thursday, E Thursday — impossible, so the week cannot work
- D) W Friday, E Thursday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** S is pinned to Tuesday. The pack is finalised Wednesday, and the unit heads need one full working day with it (Thursday) before W, so W cannot happen before Friday — eliminating A and C. P falls the day immediately before W, so Thursday. E, allowed Monday or Thursday, must be Monday since Thursday now holds P. The unique schedule is E Monday, S Tuesday, P Thursday, W Friday — option B. This item rewards anchoring the immovable constraint first, propagating each dependency forward, and letting eliminations choose among survivors — and it tests whether a candidate silently drops a buffer constraint, the ones most often dropped under time pressure.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Evaluating a strategy against the evidence**

Two years ago a directorate's engagement strategy set three commitments: (a) every business unit has a named senior relationship contact; (b) relationship maturity, self-assessed annually on a 1–5 scale, improves in at least 80% of units year on year; (c) digital investment decisions cite business-unit input in 100% of cases. Current evidence: (a) is met — all 14 units have named contacts; (b) shows 9 of 14 units improved (64%), 3 static, 2 declined — both decliners citing "constant contact churn"; (c) audit shows 11 of 12 decisions cited unit input, and the twelfth was an emergency security patch decision taken in hours. As the senior evaluating whether the strategy meets business requirements, what is the most defensible assessment?

- A) The strategy has failed: two of three commitments are unmet.
- B) The strategy is broadly succeeding: (a) is met, (c)'s single miss is a defensible emergency, and (b)'s 64% against an 80% target is the real shortfall — with contact churn implicated, so the refresh should target continuity of relationships, and the emergency pathway in (c) should be formalised rather than counted as failure.
- C) The strategy has succeeded: 64% improvement still means most units improved.
- D) The self-assessed maturity data is too subjective to evaluate, so commitment (b) should be dropped.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Evaluating strategies means neither prosecuting (A) nor defending (C) but reading each commitment against its evidence. Commitment (a) is cleanly met. Commitment (c)'s miss is an hours-scale emergency the strategy never designed a pathway for — the mature response is formalising one, not recording failure. Commitment (b) is the substantive shortfall, and both declining units citing contact churn points the refresh at relationship continuity, not more engagement volume. Option D mistakes imperfect data for useless data — two units independently citing the same cause is signal. The senior pattern this item tests: evaluation's output is a targeted set of adjustments, each traceable to evidence.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Root cause through conflicting process accounts**

Complaints about slow onboarding of new starters have doubled. A candidate leads the investigation. HR says: "We process every request within our 3-day SLA — the delays are IT's." IT says: "We complete account creation within 2 days of a valid request — half the requests arrive incomplete and bounce back." Line managers say: "We submit the form weeks early, then hear nothing until the starter arrives without a laptop." The candidate pulls twenty recent cases: median elapsed time from form submission to working equipment is 19 days; the form passes through an HR validation queue (median 3 days), bounces back to managers for corrections in 9 of 20 cases (median 5 days lost), then IT (median 2 days), then equipment dispatch (median 6 days, driven by a stock shortage). Which conclusion do the data best support?

- A) HR is the bottleneck and their SLA should be tightened.
- B) Line managers are at fault for submitting incomplete forms.
- C) Every party's account is locally true; the systemic causes are a form that fails validation 45% of the time and an equipment stock shortage — the rework loop and dispatch delay, invisible in each team's own SLA, consume most of the elapsed time.
- D) The 19-day median is acceptable because each team meets its own SLA.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Every account is true and incomplete — the signature of a process problem hiding between teams. The end-to-end data shows validation (3 days) plus a 45% rework loop (median 5 days) plus IT (2 days) plus dispatch (6 days, stock-constrained) accounts for most of the elapsed time and all of the variance — none of it appearing in any single SLA. Option A tightens a queue that is not the constraint. Option B moralises a design defect — a form failing 45% of the time needs redesigning, not blaming its users, which is the opposite of **User focus**. Option D is the most instructive wrong answer: locally green SLAs with users waiting three weeks is precisely what **Service focus**'s coherent frameworks exist to reject. This item rewards moving from three conflicting accounts to one instrumented journey.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Second-order consequences of a well-meant rule**

To improve engagement consistency, a neighbouring directorate introduced a rule: all business-unit requests to digital teams must be logged through the front-door portal, and un-logged work is not resourced. Six months on: logged demand is up 60%; the backlog has tripled; unit heads praise the transparency; delivery teams report that "urgent small fixes now take six weeks"; and two units have hired their own contractors for quick changes, outside governance. A candidate's directorate is considering adopting the same rule. What is the most defensible advice?

- A) Adopt the rule unchanged: transparency has improved and demand is now visible, which is the point.
- B) Reject the rule: it triples backlogs and drives shadow IT.
- C) Adopt the front-door principle, but add what the neighbouring design lacks: a triaged fast lane for small changes with a service-level commitment, capacity planning that responds to the newly visible demand, and monitoring for shadow-IT symptoms — because the observed harms flow from an unsegmented queue, not from visibility itself.
- D) Pilot the rule in one unit and decide in a year.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separating the mechanism from the side effects: visibility (benefits) and an undifferentiated single queue for everything from strategic programmes to ten-minute fixes (harms) are two distinct design choices bundled into one rule. Options A and B each treat the two-part design as one indivisible thing. Option D buys a year's delay to learn what the neighbouring directorate's six months already teach. Option C redesigns rather than adopts-or-rejects — keeping visibility while segmenting the queue and treating demand data as capacity-planning input. This item tests whether a candidate designs frameworks that account for how people will behave when the framework inconveniences them, since the shadow IT is the system routing around a blockage.

### Administration tips

- **Watch for source-picking specifically in reconciliation items** (Questions 5 and 10) — a candidate who crowns one source and dismisses the rest is showing a real weakness for this role.
- **Score multi-constraint items (Question 8) for method** — anchoring the fixed element and propagating dependencies is the reliable technique, and a candidate's approach is as informative as their answer.
- **Note whether a candidate stops checking after the first defensible error** (Question 7) — senior error-checking items often bury the real inconsistency behind a plausible near-error.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.
- **Use the strategy-evaluation item (Question 9) as debrief material** — asking a candidate how they would actually word the refresh recommendation often reveals more than the multiple-choice answer.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding verdict-first evaluation** — reading evidence with a pass/fail frame discards the diagnostic texture a targeted adjustment needs.
- **Rewarding local-optimum reasoning** — fixing one team's metric while the end-to-end journey stays broken.
- **Under-weighting a candidate's willingness to name a systemic cause** over blaming an individual team or user group.
- **Missing over-abstraction and under-abstraction** in pattern-grouping items.
- **Letting speed abolish the second read** — the discriminating detail in hard items is nearly always in a clause skimmed too fast.
- **Rewarding pilots or deferrals** where the evidence to decide already exists.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures a candidate's ability to interpret numerical information accurately, select and execute the right calculations, and — increasingly at senior level — judge what a figure legitimately supports once computed. Calculators are usually permitted; the test is of reasoning, not arithmetic recall.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes built around tables, charts and short data-rich passages, each with two to four multiple-choice questions. Senior-normed versions weight the harder skills: multi-step calculations, weighted figures, compound growth, cost models mixing one-off and recurring elements, and interpretive judgement questions where every option is a calculation but only one is the right calculation for the decision at hand.

For a senior business relationship manager, numbers are the medium of the most consequential conversations. Spending reviews are numeric; the value cases behind re-use and commoditisation are numeric; relationship maturity, user experience and efficiency are all tracked through measures someone must read intelligently, challenge when flattering, and defend when true. Because the role leads investigations and QAs others' recommendations, a senior candidate is often the last line of defence between an optimistic benefits model and a board that will fund it.

### What it measures for the role

- **Multi-step cost and benefit modelling** maps to the duty to ensure strategic demands, such as spending reviews, are supported.
- **Weighted averages and normalised rates** map to leading activities that improve relationship maturity, value, user experience and efficiency.
- **Compound growth and trend projection** map to **Strategic thinking**.
- **Unit economics and comparison of options** map to exploring shared needs and commoditisation.
- **Interpreting survey and research data** maps to **User focus**.
- **Reading complex tables under pressure** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of demand by category**

A directorate logged 240 demand items this year: 96 were small changes, 84 were enhancements, and the rest were new-service requests. What percentage of demand items were new-service requests?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 35%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** New-service count: 240 − 96 − 84 = 60. Percentage: 60 ÷ 240 = 25%. Checking that category percentages sum to 100% (40% + 35% + 25%) catches slips — demand reports where categories sum to 108% or 92% are more common than they should be, and a candidate who checks this reflexively is showing real reporting discipline.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a maturity assessment table**

Relationship maturity self-assessments (1 = ad hoc, 5 = strategic partner) across a candidate's portfolio:

| Unit | Last year | This year |
|---|---|---|
| Operations | 2 | 3 |
| Finance | 3 | 3 |
| HR | 2 | 4 |
| Policy | 4 | 3 |
| Commercial | 3 | 4 |

How many units improved, and what is this year's mean score across the five units?

- A) 3 improved; mean 3.4
- B) 2 improved; mean 3.4
- C) 3 improved; mean 3.2
- D) 4 improved; mean 3.6

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Three units improved (Operations, HR, Commercial); Finance was static; Policy declined. Mean: (3+3+4+3+4) ÷ 5 = 3.4. "Improved" means strictly greater, an error option B anticipates. The mean alone hides the story most needed: Policy declined from the portfolio's best score, and a candidate who reads the movement table rather than just the average is showing the judgement this role needs.

**Question 3 (easy) — Ratio in a shared-service case**

Four units each run their own survey tool, at annual licence costs of £9,000, £7,500, £12,000 and £7,500. A single shared tool with capacity for all four would cost £24,000 per year. What is the ratio of current combined spend to the shared alternative, in simplest form?

- A) 2:1
- B) 3:2
- C) 4:3
- D) 5:4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combined current spend: £36,000. Ratio to shared cost (£24,000): 36,000:24,000, simplifying to 3:2. A candidate who also notes what the ratio does not settle — migration effort, differing renewal dates, whether the shared tool meets all four units' needs — is showing the judgement that opens rather than closes the commoditisation conversation.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Compound demand growth against capacity**

Demand for a directorate's delivery capacity is 1,000 story points per quarter and growing at 8% per quarter, compounding. Capacity is fixed at 1,150 points per quarter. In which quarter, counting from now (quarter 0), does demand first exceed capacity?

- A) Quarter 1
- B) Quarter 2
- C) Quarter 3
- D) Quarter 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Quarter 1: 1,000 × 1.08 = 1,080 (under). Quarter 2: 1,080 × 1.08 = 1,166.4 (over). The linear error — 8% of 1,000 is 80, so "150 ÷ 80 ≈ 2 quarters, breach in quarter 3" — misses that compounding grows the base each quarter, pulling the breach earlier. This item tests whether a candidate compounds forward step by step rather than reasoning linearly, since the same error scales badly over a spending review horizon.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted user satisfaction across channels**

A service championed by a candidate is used through three channels. This quarter's satisfaction ("satisfied or very satisfied") by channel: web 82% of 5,400 users; phone 64% of 1,800 users; face-to-face 90% of 800 users. What is the overall satisfaction rate across all users, to the nearest percentage point?

- A) 79%
- B) 78%
- C) 80%
- D) 76%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Satisfied users: web 4,428, phone 1,152, face-to-face 720, total 6,300 of 8,000 = 78.75% ≈ 79%. The unweighted mean happens to land close here, a coincidence of these numbers rather than a licence for the shortcut. The figure a strong candidate extracts is not the average at all: phone, the second-largest channel, sits well below the others, and that gap is where improvement leverage lives — refusing to let a healthy overall figure bury an unhealthy channel is what **User focus** requires.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Spending review options arithmetic**

A candidate is shaping three options for a spending review line currently costing £2.4 million per year:

- Option 1: flat cash — hold spend at £2.4m per year for three years.
- Option 2: hold year 1 at £2.4m, then apply a 5% cash reduction in each of years 2 and 3, each cut applied to the previous year's figure.
- Option 3: invest £300k extra in year 1 (total £2.7m), then reduce to £2.2m in years 2 and 3.

Which option has the lowest total cost over the three years, and by how much versus flat cash?

- A) Option 2; about £354k less than flat cash
- B) Option 2; about £360k less than flat cash
- C) Option 3; £100k less than flat cash
- D) Option 3; £200k less than flat cash

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option 1 totals £7.2m. Option 2 compounds from year 2: 2.4 + 2.28 + 2.166 = £6.846m, saving £354k. Option 3 totals £7.1m, saving £100k. Option 2 wins. Option B's £360k treats both cuts as flat percentages of the original baseline rather than compounding — a small-looking slip that scales with budget size. This item rewards pinning "5% per year" to a stated baseline and start year, and noting what the arithmetic does not decide: Option 3's extra £254k buys a year-1 investment whose value beyond year 3 is the real strategic question.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Response rates and representativeness**

An annual stakeholder survey went to 1,600 staff across four units. Responses: Unit A 180 of 400; Unit B 90 of 600; Unit C 150 of 300; Unit D 120 of 300. Overall satisfaction among respondents is 71%. Which statement should most temper how that 71% is reported?

- A) The overall response rate is only 540 ÷ 1,600 ≈ 34%, so the survey is worthless.
- B) Unit B — the largest unit — responded at only 15%, versus 45–50% elsewhere, so the overall figure under-represents the biggest stakeholder population and may not survive reweighting.
- C) Unit C's 50% response rate means its views are over-counted, so its responses should be discarded.
- D) 71% is above the 70% target, so no caveat is needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unit B holds 37.5% of the population but only 16.7% of responses — the largest unit speaks with less than half its proportional voice. If Unit B's non-respondents lean less satisfied, as low response often correlates with, the true figure sits below 71%. Option A dismisses rather than qualifies. Option C proposes discarding good data instead of weighting it. Option D reads a number against a target while ignoring what it represents. This item tests the stewardship of pre-empting "and who didn't answer?" before a board member asks it.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Unit economics of consolidation**

Six units each run local reporting tools. Annual costs: four small units pay £15,000 each; two large units pay £40,000 each. A central platform would cost £120,000 per year flat, plus £5,000 per unit onboarding (one-off), and would need a £60,000 one-off migration project. Over three years, what is the net saving (or cost) of consolidation versus the status quo?

- A) Net saving of £90,000
- B) Net saving of £150,000
- C) Net cost of £30,000
- D) Net saving of £60,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Status quo over three years: £420,000. Consolidated: £360,000 platform + £30,000 onboarding + £60,000 migration = £450,000 — a net cost of £30,000 over the window. But the steady-state economics favour consolidation: £140,000 falls to £120,000 annually, an ongoing £20,000 saving, and the £90,000 one-off investment pays back in 4.5 years. A three-year window shows a net cost for a change with a clearly better year-4-onwards position. This item tests whether a candidate declares the appraisal window explicitly, since the choice of window can make the same change look good or bad.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Percentage points, baselines and honest reporting**

A supplier's service credit regime activates if availability falls below 99.0% in a quarter. The service recorded: Q1 99.4%, Q2 99.1%, Q3 98.8%, Q4 99.3%. The supplier's annual report states: "Average availability of 99.15% comfortably exceeded the 99.0% threshold; availability varied by no more than 0.6 percentage points across the year." Which assessment is correct?

- A) The supplier's statement is accurate and complete; no credits are due.
- B) Both quoted figures are arithmetically correct, but the framing conceals that Q3 breached the quarterly threshold — the regime applies per quarter, so a credit is due for Q3 regardless of the annual average.
- C) The average is miscalculated, which invalidates the report.
- D) A 0.6 percentage point range is too large for any threshold regime, so credits are due for the whole year.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic checks out (mean 99.15%, range 0.6 points), ruling out C. But the credit regime is defined per quarter, and Q3's 98.8% sits below threshold, triggering a credit the annual averaging is constructed to bury. Option A accepts the frame the supplier chose. Option D invents a rule the regime does not contain. This item tests baseline and aggregation vigilance — checking that a counterparty's reporting period matches the regime's, since a credit unclaimed in the quarter it arose is, in most contracts, waived.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Efficiency claims under scrutiny**

A unit claims its process improvement "increased efficiency by 40%". Investigating, a candidate finds: last year the team of 10 processed 20,000 cases; this year the team of 8 processed 22,400 cases. Which statement most accurately characterises the efficiency change?

- A) The claim is understated: cases per person rose from 2,000 to 2,800, a 40% productivity increase, and total output also rose 12% with 20% less staff.
- B) The claim is correct as stated and needs no qualification.
- C) The claim is overstated: output rose only 12%.
- D) Efficiency cannot be measured because staff numbers changed.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Per-person rate: 2,000 to 2,800, a 40% rise — the claim is exactly right on productivity, and total output also rose 12% with 20% fewer staff. Option C compares raw output while ignoring the denominator change. Option D gives up on the normalisation that makes changed circumstances comparable. A strong candidate would add a caveat worth noting: a 40% per-person jump in one year invites questions about automation, case-mix change, or quality trade-offs, before this claim headlines an efficiency narrative.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Marginal thinking in a demand decision**

A shared platform team has capacity for 30 onboarding projects this year and 34 units want onboarding. Each onboarding delivers an average £25,000 annual saving to the joining unit. A director proposes hiring two contractors (£90,000 total, one year) to add 6 more onboarding slots. Assuming the savings estimates hold and onboarded units keep saving every year, which analysis best supports a decision?

- A) Reject: £90,000 for 6 slots is £15,000 per slot, and slots are worth only £25,000 once.
- B) Approve: the contractors enable at most 4 additional real onboardings (34 − 30 = 4 units actually waiting), yielding 4 × £25,000 = £100,000 of *recurring* annual savings for a one-off £90,000 — payback inside year 1 and pure saving thereafter; the two surplus slots have no demand and should not be bought, so negotiate the contractor cost toward 4 slots.
- C) Approve as proposed: 6 slots × £25,000 = £150,000 exceeds £90,000.
- D) Reject: contractors cost more per head than staff, so the work should wait for next year's capacity.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The genuine shortfall is 4 units, not 6 slots — option C values slots nobody needs. The 4 real slots deliver £100,000 recurring annually, already exceeding the £90,000 one-off cost in year 1 alone. Option A prices per nominal slot and treats a recurring saving as one-off. Option D introduces an irrelevant cost comparison and costs the organisation a year of recurring savings. This item tests marginal thinking — pricing what the spend actually buys — and rewards converting the analysis into a negotiating position rather than accepting the proposal as framed.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reading a two-way table on investment outcomes**

A review of 80 completed digital investments cross-tabulates whether business units were engaged through a relationship manager during shaping, and whether the investment met its benefits targets:

| | Met benefits target | Missed benefits target | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| BRM-engaged during shaping | 33 | 12 | 45 |
| Not BRM-engaged | 14 | 21 | 35 |
| Total | 47 | 33 | 80 |

A head of function wants to tell the executive committee: "BRM engagement more than doubles the success rate — this proves we should mandate it." Which is the most defensible version of the claim?

- A) The claim is fine as drafted: 33 successes versus 14 speaks for itself.
- B) BRM-engaged investments met their targets at 73% versus 40% for non-engaged — a strong association that supports expanding engagement, but not proof of causation: bigger, better-run, or better-sponsored initiatives may both seek BRM engagement and succeed for other reasons. Recommend expansion alongside a design that tests the effect — for example, tracking outcomes as engagement rolls out by cohort.
- C) The success rates are 73% versus 40%, which is less than double, so the claim is false and the data should not be presented.
- D) The sample of 80 is too small to say anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rates are 73.3% versus 40%, a substantial association just short of "more than doubles" — the draft overstates arithmetically and logically, since "proves" converts an observational cross-tab into a causal experiment. Option A repeats raw counts whose comparison is meaningless across different group sizes. Option C over-corrects by suppressing a genuinely favourable finding. Option D dismisses 80 outcomes with a large gap. This item tests fixing the arithmetic, keeping the legitimate strength of the finding, and proposing a design that would convert association toward evidence.

### Administration tips

- **Watch for the linear-versus-compound distinction** (Question 4) — a candidate who compounds forward step by step is showing genuine numerical maturity for this role.
- **Score the appraisal-window item (Question 8) for whether a candidate states the window explicitly**, since the choice of window changes the answer's honesty, not just its number.
- **Note whether a candidate distinguishes association from causation** in the two-way table item (Question 12) — this recurs constantly in real senior reporting.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.
- **Use the explanations to debrief on real spending-review and benefits-case practice.**

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Linear thinking about compound processes.**
- **Averaging away the story** — overall means routinely conceal the failing channel, quarter, or unit the decision actually turns on.
- **Accepting a counterparty's aggregation** without re-checking it against the regime's own period.
- **Counting capacity as value** rather than pricing the marginal used unit.
- **Mixing cashable and capacity benefits.**
- **Treating association as causation, or dismissing it as nothing** — both fail the sharpest-person-in-the-room test.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely a candidate understands, evaluates and acts on written information — the medium through which nearly all of this role's influence travels. Short passages of the kind the role handles daily — strategy commitments, governance minutes, supplier assurances, research summaries, escalation emails — are followed by items asking what each one guarantees, contradicts, or leaves open. The classic format is True / False / Cannot say, joined at senior level by inference questions, argument-evaluation questions, and clear-writing judgement.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes, with passages of 100–250 words carrying two to four questions each. The governing rule is strict: answer only from the passage. Senior-normed tests add subtlety rather than vocabulary — wrong answers are not silly but almost right, differing from the passage by one scope word, one modal verb, or one quietly substituted concept.

Why does this matter for the role? A candidate at this level ensures complex needs and challenges are understood, prioritised and communicated — and communication at this level is load-bearing: a sentence written into a spending review narrative will be quoted back at settlement, and the assurance accepted from a supplier defines what can later be enforced. Seniors are the organisation's translators, and translation errors at altitude propagate.

### What it measures for the role

- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**: recording what sources actually establish.
- **Reading commitments and conditions precisely** maps to the duty to ensure strategic demands, such as spending reviews, are supported.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: locating the load-bearing flaw in a persuasive case.
- **Distinguishing needs from desires in written evidence** maps to **User focus**.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Strategic thinking** and the duty to communicate complex challenges to relevant audiences.
- **Reading between the lines with restraint** maps to relationship leadership — noticing what a carefully drafted email avoids committing to, without inventing meanings it never carried.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot say on a strategy commitment**

Passage: "By the end of the spending review period, the department will reduce its application estate from 420 to no more than 300 applications. Reductions will prioritise applications with fewer than 50 active users. No application supporting a statutory service will be retired without ministerial agreement to a replacement plan."

Statement: "The department has committed to retiring at least 120 applications by the end of the spending review period."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is right, but check the verb: the passage commits to *reducing* the estate, while the statement claims *retiring* applications specifically. An estate can shrink by merger or consolidation as well as retirement, and the passage's own third sentence shows "retired" is a specific mechanism with its own safeguards. Cannot say. This one-word substitution is exactly how strategy commitments get misquoted, with real consequences for a candidate briefing a unit head.

**Question 2 (easy) — False versus Cannot say on a supplier assurance**

Passage (supplier email): "We can confirm that the March incident did not result in any loss of customer data. Our investigation found the root cause to be a failed storage controller, which has been replaced. We are reviewing whether similar controllers elsewhere in the estate require proactive replacement and will report by the end of the month."

Statement: "The supplier has confirmed that no similar controllers elsewhere in the estate require replacement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the supplier is reviewing that exact question, an explicitly open question that cannot simultaneously be a confirmed conclusion — contradicted, False, not Cannot say, since the passage speaks directly to the wider estate. This is the reading that protects a candidate in assurance chains: "they confirmed no data loss" is quotable; "they confirmed the estate is safe" becomes the candidate's own assurance the moment it is forwarded upward.

**Question 3 (easy) — Main point of a governance paper**

Passage: "Attendance at the design authority has averaged 40% for programme-level items this year, and three decisions have been remade within a quarter after affected units said they were not consulted. The authority's terms of reference date from 2019, before the current federated delivery model. Units now make many technology choices locally under delegated budgets. The secretariat proposes that, rather than enforcing attendance, the authority's scope and membership be redesigned around the decisions that genuinely require central agreement."

Which sentence best states the paper's main point?

- A) Design authority attendance must be enforced more strictly.
- B) The design authority's governance model predates the current operating model, and the proposal is to redesign its scope and membership rather than police attendance.
- C) Units should lose their delegated technology budgets.
- D) The design authority should be abolished.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The paper's structure is symptom (low attendance, remade decisions), diagnosis (2019 terms versus a federated model), proposal (redesign scope and membership). B captures diagnosis and proposal. Option A is the position the paper argues against. Option C reverses the paper's direction. Option D overshoots — redesign is not abolition. Main-point items at senior level reward reading for argument architecture rather than topic.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Needs versus desires in research evidence**

Passage (research summary): "In interviews, 14 of 18 caseworkers asked for the new system to include a built-in chat function 'like the one sales teams use'. Observation sessions showed that caseworkers currently interrupt casework an average of nine times daily to seek colleague input on edge cases, using a mixture of walking over, phoning, and emailing, with a median wait of 25 minutes for email responses. Interviewees consistently linked their chat request to these delays."

Which statement best distinguishes the user need from the expressed desire?

- A) The need is a chat function; 14 of 18 users requested it, which is conclusive.
- B) The underlying need is rapid access to colleague expertise during casework; built-in chat is one candidate solution, whose fit should be tested against alternatives such as a searchable decisions log or a duty-expert rota.
- C) There is no real need, since caseworkers already have three ways to reach colleagues.
- D) The need is to reduce interruptions, so colleague contact should be restricted.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** **User focus** at this level explicitly includes explaining the difference between user needs and user desires. The desire is solution-shaped (chat, borrowed from another team); the need is what the observational data reveals — frequent interruptions with painful latency. B holds that structure and keeps the solution space open. Option A converts vote-counting into a requirement. Option C mistakes the existence of channels for their adequacy. Option D optimises a metric against the users' actual goal. This item rewards honouring the desire as data while naming the need behind it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from a settlement letter**

Passage (extract): "The department's settlement provides £46m for digital and technology over the period, of which £8m is ring-fenced for cyber security improvements. Continued access to the £12m transformation tranche in years 2 and 3 is conditional on the department demonstrating, at the year 1 review point, benefits realisation of at least 60% against the year 1 profile. HM Treasury notes the department's ambition on legacy remediation and expects a costed plan by October."

Which inference is best supported?

- A) If year 1 benefits realisation reaches only 50% of profile, the department loses the entire £46m settlement.
- B) The department can spend the £8m cyber ring-fence on legacy remediation if the October plan justifies it.
- C) At most £38m of the settlement is freely allocable by the department, and £12m of that is at risk if the year 1 review shows benefits realisation below 60% of profile.
- D) HM Treasury has approved the department's legacy remediation plan.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The freely allocable maximum is £46m − £8m = £38m; within that, £12m is explicitly conditional on the 60% threshold. C states both carefully. Option A catastrophises — the condition attaches to £12m, not the whole settlement. Option B contradicts the meaning of a ring-fence. Option D over-reads a courteous acknowledgement as approval. This item tests whether a candidate knows, to the pound and the condition, what a settlement text actually says — since a unit head's planning assumption will be built on the answer.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Argument evaluation: the resistance narrative**

Passage (email from a unit head to a candidate's director, copied to the candidate): "We oppose migrating to the shared HR platform. Our current system was configured for our statutory casework and staff know it well. The shared platform failed its first go-live in the pensions unit last year, which proves it cannot handle complex requirements. Moreover, our unit's engagement survey shows staff morale is already low; a forced migration now would make us the least efficient unit in the department."

Which is the strongest criticism of the argument?

- A) The email wrongly claims staff know the current system well.
- B) A single failed first go-live, later context unknown, cannot "prove" general incapacity — and the morale claim assumes without evidence that migration causes the predicted efficiency collapse; the legitimate points (statutory configuration, familiarity, timing) are reasons to shape the migration, not shown reasons to cancel it.
- C) Unit heads should not email directors directly.
- D) Engagement surveys are not valid evidence of morale.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separating legitimate observations from logical over-reaches: statutory configuration, familiarity and timing are real considerations; "proves it cannot handle complex requirements" is a hasty generalisation from one data point, and the morale prediction is unevidenced. B names both flaws while preserving the reasonable core, making it the strongest criticism rather than merely the harshest. Options C and D attack the messenger and the instrument. This item rewards engaging the strongest version of the other side's case.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say on relationship maturity reporting**

Passage: "The annual partnering report records that 11 of 14 business units now have engagement plans agreed with their senior relationship manager, compared with 6 last year. Units with agreed plans raised 84% of this year's demand through the front-door process, compared with 51% for units without plans. The report cautions that units which agreed plans early tended to be those with the strongest prior relationships."

Statement: "Agreeing an engagement plan causes units to use the front-door process more."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and supplies its own confound: units that agreed plans early were those with the strongest prior relationships, which could independently drive both. The causal statement is neither guaranteed nor contradicted — Cannot say. This item rewards noticing that the passage's final sentence is the author pre-empting the causal over-read, the same discipline a candidate should apply when drafting their own maturity reporting.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement for a mixed audience**

A candidate must send one message to all unit heads about a change to demand submission. Which version is best?

- A) "Pursuant to the outcome of the Q2 governance review, the demand intake mechanism will transition to an enhanced triage paradigm, with concomitant adjustments to submission modalities, effective imminently."
- B) "From 1 October, submit all demand through the new front-door form (link below). Items already in the pipeline are unaffected. Small changes will get a decision within 5 working days; larger items go to the monthly board as now. Questions: contact your relationship manager."
- C) "The demand process is changing soon. More details will follow in due course. Please bear with us while we work through the implications."
- D) "As you may be aware, following extensive consultation, and in line with best practice across government, we have been considering how best to optimise our demand management approach, and after careful reflection have concluded that changes are appropriate, which this note summarises at a high level."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B answers what changes, when, what to do, what happens to in-flight items, what improves, and where to ask, in four short sentences. Option A is jargon that conceals every operative fact. Option C creates uncertainty with zero capability to act. Option D spends fifty words clearing its throat. This item tests whether a candidate judges communication by what the audience can do after reading, not by how professional it sounds.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Reading a conditional commitment chain**

Passage (minute of the investment committee): "The committee approved the citizen portal business case, subject to: (a) confirmation of GDS service assessment booking before development spend begins; (b) benefits being re-baselined once the discovery completes, with the case returning to committee only if re-baselined benefits fall more than 20% below the approved profile; and (c) quarterly reporting to the committee. The chair noted that approval lapses if development spend has not begun within six months."

The discovery completes, and re-baselined benefits are 15% below the approved profile. Development spend has not yet begun; five months have passed; the GDS assessment is booked. Which statement is correct?

- A) The case must return to committee because benefits fell below the approved profile.
- B) The team may proceed to development spend without returning to committee, provided it begins within the next month and quarterly reporting continues — the 15% shortfall is inside the 20% tolerance and condition (a) is satisfied.
- C) The approval has lapsed because benefits were re-baselined.
- D) The team may not proceed because any reduction in benefits requires re-approval.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Walking each condition: (a) is satisfied; (b)'s return-to-committee trigger requires a fall of more than 20%, and 15% is within tolerance, disposing of A and D; (c) is an ongoing obligation, not a gate. The lapse clause gives one month of validity remaining, making B's "provided it begins within the next month" exactly right. Option C misreads re-baselining — an expected activity — as a lapse trigger. This item rewards walking conditional approvals as a checklist against the facts, since misreading a tolerance in either direction costs committee time or spends money outside approval.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Synthesis across conflicting written sources**

Passage 1 (supplier performance report): "All eight service levels were met this quarter. User satisfaction with the service desk stands at 4.1/5, based on the post-ticket survey."

Passage 2 (email from an operations manager): "Getting anything fixed takes forever. My team have mostly stopped logging tickets — we fix things ourselves or live with them. The survey only goes to people whose tickets were closed, so of course the scores look fine."

Which conclusion do the two passages together best support?

- A) The supplier is falsifying its performance data.
- B) The operations manager's team is failing to follow process, which explains their poor experience.
- C) The measurement system may be structurally blind to the manager's experience: service levels and post-ticket surveys only observe logged tickets, so ticket avoidance would leave dissatisfaction invisible while every metric stays green — worth testing via unlogged-demand sampling and non-user surveys before the next supplier review.
- D) The two accounts contradict each other, so at least one must be withdrawn.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passages describe different populations rather than conflicting — every supplier metric is conditioned on a ticket existing, and Passage 2 describes people who have exited that population. A measurement system built entirely on logged demand is structurally blind to avoidance, which actually improves the visible metrics. C converts this into testable next steps. Option A leaps to an unevidenced accusation. Option B blames the users of a failing channel. Option D demands retraction when reconciliation is available. This item tests the senior business analysis skill of finding a hidden scope difference behind apparently contradictory documents.

**Question 11 (challenging) — What the passage does not commit to**

Passage (email from a director sponsoring a candidate's shared-services proposal): "I read the consolidation paper with interest. The efficiency logic is compelling, and you have clearly done the groundwork with the affected units. I would be comfortable seeing this go to the investment committee in the autumn. You should know that the chief operating officer has expressed some reservations about central platforms following the finance system programme; it would be worth reflecting on how the paper addresses resilience and exit provisions before submission."

Which reading of this email is most accurate?

- A) The director has committed to sponsoring the paper at the investment committee in the autumn.
- B) The director is signalling conditional support — comfort with an autumn submission, not a commitment to sponsor it — and is telling the candidate that COO-level reservations exist which the paper must visibly answer, particularly on resilience and exit, before it can safely proceed.
- C) The director is politely rejecting the proposal.
- D) The COO has decided to block the proposal at committee.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Comfortable seeing this go to committee" is an attitude, not an undertaking. The COO reservation, paired with specific homework, is telling the candidate where the paper will be attacked and what is needed to defend it. Option C misreads engaged, directive feedback as a brush-off — rejections do not assign homework. Option D converts secondhand reservations into a settled position. This item tests calibrating conditionality inside encouragement without hallucinating commitments or conflicts that are not there.

### Administration tips

- **Watch for whether a candidate separates what a passage states, signals, and omits** (Question 11) — conflating the three is a common and consequential error at this level.
- **Note the scope-word discipline in Questions 1, 5 and 9** — a candidate who tracks "all", "only", "at least", "subject to" precisely is showing the reading habit this role depends on.
- **Score the reconciliation item (Question 10) for whether a candidate hunts for a scope difference** before judging either source wrong.
- **Use the clear-writing item (Question 8) as a live discussion point.**
- **Time by passage, not by statement.**

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Substituting a neighbouring concept** — "reduce" for "retire", "reviewing" for "confirmed".
- **Reading conditions onto the wrong money or the wrong scope.**
- **Rewarding answers built on experience rather than the passage.**
- **Confusing the strongest criticism with the harshest** in argument-evaluation items.
- **Missing a passage's own caveat.**
- **Treating signals as commitments** — corridor language and sponsor comfort are real information but not promises.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to judge the effectiveness of possible responses — most effective, most and least effective, full rankings, or independent ratings. There is rarely a perfect answer; the scoring key rewards consistent preference for responses that are active rather than passive, collaborative rather than unilateral, honest rather than convenient, and user- and outcome-focused rather than self-protective.

The typical format is 15 to 25 scenarios over 30 to 45 minutes, often untimed. Senior-normed SJTs differ from junior ones less in format than in the shape of the dilemmas: legitimate goods are put in tension, and the response options are all superficially defensible. The differentiator at this level is second-order thinking — what does each response teach the organisation, what precedent does it set, what happens to the relationship, the team, and the system after the immediate move is made.

For a senior business relationship manager, the SJT is the closest of the four assessments to a work sample. The role's duties are behavioural through and through, performed in rooms, in inboxes, and in moments where several defensible actions compete and the candidate's choice becomes the culture.

### What it measures for the role

- **Accountability scenarios** map to **Ownership and topic**: taking accountability for issues that occur — including ones a candidate did not cause — and proactively hunting problems.
- **Competing-stakeholder scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: negotiating and removing blockers while keeping long-term relationships intact.
- **Strategic tension scenarios** map to **Strategic thinking**: providing guidance when local pressures and strategic commitments pull apart.
- **User advocacy scenarios** map to **User focus**: defending research and user needs when politically inconvenient.
- **Evidence-versus-relationship scenarios** map to **Business analysis (IT operations)**: leading investigations whose findings someone powerful will not like.
- **Capability-building scenarios** map to leadership of others.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded. For each scenario, ask the candidate to choose the option indicated.

**Question 1 (easy) — The finding the sponsor dislikes**

A candidate's investigation into a director's cherished proposal — a bespoke scheduling system for her unit — concludes that an existing shared platform meets 90% of the need at a third of the cost. The director, who has championed the candidate's function to her peers, phones the candidate: "I hope your paper isn't going to embarrass me at the board." What is the most effective response?

- A) Soften the paper's conclusion to "further options analysis required" to protect the relationship.
- B) Tell her the findings stand, brief her on them fully before the paper circulates, and work with her on the strongest version of her unit's case — including where the shared platform genuinely falls short and what would close the 10% gap.
- C) Submit the paper as written without further contact, since the evidence speaks for itself.
- D) Ask the head of function to deliver the difficult message instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The findings stand — persuasive recommendations never mean bending them — but the handling is where senior craft lives: a full private briefing before circulation removes the ambush, and co-developing the honest version of her case converts her into owner of a better position. Option A trades analytical integrity for comfort. Option C is honest but relationally reckless. Option D outsources the hardest conversation. The senior pattern: never trade the finding; always invest in the landing.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit and visibility for a candidate's team**

At a directors' meeting, a candidate's director praises "the outstanding shared-needs analysis" that reshaped the investment pipeline. The analysis was substantially built by one of the candidate's business relationship managers, who is not present and is currently under-confident after a difficult year. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the praise on behalf of the function and move on; correcting a director mid-meeting is poor form.
- B) Name the BRM in the meeting as the analysis's author, then tell her afterwards exactly what was said and by whom — and find her the next stretch piece of work with director-level visibility.
- C) Tell the BRM afterwards that the directors liked the work, keeping the meeting itself brief.
- D) Suggest the BRM present the analysis at a future meeting to earn the credit directly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Attribution in the room is cheap and compounding: a named author in a directors' meeting acquires a professional identity where reputations are formed. The follow-through matters, since under-confident people discount secondhand praise. Option A quietly banks her work as institutional credit. Option C forfeits the room. Option D defers attribution while today's incorrect impression sets. This item rewards correcting the record where it was made and converting recognition into development.

**Question 3 (easy) — The proactive problem search**

Reviewing supplier reports across a portfolio, a candidate notices a pattern nobody has raised: three different services, all dependent on the same subcontracted data centre, have each logged single minor incidents in the past month — different symptoms, same window, same underlying site. All three services are green; no stakeholder is complaining. The candidate's week is already oversubscribed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Note the observation and revisit next month if the pattern continues; three minor incidents on green services do not justify displacement of committed work.
- B) Raise it now: ask the supplier for the site's incident and maintenance history, alert the three service owners to the potential common dependency, and commission a view of what a full site failure would take down — treating an hour today as insurance against a correlated outage.
- C) Report the pattern to the director and await direction.
- D) Log a risk in the register so the observation is formally recorded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests the proactive clause of **Ownership and topic** directly. The pattern is the classic precursor signature of a correlated failure, visible only from a cross-portfolio seat — "someone would have noticed" is false, since the someone is this role. B's moves are proportionate: an information request, an alert to owners, and an impact view. Option A confuses green with safe. Option C escalates without analysis. Option D creates a record without changing anything. The senior pattern: weak signals plus a common-cause hypothesis equals act now, lightly.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the spending review squeeze on user research**

Drafting the directorate's spending review options, the finance lead proposes deleting the user research budget line from year 2 onward: "It's £180k of nice-to-have, and everything else in the options is contractually committed — it's the only discretionary line left." The submission is due in eight days. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Accept the deletion — protecting delivery contracts is objectively the priority — and plan to rebuild the research budget in a future year.
2. Escalate immediately to the director, stating that a submission that cuts research cannot be supported.
3. Bring evidence to the finance lead within two days: what the research line delivered (rework avoided, adoption gains, the failed-feature spend it prevented), then jointly build an option that scales research to the settlement rather than deleting it — presenting research as risk mitigation on the committed delivery, not as a discretionary comfort.
4. Agree to the deletion in the options paper, but plan to fund research informally from other lines if the settlement allows.

- A) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 1
- C) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 2
- D) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 3 reframes research as risk mitigation on the committed delivery, in the finance lead's own language, and offers scaling options that give the settlement room to move without deleting the capability. Response 4 is least effective — the deletion becomes official while the informal workaround is exactly the ungoverned behaviour that destroys financial credibility when discovered. Response 1 concedes a false dichotomy; response 2 escalates before evidence is assembled. Order: 3, 2, 1, 4. The senior pattern: defend user-centred investment as delivery insurance, with numbers, at the table where the cut is being drafted.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The two directors and the single truth**

Directors of two units a candidate partners with are in open dispute. Unit A's director claims Unit B's slow data-sharing sign-off is blocking her flagship service; Unit B's director claims Unit A's team submitted an incomplete request and briefed against him before he had seen it. Each has separately asked the candidate to "make clear to the centre" that the other is at fault. The candidate's own review shows both claims are partially true: the request was incomplete, *and* Unit B sat on it for three weeks before saying so. What is the most effective response?

- A) Brief the centre neutrally that "both parties share responsibility" and let governance allocate fault.
- B) Support Unit A's director — a flagship service outweighs process complaints — while privately telling Unit B's director the candidate understands his position.
- C) Get both directors in one room with the timeline: acknowledge each grievance's valid core, get agreement on a completed request and a dated sign-off, and propose fixing the process gap both fell into — a request standard and a response-time expectation — so the dispute cannot recur.
- D) Decline to be involved: inter-unit disputes are for their common director general, not for a relationship manager.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both directors asked for advocacy; the role requires being the system's advocate instead. The timeline lets the candidate acknowledge each valid core without adjudicating a winner, and the forward moves — a completed request, a dated commitment, and fixing the shared process gap — serve the flagship service directly. Option A is accurate but inert. Option B trades integrity for the more glamorous party, and a discovered two-faced move ends trust with both. Option D forfeits the core of the role. The pattern: facts on the table, grievances honoured, commitments dated, system fixed.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The pre-briefed conclusion**

A candidate's director tells them before their shared-needs investigation is complete: "Between us, the DG has already decided the answer is one consolidated platform — shape your findings accordingly. It'll land better for all of us." The evidence so far genuinely points toward consolidation for three of the five capability areas, but firmly against it for the other two, where unit needs diverge sharply. What is the most effective response?

- A) Shape the findings as requested — three-fifths alignment is close enough, and the DG's instinct is broadly vindicated anyway.
- B) Tell the director the evidence supports consolidation in three areas and opposes it in two; propose presenting it exactly that way — as a strong endorsement of the DG's direction with two evidence-based exceptions that will protect the programme from its most likely failure modes — and offer to brief the DG together.
- C) Complete the report per the evidence and submit it through normal channels without further engaging the director's request.
- D) Ask to be removed from the investigation, since its independence has been compromised.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B refuses to shape the findings while doing the persuasive work the role requires — "strong endorsement with two evidence-based exceptions" is the same facts as "three out of five", arranged to be hearable and true. The two divergent areas are precisely where a forced consolidation would generate its failures, so presenting them as protective exceptions serves the DG's actual interest. Option A launders evidence. Option C is honest but politically self-blinding. Option D is resignation as integrity theatre. The senior pattern: return with what the evidence says, packaged so the powerful can adopt it as their own.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the struggling team member**

A BRM in a candidate's team is struggling: two unit heads have separately mentioned missed follow-ups, and her pipeline records are weeks stale. The candidate also knows she flagged, months ago, that her portfolio — five units including the two complainants — is materially heavier than her peers'. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Rebalance her portfolio immediately and say nothing about the performance concerns, since the workload explains them.
2. Talk with her first: share the specific feedback, hear her account, acknowledge the workload flag she raised and act on rebalancing — and agree a short recovery plan for the two strained relationships, with visible support in both units.
3. Document the missed follow-ups and stale records formally, so that a performance process is ready if patterns continue.
4. Take the two complaining units into the candidate's own portfolio temporarily and let her rebuild with the remaining three.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 treats both truths as true — a real performance shortfall and a real organisational failure (an unaddressed overload flag) — and sequences correctly: specifics first, her account heard, the system fixed, a recovery plan with visible sponsorship. Response 3 is least effective because it builds a case against a person whose known, unaddressed overload is the most likely cause, instead of having the conversation first. Response 1 withholds feedback she deserves; response 4 relieves pressure but reads as demotion. Order: 2, 1, 4, 3. The leadership pattern: candour plus system-fix plus visible sponsorship, and documentation never precedes conversation when the manager owns part of the cause.

**Question 8 (challenging) — The strategic guidance a candidate disagrees with**

The chief digital officer's new operating model, now formally adopted, routes all business-unit demand through product managers, repositioning a candidate's BRM function from demand-shapers to "strategic relationship stewards" — a narrower role the candidate argued against during consultation, unsuccessfully. Their team is deflated; two members are quietly job-hunting; a unit head emails: "So who do we actually talk to now — you or the product people? This feels like a mess." What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell the team and the unit head candidly that the model was opposed, and commit to gathering evidence of its failures for the six-month review.
- B) Implement the model faithfully: work with the product leads to define a crisp interface between the roles, give the unit head a one-page "who to contact" answer agreed with the product community, refocus the team on the strategic-stewardship value the model actually assigns them — and channel personal reservations into the six-month review with evidence gathered from making it work, not from watching it fail.
- C) Continue operating broadly as before — units will naturally keep coming to the people they trust, and the model will adjust to reality over time.
- D) Escalate the unit head's confusion to the CDO as early evidence that the model is failing and should be paused.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The decision is made legitimately, and what remains is the difference between a leader and a casualty of change. B is loyal implementation without the loyalty lie — a crisp interface answers the operational question, a one-pager answers the stakeholder question, and refocusing the team on the model's actual value is retention through meaning. The six-month review channel keeps dissent alive in its legitimate venue. Option A licenses relitigating the model and is sabotage with a clipboard. Option C sustains the confusion the unit head is complaining about. Option D spends escalation currency prematurely. The pattern: disagree, then commit visibly, define the seams, protect the team's sense of value.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The confidence that constrains a candidate**

A unit head a candidate has partnered with for years tells them in confidence: her unit's board has agreed in principle to outsource its entire back-office function within eighteen months — which would remove the business case for the £2m workflow platform investment the candidate's function is about to take to committee on her unit's behalf next week. She asks the candidate to keep the outsourcing plan confidential "until the announcement in about two months". The committee papers are already circulated. What is the most effective response?

- A) Respect the confidence fully: let the committee approve the investment, since the outsourcing may yet fall through, and revisit if the announcement happens.
- B) Tell her the two timelines have collided: the committee cannot be allowed to commit £2m to a case that may be invalid, so together a way is needed to pause the item without exposing her plan — such as her unit requesting a two-month deferral for "internal planning alignment" — and if she cannot agree to any pause, tell her that silence cannot continue while the committee decides, and work out with her the minimum disclosure that protects both the money and her announcement.
- C) Quietly tip off the committee chair about the outsourcing plan so the item can be pulled without the candidate's fingerprints on it.
- D) Withdraw the item personally citing "emerging information", refusing to elaborate to anyone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Confidences are held within a candidate's duties, not above them. B names the conflict honestly, constructs a protective route together (an unremarkable deferral request), and only past every refused pause states plainly that silence is not an option, negotiating minimum disclosure. Option A gambles public money on "may fall through". Option C protects the money by betraying the person. Option D protects the secret and money but converts the candidate into a mystery that gets investigated. The pattern: take the collision to the confider first, engineer a route honouring both obligations, escalate only past refusal.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the maturity theatre**

A directorate reports relationship maturity annually to the executive committee. This year's self-assessments, collected by a candidate's team, average 3.8 — but the candidate's own observation says several units inflated their scores: two "level 4" units routinely bypass the front door, and one has not met its relationship manager in a quarter. The candidate's director is delighted with the 3.8 and has already trailed it to the executive committee. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Report the 3.8 as collected — self-assessment is the agreed method, and the method was followed; note privately that next year's process should include evidence checks.
2. Tell the director before the committee meeting that the 3.8 is unsafe: show the behavioural contradictions, propose reporting the self-assessed figure *alongside* behavioural indicators with an honest note on the gap, and frame the mixed picture as the maturing of the measurement itself.
3. Quietly re-score the three inflated units downward before submission, so the reported figure reflects reality.
4. Report the 3.8 but add a verbal caveat at the committee if questioned.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 protects the executive committee's trust in the function's reporting — the behavioural contradictions are evidence the headline is unsafe, and telling the director before the meeting is the loyalty move, with triangulated reporting the analytical move. Response 3 is least effective despite honest motive: unilaterally rewriting units' self-assessments corrupts the data twice over and destroys the diagnostic value of the inflation itself. Response 1 is procedurally clean dishonesty; response 4 is ambush-by-integrity in the worst room for that discovery. Order: 2, 1, 4, 3. The pattern: bad numbers are briefed upward before the meeting and redesigned into honest ones, never silently corrected.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The user harm nobody owns**

Analysis commissioned by a candidate shows that citizens who phone one of the organisation's helplines because they cannot complete the digital service are waiting 40 minutes on average — and 60% of them are calling about the same three form-design problems. The digital service is owned by a product team in another directorate; the helpline is outsourced under a contract managed by a third team; both are "green" on their own metrics. The candidate has no authority over either. What is the most effective response?

- A) Publish the analysis to both teams and their directors, and let the owning teams respond as they see fit — the candidate's job was the insight, theirs is the action.
- B) Convene the product team, the contract manager and the candidate's own function around the analysis: propose the product team prioritise the three form fixes (quantifying the call-demand reduction each would buy), the contract manager add the fix timeline to the next supplier review, and a named owner — the candidate, until a better one exists — track the end-to-end citizen journey that currently no green dashboard sees.
- C) Escalate to the executive committee that two green-rated teams are jointly causing citizen harm, and recommend a formal service review.
- D) Raise the three form problems with the product team informally and monitor whether call volumes improve over the next two quarters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This item concentrates the whole senior role: a harm visible only from the cross-organisational seat, owned by nobody because every local metric is green. B creates the missing ownership — convening uses relationship and evidence rather than hierarchy, quantifying the pitch converts analysis into the product team's own prioritisation currency, and interim ownership follows **Ownership and topic**'s founding sentence at organisational scale. Option A is insight-lobbing, technically complete and organisationally inert. Option C spends the highest-cost attention before the cheap move is tried. Option D has no forcing function. The senior pattern: where the organisation's structure leaves a gap, stand in it, with evidence and ownership held until the system grows an owner.

### Administration tips

- **Study the role summary alongside these items** — the scoring key for a senior relationship role is effectively a paraphrase of the skill definitions.
- **Apply the two-audience test when scoring**: what does an option do to the immediate problem, and what does it teach everyone watching?
- **Watch for disagree-then-commit and dissent-with-alternative patterns** (Questions 6 and 8) — the rewarded response keeps evidence intact, frames it hearably, and dissents in the right venue.
- **Locate the least effective option by its motive** (self-protection, secrecy, passivity) — it usually clarifies the correct answer too.
- **Use these scenarios as debrief material**, especially the collision cases (confidence versus duty, relationship versus finding).

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding advocacy when the role requires brokering** — picking a side, or sympathising privately with both, should score poorly.
- **Confusing implementation of a decision opposed with endorsement of it** — passive resistance and silent capitulation both score poorly; visible commitment plus legitimate-channel critique is the target.
- **Rewarding protection of numbers over protection of trust.**
- **Rewarding escalation before convening.**
- **Letting confidences outrank duties, or breaking them carelessly** — the rewarded pattern is always sequenced honesty with the confider first.
- **Fixing the person when the system is the cause, or vice versa** — the strongest options usually fix both.

## Conclusion

This document has given you a demanding set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a senior business relationship manager: cognitive ability, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tests abstracting differently worded demands into one shared need and knowing where to stop abstracting, cross-footing a spending review table, reconciling telemetry with surveys and corridor testimony, and routing incidents, workarounds and patches through the service management life cycle. The numeric section tests catching a cashability double-count before a board could fund it, pricing a consolidation across its appraisal window and steady state, re-aggregating a supplier's flattering annual average back into the quarterly regime it was hiding from, and valuing contractor capacity by the slots demand would actually use. The verbal section tests reading settlements, minutes, assurances and sponsor emails at the level where "reduce" is not "retire" and comfort is not commitment. The situational judgement section tests the collisions that define senior judgement: findings a sponsor dislikes, models argued against and then implemented, confidences that crash into duties, and harms visible only from a cross-organisational seat.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as your answer key and your talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more valuable to the candidate — and more informative to you — than the raw score.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which you administered this assessment alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate you assess for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can absorb conflicting information and produce solutions, present honest numbers survivably, and channel dissent into legitimate venues — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
