# Senior IT Service Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This document is written for you: an assessor administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for senior IT service manager, within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. At senior level, the emphasis shifts: junior assessments test whether a candidate can follow a rule; senior assessments test whether they can reason about competing rules, ambiguous evidence, and strategic trade-offs. The material draws on the artefacts a senior IT service manager genuinely handles — cross-platform availability data, compliance and risk registers, process maturity assessments, strategy documents, supplier negotiations, and conflicting stakeholder briefs — and the decisions genuinely made: absorbing large amounts of conflicting information to produce solutions, influencing decisions and removing blockers, evaluating whether current strategies still meet business requirements, and taking accountability not only for issues that occur but for the potential problems proactively hunted down.

Seniority in service management is precisely a claim about judgement under complexity. The role is responsible for service availability, performance and process maturity across multiple platforms and channels — which means the patterns to recognise span systems, the deductions must cross organisational boundaries, and the errors to catch hide inside aggregated data that already looks plausible. Cognitive, numeric, verbal, and situational judgement assessments sample exactly these capabilities, and at senior level the stakes are weighted heavily because the cost of misjudgement scales with the scope of the role.

This document is organised into four assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why it matters at this level, a mapping of the assessment's dimensions to the specific skills in the role summary, a substantial set of items with full worked explanations for use as an answer key, administration tips, and common pitfalls to watch for when scoring.

**How to administer this fairly.** Use the same items and time allowance for every candidate assessed at this role level. Hold the correct answers and explanations back from the candidate until after they have responded. Let the candidate work through each section without interruption, and note anything unusual about the conditions so the record is honest. Afterwards, use the explanations to structure a debrief — at this level, the situational judgement explanations in particular double as leadership case studies worth discussing directly. This assessment is one input among several — see the advisory below for what it should never be used for on its own.

The hard questions in this document are genuinely hard — multi-constraint, evidence-conflicted, and politically textured — because that is the terrain the role occupies.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental operations this role demands, using realistic materials rather than abstract puzzles. At senior level, the questions present cross-platform monitoring data, compliance registers, process maturity models, supplier performance portfolios, and strategy-to-operations traceability — and ask the candidate to reason about them under time pressure: recognising patterns that span services, deducing consequences from interacting policies, catching errors buried in aggregation, prioritising across incommensurable demands, and solving problems where the evidence conflicts.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a senior-level norm group; adaptive platforms raise difficulty as the candidate succeeds, and employers typically receive speed and accuracy profiles rather than a single figure.

The failures that matter at this scope are reasoning failures. A service manager who misses a cross-platform pattern buys three uncoordinated investigations; one who deduces wrongly from interacting policies signs off a compliance breach; one who accepts aggregated data uncritically presents a flattering fiction to a board. The role summary makes the point directly: absorbing large amounts of conflicting information to produce solutions is nearly a definition of senior cognitive work.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in the role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to the **Service focus** skill — seeing the bigger picture across groups of services. At senior level the meaningful patterns are cross-platform and cross-channel.
- **Logical deduction** maps to the **Service management framework knowledge** skill (expert certificate level) and the **Asset and configuration management** duty to manage service compliance and risk: compliance is applied deduction.
- **Error checking** maps to the **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill — leading investigative work and absorbing large amounts of conflicting information — because at senior level the errors worth catching are structural, not typographical.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the **Ownership and topic** skill — taking accountability for issues that occur and being proactive in searching for potential problems.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to the duty to **produce solutions** from conflicting information, and to the **Strategic thinking** skill — evaluating current strategies to ensure business requirements are being met and exceeded where possible.
- Reasoning about influence and blockers maps to the **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Cross-platform pattern recognition**

The monthly availability dashboard across an estate: web channel services on Platform A averaged 99.7%; telephony services on Platform B averaged 99.6%; four services spanning Platforms A and B (using A's front end and B's integration layer) averaged 98.9%; batch services wholly on Platform B averaged 99.8%. What is the most reasonable initial hypothesis?

- A) Platform A is the weakest component in the estate
- B) Platform B is the weakest component in the estate
- C) The integration between the platforms, rather than either platform alone, is degrading the cross-platform services
- D) The cross-platform services are simply measured more strictly

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Isolate what differs. Services wholly on A perform well; services wholly on B perform well; only services spanning both underperform. If either platform were intrinsically weak, its single-platform services would show the weakness — they do not. The distinguishing feature of the degraded group is the integration path between platforms. Option D invents a measurement difference the stem does not mention. This tests the senior version of pattern recognition: the unit of analysis is a portfolio, and the signal lives in the differences between groups — a strong candidate's answer notes the fix here is an integration-layer investigation, not two platform reviews.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from interacting policies**

Departmental policy states: (i) services holding citizen data must complete an annual security review; (ii) services that have missed their annual review must not receive functional changes until reviewed, though security patches are permitted; (iii) the licensing service holds citizen data and its last security review was 14 months ago. A programme manager requests approval for a functional change to the licensing service. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The change may proceed if the programme accepts the risk
- B) The change must not proceed until the licensing service completes its security review
- C) The change may proceed because it might be classed as a security patch
- D) The policy does not apply because the review is only two months overdue

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the rules. From (iii), the service holds citizen data, so (i) applies. Its last review was 14 months ago, so the review has been missed. From (ii), a service in that state must not receive functional changes — and the request is explicitly for a functional change, foreclosing option C. Option A invents a risk-acceptance override the policy does not contain. Option D invents a grace period. This tests whether every step is licensed by a stated rule or fact — and a strong candidate's answer notes that the senior manager's value in governance is precisely the refusal to let plausible-sounding overrides relax rules written for exactly the cases where relaxing them is tempting.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking an aggregated report**

A draft board report under review states: "Across our three channels, customer satisfaction averaged 84% (web 90% from 5,000 responses; phone 80% from 1,000 responses; face-to-face 70% from 200 responses). Satisfaction is therefore strong across all channels." Which criticism of the report is most accurate?

- A) The 84% average is arithmetically wrong given the response volumes
- B) The unweighted average of 80% should have been used
- C) The stated average is close to correct, but the conclusion "strong across all channels" misrepresents a 70% face-to-face result and a 20-point spread
- D) Satisfaction data cannot be averaged across channels under any circumstances

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first. Weighted average = (0.90 × 5,000 + 0.80 × 1,000 + 0.70 × 200) ÷ 6,200 ≈ 87.7%. The report's 84% is the unweighted average of 90, 80 and 70 — understated relative to the correct weighted figure. Option A is half-right, but the most accurate criticism on offer is C, which captures both the arithmetic issue and the graver one: "strong across all channels" is falsified by face-to-face sitting at 70% with a 20-point spread. Option D overcorrects into a false absolute. This tests checking the arithmetic and, harder, checking the inference — a strong candidate's answer notes that aggregation errors mislead a reader while aggregation-justified conclusions mislead a board.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation across the portfolio**

A morning brief contains four items: (1) a compliance report showing one service's TLS certificates expire in five days with no renewal owner assigned; (2) an invitation to present the channel strategy to the directors' group in three weeks; (3) a supplier's request to reschedule this month's service review; (4) monitoring showing a slow, three-week upward trend in authentication latency across two platforms, still within thresholds. Which item warrants attention first?

- A) Item 1 — an unowned, dated, service-affecting risk with a hard deadline
- B) Item 2 — director-level visibility outranks operational detail
- C) Item 3 — supplier relationships depend on responsiveness
- D) Item 4 — trends are more strategic than certificates

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 combines a hard deadline, a certain consequence if missed, and — decisively — no assigned owner, meaning the default outcome without intervention is failure. Item 4 deserves proactive investigation (the ownership and topic skill: proactively searching for potential problems) but has weeks of runway, not days. Items 2 and 3 are diary management. Option B commits the seniority fallacy; option D romanticises "strategic" over "urgent and certain". This tests the ranking principle: certainty × imminence × unownedness first — and a strong candidate's answer also notes that the correct action delegates with ownership rather than personally renewing the certificate, since senior prioritisation is about placing attention and accountability, not doing the task.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Conflicting evidence about a degradation**

Users report the casework service "freezes every afternoon". Evidence gathered: (i) server metrics show no afternoon CPU, memory, or response-time anomalies; (ii) network monitoring shows office bandwidth utilisation reaching 95% between 14:00 and 16:00 daily, coinciding with a video-training rollout; (iii) home-based users report no freezes; (iv) the casework application team has found no defects. What is the best-supported explanation?

- A) Users are mistaken — the server metrics prove the service is healthy
- B) Office network saturation during afternoon video training is degrading the user experience for office-based users, while the service itself remains healthy
- C) The casework application has an undetected defect that only affects offices
- D) Home-based users have better hardware

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test option B against every fact: healthy server metrics — consistent; office bandwidth saturated exactly during the freeze window — the mechanism; home users unaffected — the discriminating evidence, since home traffic does not traverse the office network; no application defects found — consistent, since there are none to find. Option A privileges one instrument over user experience. Option C posits an undetected defect while a fully evidenced alternative exists. Option D explains nothing about the timing. This tests reconciling apparently conflicting evidence into a single consistent account — the role summary's "absorb large amounts of conflicting information and use it to produce solutions" — and a strong candidate's answer notes the management corollary: the fix is a network/traffic decision, not an application workstream.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about process maturity claims**

A maturity assessment rates the incident process at level 4 ("measured and controlled") citing: comprehensive metrics, monthly reviews, and documented procedures. Separately known: (a) the metrics exclude one platform migrated last year; (b) two of the last six monthly reviews were cancelled; (c) the documented procedures were last updated before a major organisational restructure. Which conclusion is soundest?

- A) The level 4 rating is fraudulent and should be reported
- B) The rating overstates current maturity: each cited evidence base is materially incomplete or stale, so the true level is likely lower and the assessment should be reperformed with corrected evidence
- C) The rating is sound because the assessor followed the methodology
- D) Maturity models are subjective, so the rating is as valid as any other

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Evaluate the rating by evaluating its evidence. Each pillar is compromised: a coverage gap (excluded platform), a cadence gap (cancelled reviews), and a currency gap (procedures predating a restructure). None of this establishes bad faith — option A converts evidential weakness into an accusation, both logically unlicensed and politically incendiary. Option C mistakes procedural compliance for soundness. Option D retreats into relativism. This tests auditing the evidence behind a conclusion before adopting it — especially conclusions that flatter one's own operation, which are the ones nobody else will challenge.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a risk register**

Four entries from a service risk register (scores use a 1–5 × 1–5 scale):

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Score (P×I) | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1: Data centre power failure | 2 | 5 | 10 | Dual power feeds installed |
| R2: Key engineer departure | 4 | 3 | 12 | None — accepted |
| R3: Supplier insolvency | 1 | 5 | 5 | Exit plan documented |
| R4: Unpatched CVE on portal | 3 | 4 | 7 | Patch scheduled |

Which entry contains an internal inconsistency?

- A) R1
- B) R2
- C) R3
- D) R4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Verify each score against its own stated formula. R1: 2×5=10 ✓. R2: 4×3=12 ✓. R3: 1×5=5 ✓. R4: 3×4=12, but the register records 7 ✗. This tests a mechanical check that finds a defect with real consequence: registers are ranked and resourced by score, so an understated R4 sits below R1 in attention while actually tying R2 for the register's top score — a live unpatched vulnerability mis-scored downward is exactly the kind of quiet error that surfaces later in an incident review. A strong candidate's answer also distinguishes R2's questionable "accepted, no mitigation" judgement from an arithmetic inconsistency — the two are different findings.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritising strategy evaluation findings**

An evaluation of the three-year service strategy, now 18 months in, surfaces four findings: (1) the strategy's channel-shift target (60% of transactions digital) was met a year early; (2) its assumption that the legacy case system would be decommissioned by now has slipped by at least two years, and three strategy workstreams depend on that decommissioning; (3) two KPIs are still not being measured because the required tooling was never funded; (4) stakeholders report the strategy document is "hard to navigate". Which finding should lead the evaluation report?

- A) Finding 1 — lead with success to build credibility
- B) Finding 2 — a broken dependency invalidating three workstreams is the finding with the largest consequences for the strategy's remaining life
- C) Finding 3 — unmeasured KPIs undermine the whole evaluation
- D) Finding 4 — stakeholder experience should always come first

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An evaluation's lead finding should be the one that most changes what the organisation must now do. Finding 2 is structural: three workstreams rest on an assumption now known false by two years — the strategy's remaining life is partly fiction until replanned. Finding 3 is serious but weakens evaluation precision rather than invalidating delivery. Finding 1 is genuine good news, but leading with it (option A) is communications strategy, not analysis. Finding 4 is real but cosmetic by comparison. This tests evaluating current strategies against business requirements — hunting the load-bearing assumption that failed — and a strong candidate's answer sequences findings by impact rather than palatability.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Deduction across supplier obligations**

Supplier contracts in a portfolio state: Supplier X provides the hosting platform with a 99.9% availability commitment, measured monthly, excluding agreed maintenance windows. Supplier Y provides application support with a commitment to restore service within 4 hours of a fault being assigned to them. Last month, the citizen-facing portal (hosted by X, supported by Y) was down for 6 hours in one incident: 5 hours before diagnosis determined the fault was in Y's application code, then 1 hour for Y's fix. X's platform was fully available throughout; the incident fell in no maintenance window. Which statement must be true?

- A) Supplier X breached its availability commitment
- B) Supplier Y breached its 4-hour restoration commitment
- C) Neither supplier necessarily breached its commitment as written
- D) Both suppliers breached their commitments

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read each obligation exactly. X commits to platform availability, and the platform was fully available (no breach). Y commits to restore within 4 hours of assignment, and restored in 1 hour from assignment (no breach). So the service was down 6 hours while every contract was honoured. This tests precise reading of trigger clauses, and a strong candidate's answer draws the portfolio-level insight the deduction exposes: nobody owns time-to-diagnosis, the largest component of the outage — a gap invisible in any single contract, visible only in portfolio view.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint platform migration sequencing**

Four services (S1–S4) must migrate from a legacy platform across four monthly windows (March–June). Constraints: (i) S1 and S2 share a database and must migrate in consecutive windows, S1 first; (ii) S3's business owner has vetoed March and April (statutory peak); (iii) the migration team can handle only one service per window; (iv) S4 must migrate before S3 because S3's new configuration depends on S4's migrated state; (v) the legacy platform's support contract ends 30 June — all migrations must complete by then. Which schedule satisfies all constraints?

- A) S1 March, S2 April, S4 May, S3 June
- B) S4 March, S3 April, S1 May, S2 June
- C) S1 March, S4 April, S2 May, S3 June
- D) S4 March, S1 April, S2 May, S3 June

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Apply the constraints as filters. Option B: S3 in April violates (ii). Option C: S1 March, S2 May — not consecutive, violates (i). Options A and D both survive the stated constraints on first pass, so the discriminator is dependency slack against the hard deadline in (v): option A leaves S4 completing at the end of May with S3 migrating immediately in June, minimal soak time; option D gives S4's migrated state three months of stability before S3's dependency is exercised. This tests filtering by hard constraints first, then breaking ties with risk headroom — a strong candidate's answer notes that in migration planning, "satisfies all constraints" is the entry requirement, not the goal.

**Question 11 (hard) — Root cause across a multi-channel failure**

In one week: web applications fail intermittently with authentication errors (roughly 2% of logins); the telephony channel's agent desktop shows occasional "session expired" messages; the mobile app is unaffected; batch overnight jobs are unaffected. All four consume the shared identity service, but: web and telephony use the identity service's token-refresh endpoint continuously during sessions; mobile uses long-lived tokens refreshed daily; batch authenticates once per run. Identity service metrics show the token-refresh endpoint's error rate rose from 0.01% to 0.4% after last week's identity service upgrade. What is the best-supported conclusion?

- A) The identity service upgrade degraded the token-refresh endpoint, affecting only the channels that depend on continuous refresh — explaining the pattern across all four channels
- B) The web application and telephony desktop have coincidentally developed separate faults
- C) The mobile app's architecture should be adopted by all channels
- D) A 0.4% error rate is too small to explain user-visible failures

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The affected channels share exactly one relevant property — continuous dependence on token refresh — while the unaffected channels avoid that endpoint by design. The endpoint's error rate rose fortyfold immediately after an upgrade — mechanism, timing, and channel pattern all align. Option B invokes coincidence where a common cause fits better. Option D fails arithmetic intuition: small per-event probabilities become large per-session probabilities under repetition. Option C converts a diagnosis into an architecture programme the evidence does not support. This tests differential diagnosis across a shared dependency, including explaining the non-failures — a hypothesis that cannot explain why mobile is fine is incomplete.

**Question 12 (hard) — Producing a solution from conflicting information**

A recommendation is needed on whether to renew, retender, or restructure the contract for the largest supplier, which expires in 12 months. Conflicting evidence: performance data shows 24 consecutive months of OLA achievement above target; analysts privately report the supplier increasingly declines "out of scope" work that previous years' goodwill absorbed; finance shows the contract is 18% above current market benchmarks; the supplier holds deep knowledge of two fragile legacy systems with no internal documentation; a retender would cost roughly £250k and consume the best people for six months during a major channel transformation; the business's strategy commits to decommissioning both legacy systems within three years. Which recommendation best synthesises the evidence?

- A) Renew as-is — 24 months of green performance is decisive
- B) Retender immediately — an 18% price premium is indefensible
- C) Restructure: negotiate a shorter renewal at benchmarked rates with an explicit knowledge-transfer and documentation obligation, aligning contract end with the legacy decommissioning timeline and preserving retender leverage — deferring full retender until the transformation peak has passed
- D) Extend by 12 months and defer the decision until the transformation completes

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Synthesis means every piece of evidence shapes the answer. Option C uses all six facts: the green performance record justifies continuing; the scope-shrinkage signal and price premium justify renegotiating; the undocumented legacy knowledge is converted from a hostage situation into a contractual obligation; the retender cost and transformation clash justify deferral of competition, not avoidance of it; and the decommissioning strategy sets the contract length. Option A reads only the dashboard. Option B reads only the invoice. Option D spends 12 months of leverage to buy information already held. This tests synthesising conflicting evidence into a recommendation where every fact does work, rather than being averaged away.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate isolates a shared cause from a pattern that spans a portfolio**, rather than reasoning at single-service level.
- **Watch for whether a candidate audits the evidence behind a rating or conclusion** before accepting it, especially one that flatters.
- **Note whether a candidate reconciles conflicting evidence by finding a hypothesis that explains every fact, including non-failures.**
- **Note whether a candidate's recommendation uses every piece of conflicting evidence** rather than favouring the evidence that supports a single instinct.
- **Use the multi-constraint scheduling item (Question 10) to observe whether a candidate breaks a tie with risk headroom** once multiple options satisfy the stated constraints.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that trusts an aggregate without decomposing it.**
- **Crediting an answer that reads obligations into a contract or policy that the text does not contain.**
- **Missing when a candidate converts evidential weakness into an accusation of bad faith**, rather than a calibrated "reassess".
- **Accepting a diagnosis that smuggles in an unrelated agenda** ("this incident proves we need my preferred architecture").
- **Rewarding an answer that stops at the first constraint-satisfying option** without checking for a more robust alternative.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and insightfully a candidate works with quantitative information of the kind this role produces and consumes. At senior level the arithmetic remains deliberately unexotic — percentages, weighted averages, rates, ratios, trends, and compound effects — but the data arrives aggregated, multi-sourced, and occasionally constructed to mislead, and the credited answers require interpretation as much as computation: knowing which figure answers the question, which comparison is legitimate, and which conclusion the numbers do and do not license.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions built around tables, charts, and data-rich scenarios. Calculators are normally permitted, and scoring profiles both accuracy and pace against a senior norm group.

Numeracy matters at this level because the role summary makes data analytics a named expectation: the IT service reporting skill at this level is "use data analytics skills to make decisions that enhance business performance" — not produce reports, but make decisions. The senior manager is often the last line of numerical defence: analysts compute, suppliers report, finance summarises, but the senior manager who signs the board paper is accountable when the weighted average was unweighted or the baseline was cherry-picked.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Weighted analysis and aggregation** maps to the **IT service reporting** skill — using data analytics to make decisions that enhance business performance.
- **Availability and reliability arithmetic** maps to responsibility for **service availability, performance and process maturity across platforms, channels and services**.
- **Trend, rate, and compound-effect analysis** maps to the **Ownership and topic** skill and the **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill.
- **Benchmark and comparison discipline** maps to the **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill: negotiating with suppliers requires knowing when a comparison is like-for-like.
- **Investment and benefits arithmetic** maps to the **Strategic thinking** skill.
- **Compliance and risk quantification** maps to the **Asset and configuration management** skill at this level.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Weighted availability across platforms**

An estate comprises three platforms. Last month: Platform A hosted 20 services at 99.8% average availability; Platform B hosted 30 services at 99.5%; Platform C hosted 10 services at 98.9%. Weighting by service count, what was the estate's average availability?

- A) 99.40%
- B) 99.50%
- C) 99.60%
- D) 99.35%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weight each platform by its service count: (20×99.8 + 30×99.5 + 10×98.9) ÷ 60 = 5,970 ÷ 60 = 99.5%. The unweighted average (99.8+99.5+98.9)÷3 = 99.4% (option A) understates the estate here because the weakest platform is also the smallest. This tests basic weighted aggregation, and a strong candidate's answer notes that even the correct 99.5% is decision-useful only if service count is the right weight — weighting by user volume or criticality could tell a different story.

**Question 2 (easy) — Downtime budget for a compound target**

A critical service must achieve 99.9% availability over a 90-day quarter, measured continuously (24 hours). How many minutes of total downtime does the target permit?

- A) About 65 minutes
- B) About 130 minutes
- C) About 43 minutes
- D) About 195 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total minutes = 90×24×60 = 129,600. Permitted downtime = 129,600 × 0.001 ≈ 130 minutes. Option C is the monthly 99.9% budget — a classic period slip. This tests period conversion fluency, and a strong candidate's answer notes a quarterly 130-minute budget means one bad four-hour incident spends nearly two quarters' allowance.

**Question 3 (easy) — Percentage change with a base shift**

Incident volume across channels was 6,400 in Q1 and 5,440 in Q2, after a problem-management initiative. Meanwhile, active users grew from 80,000 to 85,000. What happened to incidents per 1,000 users?

- A) Fell from 80 to 64
- B) Fell from 80 to about 68
- C) Fell by 15%
- D) Rose slightly because users grew

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Q1 rate = 6,400 ÷ 80 = 80 per 1,000 users. Q2 rate = 5,440 ÷ 85 = 64 per 1,000. The rate fell from 80 to 64 — a 20% improvement, better than the raw 15% volume drop because the user base grew while volume fell. This tests normalising when both numerator and denominator move — a strong candidate's answer notes the normalised 20% is the fair measure of the initiative's success, provided the basis is shown.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reading a supplier cost table**

| Supplier | Annual cost | Tickets handled | OLA achievement |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | £480,000 | 24,000 | 94% |
| Q | £300,000 | 12,000 | 96% |
| R | £150,000 | 10,000 | 88% |

Which supplier has the lowest cost per ticket?

- A) P
- B) Q
- C) R
- D) P and R are equal

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** P: 480,000÷24,000=£20. Q: 300,000÷12,000=£25. R: 150,000÷10,000=£15. R is cheapest per ticket, and also the worst performer. This tests basic unit-cost calculation, and a strong candidate's answer draws the decision-grade question the table poses: whether R's £5-per-ticket saving is worth an eight-point OLA gap, which depends on what R's tickets are.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Compound availability of dependent components**

A citizen journey requires, in series: the web front end (99.9% available), the identity service (99.8%), the application tier (99.7%), and the payments gateway (99.5%). Failures are independent. What is the approximate end-to-end availability of the journey?

- A) 99.5% — the weakest link determines the chain
- B) About 98.9%
- C) About 99.2%
- D) 99.7% — the average of the four

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Series dependencies multiply: 0.999 × 0.998 × 0.997 × 0.995 ≈ 0.98904, about 98.9%. A shortcut: sum the unavailabilities (0.1+0.2+0.3+0.5=1.1%) for a fast first-order estimate. Option A states the common intuition the arithmetic refutes: chains are weaker than their weakest link. This tests recognising that an SLA promising 99.5% on this journey is architecturally unkeepable even though every component beats 99.5%, and a strong candidate's answer identifies the highest-leverage component by absolute contribution to exposure.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Trend projection with a capacity ceiling**

Authentication transactions are growing 8% per quarter and currently run at 2.0 million per quarter. The identity platform's tested capacity is 2.6 million per quarter. If growth continues, during which quarter from now does demand first exceed capacity?

- A) Quarter 2
- B) Quarter 3
- C) Quarter 4
- D) Quarter 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compound: Q1 2.16m, Q2 2.333m, Q3 2.520m, Q4 2.721m — first crossing of 2.6m. This tests compound growth projection against a ceiling, and a strong candidate's answer treats the crossing quarter as a present procurement decision made with runway, rather than a future incident — and notes that in a real paper, the recommendation would state the crossing quarter under multiple growth-rate scenarios.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Benchmark comparison discipline**

A supplier defends their £20 cost per ticket by citing an industry benchmark of £22. The benchmark covers full-service desks including telephony handling; the supplier handles only pre-triaged second-line tickets (the organisation's own desk performs triage at £4 per ticket); and the benchmark year predates a market-wide 5% price fall. What is the like-for-like comparison?

- A) £20 vs £22 — the supplier is 9% below benchmark, as they claim
- B) £24 vs £20.90 — adding the triage cost and deflating the benchmark shows the true combined cost is about 15% above the adjusted benchmark
- C) £20 vs £20.90 — only the price fall adjustment is legitimate
- D) £16 vs £22 — the triage cost should be subtracted from the supplier's figure

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Make both sides commensurable: full-service cost = 20 + 4 = £24; deflated benchmark = 22 × 0.95 = £20.90; comparing gives roughly 15% above the adjusted benchmark — the reverse of the supplier's claim. Option A accepts a mismatched comparison; option C corrects only one distortion; option D adjusts in the wrong direction. This tests negotiation arithmetic — every benchmark carries a scope and a date, and whoever controls the adjustments controls the conclusion.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Risk-weighted expected loss**

A register quantifies three risks for investment prioritisation. R1: probability 20% per year, impact £500,000. R2: probability 5% per year, impact £3,000,000. R3: probability 50% per year, impact £150,000. Mitigations costing £60,000 each would halve the probability of any one risk. Ranked by expected annual loss reduction per pound of mitigation, which risk should be mitigated first?

- A) R1
- B) R2
- C) R3
- D) All three are equal value

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Expected annual loss (EAL): R1 = 0.20×500,000=£100,000, halving saves £50,000. R2 = 0.05×3,000,000=£150,000, halving saves £75,000. R3 = 0.50×150,000=£75,000, halving saves £37,500. R2 gives the highest return per £60,000 spent. This tests expected-value reasoning cutting through both raw-probability and raw-impact intuitions, and a strong candidate's answer names the criterion's limit: low-probability, high-impact risks can warrant mitigation beyond their EAL ranking where the impact threatens continuity.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Interpreting a maturity-improvement trend**

Process maturity scores (1–5 scale), assessed annually:

| Process | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incident | 3.0 | 3.4 | 3.8 |
| Problem | 2.0 | 2.2 | 2.3 |
| Change | 3.5 | 3.6 | 3.6 |
| Asset | 2.5 | 3.0 | 3.5 |
| Continuity | 2.0 | 2.0 | 1.9 |

Which statement best characterises the three-year picture?

- A) All processes are improving steadily
- B) Average maturity rose from 2.6 to 3.02, but the average conceals divergence: incident and asset improved strongly, problem's gains are decelerating, change has plateaued, and continuity has stagnated then slipped
- C) The programme has failed — continuity got worse
- D) Change management is the priority because it has the highest score

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Averages: Year 1 = 2.6, Year 3 = 3.02 — genuine overall improvement. Row-level: incident and asset improved strongly; problem's increments are shrinking; change is flat; continuity has not improved in two years and is now declining. Option A is contradicted by two rows; option C damns a broadly successful programme for its weakest row; option D confuses highest score with priority. This tests reading level and row-level divergence together — a strong candidate's answer flags continuity as the priority precisely because its weakness stays invisible until an incident makes it catastrophic.

**Question 10 (hard) — Channel-shift business case with second-order effects**

A channel-shift initiative would move 200,000 annual transactions from phone (cost £6 each) to digital (cost £0.80 each). Implementation costs £400,000. 15% of shifted transactions are expected to fail digitally and return to the phone channel, incurring both costs; phone-channel fixed costs mean only 70% of the per-transaction phone saving is realisable in year one. What is the approximate realisable year-one net benefit?

- A) £640,000
- B) £240,000
- C) About £129,000
- D) About £217,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Naive saving: 200,000 × £5.20 = £1,040,000; naive net = £640,000 (option A — the figure that reaches boards and later embarrasses them). Correcting for failed shifts (successful shifts 170,000 × £5.20 = £884,000, minus 30,000 × £0.80 = £24,000 extra cost) gives £860,000. Applying the 70% realisability to the phone-side saving and subtracting digital-side costs and the failed-shift cost gives a realisable saving around £554,000, netting to roughly £127,000–154,000 after implementation cost, closest to option C. This tests building a realisable, not naive, business case layer by layer — a strong candidate's answer names the general pattern: every channel-shift case has a naive number, a volume-corrected number, and a realisable number, and the gap between them is where transformation credibility is won or lost.

**Question 11 (hard) — Detecting a misleading baseline**

A supplier's renewal proposal claims: "We have improved P1 resolution times by 40% over the contract term." Their data: contract year 1 average P1 resolution 10.0 hours (50 incidents); year 3 average 6.0 hours (50 incidents). Checking the record: year 1 included a single catastrophic 200-hour incident, without which its average across the remaining 49 P1s was 6.1 hours. What is the fair characterisation of the trend?

- A) The 40% improvement claim stands — the data is the data
- B) Excluding the outlier, underlying P1 performance is essentially flat (6.1 → 6.0 hours, about 2%), and the claimed improvement is almost entirely one year-1 outlier's arithmetic
- C) Performance has worsened because year 3 had more incidents
- D) The outlier should be included but the improvement restated as 20%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Year 1 without the outlier: 6.1 hours across 49 incidents; Year 3: 6.0 hours across 50 — essentially flat, about 2% improvement. Option A confuses "computed correctly" with "characterised fairly" — means are correct and misleading simultaneously when distributions are skewed. Option C misreads incident count (no increase; and count is a different metric anyway). This tests outlier decomposition, and a strong candidate's answer notes the constructive addition: a supplier who quietly banks an outlier's disappearance as "improvement" has also quietly stopped talking about what caused it.

**Question 12 (hard) — Capacity and cost of a resilience commitment**

The business wants the casework service's availability raised from 99.5% to 99.9% (24×7, monthly measurement). Engineering analysis: the current single-region deployment cannot exceed 99.6%; an active-passive dual-region deployment achieves 99.9% and adds £30,000 per month in run costs; an active-active deployment achieves 99.95% and adds £55,000 per month. The service's estimated cost of downtime is £2,000 per minute. Using expected downtime cost avoided versus added run cost, which option is best justified?

- A) Active-passive: comfortably clears the 99.9% requirement at the lower committed cost, with the increment to active-active resting on fragile marginal assumptions
- B) Active-active: the higher net benefit on raw expected value makes it unambiguously preferred
- C) Active-passive: about £56,000 net monthly benefit, with no further analysis needed
- D) Neither: both cost more than they save

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Monthly minutes = 43,200. Current achievable downtime (99.6%): 172.8 minutes ≈ £345,600 expected cost. Active-passive (99.9%): 43.2 minutes ≈ £86,400; saving avoided £259,200; net of £30,000 run cost ≈ £229,200. Active-active (99.95%): 21.6 minutes ≈ £43,200; saving avoided £302,400; net of £55,000 ≈ £247,400. Raw totals slightly favour active-active, but the marginal increment — 21.6 minutes for £25,000 more — rests entirely on the £2,000-per-minute estimate holding for marginal minutes, which is rarely linear. The requirement is 99.9%; active-passive meets it at the lower committed cost. This tests converting nines to minutes to money and evaluating marginal rather than total benefit — a strong candidate's answer treats 99.95% as a separate decision needing its own case, not an automatic upgrade.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate weights an aggregate correctly** and states which weight matches the decision at hand.
- **Watch for whether a candidate converts an availability percentage to minutes** rather than reasoning only in percentage points.
- **Note whether a candidate normalises a trend when both numerator and denominator move.**
- **Note whether a candidate builds a realisable, layered business case** rather than presenting a naive gross figure.
- **Use the misleading-baseline item (Question 11) to observe whether a candidate decomposes an outlier** rather than accepting a headline percentage improvement.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that accepts a naive business case without correcting for failure rates or fixed-cost stickiness.**
- **Crediting an average without weighting, or weighted by the wrong variable.**
- **Missing when a candidate treats a moved average as a trend** without checking for an outlier.
- **Accepting an SLA or target set at the weakest component's level** for a series-dependent journey.
- **Rewarding a linear projection of a compounding trend.**

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures precision reading and disciplined inference using documents of the kind this role turns on. At senior level, the passages grow more consequential and more layered: contract schedules with interacting clauses, policy extracts with exceptions and delegations, strategy documents whose commitments constrain the organisation's options, assurance reports whose careful hedges carry the real message, and stakeholder correspondence in which position, interest, and threat must be distinguished. The dominant format remains statement classification — **True**, **False**, **Cannot say** — supplemented by inference questions, best-summary questions, and clear-writing judgement.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 statements across several passages. The governing discipline — answer from the passage alone — bites harder at senior level, because a candidate's expertise generates confident expectations the passage may not share, and senior passages are drafted, as real senior documents are, with qualifications that reward the reader who notices exactly which claim was and was not made.

Senior service management is conducted substantially through high-stakes text: negotiating contract language where a clause's trigger conditions are worth six figures, reading assurance reports where "we were unable to verify" differs decisively from "we found no issues", and evaluating strategies whose commitments were drafted to be quotable.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precision reading of contractual text** maps to the **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill — negotiation leverage at senior level is substantially textual.
- **True/False/Cannot say discipline** maps to the **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill and to evidence handling in incident and compliance reviews.
- **Inference under qualification** maps to the **Service management framework knowledge** skill at expert level.
- **Strategy comprehension** maps to the **Strategic thinking** skill.
- **Reading stakeholder correspondence** maps to the duty to deal with challenging situations.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to the **IT service reporting** skill at decision level and the **User focus** leadership expectation.

### Practice questions

For Questions 1 to 5, the candidate reads this passage.

> **Extract from the Managed Hosting Agreement, Schedule 4 (Service Continuity)**
> The Supplier shall maintain a disaster recovery capability enabling restoration of Core Services within eight hours of invocation. Invocation may be declared only by the Customer's nominated representatives, listed in Annex B. The Supplier shall test the disaster recovery capability annually; the Customer may observe such tests and may, no more than once per contract year, require an additional test at the Customer's expense. Where a test identifies material deficiencies, the Supplier shall remediate within ninety days and retest at its own expense. Failure to complete remediation within ninety days entitles the Customer to withhold ten per cent of monthly charges until retest confirms remediation. Core Services are defined in Annex A; for the avoidance of doubt, reporting and analytics services are excluded.

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The Supplier must restore reporting services within eight hours of a disaster recovery invocation."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The eight-hour obligation attaches to Core Services, and the final sentence removes ambiguity: "for the avoidance of doubt, reporting and analytics services are excluded." The passage contradicts the statement. This tests reading obligations through their defining terms — a strong candidate's answer notes that this is the clause that surprises stakeholders who assumed "everything comes back in eight hours".

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Any employee of the Customer may declare a disaster recovery invocation."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The schedule states invocation "may be declared only by the Customer's nominated representatives, listed in Annex B." "Only" restricts declaration to a defined list, contradicting "any employee". This tests noticing a restrictive word, and a strong candidate's answer notes the operational consequence: invocation authority lists fail precisely when needed, at the moment the nominated representatives are unreachable.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "If the annual test identifies material deficiencies and the Supplier has not remediated within ninety days, the Customer may terminate the agreement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The schedule specifies one remedy for the ninety-day remediation failure: withholding ten per cent of monthly charges. It says nothing about termination, in either direction — not supported, but not contradicted either, since termination rights typically live elsewhere in an agreement this extract cannot rule out. This tests recognising when a text neither confirms nor excludes a claim — marking True imports an unstated remedy; marking False assumes this schedule exhausts all remedies, which it does not state.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "If the Customer requires an additional disaster recovery test beyond the annual test, the Customer pays for it — unless it is a retest following failed remediation, which the Supplier funds."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two cost rules combine accurately: Customer-requested extra tests are Customer-funded ("at the Customer's expense"), and remediation retests are Supplier-funded ("at its own expense"). The statement's "unless" correctly assigns the exception. This tests integrating provisions that sit sentences apart into a single accurate rule — a skill real contract use requires, since real questions rarely map to single sentences.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following can properly be inferred from the passage?

- A) The Supplier has never failed a disaster recovery test
- B) A Customer who is not observing tests and not checking Annex B currency could hold a contractual right to eight-hour recovery that fails in practice without any contractual remedy being triggered
- C) The ten per cent withholding is the Customer's sole remedy for continuity failures
- D) Annual testing is industry best practice

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A asserts historical fact the passage cannot supply. Option C converts silence into exclusivity, contradicted by Question 3's reasoning. Option D imports external knowledge. Option B is built entirely from the passage's own machinery: the recovery obligation is real, but its verification depends on tests the Customer merely may observe, and invocation depends on a list the passage gives no mechanism for keeping current. This tests composing the text's own provisions to expose a risk the drafting leaves open, rather than adding external facts.

For Questions 6 to 8, the candidate reads this passage.

> **Extract from the Internal Audit report: IT Service Continuity (Final)**
> Overall assurance rating: Limited. We found that continuity plans exist for all twelve critical services and are subject to annual review. However, we were unable to verify that plans for four services reflect the current supplier arrangements, as the relevant contract documentation was not provided within the audit period. Testing evidence was available for seven services; of these, five tests met their recovery objectives. Management has accepted all four recommendations, with implementation dates between three and nine months from publication. We did not examine cyber-specific recovery arrangements, which fall within a separate review scheduled for next quarter.

**Question 6 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The audit found that four services' continuity plans do not reflect current supplier arrangements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report says something importantly different: it "was unable to verify" this, because documentation was not provided in time. Inability to verify is not a finding of deficiency. The statement converts an evidential gap into an adverse finding, which the text contradicts as a characterisation of what the audit found. This tests one of the most consequential distinctions in assurance literacy — a strong candidate's answer notes that boards act on a candidate's characterisation of a report, so the characterisation must match the text's precision.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "At least two of the tested services failed to meet their recovery objectives."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "Testing evidence was available for seven services; of these, five tests met their recovery objectives." Seven tested, five met, therefore two did not. This tests extracting arithmetic embedded in prose without importing colour or losing precision — and a strong candidate's answer notes what cannot be inferred: nothing about the five services with no testing evidence at all, which is its own assurance gap.

**Question 8 (hard) — Best summary for an audit committee briefing**

The report needs summarising in two sentences for an audit committee pre-brief. Which is best?

- A) "Audit rated continuity assurance Limited: plans exist and are reviewed for all twelve critical services, but verification gaps (four services' supplier currency unverified due to late documentation), incomplete testing coverage (evidence for seven of twelve, with five meeting recovery objectives), and pending recommendations underpin the rating. Cyber recovery was out of scope and is reviewed next quarter — so this rating should not be read as assurance on cyber resilience."
- B) "Audit found our continuity planning fundamentally sound, with all plans in place and reviewed annually. A few documentation and testing details are being tidied up under accepted recommendations."
- C) "Audit rated us Limited, which is a serious criticism of the continuity function. Four plans are out of date, five of twelve tests failed, and cyber recovery was not even examined."
- D) "The audit is broadly positive but the Limited rating reflects auditor conservatism. Management has accepted the recommendations to close the matter."

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option A transmits the rating, the evidence structure beneath it, and the scope boundary, without softening or hardening. It attributes the Limited rating to its real drivers in the report's own terms and flags the scope exclusion's implication. Option B softens systematically. Option C hardens systematically, repeating the "out of date" error and miscounting the testing base. Option D editorialises the rating away. This tests summarising checkably against the source, line by line.

Statement: "If the spending review settlement is lower than planned, the 2028 consolidation deadline may move, but the commitment to consolidate remains."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "The timeline, though not the ambition, may be revised" maps exactly onto the statement's two halves. **Question 11 (hard) — Inference**

Which conclusion can properly be drawn from the passage?

- A) A service whose peak demand runs continuously year-round could not have its migration scheduled compliantly, exposing a tension the strategy does not resolve
- B) The consolidation will reduce support costs
- C) Services retired during the strategy period will lose support before decommissioning
- D) The spending review settlement will be lower than planned

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option B asserts an outcome the passage never claims. Option C contradicts the final sentence. Option D converts a contingency into a prediction. Option A follows from the text's own logical structure: the universal commitment to schedule migrations avoiding peak periods presupposes each service has non-peak periods; for a service with continuous peak demand, no compliant schedule exists. This tests stress-testing a universal commitment by its edge case without asserting the edge case exists.

**Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement**

The consolidation programme has slipped: the second migration wave will now overlap with one service's statutory peak, contrary to the strategy's commitment. The service's director needs notifying. Which opening paragraph is best?

- A) "Due to programme replanning necessitated by upstream dependency variances, wave two migration activities have been rescheduled into Q3, which we appreciate may present certain operational considerations for your area during the statutory period."
- B) "I need to tell you about a problem, propose two options, and ask for a decision by Friday. The rescheduled second migration wave now overlaps your statutory peak (August), which breaks the strategy's commitment to avoid peak periods. Option one: defer your service's migration to October, keeping you on the legacy platform eight weeks longer at a known stability risk we can quantify. Option two: proceed in August with enhanced support cover and a tested rollback plan. My recommendation is option one; either way, the choice should be yours and I will resource it fully."
- C) "Unfortunately the migration now clashes with your peak period. These things happen on complex programmes and we will do our best to minimise disruption. Let us know if you have concerns."
- D) "As you may be aware, programme timelines have evolved. We remain fully committed to the strategy's principles and to working collaboratively with your team throughout the transition period."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A director receiving bad news needs the fact, its significance, the options with their risks, a recommendation, and clarity about who decides. Option B delivers all of this early and plainly, naming the breach against the strategy explicitly. Option A is evasion register. Option C normalises the clash and transfers the burden. Option D says nothing at all with maximal warmth. This tests writing that survives hostile reading — the credited draft names the breach in the candidate's own words before it appears in someone else's escalation.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to evaluate possible responses — most effective, least effective, full rankings, or independent effectiveness ratings. At senior level the scenarios change register: fewer questions about handling a single ticket or user, more about handling competing accountabilities — a supplier relationship against a compliance duty, a director's preference against user evidence, a team's morale against a performance problem, this quarter's credibility against next year's capability. The response options are correspondingly harder to separate.

The typical format is an online assessment of 15 to 30 scenarios, generously timed, scored against the judgements of experienced senior practitioners and the organisation's professional behaviours: user focus, ownership, honest communication, collaboration, appropriate escalation, inclusion, and the behaviours of leadership.

The role summary is a catalogue of judgement-intensive expectations that no technical credential evidences: negotiate and influence, deal with challenging situations and remove blockers, take accountability for issues and proactively search for potential problems, champion user research, represent users internally, achieve excellent user outcomes.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Influence and negotiation choices** map to the **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill.
- **Accountability choices** map to the **Ownership and topic** skill.
- **User-championing choices** map to the **User focus** skill.
- **Strategy and standards choices** map to the **Strategic thinking** skill.
- **Data-integrity choices** map to the **IT service reporting** skill.
- **Compliance and risk choices** map to the **Asset and configuration management** skill.
- **Solution-production choices** map to the **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The director who wants the finding softened**

A quarterly service report includes a finding that the flagship digital service's completion rate for assisted-digital users is 61%, against 89% for unassisted users — a gap the analysis attributes partly to a support-channel change the department championed. The service's director asks for a reframe as "an improvement journey" and to drop the channel-change attribution "since causation isn't proven". What is the most effective response?

- A) Comply — causation genuinely isn't proven, and the director owns the narrative
- B) Refuse and escalate to the director's superior, citing reporting integrity
- C) Keep the figure and the gap prominent; present the attribution with calibrated language ("the timing and pattern are consistent with the channel change; causation is not established") and offer the director a joint next step — a targeted piece of research to settle attribution — so the report stays accurate while the open question is honestly framed
- D) Publish the original wording unchanged without responding to the director

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The director's two requests differ in legitimacy. Recalibrating the causal claim is partially legitimate; softening the 28-point completion gap for the most support-dependent users is not. Option C gives the director everything accuracy permits, and converts a narrative dispute into an evidence plan. Option A trades user visibility for comfort. Option B escalates before influence has been tried. Option D wins silently and loses the relationship pointlessly. This tests reporting integrity, calibrated claims, and influence before escalation.

**Question 2 (easy) — The blocked migration**

A critical service migration is blocked: the security team has not completed its assessment, now three weeks late, and the migration window closes in ten days — missing it costs six months and £200k in extended legacy support. The security team lead says they are under-resourced and this assessment is behind two others in their queue. What is the most effective action?

- A) Instruct the programme to proceed — the security team had ample notice, and the delay is their failure
- B) Accept the six-month delay and document that security caused it
- C) Work the blocker directly: establish with the security lead what a sufficient assessment requires, whether scope can be split, whether the programme can supply evidence-gathering effort to accelerate their work, and — if genuine resource conflict remains — take the prioritisation trade-off to the level that owns all three queued assessments, with the £200k and six-month consequence quantified
- D) Email the security team's director copying own management, noting the cost of delay and requesting urgent reprioritisation

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Removing blockers has an ordering: understand, shrink, resource, and only then escalate carrying numbers. Option C runs the full sequence. Option A converts a schedule risk into an unbounded organisational one. Option B mitigates nothing. Option D escalates first, skipping steps that might solve the problem faster and cheaper. This tests blocker removal as craft, collaboration with constrained teams, and escalation as a last resort with evidence.

**Question 3 (easy) — The high performer who hoards**

The strongest service manager personally handles every major incident, every senior stakeholder call, and every complex supplier negotiation on their patch. Results are excellent; their two deputies are visibly stalling in development, and the manager's own workload is unsustainable — no leave taken in eight months. What is the most effective intervention?

- A) Leave it alone — results are excellent, and intervention risks breaking what works
- B) Redistribute the portfolio by instruction, transferring the stakeholder calls and negotiations to the deputies immediately
- C) Address it as a development conversation with structural follow-through: name the pattern and its three costs (deputies' growth, single-point-of-failure risk, sustainability), agree which responsibilities the deputies will lead with coaching rather than doing, and put the manager's own next stretch — and their leave — into the plan
- D) Ask the deputies to be more assertive in claiming work

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Excellent current results are being purchased with three accruing liabilities: capability debt, continuity risk, and an exhaustion trajectory. Option C addresses the pattern at the right level, honestly, with structural follow-through. Option A books today's results and inherits tomorrow's collapse. Option B teaches that excellence attracts punishment. Option D asks the least powerful actors to solve a dynamic the manager controls. This tests developing others, proactive risk management, and sustainable performance.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The user research that threatens the deadline**

Two weeks before a committed go-live announced by a director to the board, user research on the new unified support portal finds that users over 65, 30% of the service's users, fail task completion at twice the rate of other groups, primarily due to an authentication step. Fixing it properly is estimated at six weeks. The programme manager proposes going live on schedule and "iterating post-launch"; the user researcher argues launch would knowingly ship a service that excludes the group most dependent on it. What is the most effective position to take?

- A) Support the on-schedule launch — the board commitment is fixed, and post-launch iteration is legitimate agile practice
- B) Insist launch is delayed six weeks, and inform the director the research makes this unavoidable
- C) Interrogate the options space before positioning: can the existing telephone channel carry affected users at launch with measured capacity, can the authentication step be modified short of the full fix, what does "iterate post-launch" mean concretely — dates, funding, accountability? Then take the strongest viable protection for affected users to the director with the research evidence, the options, and a recommendation — before the go/no-go becomes binary
- D) Convene the programme board and let it vote

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario stages user championing against a delivery commitment, and the credited move is to refuse the binary while it can still be refused. Option C tests whether the dilemma is real and takes options with a recommendation upward. Option A adopts the programme's framing uninterrogated. Option B may be where the candidate ends up, but reaching it without exhausting the options space converts a problem-solver into a blocker. Option D outsources a judgement that is the candidate's own to form. This tests user championing with evidence, option generation before escalation, and managing upward with recommendations rather than problems.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The compliance drift a friend is causing**

A fellow senior manager — a longtime ally — runs a portfolio in which shared platform data reveals fourteen services have quietly fallen out of the mandatory patching compliance window, with exemption records referencing a "temporary deferral" agreed eight months ago and never revisited. Nothing has gone wrong yet. What is the most effective action?

- A) Say nothing — it is their portfolio, and surfacing it would damage a relationship this role's own work depends on
- B) Raise it with them directly and promptly: share the data, ask what is being missed, and give them the opportunity to fix it themselves — while being clear that if the exposure stands, it needs to go on the risk register through the proper route regardless of who raises it
- C) Report it to the compliance function immediately without telling them, since friendship must not influence compliance
- D) Mention it casually over coffee and leave the follow-up to them

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario tests whether relationships bend standards, and whether standards require relationship destruction — both errors are available. Option B threads it: the colleague hears it first, from the candidate, with data; a legitimate explanation is genuinely allowed for; and the boundary is stated plainly. Option A prices the relationship above fourteen services' unpatched exposure. Option C spends trust unnecessarily. Option D performs the raising without substance. This tests integrity with relationship intelligence and compliance ownership beyond formal remit.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: the failing improvement programme**

A sponsored continual improvement programme is six months in: two of five workstreams are delivering well, two are behind, and one — process automation — has consumed 40% of the budget for no deployed output, its lead insisting a breakthrough is close. The programme board meets next week. Which response is the LEAST effective?

- A) Commission a rapid, evidence-based checkpoint on the automation workstream before the board: what has been built, what remains, what the honest completion estimate is — and present the findings whichever way they fall
- B) Present the programme honestly at the board: two green, two amber with recovery plans, one red under review, with the budget position stated
- C) Rebalance in advance: pause the automation workstream pending the checkpoint, and redirect its uncommitted budget to the delivering workstreams
- D) Give the automation lead one more quarter discreetly, holding it at amber for the board, since a red rating now would undermine confidence in the whole programme — and the breakthrough may land

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Option D re-rates a red workstream amber for presentational reasons, corrupting the governance signal, and its justification is the sunk-cost fallacy wearing optimism. Options A, B and C are all effective and sequence naturally: checkpoint informs rebalancing informs honest presentation. This tests whether a candidate identifies the option that manages perception where the others manage reality — the fatal option in least-effective questions is rarely the laziest one.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The channel the strategy forgot**

The organisation's digital strategy commits to "digital by default", and the transformation programme proposes closing the counter service at three regional offices, citing 4% and falling usage. Reviewing the evidence: the 4% includes a high concentration of users with complex cases, limited digital access, or accessibility needs, and counter-service users' case-resolution rate is the highest of any channel. The programme's benefits case books £1.8m from the closures, already counted in the directorate's savings target. What is the most effective position?

- A) Support the closures — 4% usage cannot justify three offices, and the strategy is explicit
- B) Oppose the closures outright as incompatible with inclusive service provision
- C) Reframe the decision with the full evidence: quantify who the 4% are and what happens to their outcomes under closure (including the cost of failure demand returning through other channels), test intermediate options — consolidated counters, appointment-based service, assisted-digital provision in partner locations — and require the benefits case to net off the costs of serving displaced users properly, so the decision is made on true numbers whichever way it goes
- D) Escalate to the strategy's owner and request an exemption for the three offices

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C converts "small percentage" into "specific people with the least channel choice and the most complex cases", costs the failure demand the benefits case ignores, and refuses the false binary between three full offices and nothing. Option B positions as sentiment against strategy and loses. Option A mistakes a strategy's default for its destination. Option D leads with escalation before decision-shaping analysis. This tests user advocacy through evidence, strategic thinking as evaluation, and benefits-case integrity.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The supplier's principal engineer**

Mid-contract, a platform supplier's principal engineer — whose knowledge underpins the two most fragile services in the estate — makes contact privately: they are leaving the supplier in eight weeks, believe the supplier will not backfill the role adequately, and wanted this heard directly. The supplier has not disclosed this. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank them, keep the information private until the supplier discloses it, and quietly begin contingency planning
- B) Immediately confront the supplier's account director with the information and demand a retention counter-offer
- C) Thank them and act on the substance without weaponising the source: accelerate the knowledge-capture work that should already be running as standard continuity practice, and raise key-person dependency generically at the next service review — pressing for the supplier's succession plan for critical roles — while treating the specific disclosure with the discretion it was offered in
- D) Ask the engineer to delay their departure in exchange for a future role

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C acts fully on the substance while protecting the source: knowledge-capture is defensible right now as standard practice, and key-person dependency is a legitimate standing agenda item at any service review. Option A gets the discretion right and the urgency wrong. Option B spends the source immediately and demands the wrong remedy. Option D is an integrity trap — soliciting a supplier's employee crosses conflict-of-interest lines. This tests acting on early warning through legitimate channels while protecting a confidential source.

**Question 9 (hard) — The metric that argues against a candidate's own case**

Four months' work has gone into a case for a £900k investment in platform resilience, drawing on last year's outage record: 14 significant incidents, 41 hours of cumulative downtime. The final data refresh before the investment board arrives, and the current year to date is dramatically better: 3 incidents, 6 hours — driven, the analysis suggests, by workarounds and luck (two near-misses were caught by a single engineer's manual intervention) rather than structural improvement. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Present last year's data as the basis of the case — it represents the structural reality; this year is a small sample distorted by luck
- B) Present both years in full, lead with the improvement, and argue the case on its true basis: the near-miss analysis showing that current performance depends on manual intervention and fortune rather than structure — converting the inconvenient data into the strongest form of the argument, and accepting the outcome if the board is not persuaded
- C) Delay the board submission until the data supports the case again
- D) Present this year's data but adjust it to include the two near-misses as incidents, since they were incidents in all but name

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B holds the line and discovers the honest case is stronger, not weaker: "our current stability rests on one engineer's manual catches" is precisely the risk profile a resilience investment addresses. Option A collapses the moment any board member asks "and this year?" Option C games the timeline. Option D fabricates a reclassification discoverable by anyone who reads the incident register. This tests reporting integrity under self-interested pressure and the courage to let a true case stand or fall on its truth.

**Question 10 (hard) — The restructure that lands on a candidate's team**

A directorate restructure will merge the service management function with another team, and the leaked-then-confirmed design shows the function losing four posts, with affected individuals not yet identified. The team's rumour mill is running; two of the best people confide they are already interviewing elsewhere; delivery is visibly slowing; and formal consultation does not begin for three weeks, before which individual outcomes may not be discussed. What is the most effective leadership approach?

- A) Say nothing until formal consultation — the rules constrain, and anything said could create legal risk
- B) Privately reassure the best people that they are safe, to stop the exodus of exactly the capability the merged function will need
- C) Communicate maximally within the real constraint: state what is confirmed (the merger, the reduction, the consultation date), what is genuinely undecided (individual outcomes), what process and support will apply, and hold to a rhythm of updates even when the update is "nothing new" — while separately pressing the restructure's owners to compress the pre-consultation limbo, and neither confirming nor denying anyone's individual position
- D) Tell the team the restructure is opposed and being fought to reverse the post reductions

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C maps the real constraint precisely: it discloses confirmed facts whose concealment protects no one, while holding the actual restricted zone — individual outcomes. The update rhythm matters as much as content. Option B is corrosive when discovered and creates the legal risk it tries to avoid. Option A is compliance-shaped abdication. Option D spends credibility on a fight the design has already settled. This tests honest communication inside real constraints and stabilising a team under uncertainty.

**Question 11 (hard) — Ranking: the Monday morning portfolio**

At 08:30 Monday, four items compete: (1) overnight, a supplier's engineer applied an emergency fix to the payments platform outside change control — it worked, service is stable, and the supplier's account manager has already emailed asking to "regularise the paperwork quietly"; (2) the casework service's authentication latency trend has crossed its warning threshold, matching the pattern that preceded last year's three-day degradation; (3) a director being cultivated for a strategic partnership has offered a rare 30-minute slot at 11:00 today; (4) the monthly compliance attestation, personally certifying the portfolio's patching and configuration status, is due to the risk committee by 17:00, and two required platform reports have not arrived. Rank the order in which these should be addressed:

- A) 2, 1, 4, 3
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 4, 1, 3
- D) 3, 2, 1, 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 2 leads: a recognised precursor pattern crossing threshold is a closing prevention window. Item 1 is second: the technical risk is stable, but an uncontrolled change to a payments platform sits unrecorded, and "quietly" is a live integrity probe that a short delay does not much worsen. Item 4 is third: the deadline is hard, but chasing two reports is delegable, and an accurate qualified attestation beats a complete guessed one. Item 3 is last, because it is the only item reschedulable without loss. This tests prevention over reaction, integrity signalling at speed, honest attestation, and relationship management that trusts relationships to survive right priorities.

**Question 12 (hard) — The consensus against the evidence**

Sitting on a cross-government design board deciding the support model for a shared platform serving eleven departments: ten departments' representatives have aligned on a fully-centralised support model, and the chair wants unanimous sign-off today after months of negotiation. The department's own user research, the only substantive research conducted by any participant, shows the centralised model's single generic front door performed poorly for the two departments (this one and one other) whose users have complex, regulated casework needs — the research recommends a thin specialist triage layer, adding modest cost. The other affected department's representative has not read the research and is ready to sign. What is the most effective action?

- A) Sign — ten of eleven departments aligned after months of negotiation is a consensus a single study should not disturb
- B) Refuse to sign and formally dissent, protecting the department's position on the record
- C) Before the vote: brief the other affected department's representative on the research findings as they concern their users, then table the evidence to the board with a specific, costed, minimal amendment — the specialist triage layer — framed as protecting the model's success for the hardest cases rather than reopening the model; if the board still declines, seek an explicit review trigger (post-launch performance data for complex-casework users) in the sign-off rather than a bare dissent
- D) Sign, but implement an unofficial specialist triage arrangement afterwards

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Briefing the other affected representative first converts one department's objection into two affected voices aligned on evidence. Tabling a specific, costed, minimal amendment respects the negotiation. The fallback review trigger is the senior alternative to noble defeat. Option A discards the only research in the room for social proof. Option B protects the record and abandons the outcome. Option D is quiet defection, undermining the shared model actually signed. This tests evidence-based influence, coalition-building with the genuinely affected, and amendment craft over veto reflex.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate refuses a binary framing** by generating intermediate options before positioning.
- **Watch for whether a candidate's answer distinguishes escalation as a decision-support tool** (evidence, options, recommendation) from escalation as complaint or abdication.
- **Note whether a candidate holds a record honest under self-interested pressure** — the retrospective and metric items are designed to surface this.
- **Note whether a candidate protects a confidential source while still acting on the substance of what they disclosed.**
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that escalates before influencing** — directors are for decisions that need their authority, not difficulties the role exists to resolve.
- **Crediting an answer that softens or delays an uncomfortable truth to protect standing.**
- **Missing when a candidate reassures selectively under uncertainty**, making promises they cannot keep.
- **Accepting an answer that confuses a strategy's default with its destination**, treating a general rule as though it has no legitimate exceptions.
- **Rewarding an answer that lets sunk costs argue for continued investment** without a checkpoint on evidence.

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a senior IT service manager: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The through-line is the role itself: absorb conflicting information honestly, quantify before arguing, read exactly what the text says, act within one's authority and be transparent beyond it, and champion the users whose numbers are smallest and whose alternatives are fewest.
