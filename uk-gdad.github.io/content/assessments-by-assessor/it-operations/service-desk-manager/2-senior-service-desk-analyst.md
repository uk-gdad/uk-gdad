## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. For a senior service desk analyst, that means questions built from call monitoring data, script logic, rota constraints, escalation rules, and problem records.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group; many platforms adapt question difficulty, and employers typically receive a profile of speed against accuracy.

Cognitive assessment is particularly relevant at this level because the role adds a supervisory layer to first-line thinking: reasoning about the pattern across calls — which operator is stuck, which symptom is recurring, which queue is drifting — while owning artefacts like the analyst script, whose internal logic must stay consistent as it is updated.

### How this assessment maps to the role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in the role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to the core duty of monitoring inbound calls and to the **Problem management** skill: investigating patterns and trends begins with seeing them.
- **Logical deduction** maps to ownership of the analyst script — a script is a decision tree, and updating it without breaking its branch logic is deduction in earnest.
- **Error checking** maps to **Asset and configuration management** and to the quality role of reviewing analysts' tickets, records, and the script itself for inconsistencies before they propagate.
- **Prioritisation** maps to the duties of supporting operators, intervening in difficult calls, and managing availability.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to the **Problem management** skill and the **Service focus** skill of taking inputs and establishing coherent frameworks.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a monitoring dashboard**

A call monitoring dashboard convention: green = available; amber = on a call under 10 minutes; red = on a call over 10 minutes; grey = logged out. At 10:30 the dashboard shows: Amir — amber, 4 minutes; Beth — red, 14 minutes; Carl — amber, 12 minutes; Dee — grey. Which entry is internally inconsistent?

- A) Amir
- B) Beth
- C) Carl
- D) Dee

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Amir (amber, 4 min), Beth (red, 14 min), and Dee (grey, no call) all match the colour rules. Carl (amber, 12 min) should show red. This tests verifying each record against the stated rule field by field, and a strong candidate's answer notes the display's integrity matters as much as the team's, since a broken monitor misleads exactly the decisions it exists to support.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an intervention rule**

Desk guidance: "A senior analyst should intervene in a live call if the call exceeds 15 minutes AND the analyst has flagged for help, OR if the caller asks for a supervisor." Elena's call is at 18 minutes and she has not flagged for help; the caller has not asked for a supervisor. According to the guidance, must intervention happen?

- A) Yes — the call is over 15 minutes.
- B) Yes — long calls always require intervention.
- C) No — neither condition for mandatory intervention is met.
- D) No — senior analysts never intervene unless asked by the caller.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The AND requires both parts (over 15 minutes and flagged); neither compound condition fires. Option A treats the AND as an OR. Option D invents a rule that does not exist — judgement to check in remains available even without a mandatory trigger. This tests reading a compound condition exactly, the same discipline that governs script branches.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a script update**

A draft script for password resets reads: "Step 1: Verify identity using two approved checks. Step 2: If verification fails, proceed to reset the password. Step 3: If verification succeeds, advise the caller that no reset is possible and close the call. Step 4: Log the contact." What is wrong with the draft?

- A) Step 1 should use three checks, not two.
- B) Steps 2 and 3 have their outcomes reversed.
- C) Step 4 is unnecessary.
- D) Nothing is wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** As drafted, a caller who fails verification gets a reset while a caller who succeeds is refused — the exact inversion of safe practice. This tests tracing a script's logic as a naive reader rather than its author would, since an error here is executed faithfully by every analyst who trusts the script.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across supervisory demands**

It is 11:00 on a busy Wednesday. Four things compete for attention:

1. The dashboard shows a new analyst has been on a call for 22 minutes and has just flagged for help.
2. A manager has asked for the weekly service report by 15:00 today.
3. An analyst has emailed asking to swap their shift next Tuesday.
4. The queue's average wait has crept from 2 to 6 minutes over the past hour.

Which order of attention is most defensible?

- A) 2, 1, 4, 3 — the manager's deadline outranks live calls
- B) 1, 4, 2, 3 — live intervention first, then investigate the queue drift, then the report, then the swap
- C) 4, 1, 2, 3 — queue metrics before individual calls
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — handle requests in the order they arrived

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is urgent and squarely the role's function. Item 4's tripling of average wait is a live service signal worth quick diagnosis before it worsens the afternoon; the report's deadline has slack by comparison. Item 3 concerns next Tuesday. This tests ranking live people first, degrading trends second, deadlines with slack third, future logistics fourth.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Classifying a problem's level**

Problems can be strategic, tactical, or operational. Consider: (i) the ticket tool crashes for all users every time a specific report is run — a workaround exists but wastes 20 minutes daily; (ii) the department's five-year contract for the ticket tool expires next year and the market has changed substantially; (iii) this morning's ticket tool slowness turned out to be one analyst's browser needing a restart. Which classification is most accurate?

- A) (i) operational, (ii) strategic, (iii) not a problem at all — it was an incident, resolved
- B) (i) strategic, (ii) tactical, (iii) operational
- C) (i) tactical, (ii) strategic, (iii) operational
- D) (i) operational, (ii) operational, (iii) tactical

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** (i) is a recurring fault with a known workaround — operational. (ii) concerns a multi-year contract and market change — strategic. (iii) is a single occurrence resolved by a restart with no evidence of recurrence — an incident, not a problem at all. This tests triage: knowing what deserves investigation, at what altitude, and what is simply done, rather than promoting every resolved incident into the problem register.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in coaching data**

First-contact resolution (FCR) rates over three months. Amir: 68%, 70%, 71%. Beth: 74%, 73%, 74%. Carl: 71%, 62%, 54%. Dee: 58%, 63%, 67%. Which reading of the data is most accurate?

- A) Carl is the weakest analyst and Dee the strongest.
- B) Beth is coasting and needs a stretch target.
- C) Carl shows a marked decline that needs a supportive conversation soon; Dee shows strong improvement worth recognising; Amir and Beth are stable.
- D) The differences are noise; no action is needed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Carl's 17-point fall over two months matters more than his current level; Dee's 9-point rise from the lowest starting point is worth recognising. Amir and Beth are stable. Option A judges by the latest month alone, mislabelling Dee "strongest" when her 67% is still below Amir and Beth's. This tests reading trends rather than snapshots.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction across linked tickets**

Escalation guidance: (i) incidents caused by a change in the last five working days go to the change implementer's team; (ii) recurring incidents with no linked change go to problem investigation; (iii) single incidents with no linked change stay in the normal queue. Today is Thursday. The email client has produced the same error for six users this week; the email client was last changed nine working days ago; the network was changed on Monday (three working days ago), but network diagnostics are clean. Where should this cluster go?

- A) To the email change implementer's team, under rule (i)
- B) To the network change implementer's team, under rule (i)
- C) To problem investigation, under rule (ii)
- D) To the normal queue, under rule (iii)

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The email change is outside the five-day window (rule (i) fails). The network change is within the window but has no connecting mechanism to the email symptom (assumption, not deduction). With six users affected this week — clearly recurring — rule (ii) applies. This tests exact time-window arithmetic and refusing to promote a coincidental change into a cause without a connecting mechanism.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking a rota**

A rota must satisfy: at least three analysts on phones at all times between 08:00 and 18:00; every analyst gets a lunch break; the senior counts as cover only during breaks. The draft rota for Monday: Amir 08:00-16:00 (lunch 12:00-13:00), Beth 09:00-17:00 (lunch 12:00-13:00), Carl 10:00-18:00 (lunch 13:00-14:00), Dee 08:00-16:00 (lunch 13:00-14:00). At which time does the rota first break the three-on-phones rule, assuming senior cover for at most one absent analyst at a time?

- A) 08:00
- B) 09:00
- C) 12:00
- D) 17:00

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** At 08:00 only Amir and Dee have started (Beth starts 09:00, Carl 10:00) — two analysts, and 08:00 is not a break, so senior cover cannot legitimately apply. This tests scanning a rota's boundaries — start times, end times, overlapping breaks — since coverage failures live at the edges, not the comfortable middle.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Applied problem solving from mixed evidence**

Since Monday, analysts report the ticket tool "freezes randomly". Facts: (i) freezes affect analysts on both sites; (ii) freezes happen only when attaching files to tickets; (iii) the attachment storage service was migrated at the weekend; (iv) two analysts who claim freezes "all the time" show freezes only during attachment upload in their activity logs; (v) browsing and updating tickets without attachments never freezes. What is the strongest supported conclusion?

- A) The ticket tool needs to be replaced — it is unreliable.
- B) The freezes are specific to attachment upload and began after the storage migration, making the migrated storage service the leading suspect for investigation.
- C) The two analysts are exaggerating and the issue can be deprioritised.
- D) Both sites' networks developed simultaneous faults.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Facts (ii) and (v) isolate the failing operation precisely. Fact (i) rules out anything site-local. Fact (iii) supplies a recent change to exactly the affected component. Fact (iv) actually strengthens the pattern rather than discrediting the analysts. This tests converging on a mechanism from mixed evidence and stating a leading suspect, not a verdict.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-constraint coaching schedule**

Four one-hour coaching sessions this week — Amir (call control), Beth (complaint handling), Carl (FCR recovery), Dee (progression discussion) — at most one session per day, Monday to Friday. Constraints: (i) Carl's session must happen before the team meeting on Wednesday morning, where new quality standards are announced; (ii) Beth is on leave Monday and Tuesday; (iii) Dee's progression discussion must follow the Wednesday morning meeting; (iv) Friday is committed to desk cover, no coaching. Which schedule works?

- A) Carl Monday, Amir Tuesday, Beth Wednesday afternoon, Dee Thursday
- B) Carl Monday, Beth Tuesday, Amir Wednesday afternoon, Dee Thursday
- C) Amir Monday, Carl Wednesday afternoon, Beth Thursday, Dee Friday
- D) Amir Monday, Carl Tuesday, Dee Wednesday afternoon, Beth Friday

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option B places Beth during her leave. Option C places Carl after the meeting and Dee on Friday. Option D places Beth on Friday. Only A satisfies every constraint. This tests anchoring the most restrictive constraints first — the two people tied to the Wednesday meeting in opposite directions — then fitting the rest around them.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Trend versus artefact in monitoring data**

Average call duration rose from 6.0 to 7.5 minutes between April and May. Facts: (i) in May, new starter Dee began taking live calls, averaging 11 minutes; (ii) excluding Dee, the team's May average was 6.1 minutes; (iii) April had no equivalent new starter; (iv) call volumes were similar in both months. Has the desk slowed down?

- A) Yes — duration rose 25%, the desk is slower.
- B) No — excluding the expected effect of a new starter learning the role, the established team's performance is essentially unchanged (6.0 to 6.1); the rise is a composition effect, and Dee's duration should be tracked as she develops.
- C) No — the data must be wrong.
- D) Yes — and Dee should be taken off the phones until she is faster.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The headline rise is real arithmetic but misleading interpretation: adding one new starter mechanically lifts a team blend even though nobody slowed down. Fact (ii) is the decisive test. This tests decomposing an aggregate before concluding, and a strong candidate's answer adds the forward-looking element: track Dee's trajectory as coaching helps it fall.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate checks a record against its own stated rule** field by field.
- **Watch for whether a candidate reads a compound condition (AND/OR) exactly.**
- **Note whether a candidate reads a team trend rather than a snapshot** in a coaching-data item.
- **Note whether a candidate decomposes an aggregate for a composition effect** before declaring improvement or decline.
- **Use the multi-constraint scheduling item (Question 10) to observe whether a candidate anchors the most restrictive constraints first.**
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that reads AND as OR** in a compound rule.
- **Crediting an answer that judges people from a single snapshot** rather than a trend.
- **Missing when a candidate promotes a coincidental change into a cause** without a connecting mechanism.
- **Accepting a conclusion that misses a composition effect** in an aggregate figure.
- **Rewarding a scheduling answer that fails even one stated constraint.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately a candidate works with the numbers this role genuinely produces and consumes: team resolution rates, SLA performance, call volumes and durations, availability and rota coverage, complaint counts, and trends across weeks and months. At senior level the questions increasingly mirror supervisory reality: combining figures across analysts and sites, working backwards from targets, and judging whether a headline number tells the truth.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions, each based on a small table or chart of workplace data. An on-screen calculator is usually available.

Numeric fluency has a distinctive purpose at this level: being the first interpreter of team data — producing reports, checking figures that look wrong, and briefing managers without introducing errors.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and SLA arithmetic** map to the **Customer service management** skill.
- **Weighted averages and aggregation** map to the **IT service reporting** skill: combining by totals, not averaging averages.
- **Rates and coverage calculations** map to the duty to manage availability.
- **Trend measurement** maps to the **Problem management** skill.
- **Fair comparison of individuals** maps to the coaching duty: rate-based, not raw-count, comparison.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — Team resolution percentage**

Yesterday a team of four analysts resolved 126 of the 140 tickets they closed within SLA. What percentage of closed tickets met the SLA?

- A) 86%
- B) 88%
- C) 90%
- D) 92%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 126÷140=90%. This tests basic SLA arithmetic, and a strong candidate's answer volunteers the complement view (14 missed) as a "miss budget" framing useful for later questions.

**Question 2 (easy) — Availability percentage**

An analyst is contracted for 37.5 hours this week. They spend 4.5 hours in training and 3 hours in team meetings; the rest is phone availability. What percentage of their contracted hours are phone-available?

- A) 75%
- B) 80%
- C) 82%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Non-phone hours=7.5; phone-available=30; 30÷37.5=80%. This tests basic percentage calculation, and a strong candidate's answer notes availability percentages are the currency of rota planning.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a team performance table**

| Analyst | Calls handled | Resolved at first contact |
|---|---|---|
| Amir | 90 | 63 |
| Beth | 110 | 77 |
| Carl | 60 | 36 |
| Dee | 40 | 30 |

Who had the highest first-contact resolution (FCR) rate?

- A) Amir
- B) Beth
- C) Carl
- D) Dee

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Amir 70%, Beth 70%, Carl 60%, Dee 75%. The trap is ranking by the raw resolved count (Beth's 77 is largest). This tests dividing by each person's own volume first — the numeric backbone of fair coaching.

**Question 4 (easy) — Complaint rate per thousand contacts**

Last month a desk handled 4,000 contacts and received 6 complaints. What was the complaint rate per 1,000 contacts?

- A) 0.67 per 1,000
- B) 1.5 per 1,000
- C) 6 per 1,000
- D) 15 per 1,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 6÷4,000×1,000=1.5. This tests normalising rates to make small numbers comparable across months of different sizes.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average across the team**

Using the table in Question 3, what was the team's overall FCR rate?

- A) 68.7%
- B) 68.75%
- C) 69.75%
- D) 70%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total calls=300; total resolved=206; 206÷300≈68.7%. Option B is the unweighted average of the four rates — a close trap. This tests aggregating from raw totals, never averaging analysts' percentages, since a weaker analyst's rate can carry more weight simply by volume.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change in backlog**

The desk's open-ticket backlog fell from 240 at the start of the month to 180 at the end. What was the percentage reduction, and if the same percentage reduction is achieved next month, what will the backlog be?

- A) 25% reduction; 135 next month
- B) 25% reduction; 120 next month
- C) 33% reduction; 120 next month
- D) 60% reduction; 72 next month

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Reduction=25%; applying 25% to 180 gives 45, so 180−45=135. Option B applies the same absolute fall rather than the same percentage. This tests distinguishing "same percentage" from "same amount" when projecting forward.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Coverage arithmetic for the rota**

A desk must answer an expected 540 calls next Monday. A full-time analyst handles about 45 calls; a half-day analyst handles about 22. Thirteen analysts are rostered: 10 full day, 3 on a half-day training session working the phones for the other half. Roughly how much capacity is there, and is it enough?

- A) 450 capacity — not enough
- B) 471 capacity — not enough
- C) 516 capacity — not enough
- D) 561 capacity — enough

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 10×45=450; 3×22=66; total 516, against a 540 forecast — a 24-call shortfall. Option A forgets the trainees entirely. This tests building capacity person by person, decomposing mixed days rather than rounding them to full days.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Average wait time and staffing gaps**

Between 09:00 and 10:00, 60 calls arrived and the average wait was 90 seconds. Between 10:00 and 11:00, after two analysts went into an incident review, 60 calls arrived and the average wait was 4 minutes 30 seconds. By what factor did the average wait increase, and what was the total extra waiting time experienced by the 10:00-11:00 callers compared with the earlier hour's average?

- A) Factor of 3; 180 extra minutes in total
- B) Factor of 3; 60 extra minutes in total
- C) Factor of 4.5; 270 extra minutes in total
- D) Factor of 2; 90 extra minutes in total

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 270÷90=3; extra per caller=180 seconds=3 minutes; total extra=60×3=180 minutes. This tests converting units consistently and then scaling a per-caller impact to a population impact — the arithmetic behind "losing two analysts for an hour cost our users three hours of waiting".

**Question 9 (challenging) — Working backwards from a team target**

Next month a desk must achieve 92% of incidents resolved within SLA. Volume is forecast at 1,150 incidents. Mid-month check: 620 incidents have arrived, of which 545 met SLA. How many of the remaining 530 forecast incidents can miss SLA while still achieving the monthly target?

- A) 17
- B) 33
- C) 47
- D) 92

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total miss allowance = 8%×1,150 = 92. Misses so far = 620−545 = 75. Remaining budget = 92−75 = 17. Option D quotes the whole-month budget as if none were spent. This tests converting a percentage target into a live "budget remaining" — telling a team early how much headroom is left.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Problem fix effectiveness**

A recurring printing fault generated these incident counts in the four weeks before a preventative fix: 48, 52, 46, 54. In the four weeks after the fix: 30, 24, 27, 19. By approximately what percentage did the average weekly incident count fall after the fix?

- A) 40%
- B) 50%
- C) 60%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Pre-fix average=50; post-fix average=25; reduction=50%. This tests using averages to make a fair before/after comparison despite week-to-week noise, and a strong candidate's answer reads the residual downward trend (30→19) and the non-zero floor as evidence of a partial fix or a second cause.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Fair comparison with different ticket mixes**

Two analysts' monthly figures: Amir — 80 password tickets (average 6 minutes each) and 20 hardware tickets (average 30 minutes each); Beth — 20 password tickets (average 6 minutes each) and 80 hardware tickets (average 24 minutes each). A manager notes Beth's average ticket time (20.4 minutes) is much worse than Amir's (10.8 minutes). What is the fairest reading?

- A) Amir is nearly twice as efficient as Beth.
- B) Beth is slower at both ticket types.
- C) Beth's higher overall average is driven by her hardware-heavy mix; on the shared ticket types she matches Amir on passwords (6 minutes) and is actually faster on hardware (24 versus 30 minutes).
- D) The data is insufficient to compare them in any way.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both averages are arithmetically correct and interpretively misleading. On password tickets both average 6 minutes; on hardware Beth averages 24 against Amir's 30 — 20% faster. Beth's overall average is higher purely because 80% of her workload is the long ticket type. This tests splitting by category before comparing people whose workload mixes differ — the difference between analytics and injustice.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate aggregates from raw totals** rather than averaging analysts' percentages.
- **Watch for whether a candidate distinguishes "same percentage" from "same amount"** when projecting a trend forward.
- **Note whether a candidate splits a comparison by category** when two people's workload mixes differ.
- **Note whether a candidate converts a percentage target into a live remaining budget** in a mid-period item.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level, and allow a calculator throughout.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an average of averages** across differently sized groups.
- **Crediting a percentage change divided by the wrong base.**
- **Missing when a candidate ranks people by raw counts** rather than rates.
- **Accepting a target calculation that ignores what has already been spent** mid-period.
- **Missing when a candidate trusts a headline average across different workload mixes** without splitting by category.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely a candidate reads, interprets, and communicates written material of the kind this role produces and consumes: service management framework extracts, policy notices, complaint letters, user research summaries, the analyst script itself, and reports for stakeholders. The dominant format presents a passage followed by statements to judge as **True**, **False**, or **Cannot say**. Supporting formats include best-summary questions, inference questions, and clear-writing judgements.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes covering 20 to 30 statements across several passages. The governing rule: answer from the passage alone.

Verbal precision carries particular weight at this level for a structural reason: instructions written here are executed by others. The analyst script is read literally by every analyst on every call — an ambiguous conditional or a misplaced "only" propagates into hundreds of user interactions.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise comprehension of framework text** maps to the **Service management framework knowledge** skill.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to the **Problem management** skill: investigation records must distinguish what is established, contradicted, and unknown.
- **Reading complaints accurately** maps to the **Customer service management** skill.
- **Faithful summary and inference** maps to the **IT service reporting** and **User focus** skills.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to ownership of the analyst script and the coaching duty.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under the timing above; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

For Questions 1 to 3, the candidate reads this passage.

> "Under the desk's problem management procedure, a problem record must be raised when three or more incidents with matching symptoms occur within any rolling ten-working-day window, unless the incidents are already linked to an open problem record. A problem record may also be raised at a senior analyst's discretion for fewer incidents where potential impact is high. Raising a problem record does not pause incident resolution: analysts continue to resolve or work around individual incidents while investigation proceeds. Problem records are reviewed fortnightly, and records with no progress for two consecutive reviews are escalated to the service desk manager."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A senior analyst may raise a problem record after two high-impact incidents with matching symptoms."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The discretionary route explicitly covers "fewer incidents where potential impact is high", and two is fewer, with high impact stated. This tests reading past a mandatory trigger to a discretionary extension that changes the answer.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "While a problem is under investigation, analysts should stop resolving the related individual incidents to avoid disturbing the evidence."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Raising a problem record does not pause incident resolution" directly contradicts the statement. This tests recognising that a plausible-sounding invented rationale cannot overturn an explicit sentence.

**Question 3 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Most problem records at this desk are escalated to the service desk manager."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage defines when escalation happens but gives no frequency data. This tests distinguishing a claim about a mechanism's existence from a claim about how often it fires.

For Questions 4 to 6, the candidate reads this complaint letter.

> "Dear Service Desk, I am writing to complain formally about the handling of my fault, reference INC-2026-01488. I reported on 3 June that my case management application crashes when I open documents. I was promised a call-back within one working day; the call came four days later. The workaround I was given — reopening the application each time — wastes my time and, more importantly, twice caused me to lose notes from client meetings. I accept that complex faults take time to fix. What I cannot accept is being left uninformed. I would like an explanation of the delay, a realistic timescale for a proper fix, and some assurance that lost work will not recur. I have copied my line manager. — R. Okafor, Casework"

**Question 4 (easy) — What is the complainant actually asking for?**

Which best captures what R. Okafor is requesting?

- A) Immediate replacement of the case management application.
- B) Disciplinary action against the analyst who promised the call-back.
- C) An explanation of the delay, a realistic fix timescale, and assurance against further lost work.
- D) Compensation for the lost meeting notes.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The letter states its requests explicitly. The complainant also concedes that complex faults take time — narrowing the complaint to communication failure, not fix speed. This tests reading a complaint precisely enough to distinguish stated requests from plausible-sounding but unmade demands.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The complainant lost client meeting notes on more than one occasion."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "Twice caused me to lose notes" directly supports the statement. This tests checking a quantifier in the source against the claim exactly — a small verification habit with a large effect on de-escalation.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Inference and tone judgement**

Drafting the desk's reply, which opening sentence best matches the complaint's substance and tone?

- A) "We apologise that your application keeps crashing; software faults are unfortunately common and fixes take time."
- B) "Thank you for your letter. You are right that we did not keep you informed as promised: the call-back that should have come within one working day took four, and we are sorry — both for that and for the notes you lost on two occasions."
- C) "We have investigated and found that the delay was caused by unusually high call volumes, for which we cannot be held responsible."
- D) "Your complaint has been logged with reference CMP-2026-0092 and will be processed according to our complaints procedure."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B validates the failure the complainant actually identified (communication, not fix speed) with the specific numbers, and acknowledges concrete harm. Option A apologises for the thing explicitly accepted, proving the letter was skimmed. This tests matching a response to the substance the writer actually holds against the desk.

For Questions 7 to 8, the candidate reads this script extract.

> "If the caller reports a suspected phishing email, the analyst must not ask the caller to forward the email. The analyst should instruct the caller to leave the email unopened where possible and must raise a security incident within 15 minutes of the call ending. Callers who have already clicked a link or entered credentials must be transferred directly to the security team's hotline during its staffed hours (08:00-18:00). Outside those hours, the analyst must follow the out-of-hours security escalation instead, and must not advise the caller that no action is needed."

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A caller who clicked a phishing link at 19:30 should be told that no action is needed until the security hotline opens at 08:00."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two clauses forbid this: the out-of-hours escalation must be followed instead, and the analyst must not advise no action is needed. This tests giving "must not" negative obligations the same weight as "must" positive ones, since they are among the most safety-critical lines in any script.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement on a script line**

A draft script line reads: "Analysts should try to make sure that callers are ideally encouraged, where feasible, to consider not opening emails that may potentially be suspicious." Which revision is best for a script analysts must follow under pressure?

- A) "Tell the caller not to open suspicious emails."
- B) "Analysts are encouraged to advise callers regarding email-opening best practice."
- C) "Where operationally viable, callers should be guided toward suspicious-email avoidance behaviours."
- D) Leave the line as drafted — it is thorough.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A script line's virtues are the imperative mood, one action, and zero hedges. The draft buries the instruction under five hedges. Options B and C replace hedging with abstraction, which is no better for a caller. This tests the read-aloud test: could a nervous analyst read the line verbatim on a difficult call and would the caller know what to do?

For Questions 9 to 11, the candidate reads this user research summary.

> "We interviewed 12 caseworkers and surveyed 180 desk users about the new self-service portal. Interviewees valued the password reset feature but seven of the 12 said they could not find the 'report a fault' option without help. Survey respondents rated the portal 3.9 out of 5 on average; users aged over 55 rated it 3.1. Fault reports submitted via the portal contain, on average, 40% fewer of the diagnostic details analysts need than fault reports taken by phone, and portal-submitted faults take 1.3 contacts on average to resolve, against 1.1 for phone-reported faults. The research did not examine why portal fault reports contain less detail."

**Question 9 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The majority of interviewed caseworkers struggled to locate the fault-reporting option unaided."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Seven of twelve is a majority and matches "struggled to locate unaided". This tests converting a quantifier ("majority") into arithmetic and checking it against the passage's actual numbers.

**Question 10 (challenging) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Older users rate the portal lower because they find the interface harder to navigate."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rating gap is established, but the passage explicitly states it "did not examine why". This tests distinguishing a supported factual half from an unsupported causal half in a compound claim — cause-versus-correlation discipline.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Best summary for a stakeholder report**

Summarising this passage in one sentence for a service desk manager, which is best?

- A) "Users love the portal, though older users are unhappy with it."
- B) "The portal rates well overall (3.9/5), but fault reporting shows friction: most interviewees couldn't find it unaided, portal reports carry about 40% less diagnostic detail, and portal-reported faults take slightly more contacts to resolve — why detail is lower hasn't yet been researched."
- C) "The portal is failing and fault reporting should be withdrawn until it is fixed."
- D) "Research shows the portal reduces fault resolution efficiency by 40%."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B carries the headline rating, all three fault-reporting findings, and the summary's own stated limit. Option A inflates moderate scores emotionally. Option C recommends what the evidence does not support. Option D mixes a diagnostic-detail figure with a wrong noun ("efficiency"). This tests compressing without distorting and flagging what is not yet known.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate reads past a mandatory rule to a stated exception or discretionary extension.**
- **Watch for whether a candidate gives "must not" clauses the same attention as "must" clauses.**
- **Note whether a candidate splits a compound claim into its supported and unsupported halves**, especially around "because".
- **Note whether a candidate's script-line revision passes a read-aloud test** — imperative, single action, no hedges.
- **Keep timing consistent** across candidates for this level.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that anchors on a passage's first rule** and misses a later qualifying sentence.
- **Crediting a claim judged True because its factual half is true**, ignoring an unsupported causal half.
- **Missing when a candidate treats familiarity with a topic as evidence for a specific claim about it.**
- **Accepting a summary that inflates or deflates a moderate finding.**
- **Missing when a candidate answers an unmade demand** in a complaint-reading item.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks the candidate to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Common formats include selecting the most effective response, identifying the most and least effective from a set, ranking all options, or rating each option on a scale.

The typical format is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, usually generously timed. At senior-analyst level, the SJT adds a second person to almost every dilemma — the analyst being coached, the operator being supported. The recurring tension is between doing and developing: stepping in rescues today's call but can hollow out tomorrow's analyst.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Intervening well** maps to the core duty to monitor inbound calls and intervene in difficult calls.
- **Coaching and developing** maps to the duty to coach and develop analysts: responses that build capability, not dependence.
- **Handling complex complaints** maps to the **Customer service management** skill.
- **Guarding the record and the script** maps to the **Asset and configuration management** and **IT service reporting** skills, and script ownership.
- **Escalating problems** maps to the **Problem management** skill and the availability-management duty.

### Practice questions

Present these to the candidate under generous or untimed conditions; do not reveal the correct answers or explanations until after they have responded.

**Question 1 (easy) — The struggling analyst on a live call**

Monitoring the dashboard, a newer analyst is 20 minutes into a call, visibly stressed. Listening in reveals they have misdiagnosed the fault and are leading the caller through irrelevant steps. The caller is patient but time is being wasted. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let the call run — analysts learn best from their own mistakes.
- B) Message the analyst a concise steer ("Ask whether this started after Tuesday's update — likely profile issue"), stay listening, and step in warmly only if they cannot recover; afterwards, debrief the diagnosis privately.
- C) Take over the call immediately, announcing to the caller that a senior is now handling it.
- D) Wait until the call ends, then tell the analyst everything they did wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B serves both people: the caller gets a corrected diagnosis quickly, and the analyst gets to complete their own rescue, building the capability coaching exists to build. Option A abandons the caller to an unvolunteered learning exercise. Option C fixes the call at the analyst's expense and teaches them to hide future struggles. This tests the smallest effective intervention now, with the full learning conversation afterwards.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit and correction in public**

In the team channel, an analyst posts a workaround for a common fault, clearly proud of it. The workaround works but skips a mandatory logging step, meaning affected assets will not be tracked. Several analysts have already reacted positively. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reply in the channel: "This misses the mandatory logging step — please do not use it."
- B) Delete the post and share the correct procedure directly.
- C) Reply in the channel crediting the workaround and adding the missing step ("Nice find — one addition: log the asset each time, or we lose track of affected machines"), then update the script so the complete version is the official one.
- D) Say nothing publicly but mention the logging step at the next team meeting.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C keeps the analyst's initiative rewarded, corrects the record where everyone who saw the original will see it, explains why the step matters, and institutionalises the fix in the script. Option A frames a correction as pure rebuke. Option B transfers credit and humiliates by erasure. This tests praise publicly, correct precisely, institutionalise through the proper artefact.

**Question 3 (easy) — The availability squeeze**

Two analysts ask for the same Friday afternoon off — one for a medical appointment booked weeks ago, one (the strongest performer) for an event, mentioning they "always cover when needed". Minimum staffing allows only one absence. What is the most effective response?

- A) Approve the strongest performer — retaining top talent matters most.
- B) Approve the medical appointment; explain the staffing constraint honestly to the other analyst, acknowledge their record of flexibility, and actively explore alternatives with them.
- C) Refuse both to be fair.
- D) Approve both and cover the desk personally all afternoon.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A long-booked medical appointment is a need; an event is a preference, and the response softens the cost with honesty, recognition, and genuine effort on alternatives. Option A converts performance into privilege. Option D fails the senior's own monitoring, intervention, and coaching duties. This tests making a staffing constraint visible and the decision principled, not making the constraint disappear by absorbing it personally.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The complaint about a candidate's own intervention**

A caller complains formally: during a difficult call last week, "a supervisor barged in, talked over the analyst, and made me repeat everything". The intervention was recalled as helpful, but the recording shows an interruption mid-sentence and two already-answered questions re-asked. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reply defending the intervention: it was necessary and resolved the fault.
- B) Apologise for the caller's experience, acknowledging specifically what the recording confirms — the interruption and the repeated questions — while briefly explaining the purpose of intervention; then adjust the intervention practice going forward, and share the learning with other seniors.
- C) Ask the analyst to reply, since it was their call.
- D) Apologise fully and commit to never intervening in live calls again.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The recording confirms the caller's account substantially, so acknowledgement must be specific, not generic. Option A defends the "what" (intervening) against a complaint about the "how". Option D over-corrects, trading a fixable technique for a permanent capability loss. This tests accountability paired with learning, not defensiveness or capitulation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The script shortcut under pressure**

A major incident is generating heavy call volume. An experienced analyst suggests: "Skip the script's identity verification for callers who just want the outage status — it would halve our queue." Verification exists primarily to protect account actions; a status update reveals no personal data. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree informally — common sense should prevail during a crisis.
- B) Refuse — the script must be followed exactly at all times, regardless of circumstances.
- C) Recognise the suggestion's merit, check whether the script or incident procedure already provides for it, and if not, get a rapid decision from the service desk manager to authorise a temporary, defined change — then update the script and tell the whole team at once, including exactly what remains verification-mandatory.
- D) Let each analyst use their own judgement about when verification is needed today.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A security-relevant control change during an incident belongs at manager level, published to the whole team simultaneously with clear boundaries. Option A creates an unauthorised, undocumented fork. Option D multiplies the fork by the number of analysts. This tests stewardship of a controlled artefact: the script bends quickly and formally, never silently.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The pattern nobody has time for**

Reviewing yesterday's tickets reveals five separate incidents at different sites, all closed with different causes, sharing an odd detail: each user had recently returned from leave. A dormant-account process is suspected of breaking accounts after 30 days of inactivity. The desk is busy, the tickets are all closed, and the users are all working again. What is the most effective response?

- A) Nothing — five resolved tickets are five satisfied users.
- B) Reopen all five tickets and investigate each fully personally today.
- C) Raise a problem record linking the five incidents, note the returned-from-leave pattern and the hypothesis, and flag it to the service desk manager — then monitor for recurrence while the desk continues normal work.
- D) Email all staff warning them not to take more than 30 days of leave.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The incidents are resolved, but the pattern predicts every future returner — invisible to anyone not looking across tickets. Option A mistakes closed tickets for a closed cause. Option B does the right investigation with the wrong resourcing, since problem investigation is coordinated, not solo. This tests contributing evidence through the record, at the senior's actual vantage point.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The coaching conversation that isn't working**

For six weeks, an analyst whose FCR has kept falling has been coached. Sessions are pleasant; they agree with every suggestion, then nothing changes. Today they cancel a session for the second time, citing call volume, and something beyond skill is privately suspected. What is the most effective response?

- A) Continue the fortnightly sessions — persistence will pay off eventually.
- B) Escalate to the service desk manager that the analyst is uncoachable.
- C) Change the conversation: arrange a protected, unhurried one-to-one, share the trend data honestly, say plainly that the current approach isn't working, and ask open questions about what is actually getting in the way — while making clear the intent is support.
- D) Sit beside them and re-teach the FCR techniques step by step.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Agreeable sessions plus zero behaviour change plus a worsening trend plus emerging avoidance is not a skills signature. Options A and D double the dose of a treatment six weeks of evidence say is not working. This tests noticing when development is not the actual need and responding to the real pattern.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the failed call-back promise**

A complaint is arriving: an analyst promised a vulnerable user a call-back "within the hour" three days ago, then went on leave; the promise was never logged, so nobody called. The user has called back, upset. Four possible responses:

1. Take the call, apologise specifically, resolve or firmly schedule the resolution now, log everything — then, separately, raise the systemic gap (promises made near shift-end aren't captured) for a script and handover fix.
2. Take the call and explain that the analyst is on leave, so the delay was unavoidable.
3. Have the next available analyst take the call, since a problem review is due.
4. Take the call, apologise, and offer a goodwill gesture, but leave the process gap unexamined since the analyst returns next week.

Which is the MOST effective and which is the LEAST effective response?

- A) Most: 1; Least: 2
- B) Most: 1; Least: 3
- C) Most: 4; Least: 2
- D) Most: 1; Least: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option 1 repairs the individual harm, restores the record, and treats the incident as a process specimen worth fixing. Option 2 is worst: it converts an apology into an excuse and is factually false — the delay was caused by the unlogged promise, not the leave. This tests repair, record, then re-engineer — and never explaining away where an apology is owed.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The metric, the manager, and the truth**

Preparing the weekly report reveals FCR has dropped four points, driven entirely by new starters currently being coached. A manager, under pressure from their own leadership, says: "Present it excluding the new starters — that's the 'underlying' figure, and it looks fine." There is a legitimate analytical case for showing an established-team figure; presented alone, it would hide the real service users received. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do as asked — the manager owns the report and the context.
- B) Refuse and submit the unadjusted figure alone.
- C) Propose presenting both figures with one line of explanation — headline FCR including everyone, and the established-team figure showing the trend is developmental, not systemic — noting that showing the drop alongside its benign cause is a stronger story than hiding it.
- D) Present the excluded figure as asked, but keep a private note recording disagreement.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The manager's instinct contains real analysis attached to a misleading presentation choice. Option C separates the two: both figures, honestly labelled, actually serve the manager better than a quietly filtered number that becomes a time bomb the moment anyone reconciles it. Option D protects the individual while the organisation is still misled. This tests disagreeing upward by giving a better version of what is actually needed.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Inclusion in development opportunities**

A prestigious project needs an analyst seconded to give "technical advice to the project team" two days a week. The instinct is to nominate the strongest analyst — articulate, confident, always volunteers — but the last three such nominations went to the same person on the same instinct, and a quieter analyst has equivalent technical knowledge, has expressed interest in project work at their last review, but has never been put forward. What is the most effective response?

- A) Nominate the strongest analyst — the project deserves the best, and visibility is earned.
- B) Nominate the quieter analyst without discussion — it is their turn.
- C) Treat it as the development decision it is: revisit what the project actually needs, tell both analysts about the opportunity, and weigh recorded interest and growth against the pattern noticed — with support available if the quieter analyst takes it on; separately, commit to advertising future opportunities to the whole team rather than nominating by instinct.
- D) Ask the two analysts to decide between themselves.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three consecutive instinct-based nominations concentrating opportunity on the most visible person is how development gaps compound. Option C fixes both the instance and the mechanism. Option A restates the instinct as a principle. Option B corrects the outcome while keeping the arbitrary method. This tests fixing the system that generates a noticed pattern, not just today's output.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The incident during the handover**

At 17:50, ten minutes before the evening handover to the out-of-hours provider, an analyst shows a ticket: a hospital-linked user reports their clinical-adjacent application is erroring intermittently. It is not yet a major incident — one user, intermittent — but the application's SLA class is critical, the error is unfamiliar, and the out-of-hours provider has a thinner knowledge base. The analyst asks: "Just hand it over as normal?" What is the most effective response?

- A) Yes — the out-of-hours process exists precisely for tickets that cross the boundary.
- B) No — stay late and resolve it personally before leaving.
- C) Treat it as a high-risk handover: spend the ten minutes enriching the ticket (symptoms, timeline, SLA class, what has been tried, escalation triggers and contacts), speak directly to the out-of-hours lead rather than relying on the queue, agree the conditions under which they should escalate overnight, and flag it to the on-call manager so nobody is surprised at 02:00.
- D) Ask the user to call back in the morning when the full desk is available.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Out-of-hours failures overwhelmingly happen when context fails to cross the boundary. Option C spends the available ten minutes exactly where they buy the most risk reduction. Option A follows process while abandoning its purpose. Option B substitutes heroics for a handover mechanism the desk must be able to rely on without any one person. This tests handover quality measured by context transferred, not tickets transferred.

### Administration tips

- **Score for whether a candidate's answer serves both the immediate person and the system** (script, record, process), rather than only one.
- **Watch for whether a candidate's intervention response matches the moment's stakes**, neither always rescuing nor always standing back.
- **Note whether a candidate escalates a pattern rather than a person** when raising a concern about behaviour.
- **Note whether a candidate proposes a "both figures, honestly labelled" resolution** when asked to present data misleadingly.
- **Because these scenarios reward considered, professional judgement**, allow generous or untimed conditions.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- **Rewarding an answer that rescues instead of developing**, or ignores instead of intervening.
- **Crediting a correction delivered at maximum publicity** rather than credit-first, correct-precisely.
- **Missing when a candidate absorbs a constraint personally** (covering every gap themselves) rather than making it visible and principled.
- **Accepting an answer that defends the "what" against a complaint about the "how".**
- **Missing when a candidate documents disagreement privately while a misleading report still ships.**

## Conclusion

This document has given you a substantial set of assessment material for the four types most relevant to a senior service desk analyst: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement.

The cognitive section tested the supervisory thinking the role runs on: verifying monitoring data against its own rules, parsing compound escalation conditions, catching an inverted branch in a script, triaging problems by level, and anchoring rotas around their most restrictive constraints. The numeric section tested the desk's team-level arithmetic: weighted averages, availability percentages, miss budgets, remedy effectiveness, and mix-adjusted comparisons that keep performance conversations fair. The verbal section tested the precision this role's artefacts demand: True/False/Cannot-say discipline, the inventory reading of complaints, vigilance around "must not" clauses, and the read-aloud test for script lines. The situational judgement section tested the dilemmas that define the senior analyst's position: intervening without undermining, coaching when coaching is not the need, keeping reports true under pressure, distributing opportunity deliberately, and handing over context rather than just tickets.

The thread running through all four sections is the smallest effective intervention, decomposing an aggregate before concluding, and matching a written or spoken response to the substance the reader or listener actually needs.

**Scoring and debrief.** Work through each section's worked answers against the candidate's responses, using the explanations above as an answer key and as talking points. A candidate who reasons well but lands on the wrong answer is a different signal from one who guesses. A short debrief conversation afterwards is often more informative than the raw score, and at this level connects naturally to how the candidate would actually coach an analyst, guard the script, and keep a report true under pressure.

**Records and consistency.** Keep a note of the conditions under which the assessment was administered alongside the result, and use the same items and conditions for every candidate assessed for this role level.

**One assessment among several.** This result is one input into a wider decision about whether a candidate can resolve a situation, protect the honest record, and strengthen the system that will face the situation's successor — never the whole of that decision. Pair it with the candidate's experience and a conversation, and revisit the advisory above before it informs any hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision.
