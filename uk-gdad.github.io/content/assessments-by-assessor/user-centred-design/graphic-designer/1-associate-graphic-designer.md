# Associate Graphic Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for associate graphic designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the everyday thinking a particular job actually uses. For an associate graphic designer that means checking a layout against a specification and spotting where it diverges, noticing that a colour pairing will fail for someone with low vision, working out what a set of numbers about a page is telling you, reading a standard carefully and applying it, and making sensible decisions when a stakeholder asks for something that would make a design harder to read.

An associate graphic designer creates the graphic elements that underpin interaction and service design — using layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure content is legible and readable and that users see and understand interactions. This is a functional definition, and it is deliberate: a design decision in this context is not a matter of preference, since a type size, a contrast ratio, a spacing value or an icon either lets somebody complete a task or prevents them, and the difference is measurable. An associate graphic designer is a trainee in an entry-level position, working under supervision, expected to show aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and verbal items for whether the candidate concludes only what a stated rule or set of facts guarantees, resisting a plausible-but-unproven answer, and for whether visual decisions are treated as decisions with consequences rather than as taste — the strongest responses attach a measured value to a design judgement rather than an impression. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a response that acts within remit and is transparent at its edges — attempting what can competently be done and promptly telling the right person about what cannot — over either silent struggling or reflexive escalation.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking this role genuinely demands, using practical workplace materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences — a layout against a grid specification, a type scale, a set of colour values, an icon set, an extract from an accessibility standard, a list of research observations.

Typical format: an online test lasting fifteen to thirty minutes, with questions clustering into pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a similar level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

For an associate graphic designer, so much of the early value of the role comes from precision — noticing a spacing value off the system scale, a grey that passes contrast against white but fails against a light grey panel, two icons drawn at different optical weights, a heading smaller than the body text below it. Each is a small act of comparison that protects whether somebody can read the thing.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Designing strategically**: recognising when something follows a system, and when it quietly departs from one.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**: a contrast failure or an undersized touch target is an error with a human consequence.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Evidence-based design**: framing ideas as design hypotheses to be tested.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Iterative design**: understanding how iteration supports user-centred design.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Iterative design** and **Designing together**.
- **Explaining a visual decision** maps to **Design communication**: articulating design decisions.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking against a spacing system**

A design system uses a spacing scale of multiples of 8 pixels only. A layout has these gaps: 16, 24, 20, 32, 8. Ask how many values break the rule.

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 16, 24, 32 and 8 are all multiples of 8; 20 is not (8×2=16, 8×3=24), the sole breach. This tests checking every value against a stated rule precisely, and, in a strong candidate's answer, recognising an off-scale value often signals a local layout problem nudged by eye rather than solved properly.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a design rule**

Rule: "A custom icon may be used only if no suitable icon exists in the design system, unless the icon has been approved by the lead graphic designer." A custom icon was used last week, and a suitable system icon exists. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The rule was broken.
- B) The icon was approved by the lead graphic designer.
- C) Either the icon was approved by the lead, or the rule was broken.
- D) The custom icon is worse than the system icon.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is conditional with one permitted exception, and the given facts do not establish whether the exception applies. This tests deducing only what the facts guarantee — option D introduces an unsupported quality judgement, since a custom icon may be better, sometimes exactly why an exception is approved.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a type scale**

A type scale runs: 14, 16, 19, 24, 30, 36. A new size is proposed between 19 and 24. Ask which value fits the pattern of the existing scale most closely.

- A) 20
- B) 21
- C) 22
- D) The scale has no consistent pattern

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The ratios between consecutive steps (roughly 1.14, 1.19, 1.26, 1.25, 1.2) reveal a geometric rather than arithmetic scale; a new step following this pattern is close to 21. This tests recognising that type scales are usually ratio-based rather than difference-based, since apparent size difference is proportional — option D is the trap for anyone checking differences (2, 3, 5, 6, 6) instead.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation in a design morning**

At 9:30am, four items: (1) a live page has an unreadable light-grey-on-white error message, reported by several users; (2) a senior designer asked for icon exploration by Wednesday; (3) an 11:00 design critique needing prepared work; (4) a newsletter about a design conference. Ask what should happen first.

- A) Start the icon exploration while the morning is quiet.
- B) Raise or fix the unreadable error message.
- C) Get your work ready for 11:00.
- D) Read the newsletter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An unreadable error message affects users at exactly the point where something has already gone wrong for them, and is affecting people today. This tests weighing impact and urgency together — harm to users outranks internal commitments, and an unreadable error message is a particularly sharp form of harm regardless of who can act on it directly.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking against instructions**

A senior designer requested exactly three changes: (i) increase body text from 16 to 19 pixels, (ii) change link colour to system blue, (iii) add 8px above each heading. The returned file shows all three changes plus the page margin reduced from 24 to 16. Ask how many of the changes made were requested.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three requested changes were made; the margin change was extra. This tests distinguishing what was requested from what was done — an unrequested global margin change affecting every page using the template, possibly solving a local problem by changing a global value, is worth flagging rather than letting through silently.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about contrast**

Standard: "Text smaller than 24 pixels must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1. Text 24 pixels or larger, or 19 pixels or larger and bold, must have at least 3:1." A design uses 19px bold text at 3.5:1. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) It fails the standard.
- B) It meets the standard.
- C) It meets the standard only if the text is also 24 pixels.
- D) The standard does not cover this case.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 19px bold text satisfies the second category (19px or larger and bold), where the requirement is 3:1, met by 3.5:1. This tests reading a conditional standard precisely — the "or" trap in option C misreads two alternative routes as requiring both simultaneously, a common over-compliance error.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across screen sizes**

Completion rate by viewport width: under 400px, 3,000/6,000 (50%); 400–768px, 7,200/9,000 (80%); over 768px, 4,500/5,000 (90%). Ask what this pattern most strongly suggests.

- A) The page works well overall.
- B) Completion falls sharply below 400 pixels — 50% against 80% and 90% — which points at something in the layout that breaks at the narrowest widths rather than at mobile generally.
- C) Mobile users find the page difficult.
- D) The page should be redesigned for desktop.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The gap between the two mobile bands (30 points) is three times the gap between the wider bands, suggesting a specific breakage rather than general mobile difficulty. This tests reading the shape of a drop, not just its direction — option C would send effort toward the band already performing at 80%.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking an icon set**

Standard: icons must be on the same grid, use the same stroke weight, be legible at 16px, and never be the sole indicator of meaning. A set of eight icons: all on a 24px grid, all 2px stroke, three of eight indistinguishable at 16px, all with text labels. Ask how many parts of the standard are unmet.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Only the legibility-at-16px requirement fails (three icons); the other three requirements are met. This tests distinguishing a count of failing items from a count of failing requirements — the trap in C counts the three icons rather than the one broken rule.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving in a critique**

In a design critique, two colleagues debate whether a button should be green or blue; a candidate has noticed the button's 14px text is at 3.2:1, below the 4.5:1 requirement for that size. Ask, as the most junior person present, for the most sensible thing to do.

- A) Say nothing; the colour debate will resolve itself.
- B) Wait for a pause and raise it as a question — "can I check something? The label is 14 pixels at about 3.2:1, which I think would fail the small-text requirement whichever colour we pick" — bringing the room to a decision the colour choice depends on.
- C) Interrupt to say the colour debate is irrelevant.
- D) Mention it to your senior designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The observation constrains the discussion under way, applying regardless of which colour wins, making it useful to raise at this exact moment. This tests raising a specific, measured observation at a pause with precise values — option A wastes it and lets a critique end on a design that will fail, and option D misses the moment when the decision is actually being made.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Deduction about what evidence supports**

In research with eight participants, six could not tell that a section of a page was interactive. Ask which conclusion is best supported by this evidence alone.

- A) 75% of users cannot tell the section is interactive.
- B) The section should be given a border.
- C) Six of eight participants did not recognise the section as interactive, which is a strong signal that its visual treatment does not signal interactivity — and worth testing alternatives such as a clearer affordance, a change of background, or a conventional control.
- D) The design is bad.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Six of eight is a strong qualitative signal, stated at that strength, naming the mechanism (visual treatment failing to signal interactivity) rather than jumping to one fix. This tests stating evidence at its actual strength — option A converts a sample into a population rate, and option B commits to a single solution when the solution space is genuinely wide.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation with competing pressures**

Senior designer away; at 10:00: (1) a developer needs final colour values today to finish a story; (2) a 14:00 critique with work not ready; (3) a content designer needs confirmation of heading sizes by 12:00 for a template; (4) a colleague's icons need review this week. Ask for the most sensible order.

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — your own commitments first.
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — unblock the developer, then the content designer, then your own work, then the review.
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4 — deadlines in time order.
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — as listed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 blocks another person's work today and takes minutes — leaving colour values to be picked independently risks off-system colours entering a codebase, far harder to remove than to prevent; item 3 has a hard 12:00 deadline and propagates into a template used across pages; item 2 can absorb the earlier interruptions. This tests ranking by consequence and deadline together, rather than by ownership (A) or strict time order alone (C, which puts a five-minute unblocking task behind a 12:00 deadline).

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about brand and accessibility**

A communications team requires a brand colour for body text, giving 3.9:1 against white, below the 4.5:1 requirement for text under 24px. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The brand colour must be used, so nothing can be done.
- B) The requirement is that the brand is recognisable, not that a specific value appears in every element — so the useful questions are whether a darker variant of the same hue would satisfy both, and whether the brand colour could be used for large headings, where 3:1 applies, while body text uses a compliant tone.
- C) The brand colour should be abandoned.
- D) The text should be made larger so the 3:1 threshold applies.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Brands are recognisable through a palette, typeface, tone and proportions, not one exact colour value in body text, and most brand palettes already contain a darker variant of the primary hue. This tests separating a brand's actual requirement from a specific instruction and finding a route satisfying both — option D games the threshold rather than meeting its purpose, producing oversized text that is worse for everyone.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate checks every stated rule against every value or item rather than stopping at the first fault found.
- Note whether the candidate deduces only what given facts guarantee, resisting a plausible but unproven conclusion.
- Watch for whether visual judgements are backed by a measured value rather than an impression.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes a count of failing items from a count of failing requirements.
- Note whether the candidate reads the shape of a data pattern (where a drop is sharpest) rather than only its overall direction.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer that counts failing items rather than failing rules, or vice versa, without checking which the question asks.
- Missing when a candidate reads "or" in a conditional standard as "and," producing unnecessary over-compliance.
- Rewarding a design judgement offered without a supporting measured value.
- Accepting a solution jumped to (a specific fix) before the underlying problem is named clearly.
- Missing when a candidate ranks prioritisation by ownership or by strict deadline order alone, rather than consequence.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how well a candidate works with the numbers this role produces. It is not a maths exam — graphic design is unusually full of numbers with thresholds: contrast ratios, type scales, line lengths, spacing units, grid columns, optical sizes, viewport widths.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item showing a small table, chart, or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided.

At associate level the content is modest — percentages, ratios, proportions, simple averages, and reading values from tables.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Working with ratios** maps to **Designing for everyone**: contrast ratios and type relationships are expressed as ratios, and misreading one produces an inaccessible design.
- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**: identifying common sources and types of data informing design.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Evidence-based design** and **Designing for everyone**: comparing how different groups fare is how exclusion becomes visible.
- **Understanding scale** maps to **Designing strategically**: knowing how many people a decision affects makes a case for it.
- **Interpreting figures cautiously** maps to **Design communication**: a decision justified by a misread number is hard to defend twice.
- **Working with proportional systems** maps to **Designing strategically**: the value of patterns and components lies partly in internal consistency.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A completion rate**

Of 2,400 users who reached a page, 1,800 completed it. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 65%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 1,800÷2,400=75%. This tests basic percentage calculation, checkable by estimating three quarters first.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a contrast ratio**

Standard requires 4.5:1 for text under 24px. Four pairings: 3.1:1, 4.4:1, 4.5:1, 7.2:1. Ask how many meet the requirement for 16px text.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 4.5:1 and 7.2:1 pass; 3.1:1 and 4.4:1 fail. This tests reading a boundary precisely — 4.4 is the one that catches people, close enough to feel like a pass but not one, and thresholds carry no discretionary zone.

**Question 3 (easy) — A type scale ratio**

A type scale steps from 16 to 20 pixels. Ask for the ratio between the two sizes, to two decimal places.

- A) 1.20
- B) 1.25
- C) 1.33
- D) 0.80

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 20÷16=1.25. This tests computing a ratio correctly rather than a proportion of the difference (A, 4÷20, the same denominator error as a percentage-change mistake) or inverting the ratio (D).

**Question 4 (easy) — Line length**

A column is 640px wide, average character width 8px. Ask roughly how many characters fit on a line.

- A) 40
- B) 60
- C) 80
- D) 100

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 640÷8=80. This tests basic division producing a genuine design judgement — comfortable line length sits between about 45 and 75 characters, so 80 is at or past the comfortable edge, worth saying specifically in a critique rather than "that feels a bit wide."

**Question 5 (easy) — Scale of a decision**

A page has 600,000 views a year; 10% of viewers encounter an error, and 4% of those are affected by a contrast failure on it. Ask how many people a year are affected.

- A) 2,400
- B) 24,000
- C) 60,000
- D) 240,000

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 10%×600,000=60,000; 4%×60,000=2,400. This tests completing a two-stage calculation — option C stops after the first step, the most common error in two-stage questions, and converting a defect into a specific number of people is what gets a colour actually changed.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups fairly**

Of 400 users on screens over 768px, 360 completed a task. Of 250 users under 400px, 125 completed. Ask which statement is correct.

- A) Both groups completed at similar rates.
- B) The rate was 90% on wide screens and 50% on narrow screens.
- C) Wide-screen users completed 235 more tasks, so the difference is modest.
- D) The groups cannot be compared because they are different sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 360÷400=90%; 125÷250=50%. This tests converting to rates before comparing groups of different sizes — option D wrongly claims incomparability when converting to rates is precisely the correct comparison method.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

After a type size increase, form completions rose from 720 to 900 a week. Ask for the percentage increase.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 80%
- D) 125%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (900−720)÷720=25%. This tests dividing by the original value, not the new one — option A divides by 900, the commonest error, which under-reports every improvement.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a total**

15% of users abandon a page, and this is 4,500 people a month. Ask how many reach the page each month.

- A) 5,175
- B) 6,750
- C) 30,000
- D) 67,500

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 4,500÷0.15=30,000. This tests working back from a percentage and a count to the base, verifiable by reversing the calculation.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Proportional grid**

A 12-column grid spans 960px with 24px gutters between columns. Ask for the width of one column, ignoring outer margins.

- A) 56 pixels
- B) 58 pixels
- C) 64 pixels
- D) 80 pixels

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 12 columns have 11 internal gutters: 11×24=264; (960−264)÷12=58. This tests the n−1 gutter rule — option A assumes 12 gutters and D ignores gutters entirely, the commonest error, producing a grid definition that never quite reconciles.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Choosing the right denominator**

Journey: 20,000 start, 16,000 reach a form, 12,000 reach the review page, 11,400 submit. Ask what percentage of those reaching the review page submitted.

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 11,400÷12,000=95%. This tests identifying the group the question names as the denominator — option A uses 20,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked, misdirecting design effort away from where the actual losses (4,000 before the form, 4,000 before review) sit.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Before a redesign, 40% of users completed a task on narrow screens; afterwards, 52% did. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) Completion rose by 12%.
- B) Completion rose by 12 percentage points, which is a 30% increase.
- C) Completion rose by 30 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose by 23%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 52−40=12 points; 12÷40=30% relative. This tests correctly labelling both figures — the same underlying change described as "12 points" or "30%," with the second sounding considerably more impressive, worth being precise about when articulating a design decision honestly.

**Question 12 (harder) — When an average hides something**

Seven users' times to find a link, in seconds: 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 8, 84. A colleague reports "the average was 16 seconds." Ask for the best comment.

- A) The calculation is wrong; the average is 5 seconds.
- B) The calculation is right, but the mean is misleading: six of seven users found the link within eight seconds and one extreme case is pulling the figure up. Report the median of 5 seconds with the range, and treat the 84-second session as the most informative thing in the set.
- C) The figure is fine as reported.
- D) The 84-second case should be excluded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The sum (115) ÷ 7 = 16.4, arithmetically correct but describing no actual user. This tests recognising an outlier skewing a mean and reporting the median instead — and, in a strong answer, treating the outlier itself as the most useful finding, since it usually reveals a contrast, size or hierarchy failure the other six worked around unnoticed.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads a numeric threshold at its precise boundary rather than rounding toward a convenient answer.
- Note whether the candidate completes both stages of a two-stage calculation rather than stopping at the first.
- Watch for whether the candidate converts raw counts to rates before comparing groups of different sizes.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change.
- Note whether a mean skewed by an outlier is recognised and reported alongside or replaced by a more representative statistic.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a contrast ratio or numeric threshold rounded in the candidate's favour.
- Missing when a candidate stops after the first stage of a two-part calculation.
- Rewarding a comparison of raw counts across groups of different sizes rather than rates.
- Accepting "percentage" and "percentage points" used interchangeably without distinguishing them.
- Missing when a candidate reports a mean without checking whether an outlier makes it unrepresentative.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. The documents that constrain graphic design in government are unusually precise: accessibility criteria specify ratios and thresholds, brand guidelines specify permitted and prohibited uses, design system documentation specifies when a component may and may not be used.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say — regardless of what a candidate otherwise knows. Cannot Say deserves particular attention because candidates avoid it, reaching for True or False to seem decisive when the honest answer is that the passage does not settle it. Small words carry the meaning: "all" is not "most," "may" is not "must," and "or" is not "and" — a distinction that matters more in this discipline than most, since accessibility criteria routinely offer alternative routes to compliance separated by "or."

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**: accessibility requirements are written documents.
- **Distinguishing statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**: framing ideas as hypotheses.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: explaining design decisions.
- **Understanding qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: knowing whether a pattern is mandatory or recommended.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **Designing for everyone**: noticing a specification never mentions a minimum size.
- **Following written direction exactly** maps to **Designing together**: feedback only works if understood as given.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Text smaller than 24 pixels must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background. Text 24 pixels or larger, or 19 pixels or larger and bold, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Logotypes are exempt from these requirements. Decorative images that convey no information are exempt, although any text within an image is not."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "All text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage sets a lower 3:1 threshold for large and bold text, and exempts logotypes. This tests catching an absolute word ("all") against stated exceptions — a genuinely common misquotation ("everything needs 4.5:1") that produces a needlessly dark palette on large headings.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Text inside a decorative image is exempt from the contrast requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence exempts decorative images but explicitly reverses that for any text within one. This tests reading past a sentence that appeared to have finished — "although" arriving late reverses the exemption for exactly the case being asked about.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "Bold text at 19 pixels needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 19px bold text satisfies "19 pixels or larger and bold," triggering 3:1, not 4.5:1. This tests the "or" trap — reading it as "and" would make the clause require both size conditions, which is not what it says, and is the single most consequential misreading in applying contrast criteria.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Statement: "A logotype containing text must meet the 4.5:1 requirement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Logotypes are exempt; text within images is not exempt — a logotype containing text sits at the intersection, and the passage does not say which provision governs. This tests recognising a genuine textual ambiguity rather than resolving it by assumption, exactly the kind of gap real standards usually address explicitly.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The review of the service found that users on screens narrower than 400 pixels completed tasks less often than users on wider screens. The review notes that these users also differed in other respects, including connection speed and whether they were using the service away from home. The review did not assess whether the layout reflowed correctly at narrow widths. It recommends that the team consider whether a narrow-width review would be worthwhile."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Users on screens narrower than 400 pixels completed tasks less often."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests the baseline reading, and, in a strong answer, noting the comparative framing the statement respects.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The narrow layout causes users to fail tasks."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage names two other ways the groups differed (connection speed, location) that could equally explain the gap. This tests recognising declared confounds as a warning against inferring causation — the design consequence differs entirely depending on the actual cause.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The layout reflows correctly at narrow widths."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly states this was not assessed. This tests treating declared silence as establishing nothing — the professional temptation runs toward False given the completion gap, but that would substitute expertise for evidence.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "The review recommends a narrow-width review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the team "consider whether" a review would be worthwhile, not that one be conducted. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X," a distinction that pervades real review documents.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Interactive elements must have a visible focus indicator with a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent colours. Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels, unless an equivalent target of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. Meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone. Icons used without a text label must be from the approved set."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Meaning may be conveyed by colour alone if the colour is high contrast."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states this without qualification. This tests catching an invented condition — high contrast does not address the requirement's actual purpose, which concerns people who cannot distinguish the colours at all.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "Every touch target must be at least 24 by 24 pixels."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An exception permits a smaller target if an equivalent 24×24px target exists elsewhere on the same screen. This tests catching a dropped exception — the exception exists for practical reasons, like an inline link inside a paragraph that cannot always be enlarged.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "An icon with a text label must be from the approved set."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The requirement applies only to icons used *without* a label; the passage is silent on labelled icons. This tests distinguishing a rule stating where it applies from a rule stating where it does not — a reader may reasonably infer labelled icons are exempt, but the passage never says so.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Icons must be from the approved set.
- B) Interactive elements must be perceivable and operable regardless of how a user perceives colour or how precisely they can point — through visible focus, adequately sized targets or equivalents, meaning carried in more than colour, and unlabelled icons drawn from a set users can be expected to recognise.
- C) Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels.
- D) Meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage while adding nothing. This tests identifying a summary broad enough to cover every requirement and its conditions — A, C and D are each a true detail promoted too far, and C also drops the equivalence exception.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a passage before concluding what a provision requires, rather than stopping at an early absolute-sounding sentence.
- Note whether the candidate reads "or" in a conditional standard as offering alternative routes, not requiring both.
- Watch for whether declared confounds or declared gaps in what was assessed are treated as genuine limits on what can be concluded.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes a rule stating where it applies from one stating where it explicitly does not.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary covering the whole passage rather than one true detail promoted above the rest.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate reads "or" as "and" in a conditional accessibility requirement.
- Rewarding an association read as causation despite a declared confound in the passage.
- Accepting a scope statement converted into an exemption for a case the passage does not actually address.
- Missing when a candidate selects a true-but-narrow detail as the main point rather than a summary covering the whole passage.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and specifically whether a candidate's instincts align with what the profession considers good practice at this level — the same scenario has different best answers for a trainee and for a head of profession.

Four principles carry most scenarios: users come first, specifically users who cannot see, read or operate something, since a design that excludes one group is not slightly worse for them, it is unusable; act within remit and be transparent at its edges — the pattern is attempt, then tell; address problems directly and early, with the person involved; and bring the value, not the opinion — a measured value ("3.1:1 against a 4.5:1 requirement") ends an argument that taste cannot.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Protecting legibility and access** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Explaining a visual decision** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Working with others' perspectives** maps to **Designing together**.
- **Using evidence** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Working in cycles** maps to **Iterative design**.
- **Understanding where design sits** maps to **Leading design** and **Designing strategically**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Not knowing how to do the task**

A senior designer asks for "an icon set for the new journey by Thursday, consistent with the system." The candidate has drawn icons but never produced a set to a system specification. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say yes, then work it out alone.
- B) Say yes, and add that this has not been done before and half an hour going through the grid, stroke and optical size rules would be really useful first.
- C) Say it cannot be done because of no training.
- D) Say yes, and quietly ask another associate to help rather than telling the senior.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Asking for guidance, specifically naming grid, stroke and optical size, is doing the job correctly at this level and easy for a senior to grant. This tests combining willingness with a specific, honest request — option C is least effective, declining a development opportunity and handing the work back with nothing offered.

**Question 2 (easy) — Something noticed in live content**

A live service's "cancel" and "continue" buttons are distinguished only by colour (green/red), no difference in shape or position beyond the words. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say nothing; the words are different, so it is fine.
- B) Raise it with a senior designer, describing specifically that a user who cannot distinguish red from green has only the labels to rely on, and that the colour is doing work the design is treating as decorative.
- C) Change the colours yourself.
- D) Note it for the next design review.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A user scanning quickly relies on colour to locate a button before reading it — a shortcut unavailable to someone who cannot distinguish the two hues. This tests recognising that a redundant-seeming cue people actually rely on is not redundant — option A is least effective, the tempting but wrong reading that different labels alone make the colour irrelevant.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

A senior designer requested the icon set finished this morning; a delivery manager then asks for slides for tomorrow's show and tell, calling it urgent. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Do the slides; the delivery manager is more senior.
- B) Do the icons and tell the delivery manager you are busy.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask both to confirm the priority.
- D) Try to do both by working through lunch.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Surfacing the conflict promptly with a concrete option turns the candidate into part of the solution rather than a bottleneck. This tests offering a specific workable alternative rather than bare escalation — option D is least effective, hiding the conflict and leaving nobody aware the team is over-committing.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A stakeholder asks for something that reduces legibility**

A communications colleague asks for body text in the brand's light grey, measuring 3.2:1 against white, below the 4.5:1 requirement. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Use the light grey; the brand matters.
- B) Explain the measured value and the threshold, and offer the brand's darker grey — or a darkened variant of the same hue — which keeps the palette recognisable while meeting the requirement.
- C) Refuse and use black.
- D) Use the light grey but increase the text size so a lower threshold applies.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Supplying the measured value converts a matter of taste into fact, and offering an alternative within the brand addresses what the colleague actually wants. This tests substituting rather than refusing — option D is least effective, gaming the criterion by enlarging text purely to escape the threshold, worsening readability for everyone.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Feedback that stings**

A senior designer says a layout is "cluttered and the hierarchy doesn't work," though the candidate believes the hierarchy is clear. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Explain the hierarchy you intended.
- B) Ask them to describe the order in which they read the page, since if an experienced designer read it in a different order from the one intended, the hierarchy is not working regardless of what was designed — then fix that.
- C) Accept the criticism and start again.
- D) Ask for the feedback in writing.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Asking what order somebody read in produces a concrete, checkable answer rather than an opinion, converting feedback into actionable information. This tests turning feedback into a checkable question — option A is least effective, since explaining intention does not change what a reader actually experiences.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A pattern you notice**

Auditing a service reveals four different shades of blue used for links across eight pages. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Change them all to the system blue.
- B) Document the four values and where each appears, check whether any of them fail contrast, and raise it with a senior designer — noting that the variation is probably a symptom of the system value not being available where those pages were built.
- C) Fix the ones that fail contrast.
- D) Note it in the audit.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Documenting precisely, prioritising by which values actually fail, and naming a likely cause (a missing system token) does more than proofreading. This tests identifying the likely systemic cause behind a symptom — option D is least effective, recording without escalating something that will keep recurring.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pressure to skip a check**

A delivery manager asks for design sign-off today, before the required accessibility check, because a build starts tomorrow. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Sign it off; the deadline is real.
- B) Explain what the check covers and ask what is possible — whether it can be done in the next hour, or whether the build could start with the components that are already checked — and raise it with a senior designer if no route works.
- C) Refuse and cite the process.
- D) Sign it off and do the check afterwards without telling anyone.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Both the deadline and the check are real, and asking what is possible often resolves it (a contrast and target-size check on one design takes twenty minutes). This tests engaging with a genuine constraint rather than skipping or citing it rigidly — option D is least effective, concealing that a design is unchecked and taking sole responsibility for a decision not the candidate's to make.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Something outside your remit**

A printed letter version of a form uses 9-point type, hard to read for the largely older population the service serves; print belongs to another team. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Ignore it; print is not yours.
- B) Raise it with a senior designer, describing the specific size and the audience, and ask whether there is a route to the team that owns the letters.
- C) Contact the print team directly.
- D) Change the digital version to match.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A user experiences one service, not separate screen and letter products, and 9-point type for an older audience is a legibility failure with the same consequences as a contrast failure. This tests documenting a discrepancy and seeking a legitimate route — option A is least effective, describing an org chart while a real user cannot read the letter.

**Question 9 (harder) — An accessibility problem found late**

Two weeks before launch, the focus indicator on every interactive element measures 1.6:1, well below the 3:1 requirement; fixing it touches every component. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with a senior designer and the delivery manager, describing precisely what a keyboard user experiences — no visible indication of where they are on the page — and ask for a decision, while checking whether the system's default focus style could simply be reinstated.
- C) Change the focus style yourself across the components.
- D) Launch and note the gap in an accessibility statement.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A missing focus indicator makes the service effectively unusable without a mouse, and checking whether reinstating the system default resolves it in an afternoon is the practical step nobody thinks of under pressure. This tests raising a serious, time-critical finding with a practical fix identified — option A is least effective, sending a genuine accessibility blocker to a backlog where it will remain unfixed.

**Question 10 (harder) — Being asked to design around a constraint you doubt**

A developer says a component cannot have more than 4 pixels of internal padding "because of the framework," and research shows the resulting touch targets are too small, causing mis-taps. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept it and make the icons smaller so they fit.
- B) Ask what the constraint actually is — whether the framework limits padding specifically, or the component's overall height, or a value somebody set once — since the answer determines whether the target can be enlarged by padding, by height, or by an invisible tap area extending beyond the visible control.
- C) Insist the padding is increased.
- D) Add a note to the design telling users to tap carefully.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** "Because of the framework" is a compressed claim worth unpacking, and an invisible tap area extending beyond the visible control resolves most such cases without changing the layout. This tests establishing what a stated constraint actually is before designing around it — option D is least effective, telling users to compensate for an unaddressed design defect.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

A colour token defined last week, now used across a live service, is discovered to measure 4.3:1 rather than the believed 4.5:1. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct the token quietly.
- B) Tell a senior designer immediately, explain the measured value and where the token is used, and flag that anything published with it in the last week is below the threshold.
- C) Correct it and mention it at the next stand-up.
- D) Leave it; 4.3 is close enough.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A token used everywhere makes a small error consequential — 4.3:1 is a failure across every element using it, and stating the measured value plus scope is what makes disclosure actionable. This tests prompt, precise disclosure of an own error, including its scope — option D is least effective, since a threshold has no discretionary zone and "close enough" applied once becomes the standard for every future judgement.

**Question 12 (harder) — An assumption in the room**

Someone says "we don't need to worry about colour blindness — it's rare," on a service with around 700,000 users a year, and red-green colour vision deficiency affecting roughly one in twelve men. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go; the team has decided.
- B) Bring the arithmetic into the room — that even a conservative estimate puts tens of thousands of affected users a year across a service this size — and ask what should happen to them, so the decision is made knowingly.
- C) Argue that all designs must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately with a senior designer afterwards.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** An intuition about rarity collapses against a concrete base number — tens of thousands of people a year at this service's scale. This tests contributing concrete arithmetic rather than an opinion, at the moment a decision is being made — option A is least effective, letting a numerically wrong assumption stand when the facts to correct it were available.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate attempts what can be competently handled and promptly tells the right person about what cannot, rather than struggling silently or escalating reflexively.
- Note whether the candidate backs a visual or design judgement with a measured value rather than an impression.
- Watch for whether the candidate asks what a stated technical constraint actually is before designing around it.
- Score for whether own errors are disclosed promptly, with the scope of impact stated.
- Note whether the candidate identifies a likely systemic cause behind a recurring visual inconsistency rather than only fixing instances.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a threshold treated as having a discretionary "close enough" zone.
- Missing when a candidate assumes a redundant-seeming colour cue is actually unused by anyone.
- Rewarding a criterion gamed (enlarging text to dodge a contrast rule) rather than genuinely met.
- Accepting advice telling users to compensate for an unaddressed design defect.
- Missing when a candidate explains their own intention in response to feedback rather than treating what a reader actually experienced as the fact to act on.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and error checking against spacing systems, type scales, contrast standards and icon sets; the arithmetic that makes design judgements defensible — reading thresholds at their exact boundary, computing ratios rather than differences, converting a defect into a specific number of affected people, and recognising when a mean hides an outlier worth investigating; the discipline of reading accessibility criteria, brand guidelines and stakeholder emails at exactly the strength the text supports, especially the difference between "or" and "and"; and the situational judgement that defines a strong associate graphic designer — raising problems early with a measured value attached, asking what a stated constraint actually is, and disclosing an own mistake promptly with its scope.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answer is very often the one that asks for guidance rather than acting alone. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
