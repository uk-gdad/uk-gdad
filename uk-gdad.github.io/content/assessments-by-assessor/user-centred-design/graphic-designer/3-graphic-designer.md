# Graphic Designer - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for graphic designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A graphic designer develops designs based on evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes, is trusted to make good decisions, recognises when to ask for further guidance, contributes to the development of design concepts, and interprets evidence-based research and incorporates it into design work. The role is defined functionally: creating the graphic elements underpinning interaction and service design, using layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure content is legible and readable and that users see and understand interactions. That functional definition is what makes this a role with an evidence base rather than a matter of taste — a type size, a contrast ratio, a spacing value or an icon either lets somebody complete a task or prevents them, and the difference is measurable. A good deal of what this material tests is whether the candidate reasons that way under pressure.

At this level, a candidate is rarely choosing between a right answer and a wrong one, and usually between defensible options under incomplete information, competing constraints, and real consequences. Three features of the role shape the material: the candidate is trusted to make good decisions, so the strongest answers do not escalate everything and do know which decisions belong to someone else; the role designs strategically, identifying risks and opportunities and contributing to design patterns and components; and the role plans and runs design sessions and gives and receives constructive feedback, so several items concern getting good thinking out of a room — unusually hard in a visual discipline where everybody has an opinion.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate names the mechanism behind a finding rather than stopping at the measurement, and for whether visual and design judgements are consistently backed by a measured value. Situational judgement items at this level reward a candidate who supplies the measured value that converts a matter of taste into a matter of fact, who addresses problems directly and early with the person involved, and who fixes the systemic cause (the token, the pattern) rather than only the instance in front of them.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At this level the materials get messier and the reasoning less mechanical: evidence supporting two readings, constraints that turn out to be negotiable and others that do not, and prioritisation problems with no dominant option.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

Two things distinguish this section at this level. First, a candidate is expected to reason about risk and opportunity rather than only about problems — a colour token, a type scale or a component style is a decision made once and paid for everywhere, and its accessibility characteristics propagate to every place it is used. Second, several questions have no clean answer, only a best one, and the discriminating factor is whether the candidate can identify the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence and state its limits.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Evidence-based design**, analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining evidence relevant to users or a service.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Design communication**, clearly explaining design decisions using appropriate tools and methods.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**, ensuring a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations.
- **Prioritisation and risk reasoning** map to **Designing strategically**: identifying risks and opportunities and contributing to patterns and components.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Iterative design**: prototyping at different fidelities and iterating patterns and components.
- **Reasoning about positions** maps to **Designing together**: identifying and engaging the right people throughout the design process.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Standard: every interface component must meet 4.5:1 contrast for text under 24 pixels, have a visible focus indicator at 3:1, have touch targets of at least 24 by 24 pixels, use spacing from the system scale, and never convey meaning by colour alone. A component has: 14px text at 5.2:1; a focus indicator at 2.4:1; 44px targets; spacing values of 12 and 20 pixels against a scale of multiples of 8; states distinguished by colour and icon. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Text passes (5.2:1 > 4.5:1); the focus indicator fails (2.4:1 < 3:1); targets pass (44px > 24px); spacing fails as one requirement, since 12 and 20 are both off-scale but count as a single failed requirement, not two; colour-and-icon satisfies "not colour alone". Two unmet. This tests distinguishing a count of failed requirements from a count of failed values (the trap in C) and checking the least-inspected property — focus indicators only appear under keyboard use and are easy for a candidate reviewing with a mouse to miss.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Standard: "A custom colour value may be used only if no system token is suitable, unless the value has been approved by the lead graphic designer." A developer reports: "We shipped a custom colour last sprint, and there is a suitable token." Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) The value was approved by the lead.
- C) Either the value was approved by the lead, or the standard was breached.
- D) The custom colour fails contrast.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, licenses only the disjunction — a custom value may be perfectly compliant, sometimes approved precisely because it solves a contrast problem the token does not, so D imports an unsupported claim. This tests concluding only what the facts guarantee, and a strong candidate's answer would open by establishing which branch applies rather than assuming either.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Competing explanations**

Task completion is 88% on screens wider than 768px and 54% on screens narrower than 400px. A product manager proposes a mobile redesign. Ask for the most rigorous analysis.

- A) Agree; the mobile layout is failing.
- B) The gap is real and large, and narrow-screen users differ in more than screen size — connection speed, whether they are away from home, whether they have documents to hand — so before committing to a redesign the cheap step is opening the page at 375px and at 400% zoom, which takes ten minutes and distinguishes a layout failure from a context effect.
- C) A 34-point gap can only be explained by the layout.
- D) Narrow screens should be unsupported.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Screen width correlates with more than screen width, and treating the gap as self-explanatory risks a redesign that fixes nothing. This tests holding a finding seriously while naming confounds and, distinctively for this role, identifying the cheap empirical test that can settle it directly — a table forcing horizontal scroll at 375px, or a primary action below the fold, would answer the question outright.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritising by what can change**

Capacity for one substantial piece of work this quarter: (1) a colour and type token set for a new service, fixed next month, inherited by five services; (2) a live page with a known contrast failure on a secondary label; (3) exploratory visual concepts for next year; (4) revisiting a layout produced six weeks ago after minor copy changes. Ask which is strongest, and why.

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will be inherited by five services, and a token set embeds its accessibility characteristics everywhere it is used — far harder to change later than to get right now.
- C) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.
- D) Item 4, because unverified changes are a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack — a token inherited by five services propagates every property, good or bad, and changing it after adoption means coordinating five teams. This tests ranking by what still can be changed and by how far a decision propagates, rather than by immediacy alone (A confuses urgency with size of effort required; item 2 is a single label with a fix that takes an hour).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Risk reasoning about a token**

A proposed grey for secondary text measures 4.6:1 against white and will be added to the token set. Ask for the most complete risk analysis.

- A) It passes 4.5:1, so it is fine.
- B) It passes against white by a margin of 0.1, meaning it will fail against tinted panel backgrounds already in the system — and because tokens are used wherever they look right, without being re-measured in context, a value with almost no headroom is a defect waiting to be discovered on a page nobody has audited.
- C) It should be darker.
- D) Secondary text should not exist.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests naming the mechanism rather than the measurement — the moment the grey sits on a light grey panel, which token sets invariably contain, the effective ratio drops below threshold, and because a token is available everywhere nobody re-measures it in context. Option C reaches the right conclusion without the reasoning that would let the candidate defend it when asked why 4.6 is not enough.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Strength of a finding**

Nine of twelve participants failed to notice a secondary action. Ask which conclusion is best supported.

- A) 75% of users do not notice it.
- B) Its visual treatment is not signalling that it is available, evidenced across most participants, warranting a change to its prominence or position and a follow-up check on whether the change helps.
- C) It should be made a primary button.
- D) The page is badly designed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Nine of twelve is a strong qualitative signal, stated at that strength while naming the mechanism, rather than converting it into a population percentage (A, the commonest overreach in design reporting). This tests resisting a jump to one specific fix — making a secondary action look primary usually creates a competing call to action and weakens both, so the solution space (position, spacing, heading, whether the action belongs on the page at all) is wider than option C allows.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Designing under a constraint**

A communications team requires the brand's primary colour, measuring 3.4:1 against white, for interface text. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Use it; the brand takes precedence.
- B) Establish what the brand requirement actually protects — recognisability — then propose the primary at full strength where 3:1 applies, such as large headings and non-text elements, with a darker variant of the same hue for body text, so the palette reads as the brand while every element meets its threshold.
- C) Refuse and use black.
- D) Increase all text to 24 pixels so 3:1 applies.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests separating a brand's purpose from a specific instruction and finding a route satisfying both — most brand palettes already contain a darker variant of the primary, and naming where the primary can appear at full strength is what makes the counter-proposal acceptable rather than adversarial. Option D games the criterion rather than meeting its purpose, producing oversized text that scans badly for everyone.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A relied-upon study ran on a single high-quality external monitor at full brightness. Four findings: (i) participants could not find a secondary action; (ii) participants read the body text comfortably; (iii) participants misinterpreted an icon; (iv) participants found colour-coded status labels easy to distinguish. Ask which findings are most compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; display conditions do not matter.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iv) are most compromised, because a bright high-quality display flatters legibility and colour discrimination in ways a phone in daylight or an older office monitor will not, while (i) and (iii) concern position and comprehension, which the display affects far less.
- D) Only (iv).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests scoping a methodological flaw precisely rather than treating it as invalidating everything or nothing — the display flatters exactly the properties findings (ii) and (iv) claim, while (i) and (iii) concern where something is and what an icon means, barely touched by display quality. Display conditions are the sampling-frame problem of visual disciplines: testing on the best screen in the building is the equivalent of recruiting only confident users.

**Question 9 (harder) — Spotting the opportunity**

A page's error rate on one field is 4%, but 78% of users who make the error correct it on the second attempt; the team treats the high recovery rate as evidence the page is fine. Ask for the strongest observation.

- A) Agree; users recover.
- B) A 78% recovery rate means 22% do not — and more usefully, everyone who makes the error pays a cost in time and confidence even when they recover, while the error itself signals the field's label, format hint or input type is producing a predictable mistake that could be prevented rather than recovered from.
- C) The error rate is acceptable.
- D) The error message should be improved.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests reading a recovery rate as masking two things: a real tail of non-recovery, and a predictable error that is a design problem upstream of the message. Option D treats the recovery rather than the cause — a real improvement, and the second-best answer most teams reach for first.

**Question 10 (harder) — Two things moving apart**

After body text grew from 16 to 19 pixels, time on page rose 20% and completion was unchanged. A colleague concludes the larger text slowed people down. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) Agree; larger text means more scrolling.
- B) Time on page is an ambiguous measure — it rises both when people struggle and when they read more of the content — so unchanged completion with longer dwell is as consistent with users now reading text they previously skipped as with being slowed down, and the way to distinguish them is whether errors or contact volume moved.
- C) The text should be reverted to 16 pixels.
- D) The redesign failed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests recognising a directionless metric — a page people cannot read produces long dwell, and so does a page people can finally read — and naming what would resolve the ambiguity (errors, contact volume, both directional). Reverting on this evidence (C) risks undoing a genuine improvement.

**Question 11 (harder) — A constraint that may not be one**

A developer says a component's touch targets cannot be enlarged "because of the layout"; research shows users mis-tapping. Ask for the most productive line of enquiry.

- A) Accept it and make the icons smaller so they fit.
- B) Ask what specifically constrains the size — the visible control, the space between controls, or a container height — and raise the option that resolves most cases: extending the interactive area invisibly beyond the visible graphic, since a touch target does not have to match the size of what is drawn.
- C) Insist the layout changes.
- D) Add spacing between the controls only.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Because of the layout" is a compressed claim, and this tests establishing what it actually means before designing around it — a 16px icon can carry a 44px target without changing the layout at all. Option C asserts without understanding, and a candidate who treats every constraint as negotiable is not believed about the ones that are.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in your own work**

Three weeks after a token set now used in two services was defined, the secondary text token is found to measure 4.4:1 rather than the recorded 4.6:1. Ask what should happen.

- A) Note it for the next token review.
- B) Tell the teams and the lead now, state the measured value and where the token is used, correct it, and check whether any other tokens were measured against the wrong background — since the error suggests a method problem rather than a one-off slip.
- C) Correct the token quietly.
- D) Leave it; 4.4 is close.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A token used in two services means the error is live in both, and 4.4:1 is a failure rather than a near miss. This tests prompt, precise disclosure with a diagnostic follow-up — a mis-measured token usually means the value was checked against the wrong background or tool, making everything defined in the same session suspect. Option D is least defensible: "close" applied once to a threshold becomes the standard for every future judgement.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes a count of failed requirements from a count of failed values.
- Note whether the candidate names the mechanism behind a finding rather than stopping at the measurement.
- Watch for whether the candidate identifies a cheap empirical test that could settle an ambiguous finding directly.
- Score for whether the candidate ranks work by how far a decision propagates (reuse, inheritance) rather than by immediacy alone.
- Note whether the candidate resists jumping to a single fix when the underlying cause could be several things.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting participants converted into a population percentage.
- Missing when a candidate treats a threshold as having a discretionary zone — a value just below is still a fail.
- Rewarding a value chosen at the boundary of a threshold without noting the lack of headroom.
- Accepting an answer that treats a study's flaw as invalidating everything or nothing, rather than scoping it precisely.
- Missing when a candidate prescribes a specific fix before the underlying problem is properly named.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles the quantitative material this role produces and consumes. The discipline is unusually full of values with thresholds attached — contrast ratios, type scales, line lengths, spacing units, grid arithmetic, target sizes, viewport widths — and a great many of them can be passed or failed rather than merely preferred.

At this level the emphasis shifts from calculating to interrogating: candidates still need to compute correctly, but the harder items ask whether a figure means what somebody claims. This is professionally consequential for a graphic designer specifically, because visual decisions are the ones colleagues feel most entitled to have opinions about, and a measured value is the only thing that reliably converts a matter of taste into a matter of fact.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided. Distractors are built from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value used as the base in a percentage change, off-by-one errors in grid arithmetic, and thresholds read as approximate.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Working with ratios and thresholds** maps to **Designing for everyone**: meeting a standard is a numerical question with a definite answer.
- **Reading analytics accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing and clearly explaining evidence relevant to users.
- **Comparing groups fairly** maps to **Designing for everyone**: a gap between groups is the first numerical trace of exclusion.
- **Grid and scale arithmetic** maps to **Designing strategically**: the value of patterns and components rests on internal consistency.
- **Quantifying impact** maps to **Design communication**: a measured consequence is the most persuasive form a design argument takes.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **Evidence-based design**: a wrong measure sends design effort to the wrong place.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Of 4,800 users who reached a page, 3,600 completed it. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 3,600÷4,800=0.75=75%. This tests basic percentage calculation, checkable by estimating three quarters first — since 2,400 would be half.

**Question 2 (easy) — Thresholds at the boundary**

Standard: 4.5:1 for text under 24px, 3:1 for text 24px or larger. Four cases: 14px at 4.4:1; 16px at 4.5:1; 24px at 2.9:1; 30px at 3.0:1. Ask how many meet the standard.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 14px needs 4.5:1 — 4.4 fails; 16px needs 4.5:1 — 4.5 meets it exactly, "at least" being inclusive; 24px needs 3:1 (it is "24 or larger") — 2.9 fails; 30px needs 3:1 — 3.0 passes. Two pass. This tests three precise boundary readings at once: 4.4 fails though it rounds to 4, 4.5 passes because "at least" is inclusive, and 24px falls into the larger-text category because the wording is "24 or larger" rather than "over 24" — reading any of these loosely produces false failures or false passes.

**Question 3 (easy) — Grid arithmetic**

A 12-column grid spans 1,152px with 32px gutters between columns and no outer margin. Ask for one column's width.

- A) 64 pixels
- B) 67 pixels
- C) 96 pixels
- D) 100 pixels

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 11 gutters (not 12): 11×32=352; remaining 1,152−352=800; 800÷12≈66.67, rounding to 67. This tests the n−1 gutter rule — option A assumes 12 gutters (a suspiciously round 64, part of why the error survives) and option C ignores gutters entirely, the commonest error of all.

**Question 4 (easy) — Line length**

A column is 720px wide with 18px type at an average character width of 9px. Ask how many characters fit on a line, and what that suggests.

- A) 80 characters, comfortably within the usual range
- B) 80 characters, at or slightly above the upper end of the comfortable range for continuous reading
- C) 40 characters, uncomfortably short
- D) 60 characters, ideal

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 720÷9=80 characters; the comfortable measure for continuous reading sits roughly between 45 and 75 characters, so 80 is at or just beyond the upper edge. This tests pairing an arithmetic result with the range that gives it meaning — the arithmetic alone (correctly done in both A and B) does not produce a judgement without knowing what 80 characters actually means for reading.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Comparing groups fairly**

Of 900 users on wide screens, 810 completed a task. Of 600 users on narrow screens, 300 completed. Ask for the gap in percentage points.

- A) 30
- B) 40
- C) 50
- D) 90

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Wide: 810÷900=90%. Narrow: 300÷600=50%. Gap: 90−50=40 points. This tests converting to rates before comparing, since options C and D are the two group rates offered as if they were the gap — a distractor pattern that recurs because numbers from a candidate's own working frequently appear among the options.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change**

After a type size increase, weekly completions rose from 640 to 800. Ask for the percentage increase.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 80%
- D) 125%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 160÷640=25%. This tests dividing by the original value — option A (160÷800) is the most common error in this question type and under-reports every improvement being demonstrated.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Quantifying impact**

A service handles 800,000 sessions a year; an unreadable error message affects the 6% of sessions in which the error occurs. Ask how many people a year are affected.

- A) 4,800
- B) 48,000
- C) 80,000
- D) 480,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 0.06×800,000=48,000. This tests converting a defect into a number of affected people — the move that makes a visual argument land, since "the contrast is a bit low" is a matter of taste and "48,000 people a year hit an error message they may not be able to read" is not.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a base**

12% of users abandon at a step, equal to 3,600 people a month. Ask how many reach the step.

- A) 4,032
- B) 15,000
- C) 30,000
- D) 43,200

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 3,600÷0.12=30,000. This tests working back from a percentage and count to the base, verifiable by reversing (12% of 30,000 is 3,600) — the skill matters because colleagues quote percentages far more readily than bases, and 12% of 30,000 is a very different problem from 12% of 300.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

Journey: 40,000 start, 32,000 reach a form, 24,000 reach review, 22,800 submit. Ask what proportion of those who reached review submitted.

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 22,800÷24,000=95%. This tests identifying the group the question names as the denominator — option A uses 40,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked, and misdirects effort away from where the losses (8,000 before the form, 8,000 before review) actually sit.

**Question 10 (harder) — A ratio with no headroom**

A token measures 4.6:1 against white; the system contains panel backgrounds at various light tints. Ask for the most useful observation.

- A) It passes, so it is fine.
- B) It passes against white by 0.1 and will fail against any tinted panel, because contrast falls as the background darkens towards the text — and since tokens are used wherever they look right rather than re-measured in context, a value with no headroom becomes a defect discovered on a page nobody audited.
- C) It should be tested on each panel.
- D) Panels should be white.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests naming the mechanism rather than the measurement, and drawing the practical conclusion — build headroom into anything reusable. Option C is not wrong and is impractical as a standing requirement, since new panels appear faster than audits do.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion on narrow screens rose from 50% to 62%. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) A 12% increase.
- B) A 12 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 24% relative increase.
- C) A 24 percentage point increase.
- D) A 19.4% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Arithmetic gap 62−50=12 points; relative increase 12÷50=24%. This tests labelling both figures correctly — "completion rose 24%" and "rose 12 percentage points" describe the same change and one sounds twice as impressive, so a candidate who supplies both is showing the kind of numeric trustworthiness colleagues rely on before accepting a visual argument.

**Question 12 (harder) — A directionless metric**

After a type size increase, time on page rose 20% and completion was unchanged. A colleague concludes users were slowed down. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) Agree; longer text means more scrolling.
- B) Time on page has no direction — it rises both when people struggle and when they read more of the content — so unchanged completion with longer dwell is as consistent with people now reading text they previously skipped as with being slowed, and the way to distinguish them is whether errors or contact volume moved.
- C) Revert the change.
- D) The change failed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests recognising a metric with no direction and naming what would resolve the ambiguity. Reverting on this evidence (C) risks undoing an improvement, a specific hazard for legibility changes whose benefits show up in comprehension rather than speed.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads a numeric threshold at its precise boundary rather than rounding toward a convenient answer.
- Note whether the candidate converts raw counts to rates before comparing groups of different sizes.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change.
- Score for whether the candidate converts a defect into a number of affected people, rather than leaving an argument qualitative.
- Note whether the candidate recognises a directionless metric (such as time on page) and names what would actually resolve its ambiguity.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a contrast ratio or grid calculation rounded in the candidate's favour.
- Missing when a candidate ignores or miscounts gutters in grid arithmetic.
- Rewarding a comparison of raw counts across groups of unequal size.
- Accepting a token or spacing value chosen exactly at a threshold, without the candidate noting the lack of headroom.
- Missing when a candidate reverts a design change on the strength of an ambiguous, directionless metric.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. The documents constraining graphic design in government are unusually precise: accessibility criteria specify ratios and thresholds, brand guidelines specify permitted and prohibited uses, design system documentation specifies when a component may and may not be used. Reading any of them slightly wrong produces either a design that fails an audit or an unnecessary argument — and in this discipline the second costs as much as the first, since credibility spent on an unnecessary argument is not available for a necessary one.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify True, False, or Cannot Say — True where the passage establishes it, False where the passage contradicts it, Cannot Say where the passage settles it neither way. The governing rule is that the candidate answers from the passage alone; professional knowledge must be set aside, which grows harder with experience since the more accessibility criteria a candidate has applied, the more readily they supply the clause they expect.

One word matters more in this discipline than most: "or". Accessibility criteria routinely offer alternative routes to compliance separated by "or", and reading it as "and" produces unnecessary work, needlessly dark palettes, and avoidable arguments with whoever chose the brand.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**: ensuring a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: clearly explaining problems and design ideas to others.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: knowing whether a pattern is mandatory or recommended.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**: noticing a specification never states a minimum has a consequence.
- **Reading feedback accurately** maps to **Designing together**: giving and receiving constructive design feedback.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Text smaller than 24 pixels must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background. Text 24 pixels or larger, or 19 pixels or larger and bold, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Non-text elements that convey information, including icons and the boundaries of form fields, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Logotypes are exempt."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "All text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage sets 3:1 for large and bold text and exempts logotypes, contradicting "all". This tests the most reliable pattern in the section and also the most common real misquotation — "everything needs 4.5:1" — which applied to a large heading produces a needlessly dark palette and an avoidable argument.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "The boundary of a form field must meet a contrast requirement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The third sentence names field boundaries explicitly as needing at least 3:1. This tests catching the provision designers most often overlook — a very light hairline around an input can look elegant while measuring well below threshold, which is exactly why the passage names it explicitly.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "Bold text at 20 pixels needs 4.5:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Bold text at 20px satisfies "19 pixels or larger and bold," triggering 3:1. This tests the "or" trap — read as "and", the clause would require text to be both 24 pixels and bold, nearly useless and pushing designers towards unnecessarily dark palettes.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "A decorative icon must meet 3:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The 3:1 requirement applies to non-text elements "that convey information"; a decorative icon by definition conveys none, but the passage does not explicitly exempt decorative elements, only state where the requirement applies. This tests the distinction between stating where a rule applies and stating where it does not — a fine distinction that recurs constantly in standards.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The review found that users on screens narrower than 400 pixels completed tasks less often than users on wider screens. The review notes that these users also differed in connection speed and in whether they were using the service away from home. The review did not assess whether the layout reflowed correctly at narrow widths. It recommends that the team consider whether a narrow-width review would be worthwhile."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Narrow-screen users completed tasks less often."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, respecting the comparative framing. A straightforward baseline the harder items in this passage build on.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The narrow layout causes users to fail tasks."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage names two confounds (connection speed, location) that could equally explain the gap. This tests treating a declared association as an association, not a cause — the design consequence differs entirely depending on the actual cause, layout versus context.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The layout reflows correctly at narrow widths."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states this was not assessed. This tests treating declared silence as establishing nothing — the professional temptation runs towards False given the completion gap, and marking False would substitute expertise for the evidence actually given.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "The review recommends a narrow-width review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the team "consider whether" a review would be worthwhile — including the possibility of concluding it would not. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X", a distinction that pervades real review documents.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Interactive elements must have a visible focus indicator with a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent colours. Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels, unless an equivalent target of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. Meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone. Where a component is provided in the design system, services must use it unless an exemption has been agreed with the lead graphic designer."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Meaning may be conveyed by colour alone if the contrast is high."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states this without qualification. This tests catching an invented condition — high contrast does not address the requirement's purpose, which concerns people who cannot distinguish the hues rather than people who cannot see them clearly.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "Every touch target must be at least 24 by 24 pixels."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An exception permits a smaller target if an equivalent 24×24px target exists elsewhere on the same screen. This tests catching a dropped exception — the exception exists so an inline link inside a paragraph need not be fought into a larger size when the same action is reachable elsewhere.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "A service using a custom component instead of a design system component has breached the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Services must use the system component "unless an exemption has been agreed"; whether an exemption was agreed is not stated. This tests the same rule-plus-exception structure as the cognitive section's logic items, appearing here in reading form — resisting the conclusion that feels obvious from the general rule when the facts are silent on the exception.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Services must use design system components.
- B) Interactive elements must be perceivable and operable regardless of how a user perceives colour or how precisely they can point, and services should draw on shared components unless an exemption is agreed — with visible focus, adequately sized targets or equivalents, and meaning carried in more than colour.
- C) Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels.
- D) Meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary covering the whole passage without adding anything — A, C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest, with C also dropping the equivalence exception. A summary that drops an exception is how a standard acquires a reputation for being unreasonable.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a passage before concluding what a provision requires.
- Note whether the candidate reads "or" in a conditional standard as offering alternative routes, not requiring both.
- Watch for whether declared confounds or declared gaps in what was assessed are treated as genuine limits on what can be concluded.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes stating where a rule applies from stating where it does not.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary covering the whole passage, including its exceptions, rather than one true-but-narrow detail.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged from the candidate's own accessibility knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate reads "or" as "and" in a conditional accessibility requirement.
- Rewarding an association read as causation despite a declared confound.
- Accepting a summary that drops an exception the passage carries.
- Missing when a candidate marks False on something merely suspected rather than something the passage actually contradicts.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. A graphic designer is a confident, competent practitioner trusted to make good decisions, who recognises when to ask for guidance, contributes to design concepts, plans and runs design sessions, and gives and receives constructive feedback. The strongest answers involve exercising real professional judgement, holding a position under pressure when the evidence warrants it, and knowing which decisions belong to someone else.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Users come first, specifically those who cannot see, read or operate something. Bring the value, not the opinion — the distinctively graphic-design signature of a strong answer, since visual decisions are the ones colleagues feel most entitled to have opinions about, and a measured value ends an argument that taste cannot. Address problems directly, early, with the person involved. And fix the token, not the instance — noticing that one off-system colour means the token was unavailable, and doing something about that, is a marker of the seniority the role is working towards.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Explaining and defending design decisions** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Protecting legibility and access** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Running sessions and engaging the right people** maps to **Designing together**.
- **Giving and receiving feedback** maps to **Designing together**, named in both directions.
- **Using evidence** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Risk, opportunity and contribution to patterns** map to **Designing strategically**.
- **Supporting colleagues** maps to **Leading design**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A session where everyone has an opinion**

Twenty minutes into a design session on visual hierarchy, four people are debating a colour and nobody has mentioned the heading structure the session was convened to resolve. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it run; the colour matters too.
- B) Note the colour question for later, restate the decision the session needs to reach, and change the format — for instance asking everyone to mark on a printout the order in which they would read the page, producing evidence rather than preferences.
- C) Tell the group the colour debate is off-topic.
- D) End the session and reschedule.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Colour is the most opinion-attracting element in any design conversation. This tests adapting a session format that is producing preferences rather than evidence — asking people to mark reading order converts a discussion into data very hard to argue with — over discarding twenty minutes and everyone's availability (D) or spending goodwill unnecessarily (C).

**Question 2 (easy) — A brand constraint that reduces legibility**

A communications colleague requires the brand's light grey for body text, measuring 3.2:1 against a 4.5:1 requirement. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Use it; the brand matters.
- B) State the measured value and the threshold, and offer the brand's darker grey — or a darkened variant of the same hue — which keeps the palette recognisable while meeting the requirement.
- C) Refuse and use black.
- D) Use the light grey and enlarge the text so a lower threshold applies.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Supplying the measured value converts a matter of taste into fact, and offering an alternative from within the brand addresses what the colleague actually wants. This tests substituting rather than refusing outright — option D is least effective, gaming the criterion by enlarging text purely to escape the threshold, producing oversized text that scans badly for everyone including the people the rule protects.

**Question 3 (easy) — An off-system value**

A spacing value of 20 pixels is found in a component, against a system scale of multiples of 8. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Change it to 24.
- B) Find out what the 20 was solving before changing it — an off-scale nudge usually indicates a real layout problem somebody fixed by eye — then either resolve that properly or, if the scale genuinely cannot accommodate the case, raise it as a gap in the system.
- C) Leave it; 20 is close to 24.
- D) Raise a ticket.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** An off-scale value is usually a symptom, and changing it without understanding it risks reintroducing the underlying problem. This tests investigating before fixing, and naming the possibility that the system itself has a gap — a contribution to patterns and components — over normalising a value that breaks a scale's core property, predictability (C, least effective).

**Question 4 (moderate) — Feedback that stings**

In a critique, a senior colleague says a layout is "cluttered and the hierarchy doesn't work," though the candidate believes it is clear. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Explain the hierarchy that was intended.
- B) Ask what order they read the page in, since if an experienced designer read it in a different order from the one designed, the hierarchy is not working regardless of intention — then fix that specific thing.
- C) Accept it and start again.
- D) Ask for the feedback in writing.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Both things can be true — a hierarchy was designed, and the page does not communicate it. This tests converting feedback into a checkable question rather than defending intention — option A is least effective, since explaining intention does not change what a reader experiences, and it teaches the room that feedback will be met with a defence.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A developer's constraint**

A developer says touch targets cannot be enlarged "because of the layout," and research shows users mis-tapping. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Make the icons smaller so more fit.
- B) Ask what specifically constrains the size, and raise the option that resolves most cases: extending the interactive area invisibly beyond the visible graphic, since a touch target need not match the size of what is drawn.
- C) Insist the layout changes.
- D) Add a note asking users to tap carefully.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This tests establishing what a stated constraint actually is before designing around it, and bringing a mechanism (an invisible extended target) that resolves most cases without changing anything visible. Option D is least effective, asking users to compensate for an unaddressed defect — the clearest possible statement that the problem was noticed and not fixed.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Evidence that contradicts your design**

In testing, seven of ten participants missed a secondary action positioned deliberately. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Note that the participants were rushed and keep the design.
- B) Treat it as a signal about the design, look at what drew attention away — position, contrast, competing weight, or whether the action answers a question the page has not yet raised — and propose a change to test next round.
- C) Make it a primary button.
- D) Ask for more participants.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Seven of ten missing the same element is a signal, not a coincidence. This tests treating consistent behaviour as a signal and considering the full range of mechanisms, including sequencing — an action can be visible and still ignored if the user has no reason to want it yet. Option A is least effective, dismissing consistent behaviour as participant error, the most reliable way to stop learning from research.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Contributing to the system**

A recurring layout problem has been solved with a component variant that works well in one service. Ask for the most effective next step.

- A) Keep using it locally.
- B) Document it with the reasoning and the evidence, including what was tested and what was not, and propose it to whoever maintains the system — accepting it may be changed or rejected, which is preferable to five services each inventing their own version.
- C) Add it to the system directly.
- D) Share a screenshot in the design channel.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests contributing to shared patterns rather than keeping a solved problem local — an honest note about what was not tested is what prevents a variant acquiring more authority than it earned. Option A is least effective, keeping a solution while others repeat the work and diverge.

**Question 8 (harder) — An accessibility problem found late**

Two weeks before launch, the focus indicator across every component is found to measure 1.6:1, well below the 3:1 requirement. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with the delivery manager and service owner, describe precisely what a keyboard user experiences — no visible indication of where they are — ask for an explicit recorded decision, and check whether the system's default focus style can simply be reinstated.
- C) Change the focus style across the components directly.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A failing focus indicator makes the service effectively unusable without a mouse, and weighing that against a launch date is a decision for people with authority, though raising it precisely and now is entirely the candidate's to do. This tests securing an explicit recorded decision and checking the practical fix nobody thinks of under pressure — reinstating a compliant default may resolve everything in an afternoon. Option A is least effective, sending a genuine blocker to a backlog where it will remain unfixed.

**Question 9 (harder) — Consensus that would exclude people**

A team has converged on a status system using colour alone, which the evidence indicates will fail users with colour vision deficiency, and reopening it reverses a decision people are relieved to have made. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go; the team has decided.
- B) Raise it once, clearly, with the arithmetic — that across this service's user base the affected group runs into tens of thousands a year — and propose the minimal mitigation of adding an icon or text label, then if the team proceeds ensure the risk is documented and propose measuring the outcome.
- C) Escalate to the service owner.
- D) Keep raising it until the team changes course.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests raising evidence once, with a minimal proposed mitigation rather than a full redesign, and documenting the risk if the decision stands — threading the obligation to say the thing against the need to keep working with the team. Option A is least effective, since a consensus excluding people who already have fewer options is not a value, and silence with evidence in hand is complicity.

**Question 10 (harder) — A token you defined**

A colour token, now used in two services, is found to measure 4.4:1 rather than the recorded 4.6:1. Ask what should happen.

- A) Correct it quietly.
- B) Tell both teams and the lead now, state the measured value and where the token is used, correct it, and check whether other tokens defined in the same session were measured the same way — since the error suggests a method problem rather than a slip.
- C) Correct it and mention it at stand-up.
- D) Leave it; 4.4 is close.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A token used everywhere is what makes a small error consequential, and this tests prompt, precise disclosure with the diagnostic follow-up that distinguishes a fix from a proper response. Option D is least defensible: "close" applied once to a threshold becomes the standard for every future judgement.

**Question 11 (harder) — An assumption in the room**

In a session, someone says "colour blindness is rare, we don't need to design for it," on a service with around 700,000 users a year. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go.
- B) Bring the arithmetic — around one in twelve men and one in two hundred women have red-green colour vision deficiency, meaning tens of thousands of people a year across a user base this size — and ask what should happen to them, so the decision is made knowingly.
- C) Argue that all designs must be accessible.
- D) Raise it privately afterwards.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** An intuition about rarity collapses against a concrete base number. This tests contributing arithmetic that keeps the conversation about the design rather than about who is right, at the moment the decision is being made — option A is least effective, letting a numerically wrong assumption stand when the facts to correct it were available.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in your own work**

Three weeks after supporting a design decision, the evidence behind it turns out to have come from testing on a single high-quality monitor at full brightness. Ask what should happen.

- A) Note it as a limitation next time.
- B) Tell the team now, scope precisely which conclusions are affected — legibility and colour discrimination findings, rather than findings about position or comprehension — and propose a quick re-check on a phone in daylight and a dimmed laptop, which costs an hour.
- C) Quietly re-check and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Say nothing; the decision was probably fine.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Testing on the best screen available is the display equivalent of recruiting only confident users. This tests scoping the damage precisely — some findings survive a flawed condition, some do not — rather than minimising or catastrophising, and proposing a fix proportionate to the problem. Option D is least defensible, substituting a guess for the evidence originally relied on.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate supplies a measured value that converts a matter of taste into a matter of fact.
- Note whether the candidate addresses a problem directly and early with the person involved, rather than routing around them or staying silent.
- Watch for whether the candidate fixes a systemic cause (a token, a pattern) rather than only the instance found.
- Score for whether an own error is disclosed promptly, with the scope of impact and a diagnostic follow-up.
- Note whether the candidate treats consistent research findings as a signal to investigate rather than dismissing them as participant error.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a design decision defended by explaining intention rather than by what a reader actually experienced.
- Missing when a candidate dismisses consistent research findings as participant error.
- Rewarding a single specific fix proposed before the underlying problem is properly diagnosed.
- Accepting a threshold treated as having a discretionary "close enough" zone.
- Missing when a candidate keeps a solved systemic problem local rather than contributing it back to a shared system.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and risk reasoning across accessibility standards, colour tokens, and component audits; the arithmetic that makes design judgements defensible — reading thresholds at their precise boundary, converting a defect into a specific number of affected people, and recognising when a metric such as time on page has no direction; the discipline of reading accessibility criteria, brand guidelines and review documents at exactly the strength the text supports, especially the difference between "or" as alternative routes and "and" as a stricter combined requirement; and the situational judgement that defines a strong graphic designer at this level — bringing a measured value rather than an opinion, addressing problems directly and early, and fixing the systemic cause rather than only the instance in front of them.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and the strongest answers in this material are the ones that stay honest about uncertainty rather than sound most decisive. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
