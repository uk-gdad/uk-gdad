# Lead Graphic Designer - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for lead graphic designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A lead graphic designer is an expert practitioner who influences and mentors others, works with service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts, sets direction and assures the quality of design delivery across teams, and leads multiple or highly complex services — gaining support for design strategy from senior leaders, communicating it across organisational boundaries, coaching others, measurably improving inclusive design practice across multiple teams, embedding iterative working, and creating the conditions in which designers can do good work.

Three features of the role shape this material. First, a lead assures work they did not produce, on services they have not seen, judged from an output rather than the thinking behind it. Second, a lead works at the level of shared assets — tokens, type scales, component styles, grids, and standards — decisions paid for many times over, whose accessibility characteristics propagate to every place they are used. Third, a lead's authority is largely informal, and in a visual discipline that is a particular difficulty: everybody has opinions about colour and type in a way they do not about database schemas, so influence rests almost entirely on evidence and measurement rather than craft authority.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate diagnoses a mechanism behind a recurring problem rather than treating each instance separately, and for whether they scope a methodological flaw precisely rather than condemning or dismissing it wholesale. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a candidate who fixes the shared asset rather than the instance, who absorbs pressure downward so a junior colleague is not left arguing alone, and who brings a measured value in place of an opinion.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic: work produced by other people, standards applied unevenly, evidence gathered under conditions nobody recorded, and prioritisation problems whose units are shared assets rather than screens.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

Three things characterise this section at this level. First, reasoning at a distance — a candidate rarely sees the file, only a rendered page, a screenshot in a report, or a claim in a board paper, and must judge from visible traces whether the thinking underneath holds. Second, the interesting questions concern mechanisms: off-system values, contrast failures and inconsistent components are nearly always produced by something — a token unavailable in the tool teams actually use, a scale missing a step designs need, a review that happens too late to change anything — and fixing the instance leaves the mechanism intact. Third, the multiplier: a token adopted by ten services carries its properties ten times, and several items ask a candidate to weigh a decision by how many times it will be paid for.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing at a distance** maps to setting direction and assuring the quality of design delivery across teams.
- **Cross-service synthesis** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and using evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Systemic diagnosis** maps to **Iterative design**: iterating delivery processes or team structures to help teams work effectively.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Designing strategically**: using risks, opportunities and constraints to shape design.
- **Diagnosing structural exclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**: measurably improving inclusive practice across multiple teams.
- **Reasoning about positions** maps to **Design communication**: gaining support from senior leaders and communicating across boundaries.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Standard: every component entry must state the problem it solves, when not to use it, its contrast values, its minimum target size, known limitations, and the date last reviewed. An entry states the problem, contrast values, target size, and date; describes limitations as "none known"; has no "when not to use". Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Problem, contrast values, target size and date are present; "none known" addresses the known-limitations requirement as written, even thinly; "when not to use" is absent. One unmet. This tests applying the standard as written rather than a stricter personal judgement — rejecting "none known" is a defensible design instinct but the wrong audit judgement, since if that phrase should not satisfy the standard, the standard itself needs to require something more specific.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Standard: "A custom colour value may enter the token set only if no existing token is suitable, unless approved by the head of graphic design." A team reports adding a custom value last month with no such approval. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) No existing token was suitable.
- C) Either no existing token was suitable, or the standard was breached.
- D) The value fails contrast.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** With approval ruled out, the only remaining compliant route is the general rule — no suitable existing token — so the disjunction between that and a breach is all the facts guarantee. This tests reading a rule with two separate conditions apart precisely: the general permission and the exception attach differently, and D imports a claim nothing supports.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading conditions from an output**

A report under review states: "Participants read the interface comfortably and distinguished all status colours easily." Sessions ran in a usability lab on a calibrated 27-inch monitor. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) The sample is too small to support the claim.
- B) A calibrated lab monitor at controlled brightness flatters exactly the two properties claimed — legibility and colour discrimination — so the finding describes performance under near-ideal display conditions rather than the conditions users have, and cannot support a general claim.
- C) "Comfortably" is not measurable.
- D) The study should have used a validated legibility scale.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The finding and the test conditions match too neatly to be coincidence: a calibrated monitor at controlled brightness makes low-contrast text look fine and similar hues look distinct, the display equivalent of a sampling frame problem specific to visual disciplines. This tests naming the mechanism rather than a generic critique — option D proposes a technique fix that leaves the flattering conditions untouched, and a validated scale administered on the same monitor would produce the same result with more authority.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

Substantial capacity for one thing this quarter: (1) a colour and type token set fixed next month for adoption by seven services; (2) a live service with a known contrast failure on a secondary label; (3) exploratory visual concepts for next year; (4) a directorate wants a brand refresh applied to existing services. Ask which is strongest.

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will be inherited by seven services, and a token set propagates its accessibility characteristics everywhere it is used — making it far harder to change after adoption than to set correctly now.
- C) Item 4, because brand consistency affects everything.
- D) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack — a token inherited by seven services propagates every property, good or bad. This tests weighting by adoption and by decisions that are closing rather than by immediacy alone (item 2 is one label with a fix that takes an hour); item 4 is the interesting distractor, since a brand refresh applied to existing services without a contrast check against every context it will meet is a substantial risk, work to shape rather than lead first.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

Three items in a month: one team reports users missing a status change; a second service's analytics show high abandonment at a page whose only state indicator is colour; an accessibility audit of a third flags colour-only status labels. Ask for the strongest synthesis.

- A) Three teams have made the same mistake.
- B) The organisation has a systemic gap: colour-only status is appearing across services, which suggests the token set or component library offers colour states without a paired non-colour indicator — so the fix is in the shared asset rather than in three teams' pages.
- C) Teams should be reminded not to use colour alone.
- D) Each team should add an icon.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests asking why three teams independently made the same choice — usually because a shared asset made it the easy one, so three teams using a component correctly all produce colour-only status, and none of them made a mistake exactly. Option D fixes three instances and leaves a fourth team to repeat it; option C is the weakest available response to a recurring problem, since teams that used the component as supplied did not need a reminder, they needed a variant.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a token proposal**

A designer proposes a new secondary text grey at 4.6:1 against white for the token set. Ask for the most useful intervention.

- A) Approve it; it passes 4.5:1.
- B) Note that 4.6:1 leaves 0.1 of headroom and will fail against any tinted panel in the system, and that tokens get used wherever they look right rather than re-measured in context — so ask what backgrounds it will meet and propose a value with enough headroom to survive them.
- C) Reject it.
- D) Require it to be tested on every background.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The value passes by almost nothing, and this tests naming the mechanism (contrast falling as a background darkens towards the text) rather than the measurement — since a token is available everywhere, nobody re-measures it in context, which is both the convenience of tokens and their risk. Option C reaches a defensible conclusion without the reasoning that would survive being challenged; option D is impractical as a standing rule since new panel colours appear faster than audits do, which is the argument for headroom rather than testing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Six teams consistently use off-scale spacing values, each with a plausible local reason. Ask for the strongest diagnosis and response.

- A) Remind teams to use the scale.
- B) Six teams doing the same thing for plausible reasons is one systemic cause with six rationalisations; establish which it is — the scale lacks a step designs genuinely need, the tokens are unavailable in the tool teams design in, or the component defaults do not sit correctly on the scale — and fix that, since the remedies differ entirely.
- C) Add spacing to the design review checklist.
- D) Accept it; some layouts need bespoke values.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Six teams behaving identically for individually plausible reasons is the signature of a systemic cause. This tests naming the realistic candidate mechanisms, which imply entirely different remedies — adding a scale step is a morning's decision, making tokens available in the design tool is a tooling job, and defaults sitting off-scale is a defect that will keep generating problems no matter how carefully teams work. Option D accepts the rationalisation without testing it.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study under review ran on a single calibrated monitor. Four findings: (i) participants could not find a secondary action; (ii) participants read body text comfortably; (iii) participants misinterpreted an icon; (iv) participants distinguished status colours easily. Ask which findings are most compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; display conditions are incidental.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iv) are most compromised, because a calibrated display flatters legibility and colour discrimination — while (i) and (iii) concern position and comprehension, which the display affects far less.
- D) Only (iv).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests scoping a methodological flaw precisely rather than treating it as invalidating everything or nothing — a calibrated monitor flatters exactly what findings (ii) and (iv) claim, while (i) and (iii) concern position and comprehension, barely touched by display quality. Discarding all four (A) teaches teams that declaring their test conditions invalidates their work, an incentive to stop declaring them — leaving future studies with invisible conditions, which is worse.

**Question 9 (harder) — Apparent contradiction between teams**

Two teams reach opposite conclusions about the same grey: one finds it legible, the other that users struggle. Both studies look sound. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Commission a third study.
- B) Establish what differs before treating it as a contradiction — most likely the background, since a grey legible on white fails on a tinted panel, or the type size and weight, since the threshold differs — and the synthesis is that the token is context-dependent and its documentation should say so.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apparent contradictions between sound studies are usually context differences in disguise, and this tests identifying the specific, checkable candidates — background, size, weight — and reaching a synthesis more valuable than either finding: the token has conditions attached, worth documenting. Option C abdicates the synthesis a lead exists to produce; option D picks a winner on an irrelevant criterion.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

An accessibility check is required before release; across the year sixteen of twenty services complied, and the four that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced designers. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by people who know the requirement indicates a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what the check demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the four alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-release gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests reading a concentrated pattern of failure as diagnostic of a process rather than of conduct — scattered breaches would suggest capability, but four concentrated under identical pressure by experienced people indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option D imposes friction on sixteen compliant services to catch four, how organisational burden accumulates without a candidate noticing they introduced it.

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for a brand decision**

Available evidence on a refreshed brand palette: a brand agency's assertion the palette is "accessible"; an internal check finding two of eight colours fail 4.5:1 against white; two directorates reporting the palette looks better; a cost model for applying it. Ask for the most rigorous contribution.

- A) Oppose the refresh on the contrast finding.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that "accessible" from an agency usually means the palette contains compliant combinations rather than that every colour is usable for body text, so the two findings are not in conflict — and name what would most change the decision, which is how the palette performs on the tinted backgrounds and at the text sizes the services actually use.
- C) Support the refresh on the aesthetic reports.
- D) Recommend a full audit before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests resolving an apparent contradiction by definition — an agency's accessibility claim usually means the palette contains compliant pairings, entirely consistent with two individual colours failing for body text — and naming the residual uncertainty rather than advocating or delaying. Option A opposes on partial evidence, read as design governance obstructing a decision.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your teams**

A year's output shows two teams consistently produce accessible, on-system work and five do not; both strong teams have a designer who checks every screen in a contrast tool before handing over. Ask for the most useful inference and action.

- A) The five weaker teams need training.
- B) A pre-handover contrast check is a cheap, transferable routine that plausibly explains the difference — watch how they do it to establish what it actually catches, then build it into the organisation's expectations and, if possible, into tooling so it happens automatically.
- C) Recruit more designers like those two.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the five weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests treating a pattern as an opportunity to identify a transferable practice rather than to grade people — watching first matters because the routine may be catching something other than contrast, and automating it removes reliance on discipline entirely. Option A posits a knowledge deficit without diagnosing one, and option D catches more defects while creating no capacity to prevent them.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate applies a stated standard as written, rather than substituting a stricter personal judgement.
- Note whether the candidate names the mechanism behind a recurring problem across several teams, rather than treating each instance separately.
- Watch for whether the candidate scopes a methodological flaw precisely (which findings are affected, which are not) rather than condemning or dismissing a study wholesale.
- Score for whether the candidate weighs a decision by how many services or teams will inherit it.
- Note whether the candidate resolves an apparent contradiction between two sound sources by identifying what actually differs between them.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a standard applied more strictly than its own wording supports.
- Missing when a candidate approves a value sitting exactly at a threshold, without noting the lack of headroom.
- Rewarding a reminder or a checklist addition as the response to a problem the shared asset itself is generating.
- Accepting a wholesale dismissal or wholesale acceptance of a flawed study, rather than a precise scoping of which findings are affected.
- Missing when a candidate adds friction for a compliant majority to catch a small number of non-compliant cases, rather than diagnosing why those cases occurred.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At lead level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation: candidates still need to compute correctly, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims, and in most rooms a lead enters, they may be the only person who will ask.

The professional stake is unusually high in this discipline, since visual decisions are the ones colleagues feel most entitled to have opinions about, and a measured value is the only thing that reliably converts a matter of preference into a matter of fact — a lead who can say "that token measures 4.4:1, and it is used on eleven pages" is exercising a form of authority that "that grey is too light" is not.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided. Distractors come from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value as the base in a percentage change, off-by-one errors in grid arithmetic, thresholds read as approximate, and rates averaged without weighting.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to assuring the quality of design delivery across teams.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing and using evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Comparing groups fairly** maps to **Designing for everyone**: measurably improving inclusive practice.
- **Threshold and headroom reasoning** maps to **Designing strategically**: shared assets carry their properties everywhere.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**: gaining support from senior leaders.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **Evidence-based design**: a wrong measure misdirects design effort across several teams at once.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 7,200 users who reached a page, 5,400 completed it. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 5,400÷7,200=0.75=75%. This tests basic percentage calculation, checkable by estimating three quarters first.

**Question 2 (easy) — Thresholds at the boundary**

Standard: 4.5:1 for text under 24px, 3:1 for text 24px or larger. Four cases: 14px at 4.4:1; 18px at 4.5:1; 24px at 2.9:1; 28px at 3.0:1. Ask how many meet the standard.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 14px needs 4.5:1 (4.4 fails); 18px needs 4.5:1 (4.5 passes, "at least" inclusive); 24px needs 3:1, being "24 or larger" (2.9 fails); 28px needs 3:1 (3.0 passes). Two pass. This tests three precise boundary readings at once, each a real distinction — reading them loosely produces both false failures, which waste effort and make standards look unreasonable, and false passes, which ship.

**Question 3 (easy) — Grid arithmetic**

A 16-column grid spans 1,440px with 24px gutters between columns, no outer margin. Ask for one column's width.

- A) 67.5 pixels
- B) 72 pixels
- C) 76.5 pixels
- D) 90 pixels

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 15 gutters (not 16): 15×24=360; remaining 1,440−360=1,080; 1,080÷16=67.5. This tests the n−1 gutter rule — option D (1,440÷16) ignores gutters entirely. At this level the consequence is not one wrong sum but a system definition whose numbers never reconcile, which every designer using it will notice and nobody can explain.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Time to locate a control across eleven participants, in seconds: 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 14, 96. Ask which summary is most defensible.

- A) Mean 15.2 seconds
- B) Median 7 seconds, range 3 to 96, with the 96-second case described separately
- C) Median 7 seconds
- D) Mean 15.2 seconds, range 3 to 96

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean (167÷11=15.2) exceeds ten of the eleven observations; the median (7) describes the group well but alone conceals a session over thirteen times the typical duration. This tests supplying centre, spread, and explicit treatment of an outlier — in a visual discipline an extreme case usually means a contrast, size or hierarchy failure the rest worked around unnoticed, and a central figure should never travel alone in a report.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four services report accessibility defect rates per hundred components: 2 on 500, 8 on 100, 4 on 200, 6 on 200. Ask for the overall rate per hundred across all 1,000 components.

- A) 3.8
- B) 4.0
- C) 4.4
- D) 5.0

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 10+8+8+12=38 defects across 1,000 components = 3.8 per hundred. This tests weighting by component count rather than averaging the four rates directly (5.0, option D), which over-weights the 100-component service against the 500-component one that should dominate — the weighted figure describes what users actually encounter across the estate.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups**

Of 1,200 users on wide screens, 1,080 completed. Of 800 on narrow screens, 400 completed. Ask for the gap in percentage points.

- A) 30
- B) 40
- C) 50
- D) 90

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Wide 1,080÷1,200=90%; narrow 400÷800=50%; gap 40 points. This tests converting to rates before comparing — a 40-point gap this large is worth checking against other services using the same component library, since a reflow failure in a shared component produces the same pattern everywhere it is used.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Reported accessibility defects across the estate fell from 480 to 360 a quarter after a change to the review process. Ask for the percentage decrease.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 33%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (480−360)÷480=25%. This tests dividing by the original value, and, professionally, questioning the figure itself — reported defects can fall because there are fewer defects or because fewer are being reported, and a review process change plausibly affects both.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Quantifying impact across an estate**

Eleven services use a component whose focus indicator fails contrast; combined annual sessions are 4.2 million, roughly 3% involving keyboard-only navigation. Ask roughly how many sessions a year are affected.

- A) 12,600
- B) 126,000
- C) 1,260,000
- D) 42,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 0.03×4,200,000=126,000. This tests converting a shared-component defect into an affected-session count — "the focus indicator is below 3:1" competes for attention with everything else in a backlog, while "126,000 sessions a year in which the user cannot see where they are" does not, because the multiplier is what makes shared assets worth a lead's attention in the first place.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

A journey shows 60,000 start, 48,000 reach a form, 36,000 reach review, 34,200 submit. Ask what proportion of those reaching review submitted.

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 34,200÷36,000=95%. This tests identifying the denominator the question names, rather than the whole-journey total (option A) — the funnel shows losses of 12,000 before the form and 12,000 before review against 1,800 at submission, information that lets a lead redirect a proposed redesign in the meeting rather than after a sprint.

**Question 10 (harder) — A value with no headroom**

A proposed token measures 4.6:1 against white; the system contains six panel tints. Ask for the most useful observation.

- A) It passes, so it is fine.
- B) It passes against white by 0.1 and will fail against every tint, since contrast falls as the background moves towards the text — and because tokens are used wherever they look right rather than re-measured, a boundary value becomes a defect discovered on a page nobody audited.
- C) It should be tested on each of the six tints.
- D) The tints should be removed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests naming the mechanism and drawing the practical conclusion — require headroom in reusable values, a standard that can be written once rather than a check that must be repeated. Option C is impractical as a standing rule, since new tints appear faster than audits do, which is the argument for headroom over testing.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion on narrow screens rose from 50% to 62% after a component fix. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) A 12% increase.
- B) A 12 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 24% relative increase.
- C) A 24 percentage point increase.
- D) A 19.4% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Gap 62−50=12 points; relative 12÷50=24%. This tests labelling both figures — at this level, the useful move is making both a standing convention rather than a case-by-case judgement, removing any suggestion that the flattering figure was selected, which matters more in a discipline already suspected of advocacy than the accuracy alone.

**Question 12 (harder) — A directionless metric**

After a type size increase across a service, time on page rose 18% and completion was unchanged; a director concludes the change slowed users down. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) Agree; larger text means more scrolling.
- B) Time on page has no direction — it rises when people struggle and when they read more of the content — so unchanged completion with longer dwell is as consistent with people reading text they previously skipped as with being slowed, and the way to distinguish them is whether errors or contact volume moved.
- C) Revert the change.
- D) The change failed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests recognising a directionless metric and naming what would resolve the ambiguity, and, at this level, saying so to the director before the conclusion becomes a decision — reverting (C) is a specific hazard for legibility improvements, whose benefits appear in comprehension and error rates rather than in speed, so a service judging them on dwell time will systematically undo them.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads a numeric threshold at its precise boundary rather than rounding toward a convenient answer.
- Note whether the candidate weights an aggregate by the underlying counts rather than averaging rates directly.
- Watch for whether the candidate converts a shared-asset defect into a number of affected sessions or people, rather than leaving an argument qualitative.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change, and treats giving both as a standing convention.
- Note whether the candidate recognises a directionless metric (such as time on page) and questions a figure's own construction (such as whether "fewer reported defects" means fewer defects or less looking) rather than accepting it at face value.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an average of service-level rates that is not weighted by the underlying counts.
- Missing when a candidate approves a value sitting exactly at a threshold without noting the lack of headroom.
- Rewarding a percentage-point change described as a percentage change, or vice versa.
- Accepting a reported-defect trend as evidence of improvement without questioning whether reporting itself changed.
- Missing when a candidate reverts a legibility improvement on the strength of an ambiguous, directionless metric.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. At this level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, organisational standards, accessibility regulations, audit reports, brand guidelines, and contracts — a misread exception clause becomes a compliance failure across several teams, and a misread audit finding becomes advice given confidently to a director.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to mark True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, and the part specific to this level is that expertise actively costs marks: the more accessibility criteria a candidate has applied, the more readily they supply the clause they expect. Cannot Say is the answer experienced practitioners avoid most, because forming a view under incomplete information is what the job consists of.

One word matters more in this discipline than most: "or". Accessibility criteria routinely offer alternative routes separated by "or", and reading them as conjunctions produces needlessly dark palettes, unnecessary work, and avoidable arguments — costing credibility needed for the requirements that are genuinely non-negotiable.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to setting direction and assuring the quality of design delivery across teams.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: gaining support from senior leaders and communicating across boundaries.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: knowing which constraints are hard tells a candidate where design can move.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Reading governance prose accurately** maps to assuring across teams, where a misreading propagates.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Text smaller than 24 pixels must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background. Text 24 pixels or larger, or 19 pixels or larger and bold, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Non-text elements that convey information, including icons and the boundaries of form fields, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Logotypes are exempt, as is text that is part of an inactive user interface component."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "All text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage sets 3:1 for large and bold text and exempts logotypes and text in inactive components. This tests the most common real misquotation in the field — applied to a large heading, it produces a needlessly dark palette and an avoidable argument with communications, which then makes the necessary arguments harder.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Bold text at 20 pixels needs 4.5:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Bold text at 20px satisfies "19 pixels or larger and bold," triggering 3:1. This tests the "or" trap — read as "and", the clause would require text to be both 24 pixels and bold, nearly useless.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "The boundary of a disabled form field must meet 3:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Field boundaries expressly need 3:1; the exemption covers *text* in an inactive component, and a field boundary is not text, so the exemption does not reach it and the requirement stands. This tests noticing exactly what an exemption is drafted for — a reader who generalises "inactive components are exempt" too broadly answers False, when the practical distinction matters: a disabled field whose boundary disappears is invisible, a different problem from a label nobody needs to read.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "A decorative icon must meet 3:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The 3:1 requirement covers non-text elements "that convey information"; a decorative icon conveys none, but the passage never explicitly exempts decorative elements, only states where the requirement applies. This tests the distinction between stating where a rule applies and stating where it does not — the useful practical move is asking whether an icon is genuinely decorative, since designers describe icons that way more often than users treat them as such.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The audit of design practice found considerable variation between teams. Four teams run a contrast check before handover; the remainder check at review. Teams checking before handover reported fewer defects reaching live, although the audit did not examine whether their designs were more accessible overall. The audit recommends that the head of graphic design consider whether a common check point would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Some teams do not run a contrast check before handover."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Four teams check before handover and the remainder check at review, establishing directly that some do not check before handover. A straightforward baseline.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Checking before handover produces more accessible designs."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer defects reaching live and explicitly states overall accessibility was not examined — an earlier check catching the same defects sooner produces the same statistic without necessarily meaning better underlying designs. This tests resisting a claim that a candidate at this level might want to be true, since it would support proposing a common check point — citing it without the caveat is how a good proposal loses when challenged for evidence.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The audit recommends introducing a common check point."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The audit recommends the head of graphic design "consider whether" a common check point would be proportionate, including the possibility of concluding it would not be. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X" — at this level a candidate advising the recommendation's addressee could otherwise commit the organisation to something nobody asked for.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "Checking at review is a weaker practice than checking before handover."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports one outcome difference while declining to assess overall accessibility, making no judgement about relative strength. This tests noticing when a candidate is responding to a document's implied stance (mentioned first, associated with a favourable outcome) rather than its actual assertions — audit reports routinely imply more than they state.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Interactive elements must have a visible focus indicator with a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent colours. Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels, unless an equivalent target of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. Where a component is provided in the design system, services must use it unless an exemption has been agreed with the head of graphic design. Components added to the system must be documented before use."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Every touch target must be at least 24 by 24 pixels."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An exception permits a smaller target if an equivalent 24×24px target exists elsewhere on the same screen. This tests catching a dropped exception, which exists for a practical reason — an inline link inside a paragraph cannot always be enlarged without breaking the text.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service using a custom component has breached the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Services must use the system component "unless an exemption has been agreed"; whether one was agreed is not stated. This tests the rule-plus-exception structure in reading form — a candidate at this level might be the person who would have granted such an exemption, worth remembering before an accusation that has to be withdrawn.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "An undocumented component in the system has breached the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Documentation is required "before use" — a component added but not yet used has not reached the deadline, and the passage does not say whether it is in use. This tests reading a deadline-trigger clause precisely: "before use" locates the obligation at the point of use, not at the point of addition.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Services must use design system components.
- B) Interactive elements must be perceivable and operable regardless of how a user perceives or points, and services should draw on documented shared components unless an exemption is agreed — with visible focus, adequately sized targets or equivalents, and documentation preceding use.
- C) Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels.
- D) Components must be documented before use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary covering the whole passage without adding anything — A, C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest, and C also drops the equivalence exception. A summary that drops an exception is how a standard acquires a reputation for being unreasonable, and at this level the candidate is the person who would have to defend it.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a passage before concluding what a provision requires, resisting the pull of their own professional expertise.
- Note whether the candidate reads "or" in a conditional standard as offering alternative routes, not requiring both.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes what a study's outcome measure actually shows from a broader claim it might be used to support.
- Score for whether the candidate notices a document's implied stance (order, favourable association) is not the same as its actual assertions.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary covering the whole passage, including its exceptions, rather than one true-but-narrow detail.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged from the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than from the passage.
- Missing when a candidate reads "or" as "and" in a conditional accessibility requirement.
- Rewarding a claim marked True because it would support a proposal the candidate favours, rather than because the passage establishes it.
- Accepting a proxy outcome (fewer reported defects) treated as equivalent to the underlying thing it is a proxy for (greater accessibility).
- Missing when a candidate drops an exception from a summary or main-point answer.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. A lead graphic designer is an expert practitioner who sets direction and assures design quality across teams, leads multiple or highly complex services, gains support for design strategy from senior leaders, coaches others, measurably improves inclusive practice, and creates the conditions in which designers can work well.

Three features distinguish this level. A lead acts on systems rather than instances — fixing a failing colour is a graphic designer's job; fixing it without asking why the token allowed it is only half of a lead's. A lead's authority is largely informal, and in a visual discipline everybody has opinions about colour and type in a way they do not about infrastructure, so options relying on assertion of craft authority score poorly and options bringing measurement score well. And a lead absorbs pressure downwards — when a director tells a designer their brand colour is non-negotiable, the designer should not be left holding that line alone.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Assuring across teams** maps to setting direction and assuring the quality of design delivery.
- **Coaching and capability** map to **Leading design**: mentoring, coaching, and creating an inclusive, productive environment.
- **Influence with senior leaders** maps to **Design communication**: gaining support for design decisions and strategy.
- **Organisational inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**: measurably improving inclusive practice across multiple teams.
- **Embedding practice** maps to **Iterative design**: iterating delivery processes and team structures.
- **Working across boundaries** maps to **Designing together**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A designer under pressure from a director**

A designer reports that a director has instructed them to use the brand's light grey for body text, which measures 3.2:1, and has said the brand is non-negotiable. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell the designer to refuse and cite your instruction.
- B) Work out with them the alternative from inside the brand — a darker variant of the same hue — so they have something to offer rather than a refusal, and take the conversation with the director yourself.
- C) Contact the director and cite the accessibility standard.
- D) Approve the light grey.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This tests absorbing pressure downward — arriving with a darker variant turns the conversation into a substitution rather than a refusal, and taking the director conversation directly is what a lead's role exists to do. Option D is least effective, publishing text some people cannot read because of who asked, establishing that the argument works.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard nobody follows**

Teams routinely use off-scale spacing despite a documented system, describing the scale as too coarse. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Reissue the scale with a message from the lead.
- B) Review recent work with the teams to establish whether the scale genuinely lacks steps designs need or whether the tokens are simply unavailable in the tool they design in, then fix whichever it is — adding steps, or getting the tokens into the tool — and revise the scale with them.
- C) Introduce compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the scale.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard teams route around has already failed. This tests treating the objection as data and diagnosing which of two realistic causes applies, since revising the scale with the teams also produces authorship — the only mechanism by which a standard becomes self-sustaining. Option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act while changing only the date.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for the team**

Graphic designers are embedded one per service across eight services, pulled into delivery work, with no time for critique and rarely seeing each other; two have left in six months. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Discuss workload with each delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as structural: establish a protected routine bringing designers together for critique, negotiate the time with delivery leadership by showing what the pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how designers are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two departures alongside no critique and no professional contact is a structural pattern. This tests addressing deployment directly — a graphic designer working alone has nobody to check their work, and visual judgement degrades faster than most when unchecked. Option D is least effective, reproducing the attrition on a lag in a small market where reputation travels.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A team the candidate does not manage has shipped a component whose focus indicator measures 1.8:1, now in three services. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Advise the three services to stop using it.
- B) Contact the designer and their lead with the measured value and a reproduction, agree who fixes it and by when, and check whether the component's focus style came from the system default or replaced it — while making clear this is live in three services.
- C) Raise it at the design community.
- D) Report it to the service owners.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A focus indicator at 1.8:1 means keyboard users cannot see where they are in three services, so speed matters. This tests going to the people who can fix it with a measured value and a diagnostic question — a failing custom focus style has often replaced a compliant system default, in which case reinstating it fixes everything quickly. Option C is least effective, deferring a live defect to a scheduled forum and broadcasting a team's error before they can fix it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A recurring problem across teams**

Six teams keep producing colour-only status indicators, each saying the component library offers coloured chips and nothing else. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Remind teams that meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone.
- B) Accept the diagnosis they have given — the library offers no non-colour variant — and fix the library by adding chips with icons or text, since six teams using the supplied component correctly are not the problem.
- C) Add colour-only status to the review checklist.
- D) Require each team to add an icon.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Six teams giving the same plausible explanation is unusually good diagnostic information. This tests fixing the shared asset rather than treating six correctly-behaving teams as the problem. Option A is least effective and the weakest available response to any recurring problem, telling six teams a principle they already know and cannot act on.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want the candidate's design capacity in the same quarter — one has a failing live service, the other a ministerially visible launch — and both have raised it with the candidate's own director. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what design work would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and the candidate's own director together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the visible launch.
- D) Ask your own director to decide.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests making a trade-off explicit and visible simultaneously to both parties, preventing a parallel-negotiation dynamic where trust collapses when directors compare notes, and making a recommendation rather than presenting bare options. Option A is least effective — splitting constrained capacity produces two efforts too thin to inform either decision, and half-finished visual work looks finished and gets built.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A brand decision already made**

A brand refresh has been approved; two of eight new colours fail 4.5:1 against white, and the palette has not been checked against the tinted panels in the system. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Oppose the refresh.
- B) Accept the decision and make it workable: establish exactly which colours fail in which contexts, propose a usage specification saying where each colour may be used — headings, non-text elements, panels — and identify the darker variants needed for body text, framing it as completing the palette rather than correcting it.
- C) Require the palette to be re-designed.
- D) Document the risk and proceed.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The decision is made and reopening it will fail, so the useful work is making it workable — a usage specification is a normal part of any brand palette, so proposing one completes the work rather than correcting somebody's judgement. Option D is least effective; documenting a risk with nothing following is how an exclusion becomes permanent while everyone feels it has been handled.

**Question 8 (harder) — Inclusion practice that is not improving**

After a year of guidance, templates and talks, components still ship with contrast failures. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Produce clearer guidance and repeat the training.
- B) Stop adding guidance and find out what is preventing it — most likely that nobody checks before handover, or that checking is manual and easy to skip, or that failures are found only in an audit months later — then put a check where the work already flows, ideally automated so it does not depend on anyone remembering.
- C) Introduce a mandatory accessibility checklist.
- D) Report the failure to your director.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A year of guidance that has not changed practice is evidence the barrier is not knowledge. This tests diagnosing when and how checking happens rather than whether people know it should, and pointing at the strongest available remedy — contrast can be checked automatically, removing reliance on discipline entirely. Option A is least effective, repeating an intervention that demonstrably did not work.

**Question 9 (harder) — Consensus that would exclude people**

A team the candidate advises has converged on a status system using colour alone; the evidence indicates it will fail users with colour vision deficiency. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go.
- B) Raise it once with the arithmetic — that across this service the affected group runs into tens of thousands a year — propose the minimal mitigation of a paired icon or text, and if the team proceeds ensure the risk is documented and propose measuring the outcome.
- C) Escalate to the service owner.
- D) Keep raising it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests threading an obligation to raise evidence against the need to keep advising the same people — leading with arithmetic makes the point unarguable, and the minimal mitigation is more likely to be accepted than a full redesign. Option A is least effective; a consensus excluding people who already have fewer options is not a value.

**Question 10 (harder) — A token you approved**

A token approved nine months ago, now used in four services, is found to measure 4.4:1 rather than the recorded 4.6:1. Ask what should happen.

- A) Note it for the next token review.
- B) Notify all four services now, state the measured value and where the token is used, correct it, and check whether other tokens approved in the same session were measured the same way — since the error suggests a method problem rather than a slip.
- C) Correct the token quietly.
- D) Leave it; 4.4 is close.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A token used everywhere is what makes a small error consequential across four services. This tests prompt, precise disclosure with a diagnostic follow-up. Option D is least defensible — "close" applied once to a threshold becomes the standard for every future judgement.

**Question 11 (harder) — Working across a boundary**

A technology directorate has adopted a component library whose focus styles fail the standard; five services already use it. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Require them to stop using the library.
- B) Establish precisely which components fail and how, propose a shortlist to override with compliant styles — which for focus indicators is usually a single global change — and a route to remediate the five services, framing it as making their choice workable.
- C) Escalate to your own director.
- D) Document the risk and proceed.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Focus styles are usually set once globally rather than per component, making this a considerably cheaper fix than it first appears — this tests establishing that before treating the conversation as unwinnable. Option D is least effective; documenting a risk with nothing following is how an exclusion becomes permanent.

**Question 12 (harder) — A standard that will be resisted**

The candidate intends to require every token entry to state its contrast against every background in the system, and anticipates resistance because the combinations are too numerous. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Introduce it and handle objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is partly right: the full matrix is large. Frame the requirement as stating contrast against the backgrounds the token is *approved for*, with a rule that using it elsewhere requires a check — and pair it with a tool or script that generates the matrix, so the work is automated rather than manual.
- C) Introduce it as guidance.
- D) Require it only for text tokens.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The objection is real — eight tokens against six backgrounds is forty-eight combinations, a job nobody will do twice manually. This tests resolving the tension by changing what is required (approved backgrounds, not all) and automating the rest. Option A is least effective, introducing a known-contestable requirement and arguing it case by case, spending enormous credibility for a worse outcome.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate absorbs pressure downward rather than leaving a junior colleague to argue alone.
- Note whether the candidate fixes a shared asset (a token, a component library) rather than reminding teams of a principle they already know and cannot act on.
- Watch for whether the candidate frames a correction to an already-made decision as completing the work rather than as opposing it.
- Score for whether an own or team error is disclosed promptly, with its scope and a diagnostic follow-up.
- Note whether the candidate makes a trade-off explicit and visible to all parties at once, rather than negotiating separately or splitting a scarce resource.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that reissues or restates a standard teams have already shown they will not follow.
- Missing when a candidate treats documenting a risk, with no action attached, as equivalent to managing it.
- Rewarding opposition to a decision already taken, rather than work that makes it workable.
- Accepting a threshold treated as having a discretionary "close enough" zone.
- Missing when a candidate splits scarce design capacity between two substantial demands rather than recommending a sequence.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and systemic diagnosis across colour tokens, component libraries, and audit findings; the arithmetic that supports organisational-scale design argument — weighted aggregation across services, thresholds read at their exact boundary, and converting a shared-component defect into a number of affected sessions; the discipline of reading precisely the governance documents, standards and audit reports that constrain and justify design work at this level, especially conjunctive conditions and the gap between a proxy outcome and the thing it stands in for; and the situational judgement that defines a strong lead graphic designer — fixing the shared asset rather than the instance, absorbing pressure downward, and bringing a measured value rather than an opinion.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answers are consistently the ones that act on systems rather than instances. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
