# Head of Graphic Design - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of graphic design. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A head of graphic design is an expert practitioner with broad industry experience who defines and assures best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others — influencing both design and organisational strategy and priorities, collaborating with counterpart colleagues across government, and focusing on ensuring the right conditions and environment for designers to work effectively. That last element distinguishes this role from every level below it: the primary output is no longer design, nor even the assurance of design, but the conditions under which good design becomes the normal result — the tokens, the tooling, the checks, the standards and the working arrangements that make the right answer the easy one.

Four features of the role shape this material. First, a head of graphic design is accountable for work produced by people they did not train, on services they have not seen, judged from a rendered output rather than the thinking behind it. Second, they own shared assets whose properties propagate everywhere they are used, making the arithmetic of a single colour value more consequential in a token set than on a page. Third, they influence senior stakeholders in a discipline where everybody has opinions, so authority rests on measurement rather than craft standing. Fourth, they build capability — the practice left behind matters more than any single component approved.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate finds durable, systemic remedies (a standard rewritten, a requirement automated) rather than solving a single instance, and for whether they scope a methodological flaw precisely. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a candidate who changes a shared asset, a standard or a set of conditions rather than acting through personal intervention, who absorbs pressure downward, and who finds an owner for an unowned organisational problem rather than filing it.

## Advisory

This assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance colleagues.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-profession level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic and strategic: work produced by other people, standards applied unevenly, evidence gathered under conditions nobody recorded, and prioritisation problems whose units are shared assets and capabilities.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority.

Three things characterise this section at this level. First, reasoning at a distance — a candidate rarely sees the file, only a rendered page, a screenshot, or a claim in a board paper, and must judge from visible traces whether the thinking underneath holds. Second, the interesting questions concern mechanisms: off-system values, contrast failures and inconsistent components are nearly always produced by something, and fixing the instance leaves the mechanism intact. Third, the multiplier — a token adopted by fifteen services carries its properties fifteen times and is far harder to change than to set.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing at a distance** maps to this role level's requirement to define and assure best practice.
- **Cross-service synthesis** maps to **Evidence-based design**: using evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Systemic diagnosis** maps to **Iterative design** and **Leading design**: measurably improving design maturity.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Designing strategically**: ensuring design is part of organisational strategy and included early enough to inform decisions.
- **Diagnosing structural exclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**: guiding organisations to adopt inclusive practices.
- **Reasoning about positions** maps to **Design communication**: gaining support from senior leaders and communicating across boundaries.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Standard: every token entry must state the problem it solves, approved backgrounds, contrast values against those backgrounds, minimum text size where applicable, known limitations, and the date last reviewed. An entry states the problem, contrast against white, minimum text size, and date; lists no approved backgrounds; gives limitations as "none known". Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Problem, text size and date are present; "none known" addresses the known-limitations requirement, even thinly, as written; approved backgrounds are absent, so that fails; contrast is stated but only against white, and the more defensible reading is that the contrast requirement is met in form while the backgrounds requirement is not. One unmet. This tests applying the standard as written and, distinctively at this level, spotting that the standard itself is defective — it makes contrast values conditional on a field that can be left blank, so a designer can satisfy the contrast requirement by omitting backgrounds, a drafting flaw the candidate is expected to notice and fix.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Standard: "A custom colour may enter the token set only if no existing token is suitable, unless approved by the head of graphic design." A directorate reports adding one last month without that approval. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) No existing token was suitable.
- C) Either no existing token was suitable, or the standard was breached.
- D) The value should be removed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** With the approval branch ruled out, the disjunction between "no suitable token existed" and "the standard was breached" is all the facts guarantee; D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true. This tests reading a rule where the exception routes through the candidate's own approval — the practical edge being that a candidate in this position can settle half the disjunction from their own records, a reason to keep those records precisely and to be wary of approvals given informally.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading conditions from an output**

A report states: "Participants read the interface comfortably and distinguished all status colours easily." Sessions ran in a usability lab on a calibrated 27-inch monitor. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) The sample is too small.
- B) A calibrated lab monitor at controlled brightness flatters exactly the two properties claimed — legibility and colour discrimination — so the finding describes near-ideal display conditions rather than the conditions users have.
- C) "Comfortably" is not measurable.
- D) A validated legibility scale should have been used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The finding and the test conditions match too neatly to be coincidence — a calibrated monitor flatters exactly the two properties claimed, the display equivalent of a sampling frame problem specific to visual disciplines. This tests naming the mechanism, not just a generic critique — option D proposes a technique fix leaving the flattering conditions untouched, producing the same result with more authority, which is worse because it is harder to challenge.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

Substantial capacity for one thing this quarter: (1) a token set fixed next month for adoption by eight services; (2) a live service with a known contrast failure on a secondary label; (3) exploratory visual concepts for next year; (4) a directorate wants a brand refresh applied across existing services. Ask which is strongest.

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will be inherited by eight services, and a token set propagates its accessibility characteristics everywhere it is used — making it far harder to change after adoption than to set correctly now.
- C) Item 4, because brand consistency affects everything.
- D) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack. This tests weighting by adoption and decisions closing rather than immediacy alone; item 4 is the interesting distractor, since a brand refresh across existing services without a contrast check against every context is a substantial risk, and the right intervention is a usage specification rather than a quarter of capacity.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

Three items in a month: one team reports users missing a status change; a second service's analytics show abandonment at a page whose only state indicator is colour; an audit of a third flags colour-only status. Ask for the strongest synthesis.

- A) Three teams have made the same mistake.
- B) The component library almost certainly offers coloured status chips with no non-colour variant, so three teams using it correctly produced colour-only status — the fix is in the shared asset, and it removes the problem for services that have not built the page yet.
- C) Teams should be reminded not to use colour alone.
- D) Each team should add an icon.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests locating the fix in the shared asset rather than correcting three instances (D) or issuing a reminder to teams who used the component correctly (C, the weakest available response to a recurring problem). Misattributing a systemic gap to individual error (A) has a compounding cost at this level: teams told they erred when they used the library correctly trust the library, and the head of profession, less.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a token proposal**

A lead proposes a new secondary text grey at 4.6:1 against white for the token set. Ask for the most useful intervention.

- A) Approve it; it passes 4.5:1.
- B) Note that 4.6:1 leaves 0.1 of headroom and will fail against any tinted panel in the system, and that tokens get used wherever they look right rather than re-measured — so ask what backgrounds it will meet and require enough headroom to survive them.
- C) Reject it.
- D) Require testing on every background.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The value passes by almost nothing. This tests naming the mechanism and, distinctively at this level, expressing the response as a requirement rather than a one-off judgement — headroom is a rule written into the standard once, whereas re-litigating individual values never ends. Option D is impractical as a standing rule since new tints appear faster than audits, which is the argument for headroom over testing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Eight teams consistently use off-scale spacing values, each with a plausible local reason. Ask for the strongest diagnosis and response.

- A) Remind teams to use the scale.
- B) Eight teams doing the same thing for plausible reasons is one systemic cause with eight rationalisations; establish which — the scale lacks a step designs need, tokens are unavailable in the design tool, or component defaults sit off-scale — and fix that, since the remedies differ entirely.
- C) Add spacing to the review checklist.
- D) Accept it; some layouts need bespoke values.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eight teams behaving identically for plausible reasons is the signature of a systemic cause. This tests naming the realistic candidate mechanisms rather than accepting a rationalisation that is occasionally true and constantly used (D) or adding a checklist item for a defect the system itself generates (C).

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study under review ran on a single calibrated monitor. Four findings: (i) participants could not find a secondary action; (ii) participants read body text comfortably; (iii) participants misinterpreted an icon; (iv) participants distinguished status colours easily. Ask which findings are most compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iv) are most compromised, because a calibrated display flatters legibility and colour discrimination — while (i) and (iii) concern position and comprehension, which the display affects far less.
- D) Only (iv).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests scoping a flaw precisely rather than treating it as invalidating everything or nothing. Discarding all four (A) does organisational damage at this scale: a head of profession who condemns wholesale teaches the organisation that declaring test conditions invalidates work, an incentive to stop declaring them — leaving future studies with invisible conditions, which is worse than the present situation.

**Question 9 (harder) — Apparent contradiction between teams**

Two teams reach opposite conclusions about the same grey: one finds it legible, the other that users struggle. Both studies look sound. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Commission a third study.
- B) Establish what differs before treating it as a contradiction — most likely the background, since a grey legible on white fails on a tint, or the size and weight, since the threshold differs — and the synthesis is that the token is context-dependent and its documentation must say so.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests reaching a synthesis more valuable than either finding — a concrete documentation change telling every team the token has conditions attached, exactly the kind of generalisable rule this role exists to extract. Option C abdicates that synthesis; "note the inconsistency" is how a usable finding gets filed as a caveat.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

An accessibility check is required before release; sixteen of twenty services complied, and the four that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced designers. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by people who know the requirement indicates a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what it demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the four alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-release gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests reading concentrated failure as diagnostic of a process rather than of conduct. Option D imposes friction on sixteen compliant services to catch four — how organisational burden accumulates without a candidate noticing they introduced it, and what they will later find themselves defending when asked why sign-off is slow.

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for a brand decision**

Available evidence on a refreshed brand palette: an agency's assertion it is "accessible"; an internal check finding two of eight colours fail 4.5:1 against white; two directorates reporting it looks better; a cost model. Ask for the most rigorous contribution.

- A) Oppose the refresh on the contrast finding.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that an agency's "accessible" usually means the palette *contains* compliant combinations rather than that every colour works for body text, so the two findings are not in conflict — and name what would most change the decision, which is how the palette performs on the tinted backgrounds and at the sizes services actually use.
- C) Support the refresh on the aesthetic reports.
- D) Recommend a full audit before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests making evidence legible rather than advocating or delaying — resolving an apparent contradiction by definition, and naming residual uncertainty. Option A opposes on partial evidence, read as design governance obstructing a decision, spending standing needed for the usage specification that will actually solve this; option D reflexively calling for more evidence is how a head of profession finds themselves excluded from decisions.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your organisation**

A year's output shows two teams consistently produce accessible, on-system work and six do not; both strong teams have a designer who checks every screen in a contrast tool before handover. Ask for the most useful inference and action.

- A) The six weaker teams need training.
- B) A pre-handover check is a cheap transferable routine that plausibly explains the difference — watch how they do it to establish what it actually catches, then build it into expectations and, where possible, into tooling so it happens automatically rather than depending on anyone remembering.
- C) Recruit more designers like those two.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the six weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests treating a pattern as an opportunity to identify and propagate a transferable practice, watching first because the routine may be catching something other than contrast, and naming the strongest form of propagation — automation, which removes reliance on discipline entirely. Option D catches more defects and creates no capacity to prevent them.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies a defect in the standard itself, not just in the entry being audited against it.
- Note whether the candidate names a durable, systemic remedy (a rewritten standard, an automated check) rather than only fixing an instance.
- Watch for whether the candidate scopes a methodological flaw precisely rather than condemning or dismissing a study wholesale.
- Score for whether the candidate weighs a decision by how many services will inherit it.
- Note whether the candidate resolves an apparent contradiction between two sound sources by identifying what actually differs between them.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a standard applied literally where the drafting itself is the actual defect.
- Missing when a candidate tells teams they erred when they in fact used a shared asset correctly.
- Rewarding a reminder or checklist addition as the response to a problem a shared asset itself generates.
- Accepting a wholesale dismissal or wholesale acceptance of a flawed study, rather than a precise scoping.
- Missing when a candidate adds friction for a compliant majority to catch a small number of non-compliant cases, rather than diagnosing why they occurred.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At this level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation — candidates still need to compute correctly, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims, and in most rooms a head of profession enters, they may be the only person who will ask.

The professional stake is unusually high in this discipline. Visual decisions are the ones colleagues feel most entitled to have opinions about, which leaves design leadership perpetually at risk of being treated as taste. A measured value is the only thing that reliably converts a matter of preference into a matter of fact, and this section underwrites the rest of the role.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Evidence-based design**: using evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Comparing groups fairly** maps to **Designing for everyone**: guiding organisations to adopt inclusive practices, where "measurably" distinguishes guidance from exhortation.
- **Threshold and headroom reasoning** maps to **Designing strategically**: shared assets carry their properties everywhere.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **Evidence-based design**: a wrong measure misdirects effort across an estate.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 9,600 users who reached a page, 7,200 completed it. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 7,200÷9,600=0.75=75%. This tests basic percentage calculation, checkable by estimating three quarters first.

**Question 2 (easy) — Thresholds at the boundary**

Standard: 4.5:1 for text under 24px, 3:1 for text 24px or larger. Four cases: 14px at 4.4:1; 18px at 4.5:1; 24px at 2.9:1; 28px at 3.0:1. Ask how many meet the standard.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 14px needs 4.5:1 (4.4 fails); 18px needs 4.5:1 (4.5 passes); 24px needs 3:1, being "24 or larger" (2.9 fails); 28px needs 3:1 (3.0 passes). Two pass. This tests three precise boundary readings — at this level, the useful move is writing those readings into guidance explicitly, since they are exactly what teams get wrong and what a one-line note can prevent.

**Question 3 (easy) — Grid arithmetic**

A 16-column grid spans 1,440px with 24px gutters between columns, no outer margin. Ask for one column's width.

- A) 67.5 pixels
- B) 72 pixels
- C) 84 pixels
- D) 90 pixels

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 15 gutters: 15×24=360; remaining 1,440−360=1,080; 1,080÷16=67.5. This tests the n−1 gutter rule — at this level the consequence of getting it wrong is a system definition whose numbers never reconcile, which quietly licenses the off-scale values the candidate will later be trying to eliminate.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Time to locate a control across eleven participants, in seconds: 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 14, 96. Ask which summary is most defensible.

- A) Mean 15.2 seconds
- B) Median 7 seconds, range 3 to 96, with the 96-second case described separately
- C) Median 7 seconds
- D) Mean 15.2 seconds, range 3 to 96

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean (167÷11=15.2) exceeds ten of the eleven observations; the median (7) describes the group well but alone conceals a session thirteen times the typical duration — often the exact defect that reaches live because nobody articulates it. This tests supplying centre, spread, and explicit treatment of an outlier: a central figure should never travel alone in a report.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Five services report accessibility defects per hundred components: 2 on 600, 8 on 100, 4 on 200, 6 on 200, 3 on 400. Ask for the overall rate per hundred across all 1,500.

- A) 3.1
- B) 3.5
- C) 4.0
- D) 4.6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 12+8+8+12+12=52 defects across 1,500 components=3.47, about 3.5. This tests weighting by component count rather than averaging the five rates directly (4.6, option D), which over-weights the 100-component service against the 600-component one that should dominate — the weighted figure is the number a board should see.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups**

Of 1,500 users on wide screens, 1,350 completed. Of 1,000 on narrow screens, 500 completed. Ask for the gap in percentage points.

- A) 30
- B) 40
- C) 50
- D) 90

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Wide 1,350÷1,500=90%; narrow 500÷1,000=50%; gap 40 points. This tests converting to rates before comparing — a 40-point gap this large is worth checking across other services using the same component library, since a reflow failure in a shared component produces the same pattern wherever it is used.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change with a caution**

Reported accessibility defects across the estate fell from 600 to 450 a quarter after a change to the review process. Ask for the change, and what should be checked.

- A) 25% decrease, and it demonstrates the process change worked
- B) 25% decrease, but reported defects can fall because there are fewer or because fewer are being reported, and a review process change plausibly affects both — so check whether the same people are looking as hard
- C) 33% decrease, and it demonstrates the change worked
- D) 33% decrease, with the same caution

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (600−450)÷600=25% (C and D divide by the new figure). This tests both correct arithmetic and the professional caution — a reported-defect count is a product of the defect rate and inspection effort, and changing a review process plausibly alters both, a distinction that matters when a 25% improvement is the figure that goes into an annual report under a candidate's own signature.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Quantifying impact across an estate**

Fourteen services use a component whose focus indicator fails contrast; combined annual sessions are 5.6 million, roughly 3% involving keyboard-only navigation. Ask how many sessions a year are affected.

- A) 16,800
- B) 168,000
- C) 1,680,000
- D) 56,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 0.03×5,600,000=168,000. This tests converting a shared-component defect into an affected-session count — an argument that works at board level, which a contrast ratio alone does not, and precisely why the multiplier is what makes shared assets worth this level's attention.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

A journey shows 80,000 start, 64,000 reach a form, 48,000 reach review, 45,600 submit. Ask what proportion of those reaching review submitted.

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 45,600÷48,000=95%. This tests identifying the named denominator rather than the whole-journey total (option A) — the funnel shows losses of 16,000 before the form and 16,000 before review against 2,400 at submission, information that redirects a plan in the meeting rather than after a quarter.

**Question 10 (harder) — A value with no headroom**

A proposed token measures 4.6:1 against white; the system contains seven panel tints. Ask for the most useful observation.

- A) It passes.
- B) It passes by 0.1 and will fail against every tint, since contrast falls as the background moves towards the text — and because tokens are used wherever they look right rather than re-measured, a boundary value becomes a defect on a page nobody audited; the durable answer is a headroom requirement in the standard rather than a test.
- C) It should be tested on each of the seven tints.
- D) The tints should be removed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests naming the mechanism and, at this level, the durable remedy over the immediate one — a headroom requirement written once into the standard removes this whole class of problem, whereas testing (option C) solves this token and leaves the next one, and is impractical anyway since new tints appear faster than audits.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion on narrow screens rose from 50% to 62% after a component fix. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) A 12% increase.
- B) A 12 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 24% relative increase.
- C) A 24 percentage point increase.
- D) A 19.4% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Gap 62−50=12 points; relative 12÷50=24%. This tests labelling both figures — at this level, making both a standing reporting convention removes any suggestion the flattering figure was chosen, worth more in a discipline already suspected of advocacy than the accuracy alone.

**Question 12 (harder) — A directionless metric**

After a type size increase across a service, time on page rose 18% and completion was unchanged; a director general concludes the change slowed users down. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) Agree.
- B) Time on page has no direction — it rises when people struggle and when they read more — so unchanged completion with longer dwell is as consistent with people reading text they previously skipped as with being slowed; the way to distinguish them is whether errors or contact volume moved, and it is worth saying so before the conclusion becomes a decision.
- C) Revert the change.
- D) The change failed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests recognising a directionless metric and, at this level, saying so before a director general's stated view hardens into a decision expensive to revisit. Reverting (C) is a specific hazard for legibility improvements, whose benefits appear in comprehension and error rates rather than speed.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads a numeric threshold at its precise boundary rather than rounding toward a convenient answer.
- Note whether the candidate weights an aggregate by the underlying counts rather than averaging rates directly.
- Watch for whether the candidate converts a shared-asset defect into a number of affected sessions or people, and pitches it at board level.
- Score for whether the candidate treats giving both percentage points and relative change as a standing convention.
- Note whether the candidate questions a figure's own construction (such as whether "fewer reported defects" means fewer defects or less looking) before it becomes a claim in a public report.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an average of service-level rates that is not weighted by the underlying counts.
- Missing when a candidate approves a value sitting exactly at a threshold without noting the lack of headroom.
- Rewarding a reported-defect trend treated as proof of improvement without questioning whether reporting itself changed.
- Accepting a percentage-point change described as a percentage change, or vice versa.
- Missing when a candidate reverts a legibility improvement on the strength of an ambiguous, directionless metric.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. At this level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, organisational standards, accessibility regulations, audit reports, cross-government guidance, brand guidelines, and contracts — a misread exception clause becomes an organisation-wide compliance failure, and a misread audit finding becomes advice given confidently to a permanent secretary.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to mark True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, and expertise actively costs marks at this level — the more accessibility criteria a candidate has applied, the more readily they supply the clause they expect. Cannot Say is the answer experienced leaders avoid most, and the trap is sharpest where a claim would support something the candidate wants.

One word matters more in this discipline than most: "or". Accessibility criteria routinely offer alternative routes separated by "or", and reading them as conjunctions produces needlessly dark palettes, unnecessary work and avoidable arguments — spending credibility needed for the requirements that are genuinely non-negotiable.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: gaining support from senior leaders and communicating across boundaries.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: knowing which constraints are hard tells a candidate where design can move.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Reading cross-government prose accurately** maps to this level's collaboration with counterparts, where shared standards are the medium.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Text smaller than 24 pixels must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background. Text 24 pixels or larger, or 19 pixels or larger and bold, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Non-text elements that convey information, including icons and the boundaries of form fields, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Logotypes are exempt, as is text that is part of an inactive user interface component."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "All text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage sets 3:1 for large and bold text and exempts logotypes and text in inactive components. This tests the most common real misquotation in the field — applied to a large heading, it produces a needlessly dark palette and an avoidable argument with communications, making necessary arguments harder to win.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Bold text at 20 pixels needs 4.5:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Bold text at 20px satisfies "19 pixels or larger and bold," triggering 3:1. This tests the "or" trap — read as "and", the clause would require both size conditions simultaneously, nearly useless.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "The boundary of a disabled form field must meet 3:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Field boundaries expressly need 3:1; the exemption covers *text* in an inactive component, and a boundary is not text, so the exemption does not reach it. This tests noticing exactly what an exemption is drafted for — a reader who generalises "inactive components are exempt" too broadly answers False, when a disabled field whose boundary disappears is invisible, a different and worse problem than a label nobody needs to read.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "A decorative icon must meet 3:1."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The 3:1 requirement covers non-text elements "that convey information"; a decorative icon conveys none, but the passage never explicitly exempts decorative elements, only states where the requirement applies. This tests the distinction between where a rule applies and where it does not — at this level, exactly the kind of gap that produces two directorates applying the same standard differently, both defensibly, which is a drafting problem for the candidate rather than a compliance problem for them.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The cross-government review of design assurance found considerable variation between departments. Five departments run an automated contrast check in their build pipeline; the remainder check manually at review. Departments with automated checks reported fewer defects reaching live, although the review did not examine whether their designs were more accessible overall. The review recommends that the profession board consider whether a common approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Some departments do not run an automated contrast check."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Five departments run automated checks and the remainder check manually, establishing directly that some do not. A straightforward baseline.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Automated checks produce more accessible designs."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer defects reaching live and explicitly states overall accessibility was not examined — automation might produce better designs, or catch the same defects earlier, which produces the same statistic without meaning better underlying designs. This tests resisting a claim a candidate would like to be true because it would support a proposal for a common approach — citing it without the caveat is how a good proposal loses when challenged.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common approach."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the board "consider whether" a common approach would be proportionate, including concluding it would not be. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X" — at this level a candidate is likely both the recipient and the person reporting delivery, so misreading commits the organisation to something nobody asked for.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "Manual checking at review is a weaker practice than automated checking."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports one outcome difference while declining to assess overall accessibility, making no judgement about relative strength. This tests noticing a document's implied stance (mentioned first, favourable association) is not the same as its assertions — audit reports routinely imply more than they state.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Interactive elements must have a visible focus indicator with a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent colours. Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels, unless an equivalent target of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. Where a component is provided in the design system, services must use it unless an exemption has been agreed with the head of graphic design. Components added to the system must be documented before use."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Every touch target must be at least 24 by 24 pixels."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An exception permits a smaller target if an equivalent 24×24px target exists elsewhere on the same screen. This tests catching a dropped exception, which exists for a practical reason — an inline link inside a paragraph cannot always be enlarged without breaking the text.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service using a custom component has breached the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Services must use the system component "unless an exemption has been agreed with the head of graphic design"; whether one was agreed is not stated. This tests the rule-plus-exception structure — at this level, since the candidate is the approver, they could settle half of this from their own records rather than inferring it, a reason to be careful about exemptions granted informally.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "An undocumented component sitting in the system has breached the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Documentation is required "before use" — a component added but not yet used has not reached the deadline. This tests reading a deadline-trigger clause precisely: "before use" locates the obligation at use, not addition, a distinction worth knowing before telling a team they are non-compliant.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Services must use design system components.
- B) Interactive elements must be perceivable and operable regardless of how a user perceives or points, and services should draw on documented shared components unless an exemption is agreed — with visible focus, adequately sized targets or equivalents, and documentation preceding use.
- C) Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels.
- D) Components must be documented before use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary covering the whole passage without adding anything — A, C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest, and C drops the equivalence exception. A summary that drops an exception is how a standard acquires a reputation for being unreasonable, and at this level the candidate is the person who would have to defend it.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a passage before concluding what a provision requires, resisting the pull of their own professional expertise.
- Note whether the candidate reads "or" in a conditional standard as offering alternative routes, not requiring both.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes a proxy outcome (fewer reported defects) from the underlying thing it stands in for (greater accessibility).
- Score for whether the candidate notices a document's implied stance is not the same as its actual assertions.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary covering the whole passage, including its exceptions, rather than one true-but-narrow detail.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged from the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than from the passage.
- Missing when a candidate reads "or" as "and" in a conditional accessibility requirement.
- Rewarding a claim marked True because it would support a proposal the candidate favours.
- Accepting a candidate's approval or exemption asserted as fact without noting it should be evidenced from records.
- Missing when a candidate drops an exception from a summary or main-point answer.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. A head of graphic design is an expert practitioner who defines and assures best practice, influences design and organisational strategy, collaborates with counterparts across government, and focuses on ensuring the right conditions and environment for designers to work effectively.

Three features distinguish this level. First, almost nothing a head of profession does is executed by them personally — an option solving a problem through personal intervention is usually second-best, since it does not survive their absence or scale past their calendar; the reliable tell of a strong option is that it changes a shared asset, a standard, a routine, or a set of conditions. Second, authority is informal and contested by default in a visual discipline, so options relying on craft assertion score poorly and options bringing measurement score well. Third, a head of profession absorbs pressure downwards — when a director general tells a designer their brand colour is non-negotiable, the designer should not be left holding that line.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Defining and assuring best practice** maps to this role level's core accountability.
- **Conditions and culture** map to **Leading design**: supporting a culture of learning and improving design maturity.
- **Influence with senior leaders** maps to **Design communication** and **Designing strategically**.
- **Cross-government collaboration** maps to collaborating with counterparts and to **Designing together**.
- **Organisational inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Embedding evidence practice** maps to **Evidence-based design**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A designer under pressure from a director general**

A lead reports that a director general has instructed a designer to use the brand's light grey for body text, at 3.2:1, saying the brand is non-negotiable. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell the lead to instruct the designer to refuse.
- B) Work out with the lead the alternative from inside the brand — a darker variant of the same hue — so there is something to offer rather than a refusal, and take the conversation with the director general personally.
- C) Contact the director general and cite the accessibility regulations.
- D) Approve the light grey.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the text becomes readable, and neither the lead nor the designer is left arguing with a director general about a brand. This tests taking the conversation personally with a workable alternative, arriving as a substitution rather than a refusal. Option D is least effective, establishing that seniority settles design questions — a precedent that costs every subsequent argument.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard teams route around**

Teams routinely use off-scale spacing despite a documented system, describing the scale as too coarse. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Reissue the scale with a message from the head of profession.
- B) Review recent work with the teams to establish whether the scale genuinely lacks steps or whether the tokens are unavailable in the tool they design in, then fix whichever it is and revise the scale with them.
- C) Introduce compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the scale.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard teams route around has already failed. This tests diagnosing which of two realistic causes applies and revising with the teams, which produces authorship — the only mechanism by which a standard becomes self-sustaining. Option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act while changing only the date.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for designers**

Graphic designers are embedded one per service across ten services, pulled into delivery work, with no time for critique and rarely seeing each other; three have left in eight months. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Discuss workload with each delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as structural: establish a protected critique routine, negotiate the time with delivery leadership by showing what the pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how designers are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Three departures alongside no critique and no professional contact is a structural pattern, and this role explicitly focuses on the conditions for designers. This tests addressing deployment directly, since a graphic designer working alone has nobody to check their work and visual judgement degrades faster than most when unchecked. Option D is least effective, reproducing the attrition on a lag in a small market where reputation travels fast.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate not managed by the candidate has shipped a component whose focus indicator measures 1.8:1, now in four services. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Advise the four services to stop using it.
- B) Contact the designer and their lead with the measured value and a reproduction, agree who fixes it and by when, and ask whether the focus style replaced a compliant system default — while making clear this is live in four services.
- C) Raise it at the design community.
- D) Report it to the service owners.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** This tests going to the people who can fix a live defect with a measured value and a diagnostic question, since a failing custom focus style has usually replaced a compliant default, in which case reinstating it fixes everything at once. Option C is least effective, deferring a live defect to a scheduled forum and broadcasting a team's error before they can fix it, costing their cooperation for a year.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A recurring problem with a clear cause**

Six teams keep producing colour-only status indicators, each saying the component library offers coloured chips and nothing else. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Remind teams that meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone.
- B) Accept the diagnosis they have given and fix the library by adding chips with icons or text, since six teams using the supplied component correctly are not the problem.
- C) Add colour-only status to the review checklist.
- D) Require each team to add an icon.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Six teams giving the same plausible explanation is unusually good diagnostic information. This tests fixing the shared asset, since teams using a supplied component as supplied have not erred. Option A is least effective, telling six teams a principle they already know and cannot act on, and teaching them that reporting a library gap earns a lecture.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want the candidate's design capacity in the same quarter — one has a failing live service, the other a ministerially visible launch — both raised with the candidate's own director general. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what design work would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and the director general together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the visible launch.
- D) Ask the director general to decide.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests making a trade-off explicit and simultaneously visible to both parties, preventing a parallel-negotiation dynamic, and making a recommendation rather than presenting bare options. Option A is least effective — splitting constrained capacity produces two efforts too thin to inform either decision, and half-finished visual work looks finished and gets built.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A brand decision already taken**

A brand refresh has been approved; two of eight new colours fail 4.5:1 against white, and the palette has not been checked against the candidate's tinted panels. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Oppose the refresh.
- B) Accept the decision and make it workable: establish which colours fail in which contexts, propose a usage specification saying where each colour may be used, and identify the darker variants needed for body text — framing it as completing the palette rather than correcting it.
- C) Require the palette to be redesigned.
- D) Document the risk and proceed.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The decision is made and reopening it will fail, so this tests making it workable — proposing a usage specification completes the work rather than correcting somebody's judgement. Option D is least effective; documenting a risk with nothing following it is how an exclusion becomes permanent while everyone feels it has been handled.

**Question 8 (harder) — Practice that is not improving**

After a year of guidance, templates and talks, components still ship with contrast failures. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Produce clearer guidance and repeat the training.
- B) Stop adding guidance and find out what is preventing it — most likely that nobody checks before handover, or that checking is manual and easy to skip — then put a check where the work already flows, automated so it does not depend on anyone remembering.
- C) Introduce a mandatory accessibility checklist.
- D) Report the failure to your director general.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A year of guidance that has not changed practice is evidence the barrier is not knowledge. This tests diagnosing when and how checking happens and pointing at the strongest available remedy — automated checking, which removes reliance on discipline entirely. Option A is least effective, repeating an intervention that demonstrably did not work.

**Question 9 (harder) — An unowned problem**

Synthesising across services, the candidate concludes the organisation's largest visual problem is inconsistency at the same moments across services — different focus styles, status conventions, error treatments — for users who move between them, and no director owns cross-service consistency. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Report it in the annual synthesis.
- B) Build the case with evidence from the affected services, quantify the affected population using analytics, identify who would need to own it, and take it to a forum with cross-service authority — accepting that securing an owner is itself months of work.
- C) Raise it with each service owner separately.
- D) Propose research on cross-service journeys.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Unowned problems are the characteristic failure of service-shaped organisations. This tests recognising that the hard part is ownership rather than insight, and building a case a forum with cross-service authority can act on. Option A is least effective; correct and filed, it changes nothing, the quiet fate of most organisational insight.

**Question 10 (harder) — Cross-government collaboration**

Three departments are independently building the same complex component, each solving it differently, none confident about its accessibility; the candidate has no authority over the other two. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Focus on your own department's version.
- B) Convene the three departments' heads of design, establish whether the requirement is genuinely common, and if so propose one properly tested shared component or a joint contribution — accepting that this is building consensus rather than exercising authority, and noting that one well-tested component is more achievable than three adequately tested ones.
- C) Ask the cross-government profession board to mandate a common component.
- D) Share your version for information.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three departments building one complex component three ways, none confident about accessibility, is precisely the waste cross-government collaboration exists to prevent. This tests calibrating action to the authority actually held — convening is available, mandating is not. Option A is least effective, accepting solvable waste and leaving three inadequately tested components in public services.

**Question 11 (harder) — A token you approved**

A token approved nine months ago, now used in five services, is found to measure 4.4:1 rather than the recorded 4.6:1. Ask what should happen.

- A) Note it for the next review.
- B) Notify all five now, state the measured value and where the token is used, correct it, and check whether other tokens approved in the same session were measured the same way — then change what the approval process requires.
- C) Correct the token quietly.
- D) Leave it; 4.4 is close.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A token is used wherever it is available, so 4.4:1 is a failure across five services. This tests prompt, precise disclosure, a diagnostic follow-up, and, distinctively at this level, changing what the approval process itself requires — the only part that prevents the next one. Option D is least defensible; "close" applied once to a threshold becomes the standard for every future judgement.

**Question 12 (harder) — A standard that will be resisted**

The candidate intends to require every token entry to state its contrast against every background it is approved for, and anticipates resistance because the combinations are too numerous. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Introduce it and handle objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is partly right: the full matrix is large. Require contrast against *approved* backgrounds only, with a rule that using a token elsewhere requires a check — and pair it with a script that generates the matrix, so the work is automated rather than manual.
- C) Introduce it as guidance.
- D) Require it only for text tokens.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The objection is real — forty-eight combinations documented manually is a job nobody does twice. This tests resolving the tension by changing what is required and automating the rest. Option A is least effective, introducing a known-contestable requirement and arguing it case by case, spending enormous credibility for a worse outcome.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate changes a shared asset, standard, or set of conditions rather than solving a problem through personal intervention alone.
- Note whether the candidate absorbs pressure downward rather than leaving a junior colleague or lead to argue alone.
- Watch for whether the candidate finds an owner for an unowned organisational problem, rather than filing it in a report where it changes nothing.
- Score for whether an own or team error is disclosed promptly, with the underlying process changed so it cannot recur the same way.
- Note whether the candidate calibrates action to the authority actually held (convening versus mandating) rather than overreaching or under-reaching.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that reissues or restates a standard teams have already shown they will not follow.
- Missing when a candidate treats documenting a risk, with no action attached, as equivalent to managing it.
- Rewarding opposition to a decision already taken, rather than work that makes it workable.
- Accepting a threshold treated as having a discretionary "close enough" zone.
- Missing when a candidate reports a strategic insight rather than actively finding it an owner.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and systemic diagnosis across colour tokens, component libraries, and organisation-wide audit findings; the arithmetic that supports board-level design argument — weighted aggregation across services, thresholds read at their exact boundary, and converting a shared-component defect into a number of affected sessions; the discipline of reading precisely the governance, standards and audit reports that constrain and justify design work at organisational scale, especially the gap between a proxy outcome and the thing it stands in for; and the situational judgement that defines a strong head of graphic design — changing shared assets and conditions rather than acting through personal intervention, absorbing pressure downward, and finding an owner for a problem that belongs to no one.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answers consistently act on systems and conditions rather than instances. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
