# Head of Accessibility - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of accessibility. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A head of accessibility is an expert practitioner who creates an accessibility vision or strategy for an organisation: defining and assuring best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others; influencing and developing organisational strategy with counterparts across government; ensuring the right conditions for accessibility specialists to work effectively; developing departmental capability by shaping career paths and recruiting talent; and owning the overall approach to accessibility risk, including how that risk is communicated and how corporate legal compliance risk is managed.

That last item distinguishes this role from every level below it — owning how an organisation understands its own exposure, not merely whether individual services conform, but whether the organisation knows what it does not know, whether its statements are accurate, and whether risk sits somewhere it can be seen and managed.

Four features shape what the assessment is built to test. First, accountability for conformance not personally verifiable, across an estate not personally seen, judged from reports produced by others. Second, this role occupies the boundary between a design discipline and a legal exposure, so errors carry weight — a head of accessibility who overstates a requirement damages an organisation's willingness to act on genuine ones. Third, this role influences senior stakeholders in a domain most find abstract, putting unusual weight on expressing barriers in terms of people and consequence. Fourth, this role builds capability and conditions — the practice left behind matters more than any audit signed personally.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate reasons at organisational distance — treating an audit's scope, a metric's construction, and the difference between known and unknown risk as decisive rather than accepting a reassuring figure at face value. Situational judgement items consistently reward a response that absorbs pressure downward from senior stakeholders rather than leaving a junior specialist to hold a line alone, treats a false disclosure as worse than a documented gap, and builds capability that survives the candidate's own absence rather than solving problems through personal intervention.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-profession level the materials are organisational: audit summaries produced by others, conformance claims from suppliers, risk registers, remediation programmes, and standards applied unevenly across an estate.

Typical format: an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority.

Three things characterise this section at this level. First, reasoning at a distance — judging from reports rather than from services makes reading an output for the shape of the work behind it a core competence, and the absence of information as informative as its content. Second, the interesting questions concern mechanisms — recurring defects are produced by something (a component library, a framework's defaults, a procurement process, a review timed too late, a recruitment market), and fixing services one at a time leaves every mechanism intact. Third, this role owns risk rather than compliance — an organisation with known, disclosed, planned gaps is in a materially better position than one with the same gaps unexamined.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing at a distance** maps to this role level's requirement to define and assure best practice.
- **Systemic diagnosis** maps to **Leadership and guidance**, changing organisational structures to fixable and sustainable designs.
- **Risk reasoning** maps to this role level's ownership of the overall approach to accessibility risk and corporate legal compliance risk.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Leadership and guidance**, making and justifying decisions characterised by high risk, impact and complexity.
- **Governance analysis** maps to **Governance and assurance (accessibility)**, analysing governance systems and adding measures to build accessibility into all project and change activity.
- **Reasoning about positions** maps to **Communicating information**, mediating, managing expectations and moderating discussions about high risk and complexity.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading an audit summary**

A summary states: "Service X: 41 findings, no blockers." Ask for the most important thing to establish.

- A) How the 41 break down by severity.
- B) What the audit covered — because "no blockers" across an estate is a claim about what was examined as much as about what was found, and an audit of five representative pages that missed the authenticated journey has told you nothing about where blockers usually live.
- C) When the audit was conducted.
- D) Who conducted it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Blockers cluster in exactly the places lightweight audits skip — authenticated journeys, error states, upload flows, payment steps, and anything behind a form — so an audit of public pages reporting no blockers is entirely consistent with a service trapping keyboard users at checkout. This tests treating "no blockers" as a prompt for a scoping question rather than reassurance, since reassuring reports are the ones least likely to be interrogated and most likely to be repeated.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a regulatory scope**

A regulation exempts "pre-recorded time-based media published before 23 September 2020" and "content on an intranet published before 23 September 2019 and not substantially revised since." A directorate states: "Our training videos are exempt; they date from 2019." Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) They are exempt.
- B) They are not exempt.
- C) They are exempt if pre-recorded and published before the 2020 date; the intranet exemption is irrelevant unless they are intranet content, in which case the earlier date and the revision condition apply instead.
- D) The regulation does not cover video.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two exemptions with different dates and conditions are in play, and the directorate has cited a date without saying which exemption is being claimed. This tests recognising that neither exemption is fully established from the given facts — a wrongly granted exemption becomes an organisational position that later has to be publicly reversed, which is why accepting or rejecting on the cited date alone does the analysis badly in either direction.

**Question 3 (moderate) — What a conformance percentage conceals**

A dashboard reports the estate as "93% conformant." Ask for the strongest observation.

- A) 93% is a reasonable position.
- B) Conformance is binary per criterion and per page, so a percentage is a count of passed checks — it can be high while a service is unusable, and it obscures whether the 7% contains cosmetic issues or total barriers; the meaningful question is which criteria are unmet and on which journeys.
- C) The remaining 7% should be prioritised.
- D) The figure should be broken down by service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A service failing one criterion — a keyboard trap — can score 99% while being unusable for a group of people, and this is a category error common in dashboards across government. This tests recognising the category error and naming the meaningful alternative question, rather than accepting the framing (C) or improving its granularity while still reporting the wrong quantity (D) — a metric that cannot distinguish a missing alt attribute from a keyboard trap will produce complacency indefinitely.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

Substantial capacity can be directed to one of four things this year: (1) embedding an automated accessibility check in the shared build pipeline used by most services; (2) auditing the twelve services not audited in three years; (3) delivering training to every delivery team; (4) rewriting the organisation's accessibility statements. Ask which is strongest.

- A) Item 2, because unaudited services are the unknown risk.
- B) Item 1, because it prevents a defined class of defect across most services continuously, whereas the others detect, inform or document — and prevention is the only one of the four that reduces the rate at which defects are created.
- C) Item 3, because capability is foundational.
- D) Item 4, because statements are the legal exposure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The four options represent prevention, detection, education and documentation, and only one changes the rate at which defects appear — an automated check catches missing accessible names, invalid ARIA and missing language attributes on every commit, in every service using it, without anyone remembering. This tests distinguishing prevention from detection, education and documentation — option A produces a list rather than a change, and the twelve services regenerate defects afterwards without a prevention mechanism.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-estate synthesis**

Three signals reach in a month: an audit flags missing form labels in one service; a second service's user research reports screen reader users abandoning at forms; a third service's contact centre reports form completion queries. Ask for the strongest synthesis.

- A) Three services have form problems.
- B) Three independent signals converging on forms across three services suggests a shared cause — most likely a form component or framework that permits or produces unlabelled fields — so the diagnostic step is to check whether the three use the same component, and if so the fix is one change rather than three.
- C) Forms are difficult; teams need training.
- D) All three should be audited.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three signals of different kinds converging on the same interaction across three services is unusually strong evidence, and a shared form component is checkable in an afternoon. This tests synthesising across different evidence types to identify a multiplier — one fix resolving all three and preventing a fourth — rather than describing without synthesising (A) or reaching for training before diagnosis (C), which will not help teams using a faulty component correctly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — An inaccurate accessibility statement**

A service's published accessibility statement claims full conformance, while an internal audit six months ago found four unresolved serious issues. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Correct the statement.
- B) Correct the statement immediately, since a published claim of full conformance that the organisation knows to be false is a materially worse position than a disclosed gap — then establish how it came to be published, because a statement contradicting an internal audit indicates that whoever writes statements does not see audit output, which will be true across the estate.
- C) Escalate to legal.
- D) Prioritise fixing the four issues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The four issues are a conformance problem; the statement is a disclosure problem and the more serious of the two, since an organisation with known gaps and an honest statement is defensible while one publishing a false claim has converted a remediation issue into a misrepresentation. This tests correcting the disclosure first and then asking the structural question specific to this level — the disconnection between the audit process and the statement process will not be confined to one service.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a capability problem**

Experienced accessibility specialists cannot be recruited; posts sit vacant for months. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Keep advertising and escalate the difficulty.
- B) Treat it as capability design rather than recruitment: build entry routes from adjacent roles, since testers, front-end developers and content designers convert well and are easier to find; invest in structured development for the people already in post; and reduce the demand by devolving routine checks to teams so that specialist time is spent where specialist judgement is needed.
- C) Lower the stated experience requirements.
- D) Use contractors.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Months of unfilled posts in a small, competitive market is not a problem more advertising solves. This tests working on multiple fronts — entry routes from adjacent roles that need standards knowledge, not a rebuilt foundation, and reducing demand as the only lever that works quickly — option C is least effective, setting people up to fail in a role where errors carry legal weight without changing the support structure, and D leaves when the contract ends and builds nothing.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw in an audit**

An audit relied on was conducted using automated tooling only. Four findings: (i) 47 missing alt attributes; (ii) no keyboard traps detected; (iii) 12 contrast failures; (iv) all form fields correctly labelled. Ask which findings are most compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; automated tooling is reliable for what it tests.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iv) are most compromised, because they are negative findings — automated tools detect keyboard traps poorly or not at all, and can confirm a label exists but not that it is meaningful or matches the visible text — while (i) and (iii) are positive detections in areas tooling handles reliably.
- D) Only (iii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Automated tooling reliably finds missing alt attributes and measures contrast, so (i) and (iii) can be trusted as far as they go, but "no keyboard traps detected" is a statement about the tool's capability, and "all form fields correctly labelled" confirms only that labels exist — a field labelled "Field 3" passes automated checking and fails users. This tests distinguishing what a tool detects from what its silence means — option B is the error that produces organisations believing they are compliant on the strength of a tool that examines perhaps a third of the criteria.

**Question 9 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

Five services released with unresolved blockers over the year, all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, and all with accessibility sign-off recorded. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the five and require remediation.
- B) The recorded sign-offs are the most informative feature: five services signed off and released with blockers means the sign-off is not detecting them, so investigate what sign-off actually involves in that directorate — most likely a form completed rather than a service tested — and fix the mechanism, while remediating the five.
- C) Remind the directorate of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a second sign-off stage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The sign-off being recorded changes this from a compliance failure to an assurance failure — the control existed, was operated, and did not work, more serious because the organisation believed it was protected. This tests investigating why an operated control produced false assurance — option D adds a second stage to a first stage that does not work, which is how governance accumulates without improving.

**Question 10 (harder) — Owning the risk position**

Stating the organisation's accessibility risk: eleven services have known unresolved serious issues; nineteen have been audited within a year; fourteen have not been audited at all. Ask for the strongest formulation.

- A) Eleven services have known issues.
- B) The known risk is eleven services with documented serious issues and dated remediation plans; the unknown risk is fourteen unaudited services, which is the larger exposure precisely because it is unquantified — and the organisation's position is materially better on the eleven than on the fourteen, even though the eleven are the ones with findings against them.
- C) Thirty of forty-four services have accessibility risk.
- D) The risk is low; most services have been audited.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A service with documented issues and a plan is a managed risk; a service nobody has looked at is unmanaged, and the absence of findings is not the absence of defects. This tests the counter-intuitive judgement that ownership of accessibility risk turns on — a board left to itself will focus on the visible eleven and treat the fourteen as fine, which option D does explicitly by inverting the logic of audit coverage.

**Question 11 (harder) — Governance analysis**

Building accessibility into project governance; existing gateway reviews cover business case, delivery confidence, and benefits. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Add an accessibility question to each gateway.
- B) Identify the decision points where accessibility is actually determined — procurement, technology selection, and the definition of done — and add requirements there, since a gateway question asked after those decisions produces a status report rather than an intervention.
- C) Introduce a separate accessibility gateway.
- D) Require an accessibility statement at each gateway.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The decisions that determine whether a service will be accessible are made long before a gateway review — which component library is bought, which framework chosen, what a team considers done. This tests placing requirements where decisions actually happen, rather than adding reporting at a review that comes after the determining decisions (A) or requiring a document rather than an outcome at a checkpoint (D).

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across specialists**

Two specialists produce audits teams act on; three produce audits teams dispute. Reading a sample, the first two consistently state user impact alongside the criterion; the others cite criteria alone. Ask for the most useful inference and action.

- A) The three need training on the standards.
- B) The difference is in how findings are written rather than in technical knowledge — stating user impact makes a finding actionable and hard to dispute, while a criterion reference invites a debate about interpretation — so the action is a reporting standard requiring location, fault, impact, and remedy, with examples from the two whose reports work.
- C) Reassign auditing to the two.
- D) Review all audits before issue.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A finding stating that a screen reader user hears seven unlabelled inputs is verifiable and actionable, while "fails 1.3.1" invites debate; disputes cluster around the second kind because there is nothing in them to check. This tests diagnosing a communication difference rather than misdiagnosing it as a knowledge deficit (A, training specialists on standards they already know will not change how they write) or creating a permanent personal bottleneck (D).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate treats a reassuring audit summary or a high conformance percentage as a prompt for a scoping question rather than as reassurance.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a positive test result from a tool's silence, particularly for defect types automated tooling cannot reliably detect.
- Watch for whether the candidate separates known, managed risk from unknown, unmanaged risk rather than aggregating or reporting only the visible half.
- Score for whether prioritisation across organisational interventions favours prevention over detection, education, or documentation alone.
- Note whether governance or process recommendations target the actual decision points rather than a reporting checkpoint that comes after them.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a reassuring report ("no blockers," a high conformance percentage) accepted without a question about its scope.
- Missing when a candidate treats an automated tool's negative finding as equivalent in reliability to a positive one.
- Rewarding known and unknown risk aggregated into a single reassuring figure.
- Accepting a diagnosis of "more training" for a defect a systemic or mechanical fix would prevent.
- Missing when a candidate adds governance reporting after a decision point rather than requirements at the point itself.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At this level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation, and the material is unusually consequential: conformance figures reported to boards, prevalence estimates used in business cases, audit coverage statistics, remediation costings, and the numbers in which an organisation's legal exposure is expressed.

The professional stake is direct: this role owns how accessibility risk is communicated, so the numbers put in front of a board become the organisation's understanding of its own position. A flattering figure produces complacency that takes years to correct; a barrier expressed as forty thousand people a year and a statutory duty competes for attention in a way a criterion reference does not.

Typical format: a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided. Four habits carry the load: establish what the question asks before looking at the data; identify the denominator and the population it represents; estimate, calculate, compare; sanity-check against the world.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interrogating reported figures** maps to ownership of how accessibility risk is communicated.
- **Prevalence and population arithmetic** maps to **Communicating information**, representing the community to large audiences and managing expectations.
- **Coverage and exposure reasoning** maps to **Governance and assurance (accessibility)**.
- **Weighted aggregation across an estate** maps to **Leadership and guidance**, where decisions of high risk and complexity rest on figures that describe reality.
- **Estimating remediation** maps to **Leadership and guidance** and resourcing decisions.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **Consultancy**, where advice built on a flattering number is hard to withdraw.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Thresholds at the boundary**

A criterion requires 4.5:1 for text under 24 pixels and 3:1 for text 24 pixels or larger. Four items: 14px at 4.4:1; 18px at 4.5:1; 24px at 2.9:1; 28px at 3.0:1. Ask how many conform.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 14px fails (4.4<4.5); 18px passes (4.5 meets the inclusive "at least"); 24px falls into the "24 or larger" category and fails (2.9<3.0); 28px passes (3.0=3.0). This tests three boundary readings simultaneously — over-application at exactly 4.5, the wrong threshold applied at 24px, and rounding 4.4 upward — each a real error that belongs in organisational guidance because they are precisely what teams and specialists get wrong.

**Question 2 (easy) — Prevalence arithmetic**

An organisation's services have 4.2 million users a year; roughly 16% of working-age adults report a disability. Ask roughly how many affected users a year, and how this should be presented.

- A) 672,000, presented as a precise figure
- B) Roughly 670,000, presented as an estimate with its source and the caveat that reported disability understates the population affected by digital barriers
- C) 42,000
- D) 1.6 million

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 16%×4,200,000=672,000, rounded honestly to roughly 670,000. This tests presenting an estimate with its source and a caveat that makes it conservative rather than contestable — self-reported disability excludes temporary impairments, situational limitations, and people who do not identify with the term — rather than presenting an estimate as a precise count, which invites a distracting challenge about the source.

**Question 3 (easy) — Audit coverage**

An organisation has 44 services: nineteen audited within a year, eleven more than a year ago, fourteen never. Ask what proportion has current audit coverage.

- A) 32%
- B) 43%
- C) 68%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 19÷44≈43%. This tests distinguishing "audited" from "audited recently" — option C is the ever-audited proportion, the figure most likely to be quoted because it sounds better, while a three-year-old audit tells you about a service since rebuilt; a head of accessibility owns the definition of currency and should state it explicitly.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reflow arithmetic**

A criterion requires content to reflow without horizontal scrolling at an effective viewport width of 320 CSS pixels. Ask at what zoom level a 1,280-pixel viewport reaches that width.

- A) 200%
- B) 300%
- C) 400%
- D) 500%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 1,280÷4=320, so 400% zoom. This tests the zoom-to-effective-width relationship — the answer to why 400% is the standard test rather than an arbitrary figure, since it is the small-screen equivalent of a common desktop width.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation across an estate**

Four directorates report defects per hundred components: 3 on 500 components, 9 on 100, 5 on 200, 4 on 200. Ask for the estate-wide rate per hundred.

- A) 4.2
- B) 4.6
- C) 5.25
- D) 6.0

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 15+9+10+8=42 defects across 1,000 components = 4.2 per hundred. This tests returning to counts before combining rates — option C, the unweighted mean, over-weights the 100-component directorate against the 500-component one, and only the weighted figure describes what users actually encounter, the figure that belongs in a board report.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups**

Of 2,000 general users, 1,800 completed a task; of 200 screen reader users, 60 completed. Ask for the gap, and how it should be expressed.

- A) 60 percentage points; screen reader users complete at 30%
- B) 60 percentage points, and the more useful expression is that screen reader users complete at one third the rate of other users
- C) 30 percentage points
- D) 90 percentage points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** General users 90%, screen reader users 30%, gap 60 points. This tests computing the correct gap and choosing the strongest form to express it — a ratio ("one third the rate") is a single comparison a senior audience can hold and repeat, while two raw percentages require the listener to do the subtraction and then judge its significance themselves.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Small-base caution**

A report states "only 5% of screen reader users completed the journey," based on 1 of 19 participants. Ask for the strongest observation.

- A) The finding is clear.
- B) The arithmetic is right and the base is too small for a percentage at that precision — one more success would make it 11% — so report "1 of 19 participants completed", which is more forceful and cannot be challenged on precision.
- C) More participants are needed.
- D) The figure should be rounded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A percentage from a base of 19 with one success implies false precision, and a stakeholder who spots it will use it to discount the whole report. This tests reporting a small base as a raw count — a specific risk at this level, since a head of accessibility's reports carry weight and are read adversarially by people who dislike their conclusions.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Scale of a shared defect**

A component with a keyboard trap is used across 12 services with 6 million combined annual sessions; around 3% involve keyboard-only navigation. Ask how many sessions a year are affected, and what should be added.

- A) 18,000
- B) 180,000, noting the estimate is conservative because a keyboard trap also affects switch, voice control and temporary keyboard users
- C) 1,800,000
- D) 60,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 3%×6,000,000=180,000. This tests the arithmetic and, more importantly, adding the caveat that makes the estimate conservative rather than contestable — the 3% figure covers only keyboard-only navigation, understating the affected population — a computation that converts "a keyboard trap in the modal component" from a ticket into a board-level decision.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied cost**

A remediation business case claims fixing accessibility defects across 12 services will cost £480,000 and take 8 months. Ask for the implied cost per service, and what should be checked.

- A) £40,000 per service, and check whether the twelve services share components — because if a substantial share of defects sit in shared code, the cost is not twelve times a per-service figure
- B) £60,000 per service
- C) £4,000 per service
- D) £480,000 is reasonable for twelve services

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £480,000÷12=£40,000. This tests interrogating a costing built by independent per-service estimation — where services share a component library, a single fix resolves the defect everywhere, and establishing which applies can move a business case by a factor of two in either direction, which is what lets a head of accessibility defend or challenge a number rather than only receive it.

**Question 10 (harder) — A coverage statistic that misleads**

A report states: "98% of pages pass automated accessibility testing." Ask for the strongest observation.

- A) 98% is a strong position.
- B) Automated testing detects perhaps a third of success criteria, so the figure says 98% of pages pass the subset a tool can check — it is entirely consistent with widespread failures in keyboard operation, focus management, meaningful alternative text and logical reading order, none of which the tool examines.
- C) The remaining 2% should be prioritised.
- D) Manual testing should supplement it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is among the most dangerous figures in accessibility reporting, because it is high, precise, automatically produced, and measures something quite different from what its readers assume. This tests catching that a service can pass automated testing entirely and be unusable — a head of accessibility owns how risk is communicated and should stop this figure being reported in this form, since it is the report most reliably producing false confidence.

**Question 11 (harder) — Known against unknown risk**

Nineteen services are audited with 41 documented issues between them; fourteen have never been audited. A board asks where the risk is. Ask for the strongest formulation.

- A) The risk is 41 documented issues.
- B) The documented issues are the managed risk, with owners and plans; the fourteen unaudited services are the unmanaged risk and probably the larger exposure, since the audited nineteen average about two issues each and there is no reason to expect the unaudited fourteen to differ — the difference is that nobody can see theirs.
- C) The risk is low; most services have been audited.
- D) The risk is 55 services' worth.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A board left to itself attends to the visible half; making the comparison explicit with an estimated unknown figure (roughly two issues per unaudited service, ≈28 undocumented issues) establishes that the unknown half is comparable in size and worse in kind, since unmanaged risk cannot be planned against. This tests naming both halves explicitly — option D aggregates known and unknown into a figure that conceals the distinction the entire answer rests on.

**Question 12 (harder) — Percentage change with attribution**

Reported defects across the estate fell from 720 to 540 over a year, after an automated check was added to the shared pipeline. Ask for the change, and what should be checked before attributing it.

- A) 25% decrease, demonstrating the check worked
- B) 25% decrease, and the check is whether the fall is concentrated in the defect types the tool detects — if it is, attribution holds; if the fall is spread evenly, something else is happening, possibly reduced reporting
- C) 33% decrease, demonstrating the check worked
- D) 33% decrease, with the same caution

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (720−540)÷720=25%, ruling out C and D's division by the new figure. This tests naming the specific test that would establish attribution — including the second possibility that reported defects fall when fewer are looked for, not just when fewer exist — since claiming credit for an unattributed fall is a position that cannot be defended when the following year's figures move.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate presents a prevalence or population estimate with its source and a conservative caveat rather than as a precise count.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes audit coverage from audit currency, and states which is being reported.
- Watch for whether the candidate returns to underlying counts before combining or averaging rates across an estate.
- Score for whether a coverage or conformance percentage from automated testing is recognised as measuring a subset, not the whole.
- Note whether the candidate makes the known-versus-unknown risk comparison explicit rather than reporting only the documented, visible half.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a high coverage or conformance percentage reported without qualification about what it actually measures.
- Missing when a candidate presents a prevalence estimate as a precise count rather than a sourced, caveated estimate.
- Rewarding an unweighted average of rates across directorates or services of unequal size.
- Accepting known and unknown risk aggregated into a single reassuring figure.
- Missing when a candidate claims attribution for a change in a metric without checking whether the intervention explains it.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. For this role it is close to a core competence: accessibility practice rests on documents written with unusual precision — success criteria, regulations, exemptions, procurement standards, conformance claims — and an organisation's legal position depends on those documents being read exactly as written.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say — regardless of what a candidate knows. This is harder for a strong candidate than for almost anyone else, since deep familiarity with these documents produces the reflex of completing the expected sentence rather than reading the one actually present. Cannot Say deserves particular attention, and the trap is sharpest where the claim would support something the candidate wants — more resource for their function, a case for a common approach, a reason to reject a supplier. Small words carry the load: "all" is not "most," "or" offers alternative routes, and "substantially," "pre-recorded," "essential" and "disproportionate burden" are the load-bearing qualifiers that have decided real disputes.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Technical understanding (accessibility)**, communicating the requirements of standards and legislation.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to ownership of accessibility risk, where a position built on an inference cannot be defended.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Communicating information**, representing the community to large audiences.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Governance and assurance (accessibility)**, where what a requirement bites on determines the risk position.
- **Reading exemptions accurately** maps to ownership of corporate legal compliance risk.
- **Reading cross-government prose** maps to this role level's collaboration with counterparts.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Public sector bodies must ensure their websites meet the accessibility standard. This does not apply to pre-recorded time-based media published before 23 September 2020, to live time-based media, to online maps provided essential information is available in an accessible alternative format, or to content on an intranet published before 23 September 2019 and not substantially revised since. A body may claim that meeting a requirement would impose a disproportionate burden, provided it carries out and documents an assessment of that burden."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "All content on a public sector website must meet the standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage lists four categories outside scope plus a fifth route via disproportionate burden, contradicting "all." This tests recognising the scale of what is genuinely excluded — a head of accessibility carrying "everything must conform" as a working assumption directs effort at content outside scope.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "A live-streamed event published in 2018 is exempt."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The live time-based media exemption carries no date condition — the 2020 date attaches only to the pre-recorded exemption. This tests attaching a date qualifier to the correct clause rather than a general "there's a 2020 date on media" rule, which reaches the right answer here for the wrong reason and fails on a live stream from a later year.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A body may claim disproportionate burden for any requirement it finds costly."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The provision requires a documented assessment, not a subjective judgement of cost. This tests recognising the condition as procedural — the most misused provision in the regulations, since teams describe work as a disproportionate burden with no assessment at all, which is not a claim, and a body relying on an undocumented claim is worse off than one that simply disclosed the gap.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "Intranet content published in 2018 and rewritten last month is exempt."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The intranet exemption requires both a pre-2019 publish date and no substantial revision since; a rewrite last month fails the second condition. This tests catching a compound condition, with the counter-intuitive practical implication that substantially revising old intranet content brings it into scope — worth communicating across an organisation so teams plan the accessibility work alongside the rewrite.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The cross-government review found considerable variation in how departments assure accessibility. Six departments run automated checks in their build pipelines; the remainder rely on periodic audit. Departments with automated checks reported fewer defects at release, although the review did not examine whether their services were more accessible in use. The review recommends that the profession consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Some departments do not run automated checks in their pipelines."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests the baseline reading, confirmed by the passage's own description of what "the remainder" does.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Automated checks produce more accessible services."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer defects at release and states explicitly that accessibility in use was not examined. This tests the specific trap of a claim a candidate would like to be true — worth arguing for automation on the grounds the evidence actually supports (earlier detection of a known defect class at low cost) rather than overstating its effect on accessibility overall.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common assurance approach."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the profession "consider whether" a common approach would be proportionate, including the possibility of concluding it would not be. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X" — misreading this commits a head of accessibility to advocating and reporting progress against something nobody actually recommended.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "Periodic audit is a weaker assurance approach than automated checking."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports one difference in outcome while explicitly declining to assess relative strength. This tests resisting a structural lean in a passage's presentation — the two approaches detect different things (a narrow class continuously versus a broad class occasionally), so "weaker" is not obviously the right axis at all.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "An accessibility statement must state the standard and level the service aims to meet, describe any non-accessible content and why, give the date of preparation and last review, and explain how to report problems. Where non-accessible content is claimed to impose a disproportionate burden, the statement must record the assessment. A statement must be reviewed at least annually and whenever the service changes substantially. A statement claiming full conformance may not be published where known non-accessible content exists."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "A statement must explain how to report problems."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests the baseline reading, and, in a strong answer, recognising why the requirement matters — a reporting route that leads nowhere converts the statement from a disclosure into a formality.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A statement reviewed eleven months ago is compliant."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The review requirement has two limbs — at least annually, and whenever the service changes substantially — and the passage does not confirm whether a substantial change has occurred in the eleven months. This tests catching a compound review obligation, which catches organisations letting a statement go stale through a major release between annual reviews.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "A service with two known minor non-accessible items may publish a statement claiming full conformance if it plans to fix them."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The prohibition on a full conformance claim applies wherever known non-accessible content exists, with no severity qualifier. This tests catching an unqualified prohibition — a false claim of full conformance is materially worse than an honest disclosure of two minor issues with a fix date, and teams reach for the full claim because it looks better.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Statements must be reviewed annually.
- B) An accessibility statement is a public disclosure that must be accurate, current and complete — naming the standard aimed at, the gaps and their reasons, any documented burden assessment, and a route to report problems — and may never overstate conformance.
- C) Full conformance may not be claimed where non-accessible content exists.
- D) Statements must record disproportionate burden assessments.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage while adding nothing — options A, C and D are each true of one provision and silent on the rest. This tests capturing that a statement is a disclosure rather than a compliance artefact, the distinction that determines whether an organisation treats it as something to get right or something to get done.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes a strong claim's own professional knowledge from what the passage actually establishes.
- Note whether the candidate attaches a qualifying date or condition to the correct clause in a passage with several related provisions.
- Watch for whether Cannot Say is used correctly even where the alternative answer would support a case the candidate might otherwise want to make (more resource, a stronger position).
- Score for whether a compound obligation (two limbs, both required) is read correctly rather than satisfied by meeting only one.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes "consider whether X" from "recommend X" in institutional or governance prose.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own deep familiarity with the subject matter rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate treats an undocumented or subjective claim (a burden argument with no assessment) as though it satisfied a documented, conditional provision.
- Rewarding a proxy outcome (fewer defects reported) read as the underlying goal itself (greater accessibility).
- Accepting a review obligation with two limbs satisfied by meeting only the calendar-based one.
- Missing when a candidate marks True on a claim that happens to support their own institutional interest.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what the candidate would do. It measures whether judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. This role creates an accessibility vision for an organisation, defines and assures best practice, influences organisational strategy, ensures the conditions for specialists to work effectively, develops departmental capability, and owns the overall approach to accessibility risk including how legal compliance risk is managed.

Four features distinguish this level. Almost nothing is executed personally — an option solving a problem through personal intervention is usually second-best, since it does not survive the candidate's absence or scale past their calendar. This role owns the risk position rather than individual conformance, so how a gap is known, disclosed and planned for matters as much as whether it exists. This role absorbs pressure downwards — when a senior stakeholder dismisses a specialist's finding, the specialist should not be left holding that line alone. And this role is accurate before it is forceful — findings carry statutory weight, and an organisation that learns the function overstates requirements will discount the genuine ones.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Owning risk and disclosure** maps to this role level's ownership of accessibility and corporate legal compliance risk.
- **Governance intervention** maps to **Governance and assurance (accessibility)**.
- **Mediation and difficult discussions** map to **Communicating information**, mediating, mending relationships and moderating discussions about high risk and complexity.
- **Building capability and conditions** maps to this role level's requirements on career paths, recruitment and conditions for specialists.
- **High-risk decisions** map to **Leadership and guidance**, making and justifying decisions of high risk, impact and complexity.
- **Cross-government work** maps to this role level's engagement with counterparts and the accessibility community.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A specialist under pressure**

A senior specialist reports that a director general dismissed their audit finding as gold-plating and instructed the team to launch. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell the specialist to hold the line.
- B) Establish with them which findings are genuinely blockers and which are proportionality questions where the director general may have a point, then take the conversation yourself — with the blockers described by their consequence and a request that the launch decision be recorded.
- C) Escalate to the permanent secretary's office.
- D) Accept the decision; it is the director general's to make.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Separating genuine blockers from proportionality questions produces a short, strong list rather than a long, contestable one, and taking the conversation personally is what absorbing pressure downwards actually means. This tests protecting a junior specialist while ensuring blockers get a proper, recorded decision — option A is least effective, leaving a specialist to refuse a director general by proxy and teaching them that support is rhetorical.

**Question 2 (easy) — A false accessibility statement**

A published statement claims full conformance for a service with four known serious issues. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct the statement.
- B) Correct it immediately, then establish how it came to be published — because a statement contradicting an internal audit means whoever writes statements is not seeing audit output, which will be true across the estate — and fix that connection.
- C) Escalate to legal.
- D) Prioritise fixing the four issues.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The false claim is worse than the four conformance issues, since an organisation with disclosed gaps is defensible while one publishing a known-untrue claim has converted remediation into misrepresentation. This tests correcting the disclosure first, then finding the structural cause specific to this level — option D is least effective, addressing the lesser problem while a false public claim stands.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for specialists**

Specialists spread across directorates spend most of their time on audits, have no time for coaching or standards work, and two of five have left in a year. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Discuss workload with each directorate.
- B) Treat it as structural: reduce audit demand by devolving routine checks to teams and automating what can be automated, protect time for coaching and standards work, and change how specialists are deployed so they are not isolated.
- C) Recruit replacements.
- D) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Specialists spending all their time auditing is the signature of a function operating as a service desk, producing exactly this attrition. This tests attacking demand structurally rather than supply alone — option C is least effective, recruiting into conditions that already produced two departures in a small market where an employer's reputation travels fast.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A governance intervention**

Building accessibility into project governance; existing gateways cover business case, delivery confidence, and benefits. Ask for the most effective approach.

- A) Add an accessibility question at each gateway.
- B) Identify where accessibility is actually determined — procurement, technology selection and the definition of done — and place requirements there, since a gateway question asked after those decisions produces a status report rather than an intervention.
- C) Introduce a separate accessibility gateway.
- D) Require an accessibility statement at each gateway.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Whether a service will be accessible is largely determined before any gateway. This tests placing requirements at the actual determining decisions — option D is least effective, requiring a document at a checkpoint (statements produced to satisfy gateways are produced to satisfy gateways) and risking normalising statements as compliance artefacts rather than disclosures.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A supplier's claim in procurement**

Procurement asks whether a supplier's "fully WCAG 2.2 AA conformant" claim is sufficient assurance for a major system. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept it; the claim is explicit.
- B) Ask what the claim covers — which components, in isolation or composed, with which assistive technologies, at what date — and advise procurement to require the scope in the contract along with a remediation obligation and the right to test.
- C) Reject it; supplier claims cannot be trusted.
- D) Commission an independent audit before contract.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Converting the claim into contractual obligations — stated scope, a remediation duty, a right to test — gives the organisation leverage for the life of the system, more valuable than any assessment made of the claim as it stands. This tests converting an unscoped claim into a lasting obligation — option A is least effective, treating a marketing statement as assurance, exactly how organisations acquire inaccessible systems with paperwork saying otherwise.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reporting to a board**

Reporting the organisation's accessibility position to a board: nineteen services are audited with 41 documented issues; fourteen have never been audited. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Report the 41 issues and the remediation plan.
- B) Report both halves and make the comparison explicit: the documented issues are managed risk with owners and dates, while the fourteen unaudited services are unmanaged risk and probably the larger exposure — because a board left to itself will attend to the visible half.
- C) Report that 43% of services have current audit coverage.
- D) Report that most services have been audited and the position is improving.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A board attends to what is visible, and a report of findings alone produces a conversation about those findings and none about the unexamined services. This tests making the counter-intuitive comparison explicit — option D is least effective, inferring safety from coverage and inverting the logic, exactly the version a board most wants to hear and therefore the most dangerous.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A specialist over-applying**

A specialist routinely reports usability problems as conformance failures, and teams have started disputing accessibility findings generally. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct the specific reports.
- B) Speak to them directly and privately with two or three examples, explain the consequence you are both experiencing — that conflating the two gives teams grounds to dispute everything including genuine failures — and agree a reporting convention that keeps usability findings visible without miscategorising them.
- C) Raise it with the whole team.
- D) Review their reports before issue.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The consequence is shared, which is what makes a direct private conversation with specific examples the right route rather than a characterisation delivered publicly. This tests addressing a colleague's damaging pattern directly and privately — option C is least effective, raising an individual's practice in a group setting, experienced as a rebuke that hardens the position.

**Question 8 (harder) — A finding that arrives at the worst moment**

Two days before a high-profile launch, a barrier on the payment step affecting screen reader users is found; the programme director says the risk is small. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Insist on a delay.
- B) Supply the arithmetic — even a conservative proportion of the user base runs to thousands a year, and a barrier at payment means those users cannot transact at all — state the statutory position, establish whether an assisted route exists as a temporary measure, and put the decision to the accountable owner with a recorded answer.
- C) Agree; the numbers are small.
- D) Escalate immediately to the director general.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Leading with arithmetic directly counters an intuition about proportion, and a temporary assisted route is the option nobody considers under pressure. This tests countering a rarity argument with concrete numbers and options — option C is least effective, an argument that would justify excluding any minority and establishing it as a precedent.

**Question 9 (harder) — Capability rather than dependency**

The function's audit queue is now eight weeks; teams cannot get reviews before release. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Recruit more specialists.
- B) Treat the queue as evidence that the function is a dependency rather than a capability: devolve the routine checks teams can reliably perform, automate the class of defect that can be automated, and reserve specialist review for complex or high-risk cases — then measure whether the queue shortens.
- C) Prioritise the queue by service criticality.
- D) Tell teams to release without review.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** An eight-week queue means teams are waiting on work much of which they could do themselves, and it will grow with the function's own reputation. This tests reframing a capacity problem as a capability problem — option D is least effective and also the outcome an unmanaged queue produces by default anyway, since a long queue stops reviews rather than releases, making the function irrelevant while appearing overloaded.

**Question 10 (harder) — Mediating between teams**

A delivery directorate and the accessibility function are in open conflict; the directorate says findings are unrealistic and late, specialists say the directorate ignores them, and both are partly right. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Defend your specialists.
- B) Convene both, establish the specific instances behind each grievance, and address the parts that are true — if findings arrive late, fix when review happens; if findings include over-applications, fix how they are written — since a conflict where both sides are partly right is only resolvable by conceding your half first.
- C) Escalate to the directorate's director.
- D) Require the directorate to accept findings.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** When both sides are partly right, nothing moves until one concedes their half, and this role has more to gain from the relationship working and more standing to make the concession first. This tests establishing specifics rather than defending positions — option D is least effective, requiring acceptance of findings that include known over-applications, which entrenches the conflict.

**Question 11 (harder) — Strategy that will not survive**

A draft accessibility strategy commits to full AA conformance across all services within two years; a realistic assessment says the estate cannot reach that. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Publish it; ambition drives progress.
- B) Revise it: replace an unachievable universal with a sequenced commitment — highest-risk and highest-volume services first, all services assessed and disclosed within a defined period, and a prevention mechanism in the pipeline — because a commitment everyone knows to be undeliverable devalues every other commitment beside it.
- C) Extend the timescale to five years.
- D) Remove the conformance commitment.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** An unachievable commitment teaches readers that a document's other commitments need not be taken seriously either, and sequencing by risk and volume puts effort where the most people are affected. This tests replacing an unbelievable universal commitment with a structured, deliverable one — option A is least effective and is the option most likely to be argued for in the room precisely because it sounds ambitious.

**Question 12 (harder) — Cross-government collaboration**

Three departments each maintain their own accessibility guidance for the same technology stack, each slightly different, with no authority over the other two. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Focus on your own department's guidance.
- B) Convene the three heads of accessibility, establish whether the guidance is genuinely addressing the same problems, and if so propose one maintained version with the evidence and testing behind it — accepting that you are building consensus rather than exercising authority, and that one well-tested guide is more achievable than three adequately maintained ones.
- C) Ask the cross-government community to mandate a common version.
- D) Publish your version for others to adopt.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three under-maintained guides on the same technical question are worse than one well-maintained one, and this option is calibrated to the authority actually available — convening rather than mandating. This tests building consensus within the actual limits of authority — option A is least effective, accepting solvable waste while three sets of developers receive inconsistent advice.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate absorbs pressure from senior stakeholders personally rather than leaving a junior specialist to hold a contested position alone.
- Note whether the candidate treats a false or misleading disclosure as a more serious problem than the underlying conformance gap it conceals.
- Watch for whether the candidate builds capability or systemic fixes that survive their own absence, rather than solving problems through personal intervention.
- Score for whether a comparison between known and unknown, or visible and invisible, risk is made explicit to a senior audience rather than left implicit.
- Note whether the candidate concedes a genuinely valid part of an opposing position (a fair proportionality point, a valid grievance) before pressing the parts that are not.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that leaves a junior specialist to defend a finding against a senior stakeholder alone.
- Missing when a candidate fixes a conformance issue without addressing a more serious accompanying disclosure failure.
- Rewarding recruitment or more of the same activity as the answer to a structural capacity or attrition problem.
- Accepting a report to a senior audience that presents only the visible, documented half of a risk picture.
- Missing when a candidate defends a position they know to be partly wrong rather than conceding it to resolve a conflict.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through organisational-distance reasoning — treating "no blockers" as a scoping question, distinguishing what an automated tool detects from what its silence means, reading a recorded sign-off that failed as an assurance failure rather than a compliance one, and separating managed from unmanaged risk with the unaudited estate named as the larger exposure; the arithmetic that turns accessibility into numbers a board acts on — thresholds read at their boundaries, prevalence presented as a caveated estimate, audit currency defined explicitly, gaps expressed as ratios, and misleading coverage or conformance percentages refused rather than repeated; the discipline of reading regulations, exemptions and statements at exactly the strength the text supports, since familiarity with these documents is a hazard rather than a protection; and the situational judgement that defines this level — absorbing pressure downward, treating false disclosure as worse than an honest gap, and building capability and governance that survive personal absence.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — this role owns how an organisation understands its own accessibility risk, and an error here is not caught by anyone else: a flattering number accepted becomes the organisation's position, a scoping question not asked becomes assurance, an exemption wrongly granted becomes a precedent. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
