# Senior User Researcher - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for senior user researcher. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A senior user researcher is an experienced practitioner who plans and leads research in larger teams and on more complex services, builds user-centred practice where it does not yet exist, aligns research with the wider plans that shape a service proposition, and supervises and develops other researchers. That is a role defined as much by judgement and influence as by technique. Three features shape the material below. First, the candidate is accountable for research quality beyond their own hands — critiquing colleagues' findings, evaluating work against professional standards, giving feedback that has to be both honest and usable — so items test whether a candidate can spot a flaw in someone else's reasoning, not only avoid one in their own. Second, the role works with sceptical people: engaging sceptical colleagues in analysis and advocating for user research with sceptical stakeholders is a polite way of saying a substantial part of the job is being disbelieved by people with more organisational power. Third, the role operates strategically, aligning research plans to service priorities rather than to curiosity.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate scopes a methodological flaw precisely — identifying which claims it compromises and which survive — rather than treating it as total or negligible, and whether they identify a value-of-information basis for prioritisation rather than ranking by importance or seniority alone. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward independent professional judgement combined with developing other people's capability, not just solving the problem personally, and calibrating escalation to genuine severity.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At senior level the materials are messier and the reasoning less mechanical: evidence supporting two incompatible readings, research designs with a flaw that compromises some conclusions but not others, prioritisation problems with no dominant option, and claims made by other people that need diagnosing rather than merely accepting or rejecting.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparably experienced practitioners.

What distinguishes this section at this level is the shift from producing sound reasoning to auditing it. Evaluating the quality of research against accepted professional standards and critiquing colleagues' findings are diagnostic acts performed on somebody else's work, often under time pressure and often where saying "this does not support that conclusion" carries a social cost. A second distinguishing feature is strategic reasoning: the strongest prioritisation answers at this level identify which research could still change which decision, and within what window, rather than ranking by importance. A third is comfort with irreducible uncertainty — several items have no clean answer, and the discriminating factor is whether a candidate identifies the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence and states its limits, sometimes holding "this is worth acting on" and "this is not yet established" simultaneously.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing evidence quality** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, evaluating research against professional standards and giving constructive feedback.
- **Reasoning under competing hypotheses** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, advising on technique choice and critiquing colleagues' findings.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Agile research practices**, understanding the strategic decisions the team needs to make.
- **Methodological problem solving** maps to **User research methods**, planning research for services with challenging user needs.
- **Diagnosing exclusion** maps to **Inclusive research**, helping teams understand user diversity.
- **Reasoning about people's positions** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, influencing stakeholders and managing relationships.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing a research plan**

The organisation's standard requires seven elements: research questions, method, recruitment approach, sample composition including access needs, analysis approach, ethical considerations, and dissemination. A plan being assured states the research questions, method, recruitment approach and analysis approach, and notes "findings will go to the sprint review and the repository." Ask how many required elements are absent.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four elements are present directly, and the dissemination requirement is satisfied by the sprint review and repository note, bringing the count to five. That leaves sample composition including access needs, and ethical considerations — two absences, giving B. This tests whether the candidate works through all seven requirements individually — option C typically comes from treating the dissemination note as insufficient — and whether they notice these are not random gaps: sample composition and ethics are the two requirements most often skipped, and the two whose absence most reliably produces research that fails excluded users or harms participants.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

A standard states: "Research programmes spanning more than one service require sign-off from the head of user research, unless every constituent study has already been individually approved through the departmental ethics process." A programme lead says: "Our three-service programme started last month without sign-off from the head of user research." Ask which must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) Every constituent study had individual ethics approval.
- C) Either every constituent study had individual ethics approval, or the standard was breached.
- D) The programme should be paused.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional rule with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, yields only the disjunction. Option D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true — a distractor worth noticing, since a sensible action is frequently offered where a logic question asks what is established. This tests whether the candidate resists assuming a branch, and recognises the professional analogue: as the likely assurer of this programme, the opening move is to establish which route was taken, framed as an enquiry rather than a finding.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Diagnosing a colleague's conclusion**

A researcher being supervised reports: "Users trust the new identity verification flow — 11 of 14 participants said they were comfortable with it." Sessions were conducted in a government building, participants recruited through the departmental panel, and the comfort question asked by the facilitator at the end of the session. Ask for the most important methodological concern.

- A) Fourteen participants is too small a sample.
- B) The setting, the recruitment source and the direct question from the facilitator all push in the same direction — towards socially desirable responses about trusting a government process — so the finding is unusually vulnerable to acquiescence bias and cannot support a claim about trust.
- C) Comfort is not the same as trust.
- D) The question should have been asked at the start.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Several concerns are legitimate, and the strongest answer identifies the compounding structure: a government building primes deference, a departmental panel selects for people well disposed to the department, and a direct question from the facilitator invites the wanted answer. This tests whether the candidate names the compounding rather than a single weakness — option C is real and a subset of the problem; option A misidentifies size as the issue when it is validity; option D would help only marginally. When supervising, useful feedback names the compounding, because fixing one factor while leaving the other two produces the same result.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation across teams**

Leading research across three teams with capacity for one substantial study this quarter: (1) Team A choosing between two service models next month, committing three years of build; (2) Team B has a live service with a persistent 30% drop-off at one step; (3) Team C is about to launch and wants pre-launch usability testing on screens already signed off; (4) a director wants research to support a business case due in six months. Ask which is strongest, and why.

- A) Option 2, because it affects the most users now.
- B) Option 1, because the decision is imminent, irreversible for three years, and research is the only thing that could change it — making the value of information dramatically higher than in the other cases.
- C) Option 4, because it has the most senior sponsor.
- D) Option 3, because launching with untested screens is a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The right frame is value of information — how much does a decision change as a result of research, and is there still time? Option 1 scores highest on both. This tests whether the candidate resists the seniority trap (C) — a live risk at this level, since directors ask directly — and recognises option 3 as the weakest despite sounding responsible: screens already signed off with a launch imminent means findings arrive with nowhere to go, the classic pattern of research performed for reassurance rather than decisions.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Competing explanations**

Analytics show application completion at 71% for users arriving from a GOV.UK guidance page and 43% for users arriving from a search engine result linking directly to the form. A product manager proposes improving the form. Ask for the most rigorous analysis.

- A) Agree; the form is clearly the problem for direct arrivals.
- B) The two groups differ in preparation, not necessarily in what they encounter: users arriving via guidance have typically read what is required and gathered documents, while direct arrivals may not know what the form needs — so the intervention with the highest expected value may be at the point of entry rather than in the form itself.
- C) The difference is too large to be explained by anything other than the form.
- D) Recommend blocking direct entry to the form.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Referral route is a marker for preparation before arrival, a common and consequential confound in service analytics. This tests whether the candidate identifies the confound and draws the practical implication — a lightweight "what you'll need" interstitial for direct arrivals rather than a form redesign — rather than acting on an unexamined correlation (A), mistaking effect size for causal certainty (C), or harming users who legitimately arrive prepared (D).

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a colleague's synthesis**

An affinity map presents six themes drawn from twelve sessions. One theme, "users want more automation," contains four quotes, three from the same participant. Ask for the most useful feedback.

- A) The theme is invalid and should be removed.
- B) Note that the theme currently rests on two participants, one of whom dominates it, and suggest either re-examining whether other sessions contain related material or re-labelling it as an observation from a small number of participants rather than a theme.
- C) Suggest running more sessions.
- D) Approve it; four quotes is enough evidence.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Quote count has been mistaken for participant breadth, one of the most common and easily missed flaws in affinity mapping. This tests whether the candidate offers two proportionate routes — re-examine or re-label — rather than discarding something potentially real (A), approving a misleading output (D), or reaching for an expensive and premature fix before re-examining existing data (C). Critiquing colleagues' findings to assure best practice means specific, fixable feedback framed so the researcher can act without feeling dismantled.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Method choice under constraint**

Research is needed on why a complex multi-week benefits application is abandoned midway, completed over several sessions, often waiting on documents from third parties, with six weeks available. Ask for the strongest design.

- A) Moderated usability sessions on the form.
- B) A diary study with participants currently mid-application, supplemented by interviews at the point of abandonment or completion, because the phenomenon unfolds over weeks and includes events outside the service that a single session cannot observe.
- C) A survey of users who abandoned.
- D) Analytics analysis of drop-off points.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Abandonment here is a process spread over weeks, heavily influenced by events outside the service. This tests whether the candidate matches the design to the shape of the phenomenon — a moderated session captures only a snapshot and misses the between-session decay where most abandonment occurs, a survey reaches only willing respondents relying on recall, and analytics locates but does not explain drop-off, though it remains a valuable recruitment input.

**Question 8 (harder) — A flaw that compromises some claims but not others**

A study being assured used a prototype with, unknown to the researcher at the time, a broken back button throughout. Four claims: (i) participants could not find the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned at the evidence step; (iii) the task took longer than expected; (iv) participants found the declaration's language confusing. Ask which claims are most compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; the back button is a minor issue.
- C) Claims (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, because inability to go back plausibly caused both abandonment and extended task times, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only claim (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests scoping a flaw precisely rather than treating it as total or trivial. A broken back button prevents recovery from a wrong turn, directly implicating abandonment and inflated task times; comprehension of content is unaffected by navigation history. Option A over-corrects, discarding two sound findings; option B under-corrects, leaving two manufactured findings in circulation; option D catches one compromise and misses the other. An audit that condemns everything is as unhelpful as one that condemns nothing.

**Question 9 (harder) — Aligning research with a proposition**

A team is developing a service proposition. The product manager wants research on which of three interface designs users prefer; the candidate believes the more consequential open question is whether users understand the service exists and what it is for. Ask how to proceed.

- A) Run the interface research as requested; it is the team's decision.
- B) Run the research on comprehension and awareness instead, since it is more important.
- C) Set out both questions explicitly, show what each would tell the team and what decision each would inform, and let the team see that a preference between three designs is a low-stakes decision while whether users can find and understand the service determines whether any design matters — then agree the sequence together.
- D) Propose splitting the study to cover both.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests whether the candidate makes the strategic reasoning visible rather than complying (A) or overriding unilaterally (B), which wins the round and costs the relationship. Option C reframes the choice in terms the product manager can evaluate — bounded downside for a wrong design choice versus a service that fails regardless of interface — usually resolving the disagreement without anyone losing an argument, and builds the team's own capacity to prioritise research. Option D dilutes both, the characteristic failure of compromise under constraint.

**Question 10 (harder) — Building practice in a sceptical team**

Joining a team that has never worked with a user researcher, where the delivery manager says research is welcome "as long as it doesn't slow us down," on a two-week sprint cadence. Ask for the strongest approach for the first month.

- A) Run a comprehensive discovery to establish user needs properly before anything else.
- B) Start with something small, fast and visibly useful that fits inside a sprint — for example testing what the team is about to build, with the whole team observing — so that the value is demonstrated in their terms before asking for more; then use what surfaces to make the case for deeper work.
- C) Explain the service standard's research requirements and the risk of non-compliance.
- D) Ask the delivery manager to allocate research time in every sprint.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A team without a reason to trust research repaying its cost needs the experience of watching a user struggle, not an explanation. This tests whether the candidate calibrates to the existing cadence and gets the team into sessions as observers, converting research from a report received into an experience had — rather than confirming the team's fear by demanding a month-long discovery (A), winning compliance at the cost of goodwill (C), or asking for resource before demonstrating return (D).

**Question 11 (harder) — Contradictory evidence across sources**

For the same service: analytics showing 84% task completion; a satisfaction survey at 79% positive; fourteen sessions in which nine participants experienced significant difficulty; a support desk reporting the service as their second-highest call driver. Ask how to synthesise this.

- A) The qualitative evidence is outweighed by the quantitative.
- B) The sources measure different populations and constructs, and taken together they suggest a service that most people complete, many find hard, and a substantial minority need help with — the support desk volume being the strongest corroboration of the session findings, since it reflects real users seeking help at scale.
- C) The sources conflict and no synthesis is possible.
- D) The service is performing well with minor usability issues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The apparent conflict resolves once each source's population and construct is named. This tests whether the candidate identifies the support desk data as the pivot — behavioural evidence at scale that corroborates the sessions using exactly the kind of data sceptics find credible — rather than deferring to scale (A), giving up on synthesis (C), or reaching the comfortable reading that ignores the most telling number in the set (D).

**Question 12 (harder) — Assuring research not commissioned by the candidate**

A team presents findings from agency research — 400 survey responses showing 88% satisfaction — used to justify closing a telephone channel. The survey was served only within the online service. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) 400 responses is a small sample for a national service.
- B) The survey could only reach people already using the online channel, so it structurally excludes the people who use the telephone channel — the exact population whose loss is at issue — and therefore cannot support a decision to close it.
- C) Satisfaction is not a good measure of channel need.
- D) The agency should have used a larger sample.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The sampling frame makes the evidence structurally incapable of addressing the question — everyone who answered was, by definition, transacting online successfully. This tests whether the candidate identifies this as more than a sample-size problem (A, D would remain useless at any size from the same frame) — a close-to-formal error, asking the people unaffected by a decision whether the decision is acceptable. Option C is fair and secondary; even a perfect measure of the wrong population would not support the conclusion.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate scopes a methodological flaw precisely — identifying which specific claims it compromises and which survive — rather than condemning or excusing a study wholesale.
- Note whether the candidate identifies a sampling frame that makes evidence structurally incapable of answering a question, distinct from a sample-size objection.
- Watch for whether the candidate prioritises by value of information (what could still change a decision, within what window) rather than by importance or sponsor seniority.
- Score for whether the candidate names a compounding bias — several factors pushing the same direction — rather than a single isolated weakness.
- Note whether the candidate builds practice or trust incrementally in a sceptical audience, calibrated to what the audience will actually accept.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that treats effect size alone as evidence of causal certainty.
- Missing when a candidate mistakes quote count for participant breadth in a qualitative synthesis.
- Rewarding a candidate who ranks by sponsor seniority rather than value of information.
- Accepting a sample-size objection where the real problem is the sampling frame.
- Missing when a candidate condemns a flawed study wholesale rather than scoping precisely which claims survive.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At senior level the emphasis shifts decisively from calculating to interrogating — whether a figure means what somebody claims it means, which is often the question the candidate is the only person in the room able to ask.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority in most organisations; a senior researcher who cannot interrogate numbers confidently loses arguments they should win, and occasionally wins arguments they should lose. Being able to ask "what is the denominator, and who could not have appeared in this data?" is a substantial part of the value the role adds.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator, and the population it represents, before calculating. Estimate, calculate, then compare. And sanity-check against the world.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, evaluating research quality against professional standards.
- **Comparing rates between user groups** maps to **Inclusive research**.
- **Judging sampling adequacy** maps to **User research methods**, advising colleagues on method choice.
- **Combining quantitative and qualitative evidence** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, influencing stakeholders and resolving issues.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where advocacy survives only if the evidence behind it does.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from a table**

Of 32 participants across a research programme, 24 completed the core task unaided. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 24 ÷ 32 = 75%, giving C. This tests basic percentage calculation and whether the candidate registers that this is 24 of 32 participants across a programme, not "75% of users" — and notes that if the 32 came from three studies with different recruitment approaches, aggregating them into a single rate may itself be questionable.

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruitment**

No-show rate 30%, 21 completed sessions needed. Ask for the number of bookings.

- A) 27
- B) 28
- C) 30
- D) 32

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 21 ÷ 0.7 = 30, giving C. The common error adds 30% to 21 (giving 27.3, pointing at A), underestimating because the percentage applies to the larger booking figure. This tests correct application of an over-recruitment calculation, worth noting at programme scale — a systematic under-recruitment of ten per cent across five studies leaves access-need and minority-group quotas failing first.

**Question 3 (easy) — Comparing group rates**

Digitally confident: 20 participants, 18 completed. Low digital confidence: 12, 6 completed. Assistive technology users: 8, 2 completed. Ask for the gap in percentage points between the highest and lowest completion rates.

- A) 40
- B) 50
- C) 65
- D) 75

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rates: 90%, 50%, 25%; gap 90 − 25 = 65 points, giving C. This tests whether the candidate calculates each rate rather than mistaking the middle group's rate for the gap (B), and recognises what a 65-point spread means for reporting: a single overall completion figure for these 40 participants would be 65%, describing no group in the study and concealing that assistive technology users are failing three times out of four.

**Question 4 (easy) — Median and spread**

Nine sessions produced task times of 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, and 54 minutes. Ask which summary is most defensible.

- A) Mean 13.1 minutes
- B) Median 8 minutes, range 5 to 54, with the 54-minute case described separately
- C) Median 8 minutes
- D) Mean 13.1 minutes, range 5 to 54

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean exceeds all but one observation; the median (8) represents the group well but alone conceals the 54-minute session — probably the most informative case in the dataset. This tests whether the candidate reports a central figure, spread, and explicit treatment of an outlier together, rather than a misleading centre (A, D) or a hidden extreme (C).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted combination**

Study 1: 10 participants, 40% completion. Study 2: 25 participants, 60%. Study 3: 15 participants, 80%. Ask for the overall completion rate across all 50.

- A) 60%
- B) 62%
- C) 63%
- D) 65%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 4 + 15 + 12 = 31 across 50, giving 62%. Option A is the unweighted mean of 40, 60 and 80, which looks plausible and silently over-weights the smallest study. This tests whether the candidate returns to counts, and — the more important half — whether they ask if aggregating across three studies is meaningful at all: if the task or population differed between studies, the combined figure is a number without a referent, and assuring research quality includes asking whether the aggregation should have been performed.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 50,000 start, 38,000 pass identity, 22,800 upload evidence, 20,520 submit. Ask what proportion of those who uploaded evidence went on to submit.

- A) 41%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 20,520 ÷ 22,800 = 90%, giving D. All four options are true statements about this funnel; only one answers the question named. This tests whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator, and notes strategically that the largest single loss is identity to upload (15,200 users) — where research effort belongs, not at the submit step, where 90% of those who get there succeed.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion rose from 35% to 49% after a change. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) A 14% increase.
- B) A 14 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 40% relative increase.
- C) A 40 percentage point increase.
- D) A 28.6% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Gap: 14 points; relative increase: 14 ÷ 35 = 40%. This tests a distinction with governance stakes: "completion rose 40%" and "completion rose 14 percentage points" describe the same change and will appear differently in a business case depending on which is chosen — being the person who notices which was chosen, and asks for both, is part of assuring the organisation's evidence.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Small bases**

A survey of 1,200 users includes 15 who identify as using a screen reader; 9 report being unable to complete a task. A report states: "60% of screen reader users cannot complete the task." Ask for the strongest objection.

- A) The arithmetic is wrong.
- B) A base of 15 cannot support a percentage stated to this precision, and the 15 are self-selected survey respondents already using the online service — so the figure both overstates its own reliability and generalises from a group that excludes those who never got far enough to answer.
- C) Screen reader users should not be reported separately.
- D) The survey needed more respondents.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is fine — ruling out A — making this a question of precision and frame. This tests whether the candidate names both faults, rather than rejecting disaggregation entirely (C, which would obscure the very failures it should surface) or offering only a forward-looking recommendation (D) rather than an objection to the current claim.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Working back from a claim**

A stakeholder states a 3 percentage point improvement in completion would be worth £2.1 million annually, with current completion at 62% on 900,000 annual starts. Ask for the implied value per additional completed application.

- A) £26
- B) £78
- C) £2.33
- D) £39

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 3% of 900,000 = 27,000 additional completions; £2,100,000 ÷ 27,000 ≈ £78, giving B. Option C divides by 900,000, valuing every start rather than every additional completion; the 62% figure is a distractor unrelated to the calculation. This tests whether the candidate reconstructs the implied unit value quickly enough to ask whether it is plausible — often the most useful contribution available in the meeting.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A dashboard reports "average time to complete: 9 minutes" for a form that sessions showed taking most participants over twenty. Ask for the most likely explanation and the right response.

- A) The dashboard is wrong.
- B) The mean is probably pulled down by sessions that were abandoned early or by returning users completing a partly filled form, and it may exclude multi-session completions entirely — so the right response is to ask for the distribution and for the definition of a completed session, rather than to dispute the number.
- C) The participants in the sessions were atypical.
- D) The two figures cannot be compared.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A mean completion time typically mixes several populations, and many implementations drop multi-day sessions entirely, removing the slowest journeys from the calculation. This tests whether the candidate asks for the distribution and the definition rather than disputing the number head-on (A), blaming participants (C), or foreclosing reconciliation (D) — the figure is probably correctly calculated and wrongly interpreted.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy for a comparison**

A proposal to measure whether a redesign improved completion using 30 moderated sessions before and 30 after; baseline 55%, a 7 percentage point improvement considered meaningful. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 60 sessions is a substantial study.
- B) Explain that a 7 point difference on 30 per group is about two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across thousands of users, and spending the 60 sessions on understanding mechanisms, which is what moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 50 per group.
- D) Run it and report whatever difference appears.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Thirty per group cannot distinguish a seven-point difference from noise. This tests whether the candidate redirects rather than merely objecting, splitting the work by what each method does well, rather than endorsing a design that cannot answer its own question (A), moving towards but not reaching adequacy while still misallocating scarce capacity (C), or — most damaging — inviting the organisation to act on noise (D).

**Question 12 (harder) — Interrogating a claim in a business case**

A business case states: "User research shows 92% of users can complete the service unaided," traced to a study of 25 participants, 23 of whom completed, recruited from the departmental panel. Ask for the most important thing to say.

- A) The figure should be 92% only if rounded correctly.
- B) A study of 25 panel participants cannot support a population rate at all; the honest statement is that 23 of 25 panel-recruited participants completed unaided, and the business case needs either analytics or a properly sampled survey if it requires a rate.
- C) The sample should have been larger.
- D) The panel is not representative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A small qualitative sample from a self-selected panel has been converted into a population statistic embedded in a document that will commit money — a category error, not a precision error. This tests whether the candidate names what the evidence actually supports and specifies a route forward, rather than treating it as a rounding question (A) or implying a bigger sample from the same frame would fix it (C). Option D is true and incomplete: even a perfectly representative sample of 25 could not support a rate stated to the nearest percentage point.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies a sampling frame problem as distinct from a sample-size problem.
- Note whether the candidate returns to counts before combining rates from groups of unequal size.
- Watch for whether the candidate reconstructs an implied unit value to test a business-case figure's plausibility.
- Score for whether the candidate asks for a distribution or a definition before disputing a metric outright.
- Note whether the candidate recognises when a proposed comparison is underpowered to detect the effect size in question.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an unweighted average of rates from groups of unequal size.
- Missing when a candidate objects to sample size where the sampling frame is the actual problem.
- Rewarding a candidate who converts a small qualitative sample into a population rate without challenge.
- Accepting a mean reported without any check on distribution or definition.
- Missing when a candidate endorses a comparison that cannot detect the effect size it is meant to measure.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. In a senior role that runs on documents — service standards, ethics frameworks, evaluation reports, ministerial submissions, business cases, contracts — a misread exception clause produces a compliance failure, and a misread finding produces bad advice given confidently.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, and here is the uncomfortable part specific to this level: expertise actively hurts. The more a candidate knows about research ethics, data protection or the Service Manual, the more readily they supply a connection the text does not make, and the more confident they feel doing it. Senior candidates lose marks on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and the mechanism is almost always this one. Cannot Say deserves particular attention: the professional habit of forming a view is deeply ingrained, and senior candidates avoid it more than most.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, evaluating research against professional standards.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, critiquing colleagues' findings.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, tailoring communication and developing communications strategies.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where advocacy that overstates its evidence loses credibility with sceptics.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Inclusive research**.
- **Reading governance prose accurately** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, setting and applying standards including ethics.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Research programmes must be registered with the profession's central register before fieldwork begins, unless the programme consists entirely of studies already registered individually. Registration requires a named senior user researcher accountable for the programme's ethical conduct. Where a programme involves participants who may be considered vulnerable, the accountable researcher must additionally confirm that a safeguarding protocol is in place; the protocol itself is not submitted. Programmes may be amended after registration provided amendments are recorded within five working days."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Every research programme must be registered before fieldwork begins."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception for programmes consisting entirely of individually registered studies; the statement's "every" contradicts it. False. This tests the most reliable pattern in the section — an absolute term in the statement, an exception in the passage — and whether the candidate registers "entirely" as narrowing the exception considerably, relevant to Question 3.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "The safeguarding protocol must be submitted to the central register."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states directly the protocol itself is not submitted; only confirmation is required. False. This tests whether the candidate reads to the end of a sentence that appeared finished — the semicolon clause carries the operative fact, and governance prose routinely places the qualification there.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A programme of four studies, three of which are individually registered, must be registered as a programme."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where a programme consists *entirely* of individually registered studies; three of four does not meet that condition. True. This tests whether the candidate holds "entirely" in mind from the first sentence through to a scenario presented several lines later, and recognises that partial compliance with an exception condition is non-compliance — teams frequently assume the reverse.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "An amendment recorded eight working days after it was made complies with the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Amendments are permitted provided they are recorded within five working days; eight breaches that condition. False. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes a failed specific condition (False) from declared silence on an unaddressed matter (Cannot Say) — the distinction underlying most of this section.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The cross-government review of research practice found considerable variation in how departments assure research quality. Four departments operate formal peer review; the remainder rely on line management oversight. The review notes that departments with formal peer review reported higher confidence in their findings, though it did not assess whether findings were in fact more accurate. The review recommends that the profession consider developing a common assurance standard."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some departments do not operate formal peer review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states four departments operate formal peer review and the remainder rely on line management oversight, establishing directly that some do not. True. A quick item worth answering fast to bank time.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Formal peer review produces more accurate findings."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports higher *confidence*, and states explicitly that it did not assess accuracy. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate resists substituting a professional intuition — that peer review probably does improve accuracy — for what the doubly-signposted text actually supports.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The review recommends developing a common assurance standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the profession *consider developing* a common assurance standard — a recommendation to evaluate, not to adopt. False rather than Cannot Say, since the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something else. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes "recommends considering X" from "recommends X."

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Line management oversight is an inadequate form of assurance."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two approaches and a difference in confidence, making no judgement about adequacy and explicitly declining to assess accuracy. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate notices the passage's overall tone leans mildly towards peer review, and distinguishes responding to an implied stance from responding to content.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Departments should design research so that findings reach decision-makers before decisions are taken. Where this is not possible, the reason must be recorded. Research findings must not be amended to reflect the preferences of stakeholders; presentation may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. Researchers who are asked to alter findings should raise the matter with the head of user research, who is accountable for the integrity of research outputs across the department."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Findings may be presented differently for different audiences."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage permits adapting presentation provided the substance is unchanged; the statement claims only that different presentation is permitted. True. This tests whether the candidate recognises a statement weaker than the passage's full provision is still True — some candidates mark such statements False for incompleteness, confusing "does not state everything" with "states something wrong."

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A researcher who is asked to alter findings must refuse."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage requires escalation to the head of user research, not personal refusal by the researcher. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes a rule about outcomes ("must not be amended") from a rule about conduct ("should raise the matter") — a requirement to escalate is not a requirement to refuse.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "Where findings cannot reach decision-makers before a decision, the department has breached the requirement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage provides for exactly this situation: the reason must be recorded, and the timing failure alone is not a breach. False. This tests whether the candidate reads a well-drafted standard as conditional rather than absolute — reading it as absolute produces both unnecessary alarm and a culture where teams conceal timing failures rather than recording them.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Research findings must never be changed.
- B) Research should inform decisions before they are made, and its substance must remain independent of stakeholder preference — with adaptation permitted in form, honesty required where timing fails, and accountability for integrity resting with the head of user research.
- C) The head of user research is accountable for research integrity.
- D) Departments should record reasons when research is late.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A overstates a conditional prohibition into an absolute one. Options C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest. This tests whether the candidate selects the option broad enough to cover the whole passage and disciplined enough to add nothing — B captures all four elements and the logic connecting them.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate sets professional expertise aside deliberately, answering from the passage rather than from what they know.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a failed condition (False) from declared silence on an unaddressed matter (Cannot Say).
- Watch for whether the candidate reads qualifications placed after a semicolon or at the end of a sentence, rather than stopping early.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes "recommends considering X" from "recommends X."
- Note whether the candidate marks a weaker-but-supported statement True rather than False for incompleteness.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer drawn from the candidate's own professional expertise rather than the passage — the commonest senior-level error by a wide margin.
- Missing when a candidate avoids Cannot Say to appear more decisive.
- Rewarding a candidate who reads confidence as accuracy, or association as causation, where the passage distinguishes them.
- Accepting a substitution ("do X instead") read as an exemption from the underlying obligation.
- Missing when a candidate responds to a passage's implied tone rather than its stated content.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. The role places a senior user researcher as an experienced practitioner leading research in larger teams and on more complex services, building user-centred practice in new teams, supervising and developing other researchers, engaging sceptical colleagues, evaluating work against professional standards, and influencing stakeholders to resolve issues. What distinguishes this level from an independent practitioner's more sharply than anything else: a candidate who fixes a colleague's flawed analysis without the colleague learning anything has solved only half the job.

Four principles carry most scenarios: participants and users come first; the integrity of the evidence is protected, and so is other people's ability to protect it — options that leave a junior colleague alone with pressure to compromise a finding are nearly as bad as compromising it directly; capability is developed while the problem is solved, not simply produced as output; and escalation is calibrated to genuine severity — safeguarding, ethics approvals, data incidents and serious misconduct go up, while methodological disagreements, stakeholder pressure and peer feedback are handled directly.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Ethical judgement and participant care** map to **Inclusive research** and the ethical core of **User research methods**.
- **Assurance and constructive feedback** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.
- **Engaging sceptics** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** and **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Building practice in new teams** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Strategic alignment** maps to **Agile research practices**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A junior researcher is under pressure**

A supervised researcher reports a product manager asked them to remove a finding about accessibility failures from a summary deck, saying it will "distract from the main story." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell them to leave the finding in and to say the candidate said so.
- B) Talk through why the finding matters and what is at stake, agree with them how it should be presented — including framing it constructively with a proposed next step — and offer to be in the meeting; then, separately, speak to the product manager personally.
- C) Contact the product manager and instruct them not to ask researchers to alter findings.
- D) Escalate to the head of user research.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the finding must survive, and the researcher must come out more capable of handling the next one. Option B builds their judgement, gives them something constructive to bring, offers backing without taking over, and addresses the product manager at the right level. Option A is least effective, leaving the researcher holding a position they cannot defend and teaching nothing while framing integrity as hierarchy rather than principle. Option C bypasses the researcher's development. Option D escalates something the candidate is equipped to handle.

**Question 2 (easy) — A new team resists research**

Joining a programme with three teams, none of which has worked with a researcher; the director is supportive, delivery managers polite and unenthusiastic. Ask for the strongest first move.

- A) Present the service standard's research requirements to all three teams.
- B) Ask the director to mandate research participation.
- C) Find the team with the nearest consequential decision, offer to answer their specific question inside their sprint, and get the whole team observing sessions — then let that team's experience do the persuading with the other two.
- D) Run a discovery across all three services simultaneously.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Scepticism yields to experience, not argument or mandate. Option C starts where appetite is highest and lets a delivery manager's account of a saved fortnight persuade the other two more than anything the candidate could say. Option B is least effective, making mandated research performed rather than used and the candidate the director's enforcement mechanism. Option A wins compliance and loses goodwill. Option D confirms every fear about research slowing things down before any credit is earned.

**Question 3 (easy) — Findings arrive after the decision, repeatedly**

Across a programme, research consistently arrives after decisions are taken; teams are appreciative but nothing changes. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Work faster.
- B) Gather specific examples across the programme showing what each late finding would have changed, take them to the programme's governance forum with a proposed change to how decisions are sequenced, and secure the director's support for the change.
- C) Raise it in each team's retrospective.
- D) Reduce the depth of research so it can be delivered sooner.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A programme-level problem needs a programme-level forum, evidence and sponsorship. Option B adds the element distinguishing senior from independent practice: securing sponsorship with authority to change how decisions are sequenced. Option A is least effective, treating a sequencing problem as a speed problem. Option C produces three local accommodations rather than structural change. Option D trades value for timeliness.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring work that is not good enough**

A researcher in another team, not reporting to the candidate, has circulated to stakeholders a report whose central claim is not supported by the evidence. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct the record with the stakeholders directly.
- B) Speak to the researcher first, privately and specifically, and agree how the claim should be corrected and communicated — supporting them to make the correction themselves; if they decline, then escalate.
- C) Say nothing; it is not the candidate's team.
- D) Raise it in the research community of practice as a general point about evidence standards.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Option B corrects the claim while the researcher remains a functioning professional afterwards, and the conditional escalation gives it a next step if refused. Option A destroys a needed working relationship and bypasses the possibility of missing context. Option C is least effective — stakeholders are currently acting on an unsupported claim, and departmental boundaries are not a reason to let that stand. Option D reads as passive-aggressive, understood by everyone as being about the specific failure.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Being asked to predetermine a conclusion**

A director says, before a programme of research begins, that it "needs to support the case for the new digital-only channel, because that decision is effectively made." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Design the research to explore the benefits of the channel.
- B) State plainly that the research will report what users experience, and reframe the conversation around what the director actually needs — if the decision is made, the useful questions become who will struggle, what support they will need, and what would signal the change is failing.
- C) Agree, then report honestly and deal with the fallout later.
- D) Refuse and report the conversation to the head of user research.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Option B declines the premise without confrontation and reframes towards something honest and valuable to the director. Option A is least effective, a rigged study surfacing eventually, usually at the worst moment. Option C leaves the director's expectation uncorrected, guaranteeing a worse conflict later. Option D may become necessary if pressure persists, but the first move is a professional conversation.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Developing someone whose practice is weak**

A supervised researcher consistently writes findings that overreach; mentioned twice, it continues. Ask for the most effective next step.

- A) Rewrite their findings before they circulate.
- B) Move from general feedback to something structured: work through one of their reports together, line by line, agreeing what each claim can support; then agree they will apply that test themselves before their next report, and review it together.
- C) Raise it in their performance review.
- D) Reduce their responsibility for reporting.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Twice-repeated general feedback failing usually means the person cannot yet see the problem in their own writing. Option B makes the abstract concrete and closes the loop with a follow-up review. Option A is least effective, removing the problem from view while guaranteeing it persists and signalling the candidate doubts they can learn. Option C is premature before a structured intervention is tried. Option D protects outputs at the cost of development.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Sceptical colleague, repeated publicly**

A lead engineer repeatedly characterises research findings as "anecdotes" in front of the team, affecting how findings are received. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Explain the methodological basis of qualitative research at the next team meeting.
- B) Invite them to observe two sessions and discuss afterwards what they saw; separately, find a question where analytics located a problem and sessions explained it, and present that jointly with whoever owns the analytics — so complementarity is demonstrated rather than argued.
- C) Ask the delivery manager to address the behaviour.
- D) Present findings with quantitative framing to satisfy them.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Methodological explanations rarely shift a position that is not really methodological. Presenting jointly with the analytics owner recruits the sceptic's preferred evidence to the case. Option D is least effective, conceding the false premise and manufacturing precisely the overreach that would justify the scepticism. Option A rarely works alone. Option C entrenches the position by converting it into a management matter.

**Question 8 (harder) — An ethical problem in another team's research**

A team is recruiting for research on a domestic abuse support service by approaching users through the service's own contact list, with sessions conducted by video and no protocol for what to do if a participant is not alone. Ask what should be done.

- A) Raise it with the researcher and offer to help them design a safer approach.
- B) Raise it immediately with both the researcher and the head of user research, set out the specific risks — that contact via the service's list may reach people whose devices are monitored, and that a video session with no aloneness protocol could expose a participant to danger — and press for fieldwork to pause until safeguards are in place.
- C) Report it to the data protection officer.
- D) Raise it at the next community of practice session.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The potential harm is severe and immediate — a monitored device could alert a perpetrator, and a video session with no aloneness check could expose a participant to danger during the session itself — justifying simultaneous escalation and a pause request rather than the sequential support of option A. Option D is least effective by a wide margin, deferring a live physical-safety risk to a scheduled meeting. Option C addresses a real but secondary concern.

**Question 9 (harder) — Evidence that contradicts a commitment already made**

Research shows a service change announced publicly by a minister will substantially disadvantage users with limited digital access; colleagues suggest framing the findings as "areas for future improvement." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Agree; the commitment is public and cannot be reversed.
- B) Report the findings accurately, and separately do the constructive work: quantify who is affected, set out what mitigation would look like, and present the mitigation alongside the finding so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report the findings starkly and let the organisation deal with it.
- D) Escalate to the head of user research before doing anything.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Softening the finding produces the worst outcome — the disadvantage still happens, nobody is accountable, and the evidence that could have driven mitigation is neutered. Option B keeps the finding intact and actionable. Option A is least effective, exactly the failure professional standards exist to prevent, disadvantaging the users least able to absorb it. Option D is worth doing in parallel but is not a substitute for doing the work.

**Question 10 (harder) — Competing demands from senior stakeholders**

Two directors want the candidate's team's research capacity in the same quarter — one a live service failing users now, the other a high-profile new programme with ministerial visibility — both escalated to the head of profession. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Split capacity between them.
- B) Set out, for each, what research would deliver, what decision it would inform, and what the consequence of deferral is — then recommend a sequence with reasoning, and take that recommendation to both directors and the head of profession together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the ministerial programme.
- D) Ask the head of profession to decide.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Making the trade-off explicit and visible to everyone at once prevents parallel-negotiation dynamics where trust collapses. Making a recommendation rather than presenting neutral options uses the candidate's expertise properly. Option A is least effective, typically producing two studies too thin to inform either decision. Option C ranks by visibility. Option D discards the candidate's own judgement.

**Question 11 (harder) — A flaw in research the candidate signed off**

Three months after assuring a study, the candidate realises its screener excluded anyone who had contacted the support desk, so the sample systematically omitted users who had already struggled; the findings shaped a live service change. Ask what should be done.

- A) Note it as a lesson for future assurance.
- B) Tell the team and the decision-makers immediately, scope precisely which conclusions are compromised and which stand, propose targeted follow-up research with the excluded group, and review why the assurance process did not catch it.
- C) Commission a follow-up quietly and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Raise it with the head of profession and let them decide how to handle it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A live service change is running on the findings, so telling people now matters, and precise scoping avoids both overstating and understating the damage. Reviewing why the candidate's own assurance process did not catch it is the part specific to this level. Option A defers everything into abstraction. Option C conceals a known flaw while the change proceeds. Option D is worth doing alongside, not instead — owning the error personally is faster and more credible.

**Question 12 (harder) — Building assurance where there is none**

Moving to a department with no research assurance process, where reports vary widely in quality and researchers work in isolation across six teams; the head of profession asks the candidate to fix it. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Write an assurance standard and require all reports to comply.
- B) Start by making the current variation visible — review a sample of recent reports with the researchers themselves, identify the recurring issues, and co-design a lightweight standard and peer review routine with them; then pilot it with willing teams before extending it.
- C) Introduce mandatory peer review immediately.
- D) Provide training on research quality.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** An imposed standard in a department with no assurance culture produces compliance theatre. Option B surfaces the problems where researchers can see them, produces a standard with authors who will defend it, and builds evidence and advocates through a pilot before extending. Option A is least effective, the fastest route to a document nobody uses. Option C introduces a mechanism without the shared understanding that makes peer review anything but a delay. Option D is a useful component and insufficient alone.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate develops a colleague's capability alongside solving the immediate problem, rather than solving it alone.
- Note whether the candidate protects a junior colleague from having to hold a difficult position alone under stakeholder pressure.
- Watch for whether the candidate calibrates escalation to genuine severity — a safety risk goes up immediately and in parallel; peer feedback goes to the person first.
- Score for whether the candidate makes a trade-off or prioritisation decision explicit and visible to all parties, rather than negotiating separately with each.
- Note whether the candidate reports their own error promptly, scopes it precisely, and reviews why an assurance process failed to catch it.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that solves a problem personally without developing the person or system around it.
- Missing when a candidate softens a finding to accommodate a commitment already made.
- Rewarding a candidate who mandates a new practice before demonstrating its value.
- Accepting a response that corrects a peer's work by going straight to stakeholders rather than to the person first.
- Missing when a candidate defers an urgent safety concern to a scheduled forum rather than escalating immediately.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and systemic diagnosis across studies the candidate did not conduct, compounding bias, and value-of-information prioritisation; the numeric judgement that keeps figures defensible in front of sceptical stakeholders — weighted rates, reconstructed unit values, distinguishing a correctly calculated metric from a correctly interpreted one, and declining to endorse an underpowered comparison; the discipline of reading governance and evaluation prose at exactly the strength the text supports, setting aside expertise that would otherwise supply connections the text does not make; and the situational judgement that defines a strong senior user researcher — developing other people's capability alongside solving problems, protecting junior colleagues from pressure they should not hold alone, and calibrating escalation to genuine severity.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and the strongest answers consistently know the limits of their own evidence and help other people learn the same. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
