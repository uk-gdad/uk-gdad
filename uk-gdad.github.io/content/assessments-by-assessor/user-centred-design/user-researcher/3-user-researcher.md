# User Researcher - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for user researcher. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A user researcher is usually embedded in a multidisciplinary team and responsible for planning and carrying out user research activities, expected to work independently. That independence is central: the candidate is no longer executing a plan somebody else designed, but defines the scope and purpose of research, chooses methods, involves the team, builds consensus with stakeholders, and presents findings colleagues can use. There is a particular reason this matters for user researchers specifically: professional credibility rests on being the person in the room whose claims can be trusted. Designers advocate for their designs, product managers for their roadmap, policy colleagues for their policy — and a user researcher is expected to say what the evidence shows, at the strength it shows it, including when that is inconvenient. The material below tests exactly that.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate reasons under genuine ambiguity — several items have no clean answer, only a best one — and whether they can state a finding at the strength the evidence actually supports rather than the strength that would be most convenient. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward independent professional judgement: holding a position under pressure when the evidence warrants it, and escalating only what genuinely requires authority the candidate does not hold.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences. At this level the materials get more realistic and the reasoning less mechanical: research plans with competing constraints, evidence that points two ways at once, and questions where the right answer depends on distinguishing what the data supports from what it merely suggests.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparably experienced practitioners.

Independent working means nobody checks a candidate's reasoning before it reaches the team. When a candidate decides that four participants hitting the same wall constitutes a finding while two constitutes a hypothesis, that judgement goes straight into a report. What rises at this level, compared with a more junior one, is the expectation of reasoning under genuine ambiguity — several items below have no clean answer, only a best one, and the discriminating factor is whether the candidate identifies the strongest reading of incomplete evidence rather than the most comfortable one, sometimes holding two things true at once: that a pattern is worth acting on, and that it is not yet established.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, distinguishing a genuine pattern from an artefact of the sample.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, ensuring research has a clearly defined scope and purpose, and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**. Advocacy built on an overreach collapses the first time someone checks it.
- **Error checking** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, building understanding of good practice standards and improving practice through reflection and feedback.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile research practices**, designing research so findings can be embedded into an agile workflow.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **User research methods** and to **Inclusive research**, working with colleagues to include many kinds of users.
- **Reasoning about people and positions** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, building and reaching consensus with stakeholders.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a research plan**

The team's plan standard requires six elements: research questions, method, recruitment approach, sample composition including access needs, analysis approach, and how findings will be shared. A colleague's draft states the research questions, method, recruitment approach and analysis approach, and includes "findings will be presented at the sprint review." Ask how many required elements are missing.

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four elements are directly present; the sprint review line satisfies the sharing requirement, bringing the count to five. The missing element is sample composition including access needs. This tests whether the candidate checks each requirement individually and recognises a brief but valid statement of the sharing approach — option C catches a candidate who overlooks that line. It also tests whether the candidate identifies the missing element correctly: sample composition including access needs is the requirement whose absence most reliably produces research that fails the people it most needs to serve.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

A standard states: "Research involving participants under 18 may proceed only with documented approval from the head of user research, unless the research is conducted entirely through an existing school partnership under the standing agreement." A colleague says: "We ran sessions with 16-year-olds last week without documented approval from the head of user research." Ask which must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) The research was conducted through the school partnership.
- C) Either the research ran under the school partnership, or the standard was breached.
- D) The sessions were unethical.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional rule with one stated exception, plus facts silent on whether the exception applies, yields only the disjunction. Option D imports an ethical judgement the item does not support. This tests whether the candidate resists assuming either branch, and — beyond the logic itself — whether they recognise the professional implication: the right move is to ask which route was used, not to assume the worst, which matters at a level building understanding of good practice standards and giving colleagues feedback.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across mixed evidence**

Qualitative sessions with ten participants found seven struggled to understand the eligibility criteria; analytics show only 4% abandon the service at the eligibility page. A product manager concludes the qualitative finding must be wrong. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The product manager is right; analytics reflect real behaviour at scale.
- B) The qualitative finding is right; analytics cannot show comprehension.
- C) The two measure different things — abandonment is not comprehension — so people may be proceeding past the page while misunderstanding it, which is potentially worse than abandoning.
- D) The datasets are incompatible and cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Analytics measure behaviour (whether someone left the page); the sessions measure understanding (whether someone knew what the criteria meant). A user who misunderstands but proceeds anyway generates no abandonment signal, and may submit an application they were never eligible for. This tests whether the candidate names what each source actually measures rather than privileging scale (A), being purely defensive (B), or giving up on reconciliation (D) — being able to explain why two sources differ, rather than defend one's own, is valuable when quantitative data is treated as automatically more authoritative.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across competing research needs**

With capacity for one substantial study in three weeks: (1) a new eligibility screen goes into build in two weeks with no research done; (2) a live service has a 38% drop-off at document upload, ongoing for months; (3) a policy team wants exploratory research on a service concept for next year; (4) the team wants to re-test a screen researched six weeks ago after minor copy changes. Ask which should be prioritised, and why.

- A) Option 1, because research must precede build.
- B) Option 2, because it affects the largest number of real users now.
- C) Option 1, because it is the only one where research findings can still change a decision within the window, while options 2 and 3 remain valuable but are not time-boxed in the same way.
- D) Option 3, because early-stage research has the highest leverage.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The deciding factor is not importance in the abstract but whether findings can still change a decision. Option 1 has a hard window; option 2 has persisted for months and will still be there in four weeks; option 3 has no deadline pressure; option 4 rarely justifies a full re-test for minor copy changes. This tests whether the candidate reasons explicitly about timing rather than asserting a slogan — option A states the right conclusion for the wrong reason, since "research must precede build" would rank option 3 oddly.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Error checking a sampling approach**

A study plans twelve participants for a service used by around four million people annually. A stakeholder objects that twelve is far too few to be representative. Ask for the most rigorous response.

- A) Agree, and increase the sample to fifty.
- B) Explain that qualitative research does not aim for statistical representativeness — it aims to find and understand problems — and that twelve well-chosen participants across the relevant user groups will surface the substantial usability issues, while a rate would require different methods.
- C) Explain that twelve is the industry standard.
- D) Explain that sample size does not matter in qualitative research.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The objection conflates two goals — discovering what problems exist and measuring how prevalent they are. This tests whether the candidate separates them rather than conceding on the wrong basis (A), appealing to convention (C), or dismissing sample considerations entirely (D) — sample size and composition matter greatly in qualitative work; what changes is what they are for.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about what a finding licenses**

Nine of twelve participants failed to notice a "save and return" link. Ask which conclusion is best supported.

- A) 75% of users do not notice the link.
- B) The link's discoverability is a substantial problem, evidenced across most participants and varied user groups, and warrants design work and a follow-up measure of whether the change helps.
- C) The link should be moved to the top of the page.
- D) The link is invisible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Nine of twelve is a strong qualitative signal, strong enough to act on, and option B states it at the right strength. Option A converts twelve participants into a population rate, the single most common overreach in research reporting. Option C prescribes a specific design solution, usually not the researcher's call. Option D overstates — not noticing is different from invisible, and the distinction points at different causes. This tests whether the candidate builds in the follow-up measurement that turns a finding into a change that can be defended.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a constraint**

Research is needed on a service used mainly by people over 70, many not online. The organisation's recruitment supplier works exclusively through an online panel, procurement for a new supplier takes eight weeks, and there are only four. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Use the online panel and note the limitation.
- B) Delay the research until a suitable supplier is procured.
- C) Work within the four weeks using routes that do not require procurement — partnering with organisations that already work with older people, running sessions in community venues or by telephone, and arranging incentives that do not require an online account — while starting the procurement in parallel for future rounds.
- D) Run the research with staff who have older relatives.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The constraint is procurement, not recruitment, and the strongest analysis separates the two — community partnerships, telephone or in-person sessions and non-digital incentives all avoid the procurement bottleneck. This tests whether the candidate solves the instance and the system together by starting procurement in parallel, rather than producing findings about a different population (A), forfeiting four weeks (B), or using a convenience sample with no credibility (D).

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reasoning about a stakeholder position**

A policy lead has repeatedly resisted research findings suggesting an application form is too long; the form's length results from statutory requirements they are responsible for defending. Ask for the most useful reframing.

- A) The policy lead is being obstructive and should be escalated.
- B) The resistance is likely about the perceived implication that they should breach statutory duties; reframing the research around how to make the required questions easier to answer, rather than fewer, addresses the real user problem without asking them to do the impossible.
- C) The findings should be softened to avoid the conflict.
- D) The research should focus on a different part of the service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Understanding why somebody resists is a reasoning task. If the questions must be asked, the researchable question becomes how to ask them — sequencing, plain language, explanation, saved progress. This tests whether the candidate reframes the problem so both the user need and the constraint can be satisfied, rather than treating a structural conflict as a personality problem (A), compromising the findings (C), or abandoning a real user problem (D).

**Question 9 (harder) — Distinguishing signal from artefact**

Across three rounds of research on a benefits service, participants have consistently described the process as "straightforward." Recruitment has run through the department's existing user panel, made up of people who previously completed applications successfully and volunteered for research. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The service is straightforward; three rounds is consistent evidence.
- B) The finding is likely an artefact of the sampling frame: a panel of people who completed successfully and then volunteered systematically excludes those who failed, gave up, or were deterred — so the consistency reflects who was asked, not how the service performs.
- C) More rounds should be run to confirm the finding.
- D) The panel should be discontinued.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is survivorship bias: everyone in the panel has cleared two filters — completing the process and volunteering afterwards. This tests whether the candidate recognises that the consistency across three rounds is three samples from the same filtered pool, rather than mistaking repetition for corroboration (A), running a fourth confirming round (C), or overcorrecting by discontinuing a panel that is useful for other questions (D).

**Question 10 (harder) — Prioritising under a genuine trade-off**

With three weeks, a choice between six sessions with a broad mix of users covering the whole journey at moderate depth, or twelve sessions concentrated on the two steps analytics show have the largest drop-offs, which the team is about to rebuild with no plans to change the rest. Ask which is stronger, and why.

- A) The broad study, because whole-journey understanding is always more valuable.
- B) The concentrated study, because the team can only act on the two steps in question, and twelve sessions there will produce findings robust enough to guide a rebuild — while broad findings about screens nobody will change cannot influence anything.
- C) The broad study, because concentrating on two steps risks missing context.
- D) Split the time evenly between both.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Research value is a function of what a team can act on, and here the actionable surface is precisely two steps. This tests whether the candidate reasons from actionability rather than applying a general preference unchecked (A) — the mitigation for option C's real concern is brief context questions at the start of each session — and avoids the characteristic failure of splitting a constrained resource down the middle (D), producing a study too shallow to guide the rebuild and too narrow to map the journey.

**Question 11 (harder) — Reconciling contradictory evidence**

A survey of 2,000 users reports 81% satisfaction; fourteen qualitative sessions found substantial frustration, with eleven participants describing significant difficulties. Ask for the most rigorous way to reconcile these.

- A) Trust the survey; 2,000 responses outweigh fourteen sessions.
- B) Trust the sessions; observed behaviour beats self-report.
- C) Examine what each is measuring and who answered: satisfaction surveys typically reach people who completed the journey and often ask about the outcome rather than the process, while the sessions observed the process itself — so both can be accurate about different things, and the discrepancy is itself a finding worth investigating.
- D) Average the two and report moderate satisfaction.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Satisfaction surveys are usually served at the end of a journey, missing people who abandoned, and often ask whether the user got what they needed rather than how hard it was. This tests whether the candidate resists declaring a winner (A, B) and instead identifies that the gap between "I got what I needed" and "that was hard" is itself worth reporting. Option D — averaging incommensurable measures — produces a number describing nothing.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in the candidate's own study**

Midway through analysis, the candidate realises their discussion guide asked, "What made the upload step difficult?" — presupposing difficulty — in all ten sessions; eight participants described difficulties. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the finding, noting the wording in the limitations.
- B) Discard the study.
- C) Treat the difficulty frequency as unusable because it was elicited by a leading question, but examine whether the specific difficulties participants described are substantive and consistent, since a leading question can prompt a topic without inventing the content — then verify with a short neutral follow-up round before reporting anything as established.
- D) Re-run all ten sessions with corrected wording.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The leading question compromises the frequency claim specifically, not automatically the content of what participants described. This tests whether the candidate separates the contaminated claim from potentially salvageable content and verifies before asserting — recognising the precise extent of a flaw, rather than treating it as total (B) or negligible (A), is characteristic of experienced analytic judgement, and admitting the flaw at all is what improving practice through reflection actually requires.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies precisely which claims a flaw in the evidence compromises and which survive it, rather than treating the flaw as total or negligible.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a genuine finding from an artefact of the sampling frame or a leading question.
- Watch for whether the candidate prioritises by whether findings can still change a decision within a window, rather than by importance alone.
- Score for whether the candidate reconciles apparently contradictory evidence by examining what each source measures, rather than declaring a winner.
- Note whether the candidate states a finding at the strength the evidence supports, resisting conversion of a qualitative sample into a population rate.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that converts "nine of twelve participants" into "75% of users."
- Missing when a candidate declares a winner between two data sources rather than reconciling by definition.
- Rewarding a candidate who mistakes repetition of a biased sample for corroboration.
- Accepting a candidate who prescribes a specific design solution rather than establishing the problem.
- Missing when a candidate applies a general preference or heuristic without checking it against the specific situation.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles the quantitative material this role produces and consumes. At this level that material is not limited to counting participants: analytics funnels, survey results alongside qualitative findings, recruitment tracking, completion rates compared between user groups, and explaining to stakeholders what a number does and does not mean.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or short paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. What distinguishes this section at this level is interpretation — several items have a correct calculation attached to a wrong conclusion, and telling them apart is the test. Quantitative data carries unearned authority in most organisations; a candidate who can say "where does that figure come from, and what is its denominator?" is valuable in a way one who can only compute is not.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator before calculating anything. Estimate roughly, then calculate precisely, and compare. Sanity-check the result against the world.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading tables and funnels accurately** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**.
- **Comparing rates between groups** maps to **Inclusive research** — a gap between groups is often the first visible trace of an accessibility barrier.
- **Interpreting quantitative evidence alongside qualitative** maps to **User research methods**.
- **Sampling and recruitment arithmetic** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.
- **Stating figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Detecting misleading numbers** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where advocacy depends on evidence surviving scrutiny.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Of 24 participants, 18 completed the core task unaided. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 18 ÷ 24 = 75%, giving C. This tests basic percentage calculation and whether the candidate reports this as "18 of 24 participants" rather than "75% of users" — a distinction explored further in harder items below.

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruitment**

No-show rate 25%, 15 completed sessions needed. Ask how many should be booked.

- A) 17
- B) 19
- C) 20
- D) 24

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 15 ÷ 0.75 = 20, giving C. The common error adds 25% to 15 (giving 18.75, rounding to 19), which underestimates because the percentage applies to the booking figure, not the target. This tests whether the candidate applies the correct denominator to an over-recruitment calculation, a quiet but common way a study ends up unable to support the claims it was designed to make.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading across a table**

First-time applicants: 15 participants, 9 completed. Repeat applicants: 10, 9 completed. Assisted digital: 5, 1 completed. Ask which group had the highest completion rate.

- A) First-time applicants
- B) Repeat applicants
- C) Assisted digital
- D) First-time and repeat applicants were equal

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First-time: 9 ÷ 15 = 60%. Repeat: 9 ÷ 10 = 90%. Assisted digital: 1 ÷ 5 = 20%. This tests whether the candidate converts to rates rather than comparing the raw completion column, where first-time and repeat both show 9 completions from different bases — option D is the distractor for that error. A strong candidate's answer also notes the assisted digital group's rate is a fifth of the repeat rate, deserving attention out of proportion to its size in this sample.

**Question 4 (easy) — Median versus mean**

Seven participants took 4, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, and 40 minutes. Ask which pair of figures best summarises the group.

- A) Mean 10.9, range 4 to 40
- B) Median 6, range 4 to 40
- C) Mean 10.9 alone
- D) Median 6 alone

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean (10.86) describes nobody's actual experience; the median (6) genuinely represents the typical case but alone hides the 40-minute session. This tests whether the candidate reports a central figure and a measure of spread together, and describes an extreme case explicitly rather than letting a summary statistic absorb it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average**

Round one: 8 participants, mean satisfaction 4.5. Round two: 12 participants, mean satisfaction 3.5. Ask for the overall mean across all 20.

- A) 3.6
- B) 3.9
- C) 4.0
- D) 4.1

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Round one contributes 36 points, round two 42; total 78 ÷ 20 = 3.9, giving B. Option C averages the two means directly (4.0), ignoring that round two had more participants. This tests whether the candidate returns to totals rather than averaging averages from unequal groups — a frequently botched calculation in real reporting, and one that usually flatters whichever group was smaller.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 20,000 start, 15,000 pass identity checks, 9,000 upload evidence, 8,100 submit. Ask what proportion of those who passed identity checks went on to submit.

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 8,100 ÷ 15,000 = 54%, giving B. Each of the other options uses a different, unasked-for denominator. This tests whether the candidate underlines the group the stem names before touching the figures — all four options are true statements about this funnel, and only one answers the question posed.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion rose from 30% to 42% after a redesign. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) Completion rose 12%.
- B) Completion rose 12 percentage points, a 40% relative increase.
- C) Completion rose 40 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose 28.6%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The gap is 12 percentage points; the relative increase is 12 ÷ 30 = 40%. This tests a distinction with real professional stakes — "completion rose 40%" and "completion rose 12 percentage points" describe the same change, and the first sounds more than three times as impressive, so being able to name the distinction calmly is part of keeping the organisation's evidence honest.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Base rates and small subgroups**

In a survey of 200 respondents, 6 identified as using a screen reader; of those 6, 4 reported difficulty submitting. A colleague writes: "67% of screen reader users cannot submit." Ask for the strongest objection.

- A) The percentage is calculated incorrectly.
- B) Four of six is a very small base; converting it to 67% implies a precision the sample cannot support, and the claim generalises from six self-selected survey respondents to all screen reader users.
- C) Screen reader users should not be analysed separately.
- D) The survey should have recruited more screen reader users.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is correct — 4 ÷ 6 is indeed about 67% — ruling out A and making this a question of interpretation. One additional respondent would move the figure to 57% or 71%. This tests whether the candidate names both faults — spurious precision and overgeneralisation — rather than concluding disaggregation itself is wrong (C, which would obscure exactly the accessibility problems it should surface) or offering only a forward-looking recommendation (D) instead of an objection to the current claim.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Working back to a base**

A stakeholder says 12% of applications are abandoned at the evidence step, representing 3,600 applications annually. Ask how many applications are started annually.

- A) 30,000
- B) 36,000
- C) 43,200
- D) 300,000

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 3,600 ÷ 0.12 = 30,000, giving A. This tests whether the candidate can work backwards from a percentage to a base — a calculation that matters because the base changes what a figure means entirely: 12% abandonment is a very different problem at 30,000 applications a year than at three million.

**Question 10 (harder) — A rate that is not what it appears**

A dashboard reports "average time on the eligibility page: 22 seconds," and a product manager concludes users find the page easy; the candidate's sessions showed participants spending several minutes reading it. Ask for the most likely explanation and the right response.

- A) The dashboard is wrong and should be ignored.
- B) The average is probably dominated by users who bounce off the page within a few seconds, pulling the mean down, and it may exclude sessions that timed out — so the figure conflates fast leavers with fast readers, and the right response is to ask for the distribution rather than the mean.
- C) The participants in the sessions were unusually slow.
- D) The two measures are incompatible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A mean time-on-page mixes together people who glanced and left with people who stayed and read. This tests whether the candidate identifies the mechanism and asks for the distribution rather than discarding the dashboard (A), blaming the participants (C), or giving up on reconciliation (D) — "can I see the distribution?" is one of the highest-value questions a researcher can bring to an analytics conversation.

**Question 11 (harder) — Comparing groups with a confound**

Completion rates: desktop 82% (n=4,100), mobile 51% (n=6,300). A stakeholder proposes prioritising a mobile redesign. Ask for the most rigorous addition to this analysis.

- A) Nothing; the gap is clear and large.
- B) Check whether the two groups differ in other relevant ways — for instance whether mobile users are disproportionately first-time applicants, or arriving from a different referral route, or attempting the journey in circumstances that differ — because device may be a marker for those differences rather than the cause of the gap.
- C) Recommend against the redesign until qualitative research is done.
- D) Recalculate the rates.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests whether the candidate identifies what device may be standing in for, since the remedy differs depending on the actual cause — a responsive redesign fixes a layout problem but not a "users do not have their documents to hand" problem. Option A acts on an unexamined correlation. Option C is too absolute, since the redesign may well be right regardless. Option D implies an arithmetic doubt the item does not raise.

**Question 12 (harder) — Sample sizing for a comparison**

Current completion is 60%, and a 5 percentage point improvement is considered meaningful. A colleague proposes testing with 20 participants before and 20 after. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 40 participants is a reasonable sample.
- B) Explain that detecting a 5 percentage point difference reliably requires a sample far larger than 40 — with 20 per group, the expected difference is well within the range of ordinary variation — so this comparison should be made with analytics on live traffic, while the 40 sessions are better spent understanding why users succeed or fail.
- C) Increase to 50 per group.
- D) Run the sessions and report the difference whatever it is.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With 20 per group, a 5 percentage point difference amounts to roughly one participant — indistinguishable from chance. This tests whether the candidate redirects rather than merely objects, recognising that live analytics can measure the rate at no recruitment cost while moderated sessions explain mechanisms — knowing which method can answer which question, and saying so before the study runs, is a defining professional skill at this level. Option D is the most damaging, inviting the team to act on noise.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator before calculating, particularly in funnel or subgroup items.
- Note whether the candidate returns to totals rather than averaging averages from groups of unequal size.
- Watch for whether the candidate questions what a metric (time on page, a small-base percentage) actually measures before accepting it.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes correlation from mechanism when comparing groups that may differ in more than the variable proposed as the cause.
- Note whether the candidate recognises when a proposed sample size cannot detect the effect size in question.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage calculated correctly but reported at spurious precision from a tiny base.
- Missing when a candidate averages two means from unequal groups without weighting.
- Rewarding a candidate who accepts a correlation between two variables (device, channel) as evidence of mechanism without checking for confounds.
- Accepting a mean reported without any check on the underlying spread or distribution.
- Missing when a candidate endorses an underpowered comparison as capable of detecting the effect size in question.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. In a role that runs on documents — service standards, ethics guidance, evaluation reports, policy submissions, stakeholder correspondence, and the transcribed words of participants — reading accuracy is the substrate everything else rests on.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone. This is counter-intuitive for experienced practitioners and is the largest single source of lost marks at senior levels specifically: the more a candidate knows, the more readily they supply a link the text does not make. Cannot Say deserves particular attention, since candidates avoid it and reach for True or False to appear decisive, when it is frequently correct.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, building understanding of good practice standards.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where findings that smuggle in an inference are the commonest failure mode.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, tailoring communication to stakeholders' needs.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where advocacy that overstates its evidence loses the credibility that makes it effective.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Inclusive research**.
- **Reaching consensus from text** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, using evidence to explain decisions.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Services must be researched with users at every stage of development, including after go-live. Research plans must be reviewed by a second user researcher before fieldwork begins, unless the research consists solely of usability sessions on an existing prototype. Where a service is used predominantly by people who may be considered vulnerable, an ethics review is additionally required. Findings from all research must be recorded in the organisation's repository within ten working days."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "All research plans require review by a second user researcher."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception for plans consisting solely of usability sessions on an existing prototype; the statement's "all" contradicts that. False. This tests the most reliable pattern in the section: an absolute term in the statement, a conditional or exception in the passage.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Research must continue after a service goes live."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The first sentence requires research at every stage and adds "including after go-live." True. This tests whether the candidate treats an "including" clause as the author deliberately flagging a case readers commonly miss.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A study involving usability sessions and interviews on an existing prototype does not require second-researcher review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where research consists *solely* of usability sessions; a study also including interviews does not meet that condition. False. This tests whether the candidate registers "solely" as the load-bearing word rather than skimming past it.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service used mainly by people who may be vulnerable requires both a second-researcher review and an ethics review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The ethics review requirement is clear and additional; whether second-researcher review also applies depends on whether the research consists solely of prototype usability sessions, which the statement does not establish. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate holds two conditions in mind simultaneously rather than resolving one and forgetting the other.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The review of research practice across the department found that teams generally followed the recruitment standard, though several reported difficulty recruiting participants with learning disabilities. Two teams had conducted research without a published plan; both were operating under time pressure from ministerial commitments. The review did not examine the quality of analysis. It recommends that the head of user research consider whether additional recruitment support is required."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some teams found it hard to recruit participants with learning disabilities."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** "Several teams reported difficulty" supports "some." True. This tests whether the candidate respects the passage's careful verb "reported" rather than converting reported experience into an established general fact.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The quality of analysis across the department is adequate."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states explicitly that the review did not examine analysis quality. Declared silence establishes nothing. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate resists the inference that a review finding no problems implies none exist — a review that did not look cannot be evidence of what it did not see.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "Time pressure from ministerial commitments caused the two teams to skip publishing a plan."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states two facts side by side without asserting a causal link between them. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate reads juxtaposition as juxtaposition rather than causation — a common error among experienced readers, because supplying a plausible mechanism feels like comprehension.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The review recommends providing additional recruitment support."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the head of user research *consider whether* additional support is required — a recommendation to evaluate, not to provide. False rather than Cannot Say, since the passage tells you what the recommendation was and it was something different. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes "recommends considering X" from "recommends X."

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Participants must be able to withdraw at any point without giving a reason. Where a participant withdraws, personal data collected from them must be deleted within five working days, unless the data has been anonymised such that the participant can no longer be identified. Anonymised research data may be retained indefinitely for the purposes of analysis and assurance. Researchers should explain the withdrawal process at the start of each session; where sessions are unmoderated, this explanation must appear in the participant information."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "A participant must give a reason if they wish to withdraw."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states participants must be able to withdraw without giving a reason. False. This is a quick item, worth answering fast to preserve time for harder ones.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "Anonymised data from a participant who withdraws must be deleted within five working days."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The deletion requirement carries an explicit exception for fully anonymised data, and the following sentence confirms anonymised data may be retained indefinitely. False. This tests whether the candidate carries the exception clause forward across a sentence boundary, where it is reinforced.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "In an unmoderated study, a researcher need not explain the withdrawal process."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Where sessions are unmoderated, the explanation must appear in the participant information — the obligation changes form, from spoken to written, rather than disappearing, and it also hardens from "should" to "must." False. This tests whether the candidate reads the whole sentence rather than assuming an exemption is coming.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Personal data must be deleted when participants withdraw.
- B) Participants retain control over their involvement and their identifiable data, with the organisation's retention rights limited to data from which they cannot be identified, and the process must be communicated in whatever form the session takes.
- C) Anonymised data may be kept indefinitely.
- D) Researchers must explain the withdrawal process at the start of each session.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Options A, C and D are each accurate about one sentence, and A also omits the anonymisation exception. Option B captures the unifying logic: participant control as the principle, identifiability as the boundary on retention, and communication adapted to session format. This tests whether the candidate selects an answer broad enough to cover the whole passage and disciplined enough to add nothing.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate treats declared silence in a passage as establishing nothing, rather than as reassurance.
- Note whether the candidate reads juxtaposed facts as juxtaposition rather than inferring causation.
- Watch for whether the candidate carries an exception clause forward across a sentence boundary rather than losing it.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes "recommends considering X" from "recommends X."
- Note whether the candidate recognises a substitution of form ("must appear in writing instead") as distinct from an exemption.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer drawn from the candidate's own professional expertise rather than the passage — the more a candidate knows, the more likely this error becomes.
- Missing when a candidate avoids Cannot Say and reaches for True or False to seem more decisive.
- Rewarding a candidate who skims past a narrowing word ("solely", "only", "entirely") in an exception clause.
- Accepting a claim of causation supported only by two facts stated side by side.
- Missing when a candidate selects a true but partial detail as a passage's main point.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level, calibrated to an independent practitioner: embedded in a multidisciplinary team, responsible for planning and carrying out research, expected to work independently, ensuring research has a clearly defined scope and purpose, building consensus with stakeholders, and getting advice from someone more experienced when needed. What distinguishes this level from a junior one: a junior researcher escalates a governance question; a user researcher is expected to know the answer to most of them, handle the situation, and escalate only what genuinely requires authority they do not hold.

Four principles carry most scenarios: participants and users come first; the integrity of the evidence is protected — options that inflate, soften, hide or mislabel findings are close to automatically wrong; problems are addressed directly, early, with the person involved; and the strongest options fix the instance, then the system.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Ethical judgement and participant care** map to **Inclusive research** and the ethical core of **User research methods**.
- **Defining and defending scope** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.
- **Building consensus and handling resistance** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Involving the team** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** and **Agile research practices**.
- **Advocating for users with sceptical colleagues** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Reflecting and improving** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A disclosure during a session**

A participant discussing a housing service mentions they are currently at risk of eviction and becomes distressed; the study is about the online form. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Acknowledge it briefly and steer back to the form, since the study has limited time.
- B) Acknowledge what they have said with care, check whether they want to continue or stop, offer the support signposting the team prepared, and follow the organisation's safeguarding process afterwards.
- C) Stop the session and contact a housing charity on their behalf.
- D) Continue and record the disclosure as evidence of user need.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A disclosure of this kind outranks the research question without qualification. Option B acknowledges, restores control, signposts without improvising a support role, and follows the safeguarding process so the organisation's mechanism operates rather than resting on the candidate alone. Option A subordinates a crisis to a schedule. Option C removes agency by acting without agreement. Option D is least effective, converting distress into a data point — a category error about what the candidate is there for.

**Question 2 (easy) — Scope creep from a stakeholder**

A week into a study on the application journey, a stakeholder asks to add questions about a proposed new feature "since you're talking to users anyway." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Add the questions; it is efficient use of the sessions.
- B) Explain that adding an unrelated topic risks both the original research questions and the quality of answers on the new one, and propose either a short separate study or adding the topic to the next round with proper design time.
- C) Refuse, since the plan is agreed.
- D) Add the questions but only ask them if there is time at the end.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's underlying need is legitimate, so the answer is redirection, not refusal. Option B names the real costs — squeezed session time, and questions without design time tending to be leading or vague — and offers two routes forward. Option A is least effective, quietly degrading the study already committed to. Option C treats an agreed plan as a wall. Option D produces data too weak to use but reportable enough to be quoted.

**Question 3 (easy) — Findings that arrive too late to matter**

Findings presented at a sprint review are received warmly, but the screens shipped two days ago; this has happened twice before. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept it; delivery pace is set by the product manager.
- B) Raise it in the team's retrospective with the specific examples, quantify what the late findings would have changed, and propose a concrete process change such as a research checkpoint before screens are locked.
- C) Speed up analysis so findings arrive sooner.
- D) Stop presenting findings that arrive after decisions.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This is a process problem needing a process forum, evidence, and a proposal. Option B supplies all three, using the team's own retrospective mechanism. Option A abdicates something the role explicitly covers — designing research so findings embed into an agile workflow means shaping the workflow too. Option C treats a timing problem as a speed problem. Option D is least effective, punishing users and the team rather than the process and removing the evidence needed to make the case.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A colleague's analysis overreaches**

A peer's draft report claims "users prefer the new design," supported by six of nine participants expressing a preference at the end of a session in which they used the new design second. Ask what should be done.

- A) Approve it; six of nine is a clear majority.
- B) Speak to them privately, note that the claim generalises from nine participants and that order effects may explain some of the preference, and suggest rephrasing to what the evidence supports along with a way to test the preference properly.
- C) Add a comment in the document flagging the problem.
- D) Raise it with their line manager.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two distinct problems exist — overgeneralisation and an order effect — and option B addresses both privately, specifically, and constructively. Option A lets a claim through that will not survive scrutiny. Option C is not wrong and may read as less generous than a conversation on a substantive methodological point. Option D is least effective as a first move, escalating a correctable draft error over a peer's head before they have had a chance to respond.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to reach a particular conclusion**

Before fieldwork, a senior stakeholder says the research "needs to demonstrate that the new service works, because the business case depends on it." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Design the research to focus on what works well.
- B) Say clearly that the research will report what participants actually experience, and reframe the conversation around what they need to know — including what would have to be true for the business case to hold, and what evidence would test that.
- C) Agree, and report the findings honestly regardless.
- D) Report the conversation to the head of profession immediately.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The request as phrased asks for a predetermined conclusion, which would make the research worthless. Option B declines the premise without confrontation and redirects to testing the business case's assumptions, real and valuable research. Option A is least effective, a rigged study that will eventually surface. Option C leaves expectations uncorrected, guaranteeing a worse confrontation later. Option D may become necessary if pressure persists, but the first move is a direct professional conversation.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Recruitment that excludes**

A centrally contracted recruitment supplier consistently delivers participants who are digitally confident and in work, though the service serves many who are neither; other teams are satisfied. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the sample and note the limitation in reports.
- B) Document the mismatch with specific evidence across rounds, supplement immediately through community organisations for the current study, and raise the systemic gap with whoever manages the contract, proposing what a suitable supply would look like.
- C) Complain about the supplier to the contract manager.
- D) Stop using the supplier and recruit independently.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two problems on different timescales need addressing together. Option B supplements the current study through community partnerships within a normal fieldwork window while raising the systemic contract gap with evidence and a proposal. Option A is least effective, since a limitations note does not make findings represent excluded users, and repeating it normalises their absence. Option C lacks evidence or a proposal. Option D abandons the systemic fix and may cut across payment, safeguarding and data protection arrangements.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Disagreement about what the evidence shows**

The candidate and a designer disagree about a finding: the candidate believes participants failed the task because the label is ambiguous, the designer believes it is because the button is visually weak — both readings fit the sessions. Ask how to proceed.

- A) Assert the candidate's own interpretation, since analysis is their responsibility.
- B) Acknowledge that the sessions support both readings, review the evidence together looking for observations that discriminate between them, and if none exist, design a quick test that would.
- C) Defer to the designer, since design is their responsibility.
- D) Report both interpretations in the findings without resolving them.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** When two hypotheses fit the same evidence, the professional move is to find a discriminating observation, or design a quick test if the sessions cannot supply one. Option B also builds the designer's engagement with research by reviewing evidence together. Option A is least effective, asserting where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous — exactly the overreach that erodes trust. Option C abdicates. Option D is honest but leaves the team without a basis for action when a resolution is available.

**Question 8 (harder) — An ethical problem in someone else's study**

Another team is recruiting participants for research on a debt service by approaching people in the queue at a support centre, without a consent process for the initial approach and with sessions conducted in the waiting area. Ask what should be done.

- A) Mind your own business; it is not your team.
- B) Raise it promptly with the researcher involved and, given the vulnerability of the participants and the setting, also with the head of user research — describing what was observed factually and asking what safeguards are in place.
- C) Report it to the organisation's data protection officer.
- D) Post about the problem in the research community channel.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** People approached while queueing for debt support may feel unable to decline, and the waiting area offers no privacy — a combination warranting escalation to both the researcher and the head of user research, factually rather than accusatorially, because of participant vulnerability specifically. Option A ignores a live risk. Option C is not wrong if personal data is mishandled, but the primary issue is research ethics. Option D is least effective, inviting a pile-on and damaging a colleague before the facts are established, while doing nothing to protect the participants who are in that waiting area today.

**Question 9 (harder) — Being asked to shorten the story**

Findings show the service works well for confident users, poorly for first-time applicants, and is effectively unusable for people using screen readers; a stakeholder asks for "a one-line summary for the board." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) "The service works well for most users, with some issues for others."
- B) "The service works for experienced users but fails first-time applicants and is not usable with a screen reader" — a single line that preserves the distinction that matters, offered with the detail behind it.
- C) Refuse to reduce nuanced findings to one line.
- D) "The service has usability problems."

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The request is reasonable, and the skill is compression without distortion. Option B preserves the only distinction a board actually needs to act on. Option A is least effective, technically defensible and functionally misleading by emphasis, burying an accessibility failure inside "some issues." Option D conveys nothing. Option C refuses a legitimate need and results in someone else writing a worse line.

**Question 10 (harder) — A flaw in the candidate's own study**

After reporting findings that influenced a design decision, the candidate realises their screener excluded anyone who had previously abandoned an application, so the sample contained only successful users. Ask what should be done.

- A) Note it in the next report's limitations.
- B) Tell the team and the decision-maker straight away, explain precisely which conclusions are affected and which still stand, and propose a targeted follow-up with the excluded group.
- C) Quietly run a follow-up study and correct the record if the findings differ.
- D) Say nothing; the design decision was reasonable anyway.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A screener that excluded non-completers means the study could not see the failures it was meant to find, and a decision has already been made on it. Option B tells the people who acted on the finding, scopes the damage precisely, and proposes the fix. Option A defers disclosure past the point of usefulness. Option C conceals the flaw while investigating it. Option D is least effective and the one that ends careers, substituting a guess for the evidence the candidate was employed to provide.

**Question 11 (harder) — Consensus that would harm users**

The team has converged on a solution everyone likes; the candidate's research indicates it will not work for the users who struggle most, and challenging it will reopen a decision people are relieved to have made. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go; the team has decided and consensus has value.
- B) Raise it once, clearly and specifically, showing what the evidence indicates will happen to those users and what a mitigation might look like — then, if the team proceeds, ensure the risk is recorded and propose measuring the outcome after release.
- C) Escalate to the service owner over the team's head.
- D) Continue raising it in every meeting until the team changes course.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Option B raises it once and properly, with evidence and a constructive mitigation, then protects users and the candidate's standing by documenting the risk and setting up measurement. Option A is least effective — consensus that harms the users who struggle most is not a value, and silence makes the candidate complicit. Option C escalates before the direct route. Option D substitutes persistence for persuasion, and a researcher who relitigates every decision stops being listened to on any of them.

**Question 12 (harder) — Advocating with a sceptical colleague**

A developer dismisses findings as "just what a few people said," saying real evidence comes from analytics, repeated in front of the team in a way that affects how findings are received. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Explain the methodological basis of qualitative research in the next team meeting.
- B) Invite them to observe the next two sessions, then talk afterwards about what they saw — and separately, look for a place where analytics and sessions together answer a question neither could alone, so the complementarity is demonstrated rather than argued.
- C) Ask the delivery manager to address the behaviour.
- D) Present findings with more quantitative framing to satisfy them.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Scepticism about qualitative research rarely yields to a methodological explanation, because watching a real person fail at something built is difficult to dismiss in a way a spreadsheet is not. Demonstrating that analytics located the problem and sessions explained it makes the developer's preferred evidence part of the case. Option A rarely works alone. Option C converts a professional disagreement into a management issue. Option D is least effective, conceding the false premise and manufacturing exactly the overreach — "60% of users" from six participants — that would justify the scepticism.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate exercises independent judgement, holding a position the evidence supports rather than deferring or capitulating under pressure.
- Note whether the candidate protects the integrity of a finding rather than inflating, softening, burying or mislabelling it to suit an audience.
- Watch for whether the candidate solves both the immediate instance and the underlying system.
- Score for whether the candidate escalates only what genuinely requires authority they do not hold, rather than escalating everything or nothing.
- Note whether the candidate reports their own methodological error promptly, with a precise account of which conclusions are and are not affected.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that predetermines a conclusion under stakeholder pressure.
- Missing when a candidate escalates over a colleague's head as a first move rather than raising it with them directly.
- Rewarding a candidate who refuses a legitimate stakeholder need rather than redirecting it.
- Accepting a compressed summary that buries a serious finding inside vague or comforting language.
- Missing when a candidate delays or conceals disclosure of their own methodological error rather than reporting it promptly and precisely.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and reasoning under genuine ambiguity — separating a finding from an artefact of a sampling frame, reconciling analytics that appear to contradict qualitative sessions, and identifying precisely which claims a flawed method compromises and which survive; the numeric judgement that keeps a study's reporting defensible — weighted averages, funnel denominators, base rates from tiny subgroups, and knowing what a given sample size can and cannot detect; the discipline of reading governance and evaluation prose exactly as written, including declared silence, juxtaposition without causation, and a recommendation to "consider" rather than to act; and the situational judgement that defines a strong user researcher — independent professional judgement, protecting the integrity of evidence, and escalating only what genuinely needs authority beyond their own.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and the strongest answers consistently know the limits of their own evidence and say so. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
