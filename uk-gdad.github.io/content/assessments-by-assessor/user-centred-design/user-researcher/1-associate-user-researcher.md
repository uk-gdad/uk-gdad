# Associate User Researcher - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for associate user researcher. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

An associate user researcher is a trainee in an entry-level role, working with more experienced user researchers. The role description names understanding of the role and potential rather than finished expertise: the candidate will need guidance and training to produce good work and develop their skills. That shapes this whole guide. No assessor should expect a candidate at this level to design a complex longitudinal study or run a stakeholder negotiation alone. What is being tested is exactly what the role description names — understanding, potential, care, and a willingness to learn. The items are pitched accordingly, and every rule, definition or piece of data a candidate needs is given inside the item itself.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate applies the rules given in the item precisely and counts the thing the question actually asks for, rather than the nearest plausible number. Score verbal items for whether the candidate answers from the passage alone rather than from what they know or expect to be true. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a candidate who puts participants first, acts within their remit while being transparent about its edges, addresses problems directly with the person involved, and reports their own mistakes quickly rather than waiting to see whether anyone notices.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking processes this role genuinely demands, using practical workplace materials rather than abstract shapes and number sequences. Rather than asking a candidate to rotate a cube in their head, it puts in front of them the sorts of things an associate user researcher really handles — a recruitment screener, a session schedule, a consent checklist, a set of research notes, a short extract from a research plan — and asks them to reason about them accurately and at a reasonable pace.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of people applying for or working at a similar level.

For an associate user researcher specifically, cognitive assessment is relevant because so much of the early-career value added comes from noticing and from carefulness. Noticing that a participant's answer contradicts something they said ten minutes earlier, that the session schedule has two participants booked into the same slot, that a consent form has not been signed, that a recruitment screener would exclude exactly the users the team most needs to hear from — each of those is a small act of cognition, and each one protects the quality of the research. Nobody is testing whether the candidate has memorised research methods, ethics codes or statistical formulae; every rule needed in the questions below is written into the question, and the task is to apply given information carefully and consistently.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, showing an awareness of the need for careful analysis of research data to produce clear findings. Analysis begins with seeing that three participants stumbled at the same step, or that one page attracts a cluster of complaints.
- **Error checking** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, explaining the importance of working to a user research plan and of following standards for user research.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** again, and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**. Sound deduction is what stops "two people said this" from becoming "users think this".
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile research practices**, describing agile working and the roles in a multidisciplinary digital team.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **User research methods** and to **Inclusive research**, showing awareness of the diversity of users of government services and the need to make services usable and accessible for everyone.
- **Careful reading of instructions** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, describing who stakeholders are and what they find important.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a session schedule**

The team's rule is that every usability session must have a fifteen-minute gap after it. Tomorrow's schedule: P1 09:00–10:00, P2 10:15–11:15, P3 11:25–12:25, P4 13:00–14:00. Ask how many gaps between consecutive sessions break the fifteen-minute rule.

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** P1 to P2 is a fifteen-minute gap, which meets the rule since it says fifteen minutes, not more than fifteen. P2 to P3 is ten minutes, breaking the rule. P3 to P4 is thirty-five minutes, comfortably satisfying it. Exactly one gap breaks the rule, giving B. This tests two habits: calculating each interval separately instead of scanning for something that "looks tight", and reading the rule precisely — a rule stating fifteen minutes is met by exactly fifteen minutes, and a candidate who assumes it means "more than fifteen" will wrongly answer C.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a consent rule**

The team's research standard states: "A session recording may be shared with the wider team only if the participant has given recorded consent for sharing, unless the recording has been fully anonymised and approved by the lead user researcher." A colleague says: "I shared yesterday's recording with the whole team, and the participant did not give consent for sharing." Ask which one must be true.

- A) The colleague broke the research standard.
- B) The recording was fully anonymised and approved by the lead user researcher.
- C) Either the recording was anonymised and approved, or the standard was broken.
- D) The participant will complain.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a conditional rule with a single stated exception, and the facts given do not establish whether the exception applies. Option A leaps to the conclusion that the standard was broken, but the exception route might have been used. Option B leaps the other way, assuming the exception was used, without evidence. Option D introduces something the item never mentions. Only option C is guaranteed in every possible version of events. This tests whether the candidate concludes only what the information actually guarantees — the workplace parallel is direct: before suggesting a colleague mishandled personal data, a researcher would quietly check whether the anonymisation route was used.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition in session notes**

Observation notes from five usability sessions record where each participant hesitated for more than ten seconds: P1 address entry; P2 address entry, confirmation; P3 upload evidence; P4 address entry; P5 address entry, upload evidence. Ask which screen shows the clearest pattern of difficulty across participants.

- A) The address entry screen
- B) The confirmation screen
- C) The upload evidence screen
- D) No pattern can be seen

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Counting the participants affected by each screen rather than the total number of mentions: address entry affected P1, P2, P4 and P5 (four of five); upload evidence affected P3 and P5 (two); confirmation affected P2 alone (one). Four out of five is the clearest pattern, giving A. This tests whether the candidate counts by people rather than by mentions, and whether they recognise that identifying the pattern is not the same as explaining it — the address screen might be badly labelled, or it might simply be the first screen where users have to type something.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation on a research day**

It is 8:45 am, the first session starts at 9:30. Four things need attention: (1) the 9:30 participant has emailed to say they cannot find the video call link; (2) a ticket has not been moved into "in progress" before the 9:15 stand-up; (3) a designer has asked for a prototype review at some point today; (4) the weekly community newsletter has arrived. Ask what should be done first.

- A) Move the ticket so the board is accurate for stand-up.
- B) Send the participant the video call link.
- C) Review the designer's prototype screen while the day is quiet.
- D) Read the newsletter before things get busy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is both urgent and high-impact: a participant who cannot join is a session lost and a gap in the evidence the team needs, the deadline is forty-five minutes away and immovable, and it takes about two minutes to fix. Item 2 fits comfortably before stand-up but after the participant is helped. Item 3 has no stated deadline beyond "today". Item 4 is least urgent. This tests whether the candidate ranks by urgency and impact together — something affecting a real person on a fixed deadline outranks internal tidiness, and internal tidiness outranks undated requests.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking a consent checklist**

Before each session the team requires four things: (i) consent form signed, (ii) recording permission recorded verbally, (iii) access needs noted, (iv) incentive payment logged. Four sessions: P1 has all four; P2 has (i), (ii), (iii) but no incentive logged; P3 has only the consent form and the incentive logged; P4 has all four. Ask how many of the four required items are missing across the whole tracker.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** P1 and P4 are complete. P2 is missing one item (the incentive log). P3 has only two of the four, missing the verbal recording permission and the access needs — two items. One plus two gives three, giving C. This tests whether the candidate counts items rather than participants — answering B would count the number of participants with a gap rather than the number of gaps, and the question explicitly asks for items.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction from a recruitment screener**

A screener requires: used the service in the last six months; aged 25 or over; does not work for the department. Four applicants: A (four months ago, 31, works for a different department); B (last week, 22, does not work for the department); C (eight months ago, 44, does not work for the department); D (two months ago, 25, works for the department). Ask how many meet all three criteria.

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A passes all three: within six months, 31 is 25 or over, and a different government department does not disqualify them since the criterion excludes only "the department" running the study. B fails on age. C fails on the six-month window. D fails because they work for the department, despite meeting the other two criteria exactly at the boundary. Only A qualifies, giving B. This tests whether the candidate reads two boundary details precisely: "25 or over" includes 25 exactly, and "the department" is narrower than "any government department".

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across accessibility data**

Five tasks run with twelve non-assistive-technology users and six screen reader users, completion rates: Task 1 92%/83%; Task 2 83%/17%; Task 3 100%/100%; Task 4 75%/67%; Task 5 92%/83%. Ask which task most clearly needs investigation for an accessibility problem.

- A) Task 1
- B) Task 2
- C) Task 4
- D) Task 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The signal is not a low score on its own but a large gap between the two groups. Differences: Task 1 is 9 points, Task 2 is 66, Task 3 is 0, Task 4 is 8, Task 5 is 9. Task 2's gap dwarfs the rest, giving B. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes a barrier specific to assistive technology from a task that is simply hard for everyone — Task 4 has the lowest overall completion but a small gap, suggesting a usability problem rather than an accessibility barrier.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking against a note-taking standard**

The note-taking standard requires notes to: record what the participant did and said, avoid recording the participant's name, avoid the researcher's own interpretation, and include a timestamp. A colleague's note: "10:14 — Sarah looked confused by the upload button and clearly doesn't understand file formats." Ask how many of the four standards this note breaks.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The timestamp is present, meeting one standard. The name "Sarah" breaks the second. "Looked confused" and "clearly doesn't understand" are interpretation rather than observed behaviour, breaking the third. Because the note substitutes interpretation for behaviour, it also fails to record what the participant actually did or said, breaking the first. Three standards are broken, giving C. This tests whether the candidate checks the note against each standard individually rather than forming a general impression.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving in a session**

Five minutes into a remote usability session, the participant's audio is clear but their screen share has failed twice, and they sound increasingly flustered, with fifty minutes and eight tasks remaining. Ask for the most sensible way to think about this problem.

- A) Keep trying to fix the screen share until it works.
- B) Reassure the participant, stop troubleshooting, and adapt the session to what is still possible — for example, asking them to describe aloud what they see and do.
- C) End the session immediately and rebook.
- D) Carry on with all eight tasks and hope the screen share recovers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The problem should be broken into what has failed and what still works: the screen share has failed; the audio, the participant's goodwill, and fifty minutes of their time have not. Option B protects the person first — reassurance costs nothing — and salvages real value from what remains, since a think-aloud description is genuinely usable data. This tests whether the candidate reasons through the trade-offs correctly: option A puts the tool ahead of the person, option C discards fifty minutes when a workable alternative exists, and option D ignores a live problem.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Deduction about what findings support**

In a study with eight participants, six said they would prefer text message updates over email. Ask which conclusion is best supported by this evidence alone.

- A) Most users of the service prefer text messages to email.
- B) The service should switch all notifications to text messages.
- C) A clear majority of the participants in this study preferred text messages, which is worth exploring further.
- D) Email notifications are unpopular and should be removed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Eight participants is a normal and useful qualitative sample, but it does not support a claim about a whole user population, so option A overreaches. Options B and D convert a preliminary finding into a service-wide decision, and D additionally invents a claim about email being unpopular that the evidence never establishes. Option C describes exactly what the evidence shows and proposes a proportionate next step. This tests whether the candidate states findings at the strength the evidence actually supports.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation with competing pressures**

Supporting a study while the senior researcher is on leave, at 11:00: (1) an 11:30 participant has just disclosed they use a screen reader, unknown when the session was set up; (2) a product manager wants headline findings from last week's sessions by end of day; (3) two of next week's five participants have dropped out and recruitment closes at 17:00 today; (4) yesterday's third session notes are still unwritten. Ask for the most sensible order of work.

- A) 4, 2, 3, 1
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4
- D) 3, 1, 4, 2

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is thirty minutes away and involves a participant's access needs, so it comes first. Item 3 has a hard 17:00 deadline and, if missed, costs the team two irreplaceable participants, so it comes second. Item 2 has an end-of-day deadline within the candidate's control. Item 4 is recoverable a day late. This tests whether the candidate ranks by deadline and consequence rather than by seniority — option C ranks by the product manager's seniority, which is the classic error, since an undated-until-5pm request does not outrank a participant's access needs half an hour away.

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about inclusion**

A team recruits for a benefits service study through an online form on social media and a research participant panel. After three rounds, every participant has been aged 24–45, digitally confident, and in work, while the service is used heavily by people out of work and by people over 60. Ask for the strongest analysis of what has gone wrong.

- A) Nothing has gone wrong; the panel simply reflects who volunteers for research.
- B) The recruitment channels themselves select for digitally confident, socially connected, working-age people, so the sample systematically excludes key user groups and the findings cannot represent them.
- C) The team should run more sessions using the same channels until older and out-of-work participants appear.
- D) The team should stop doing research until a better panel is available.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis names the mechanism, not just the symptom: both recruitment routes require someone to be online, comfortable with forms, and connected to social media or a research panel, so the channels are filtering the sample before anyone assesses a single applicant. This tests whether the candidate locates the bias upstream in the method rather than downstream in chance. Option A mistakes a fixable design flaw for an immovable fact. Option C repeats the flawed method and expects a different result. Option D overcorrects into paralysis when practical alternatives exist — job centres, libraries, charities, community organisations, telephone or face-to-face sessions, and incentives that work for people without bank cards.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate applies only the rules stated in the item, rather than substituting knowledge from their own team's practice.
- Note whether the candidate reads boundary wording precisely — "25 or over" includes 25, "at least fifteen minutes" is satisfied by exactly fifteen.
- Watch for whether the candidate counts the specific thing the question asks for (items, not participants; people, not mentions).
- Score for whether the candidate ranks a crowded schedule by deadline and consequence rather than by the seniority of who asked.
- Note whether the candidate names the systemic mechanism behind a recruitment or data pattern rather than describing only the symptom.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a conclusion that goes beyond what a conditional rule with an unresolved exception actually guarantees.
- Missing when a candidate stops scanning after finding the first problem in an error-checking item, rather than continuing systematically.
- Rewarding a candidate who lets seniority drive prioritisation rather than deadline and consequence.
- Accepting a claim that overstates a small qualitative sample as evidence about a wider user population.
- Missing when a candidate treats a recruitment bias as fixed circumstance rather than diagnosing the mechanism producing it.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles the quantitative material this role actually produces. This is not a maths exam; it measures whether a candidate can read a table correctly, pick out the right numbers from a cluttered display, choose the right operation, and interpret the result sensibly. Most platforms provide a calculator.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a small table, chart or short paragraph of figures, followed by a multiple-choice question. Test designers routinely include figures that are not needed, precisely to see whether a candidate can tell the difference.

User research is full of numbers even when it is not statistical: participant counts, no-show rates, task completion figures, analytics dashboards, incentive budgets. At associate level the numbers are deliberately modest — percentages, fractions, proportions, simple averages, ratios, and reading values from a table — and the techniques that carry a candidate through are equally modest: establish what the question asks, identify the two or three numbers needed, calculate in one clear step, then sanity-check the result.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, showing awareness of the need for careful analysis of research data to produce clear findings.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Analysis and synthesis** and to **Inclusive research**, since comparing completion rates between user groups is how accessibility barriers become visible.
- **Averages** map to **User research methods**, since summarising session length, task time or satisfaction scores is part of reporting a study honestly.
- **Simple budgeting and counting** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, working to a research plan that has numbers in it.
- **Interpreting figures cautiously** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, giving stakeholders a number that is honest about its own limits.
- **Spotting when a number is implausible** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of participants**

A study with 20 participants had 15 complete the main task without help. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 60%
- B) 66%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 15 ÷ 20 = 0.75, or 75%, giving C. This tests basic percentage calculation and whether the candidate sanity-checks with a rough estimate first — 15 out of 20 is clearly three quarters of the way up, so an answer near 75% is expected, catching a slip such as dividing 15 by 25 (giving A) or 16 by 20 (giving D).

**Question 2 (easy) — No-show rate**

24 sessions were booked, 3 participants did not attend. Ask for the no-show rate, to the nearest whole percent.

- A) 8%
- B) 12%
- C) 13%
- D) 15%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 3 ÷ 24 = 0.125, or 12.5%, which rounds to 13%, giving C. Option B is the trap for rounding down or truncating rather than rounding — standard rounding takes .5 upwards. This tests whether the candidate rounds correctly, a genuinely useful figure since no-show rates drive over-recruitment decisions.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a table**

Four tasks, out of 16 participants: A 14 completed; B 10; C 16; D 12. Ask which task had a completion rate of exactly 75%.

- A) Task A
- B) Task B
- C) Task C
- D) Task D

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Three quarters of 16 is 12, which is Task D, giving D. This tests whether the candidate converts the target percentage into a number of people first, which is faster than computing every rate — a habit worth building for timed tests.

**Question 4 (easy) — Simple average**

Five sessions lasted 45, 50, 60, 55, and 40 minutes. Ask for the mean session length.

- A) 48 minutes
- B) 50 minutes
- C) 52 minutes
- D) 55 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The total is 250, divided by 5 sessions gives 50 minutes, giving B. This tests basic averaging, and whether the candidate uses an efficient method under time pressure — working from a guessed middle value (50) and summing the differences (−5, 0, +10, +5, −10 = 0) confirms the mean quickly.

**Question 5 (easy) — Incentive budget**

12 sessions are planned, each participant receives a £40 incentive, and the budget is £600. Ask how many sessions in total the budget can cover.

- A) 12
- B) 14
- C) 15
- D) 18

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** £600 ÷ £40 = 15, giving C. This tests whether the candidate answers the exact question asked, rather than a nearby true statement — the budget's remaining amount after 12 sessions (£120) and the number of extra sessions affordable (3) are both true statements about this budget that a distractor could exploit, and the candidate must re-read the final sentence and confirm their number answers it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing two groups fairly**

9 of 12 non-assistive-technology participants completed a task; 2 of 6 screen reader participants completed it. Ask which statement is correct.

- A) Both groups had similar completion rates.
- B) The completion rate was 75% for the first group and about 33% for the second.
- C) The first group completed 7 more tasks, so the difference is small.
- D) The rates cannot be compared because the groups are different sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 9 ÷ 12 = 75%; 2 ÷ 6 ≈ 33%, giving B. This tests whether the candidate converts to rates rather than comparing raw counts across differently sized groups, as option C does — a gap of more than 40 percentage points is large enough to warrant investigation, connecting directly to inclusive research awareness.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Sessions completed rose from 25 last quarter to 32 this quarter. Ask for the percentage increase, to the nearest whole percent.

- A) 7%
- B) 22%
- C) 28%
- D) 32%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The difference is 7, divided by the original 25 gives 28%, giving C. Option B divides by the new figure (7 ÷ 32), the single most common error in percentage-change items. This tests whether the candidate applies the rule that the denominator is always where the change started.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working backwards from a percentage**

30% of survey respondents struggled to upload evidence, representing 45 people. Ask for the total number of respondents.

- A) 75
- B) 135
- C) 150
- D) 300

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 45 ÷ 0.30 = 150, giving C. This tests whether the candidate can work backwards from a percentage to a total, a skill that matters in real reporting when a stakeholder quotes a percentage and the actual number of people it represents needs establishing.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Ratio in recruitment**

A research plan requires a 2:1 ratio of previous users to non-users, and 18 participants are needed in total. Ask how many should be people who have not used the service before.

- A) 6
- B) 9
- C) 12
- D) 18

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A 2:1 ratio divides 18 into 3 shares of 6; previous users take two shares (12), non-users take one share (6), giving A. Option C answers the reversed question. This tests whether the candidate writes down what each share represents before calculating, and re-reads the final sentence to confirm which group is being asked for.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Choosing the right total**

An analytics report shows 8,000 users started an application, 6,400 reached the evidence upload step, and 4,800 submitted. Ask what percentage of those who reached the upload step went on to submit.

- A) 60%
- B) 68%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 4,800 ÷ 6,400 = 75%, giving C. Option A uses 8,000 as the denominator, answering a different and unasked question about the whole funnel. This tests whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator before calculating — the single most common trap in funnel data, with real consequences for how a finding is communicated.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Task completion rose from 40% to 50% after a design change. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) Completion rose by 10%.
- B) Completion rose by 10 percentage points, which is a 25% increase.
- C) Completion rose by 25 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose by 50%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic difference is 10 percentage points; the relative increase is 10 ÷ 40 = 25%. Option B states both correctly. This tests a distinction that trips up experienced professionals — "completion rose 25%" sounds far more impressive than "completion rose 10 percentage points", and both describe the same change, so being precise about which is meant is part of reporting research honestly.

**Question 12 (harder) — Interpreting an average that hides something**

Six participants took 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, and 33 minutes to complete a task. A colleague reports "the average task time was 9 minutes." Ask for the best comment on this report.

- A) The average is wrong; it should be 5 minutes.
- B) The average is correct, but it is misleading because one very long time pulls it well above what five of the six participants experienced.
- C) The average is fine and needs no comment.
- D) The 33-minute result should be deleted from the data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic (54 ÷ 6 = 9) is correct, ruling out A, but no participant took anywhere near 9 minutes. This tests whether the candidate recognises a mean distorted by an outlier and reports it honestly rather than letting a misleading summary stand (C) or deleting an inconvenient data point (D) — the 33-minute session is probably the most interesting result in the dataset, since finding out what went wrong for that person is the point of the research.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator before calculating, particularly in funnel or comparison items.
- Note whether the candidate sanity-checks a calculated answer against a rough estimate.
- Watch for whether the candidate converts raw counts to rates before comparing groups of different sizes.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes a percentage-point change from a relative percentage change.
- Note whether the candidate flags a mean distorted by an outlier rather than reporting it uncritically.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage change calculated by dividing by the new figure rather than the original.
- Missing when a candidate uses the wrong total as a denominator in a funnel or subgroup calculation.
- Rewarding raw-count comparisons across groups of different sizes instead of converted rates.
- Accepting a reported average without checking whether the candidate considers the underlying spread.
- Missing when a candidate answers a nearby true question rather than the one actually asked.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. User research runs on documents — research standards, ethics guidance, discussion guides, policy summaries, stakeholder emails, accessibility requirements — and getting any of them slightly wrong changes what a researcher does next.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it.

The governing rule is answering only from the passage. A candidate's own knowledge of user research, of GDPR, of what their own team does in practice — all of it must be set aside. Cannot Say deserves particular attention, because candidates avoid it, reaching for True or False to seem decisive, when in reality Cannot Say is very often correct and choosing it confidently is a mark of good reading. Small words carry most of the meaning: "all" is not "most", "may" is not "must", "should" is not "will".

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, following standards for user research as written.
- **Distinguishing statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, since a finding that quietly adds an unsupported inference is a common way research goes wrong.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, finding the one thing a stakeholder actually needs.
- **Understanding qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, since advocacy pitched at the wrong strength damages trust.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **Inclusive research**, noticing that a plan never mentions users with access needs.
- **Following written instructions exactly** maps to **User research methods**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All user research involving members of the public must be recorded in the team's research log within two working days of the session. The log entry must state the date, the method used and whether consent was obtained. Recordings of sessions may be retained for up to twelve months, after which they must be deleted unless the lead user researcher has documented a specific reason for keeping them for longer. Anonymised notes are not subject to the twelve-month limit."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "A session held on a Monday must be logged by the following Wednesday, assuming no public holidays."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two working days from Monday gives Tuesday and Wednesday, so the deadline is Wednesday, and the statement adds the correct caveat about public holidays. True. This tests whether the candidate reads "working days" rather than "days" — exactly the sort of small word that decides these items.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Anonymised notes must be deleted after twelve months."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states directly that anonymised notes are not subject to the twelve-month limit, so the statement is contradicted. False. This tests whether the candidate avoids over-reading in the other direction too — the passage says which rule does not apply, not that the notes may be kept indefinitely.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A recording kept for fourteen months is a breach of the rules."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage allows a longer retention period where the lead user researcher has documented a specific reason, and nothing establishes whether that happened here. Cannot Say. This tests the archetypal Cannot Say structure — a rule with an exception, plus facts silent on whether the exception applies — and catches a candidate who reasons from the general rule and forgets the exception clause.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "The log entry must include the participant's name."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage lists three required items — date, method, whether consent was obtained — and the participant's name is not among them, but a list of requirements does not establish that nothing else is required or that names are forbidden. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate distinguishes "not listed" from "prohibited", a genuinely subtle distinction that appears constantly in verbal reasoning material.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"Recruitment for the pilot study was carried out through an online panel. The team acknowledges that this approach may under-represent people who are not confident using digital services. Two of the twelve participants used assistive technology. The team plans to supplement the pilot with face-to-face sessions in three community locations, subject to funding being confirmed in April."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Twelve people took part in the pilot study."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states that two of the twelve participants used assistive technology, establishing twelve participants in total. True. This is a quick item — a fact stated plainly, tested plainly.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The face-to-face sessions will take place in April."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says the sessions are subject to funding being confirmed in April — a condition, not a certainty, and April is named for the funding decision, not for the sessions themselves. Cannot Say. This tests whether the candidate reads "subject to" as a conditional flag pointing towards Cannot Say.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The team is aware that its recruitment method has a limitation."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The second sentence states the team acknowledges the online panel approach may under-represent certain users, which is exactly being aware of a limitation. True. This tests whether the candidate matches the strength of the statement to the strength of the passage — a stronger claim, such as "the team knows its method excluded digitally excluded users", would shift the answer to Cannot Say.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Fewer than one in five pilot participants used assistive technology."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two of twelve is about 16.7%, and one in five is 20%, so the statement holds. True. This tests whether the candidate keeps their numeric attention switched on inside a verbal reasoning item, identifying the two numbers needed and checking the comparison direction before answering.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"The service standard requires teams to conduct research with users at every stage of a service's life, including after it goes live. Teams should include participants with a range of access needs. Where a team cannot recruit participants with a particular access need, it should record this gap and explain how it will address it. Research findings should be shared openly across the organisation unless they contain personal data or commercially sensitive information."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Research should continue after a service goes live."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The first sentence requires research at every stage, "including after it goes live". True. This tests whether the candidate treats an "including" clause as a deliberate signal the author is covering a case readers tend to miss.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A team that cannot recruit any blind participants has breached the service standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage provides a route through exactly this situation: recording the gap and explaining how it will be addressed. Failing to recruit is not itself a breach; the standard is breached only if the gap is not recorded and addressed. False. This tests whether the candidate reads the whole provision, including the get-out clause, rather than the headline expectation alone.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "All research findings must be shared openly across the organisation."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says findings should be shared openly "unless they contain personal data or commercially sensitive information" — the statement drops the exception and hardens "should" into "must". Because the passage explicitly names circumstances where findings are not shared openly, the statement is contradicted rather than merely unsupported. False. This tests whether the candidate checks an absolute term ("all", "must") against a qualifier the passage actually contains.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Research must be shared openly at all times.
- B) The service standard sets expectations for ongoing, inclusive research, while providing for honest handling of gaps and sensitive material.
- C) Teams should recruit participants with access needs.
- D) Research after a service goes live is the most important stage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A is contradicted by the exception clause. Option C is true of one sentence and ignores the rest. Option D invents a ranking the passage never makes. Option B captures all four elements: ongoing research, inclusion, honesty about gaps, and openness with defined limits. This tests whether the candidate selects an answer broad enough to cover the whole passage and disciplined enough to add nothing.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate answers from the passage alone rather than from their own knowledge of research practice or data protection.
- Note whether the candidate is willing to select Cannot Say when the passage genuinely does not establish a claim.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes "not listed" or "not mentioned" from "prohibited".
- Score for whether the candidate checks an absolute term ("all", "must", "every") against any qualifier the passage attaches to it.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a true but partial detail from the passage's actual main point.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an answer drawn from the candidate's own knowledge rather than from the passage.
- Missing when a candidate avoids Cannot Say and reaches for True or False to seem more decisive.
- Rewarding a candidate who misses an exception clause introduced by "unless" or "subject to".
- Accepting a hardened restatement — "should" read as "must", "may under-represent" read as "excluded" — as equivalent to the original.
- Missing when a candidate selects a true but partial detail as the passage's main point.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level — calibrated to an associate: someone with real responsibility for doing the work well, working under guidance, expected to raise things rather than resolve everything alone. A candidate who unilaterally redesigns a study is overstepping; one who escalates every small decision is under-performing.

Four principles carry most scenarios: participants and users come first; the candidate acts within their remit and is transparent about its edges — attempt, then tell; problems are addressed directly and early with the person involved, rather than going around them; and the strongest options both fix the immediate instance and improve the system that produced it.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Ethical judgement and participant care** map to **Inclusive research** and to the ethical core of **User research methods**.
- **Working within a plan and raising deviations** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.
- **Collaborating in a multidisciplinary team** maps to **Agile research practices**.
- **Handling requests, pressure and disagreement from stakeholders** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Representing findings honestly** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Knowing when to ask** maps to this role level itself, which states plainly that the candidate works with more experienced user researchers and needs guidance and training.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A participant becomes distressed**

Observing a session while a senior researcher facilitates, the participant describes a benefits application made after a bereavement and becomes tearful. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Keep taking notes as normal, since the senior researcher is facilitating.
- B) Interrupt to say sorry and ask whether the participant would like to stop.
- C) Stop typing, stay quiet and attentive, and follow the facilitator's lead — being ready to help if asked.
- D) Leave the call quietly so the participant has more privacy.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two things are true at once: the participant's wellbeing matters most, and the candidate is not the facilitator. Option C honours both — stopping typing is a small act of respect participants notice, and following the facilitator's lead avoids two people trying to manage one moment. Option A treats note-taking as more important than the person. Option B is well-meant but cuts across the facilitator mid-moment. Option D is least effective, since a participant who has just become upset will experience someone silently leaving as a reaction to them.

**Question 2 (easy) — Not understanding the task given**

A senior researcher asks for "a quick affinity map of the last three sessions before Thursday". The candidate has heard of affinity mapping but has never done one. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say yes, then search online and attempt it alone, hoping it comes out right.
- B) Say yes, and add that they have not done one before and would find fifteen minutes of guidance useful before starting.
- C) Say they cannot do it because they have not been trained.
- D) Say yes, and quietly ask another associate to do it with them instead of telling the senior researcher.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The role description names guidance and training as expected at this level, so asking for it is doing the job correctly. Option B combines willingness with honesty and makes a specific, cheap request. Option A risks producing something unusable while hiding the gap. Option C is least effective, declining the growth opportunity entirely without offering anything. Option D avoids awkwardness but leaves the senior researcher with an inaccurate picture and pulls a colleague off their own work.

**Question 3 (easy) — An access need disclosed late**

A participant emails an hour before their remote session to say they use a screen magnifier and may need longer than usual, against a sixty-minute slot with six tasks planned. Ask what should be done.

- A) Run all six tasks faster to fit them in.
- B) Reply to thank them for telling you, confirm the session will go at their pace, and plan to cover fewer tasks if needed.
- C) Cancel and rebook once adaptation has been worked out.
- D) Run the session as planned and note afterwards that it overran.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A participant who volunteers an access need is doing the researcher a favour and taking a small social risk, and the first move should make that feel welcome. Option B removes pressure and honestly accepts the trade-off — depth on four tasks is worth more than a rushed sprint through six. Option A is least effective, since rushing a participant who has just disclosed a need for more time actively harms them and produces poor data. Option C wastes their time. Option D drifts into an overrun without agreeing it.

**Question 4 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

A senior researcher has asked for yesterday's notes to be written up this morning. A delivery manager then asks for help with recruitment instead, calling it urgent. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Do what the delivery manager asked, because they are more senior.
- B) Do what the senior researcher asked and tell the delivery manager the candidate is busy.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, and ask the two of them to agree the priority — offering a view of what is achievable.
- D) Try to do both by working through lunch.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two legitimate instructions cannot both be served, and the conflict is not the candidate's to resolve unilaterally but is theirs to surface immediately and helpfully. Option C does that, and offering a view of what is achievable makes the candidate part of the solution. Option A ranks by seniority, which is the trap. Option B is abrupt and leaves an urgent need unaddressed. Option D is least effective, hiding the conflict and setting an unsustainable expectation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Consent has not been recorded**

About to start a remote session, the candidate realises the participant's consent form is missing from the shared folder and cannot reach the senior researcher, who is in another session; the participant is already on the call. Ask what should be done.

- A) Proceed, and sort out the paperwork afterwards.
- B) Explain the consent points to the participant, ask for verbal consent, record it clearly at the start of the session, and follow up with the written form afterwards.
- C) Cancel the session and rebook.
- D) Proceed without recording, so there is no data to worry about.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Consent is the participant's informed agreement, and the form is only evidence of it. Option B secures the substance, documents it appropriately, and repairs the record afterwards. Option A is least effective, proceeding without consent at all and treating a serious obligation as an administrative afterthought. Option C is over-cautious when a proper solution takes ninety seconds. Option D misunderstands that consent covers taking part, not just recording.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A stakeholder wants a stronger finding than the evidence supports**

Sharing a summary of five sessions with a product manager, three participants struggled to find the "save and return" link. The product manager asks for the summary to say "users cannot find the save function" so it is taken seriously in a prioritisation meeting. Ask what should be done.

- A) Make the change, since the product manager knows how to get things prioritised.
- B) Refuse and send the summary unchanged without further comment.
- C) Keep the finding accurate — "three of five participants did not find the save and return link" — and offer to add a line on the potential impact if the pattern holds.
- D) Ask the senior researcher to have the conversation instead.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The product manager's underlying goal is legitimate; the proposed method is not, since it claims something about all users that five sessions cannot establish. Option C keeps credibility intact while genuinely helping — impact framing is usually what actually moves a prioritisation meeting. Option A is least effective, since inflating one finding makes every other finding negotiable. Option B protects accuracy but forgoes a chance to help. Option D is not wrong but skips a development opportunity the candidate is capable of handling.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Recruitment is excluding people**

The most junior person on the team notices every participant recruited so far for a benefits service study is in work and confident online, because recruitment has run entirely through an online panel. Ask for the most effective action.

- A) Say nothing, since recruitment is not the candidate's responsibility.
- B) Raise it at the next stand-up or with the senior researcher, describing the pattern noticed and asking whether other recruitment routes are possible.
- C) Start contacting community organisations personally to recruit different participants.
- D) Add a note to the final report saying the sample was unrepresentative.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Spotting this pattern is exactly the awareness of the diversity of users this role asks for. Option B converts the observation into action through the right channel, early enough that the study can still be changed. Option A is least effective, since staying quiet means a study about a benefits service systematically misses the people who most rely on it. Option C acts beyond the candidate's remit and could cut across existing arrangements. Option D is better than nothing but comes too late to fix anything.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A colleague's notes contain personal data**

Reviewing shared session notes, the candidate sees a colleague has recorded a participant's full name, employer, and a health detail, against a standard requiring anonymised notes. Ask what should be done.

- A) Post in the team channel that the notes breach the standard.
- B) Edit the notes to remove the identifying details, and tell the colleague privately what was changed and why.
- C) Delete the notes entirely.
- D) Leave them, since it was not the candidate's session and the colleague is more experienced.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Personal data in a shared folder is a live problem that worsens the longer it sits, so acting quickly matters. Option B reduces exposure now and tells the colleague directly and privately so they can correct their practice. Option A is least effective, publicly criticising a colleague while also broadcasting the personal data's existence to a wider audience. Option D lets a data protection issue stand out of deference. Option C destroys legitimate research data and removes a colleague's work without discussion.

**Question 9 (harder) — Pressure to change how a session runs**

Observing their own facilitated session, ten minutes in a policy stakeholder unmutes and starts asking the participant leading questions about a policy option in a way that suggests the desired answer; the senior researcher is not present. Ask what should be done.

- A) Let them continue, since they are a senior stakeholder and it is their policy.
- B) Politely intervene straight away — thank them, explain the need to keep questions neutral, ask them to hold further questions for the debrief, and return to the guide.
- C) End the session.
- D) Message them privately during the call and hope they stop.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Leading questions contaminate the data in real time, and every minute of delay costs more of the session. Option B is firm and courteous — thanking them acknowledges their interest, explaining the reason gives a principle rather than a telling-off, and the debrief offer gives their curiosity somewhere to go. Option A is least effective, sacrificing the session's validity. Option C is disproportionate when a sentence would fix it. Option D is too slow and too quiet.

**Question 10 (harder) — Findings that nobody wants to hear**

After four months building a feature, five of six participants in the first round of testing could not complete the core journey with it, and there are five minutes at the show and tell. Ask for the most effective approach.

- A) Present the positives and mention the difficulties briefly, to keep morale up.
- B) Present what happened clearly and without blame — what participants tried, where they got stuck, what they said — and frame the next step as finding out why.
- C) Present the failure rate starkly to make sure the team takes it seriously.
- D) Ask the senior researcher to present it instead, since it is bad news.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Difficult findings are the most valuable findings, and how they are delivered determines whether they get acted on or defended against. Option B keeps focus on observed behaviour rather than the quality of anyone's work, and ending on a question invites the team into the analysis. Option A is least effective, softening a five-in-six failure rate so four months of effort continues down the same path. Option C is accurate but adversarial. Option D is understandable and deprives the candidate of the experience.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of the candidate's own**

The candidate realises they sent a recruitment email to twelve prospective participants with all email addresses in the "To" field rather than "Bcc". Ask what should be done.

- A) Send a follow-up asking everyone to delete the email, and say nothing further.
- B) Tell the senior researcher immediately, describe exactly what happened, and ask what the organisation's process is for reporting a personal data incident.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone complains.
- D) Quietly correct the process for next time and move on.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** This is a personal data breach, small but real, and organisations have defined processes that may include notifying a data protection officer within a set timeframe. Option B recognises that the decision about what to do next is not the candidate's to make alone, and that being straightforward builds trust quickly. Option C is least effective, running down the clock on any reporting obligation. Option A hides the incident from people who need to know. Option D leaves the incident unreported.

**Question 12 (harder) — Being asked to skip research**

With two weeks until a deadline, a delivery manager says there is no time for research on a new screen and asks the candidate to "just give your opinion as a user researcher instead." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Give an opinion, since it is better than nothing and it is what was asked for.
- B) Explain that opinion is not research, and propose something small that is genuinely feasible — such as three short sessions or guerrilla testing — while being clear about what that will and will not tell them.
- C) Refuse, and explain that proper research takes longer than two weeks.
- D) Agree, and label the opinion as findings so it carries weight.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The delivery manager has a real constraint. Option B respects it while protecting the distinction that makes the role worth having — three sessions reliably catch the worst problems on a single screen, and being explicit about limits lets the team make an informed choice. Option D is least effective, deliberately mislabelling opinion as findings, a straightforward integrity failure. Option A blurs professional judgement into evidence. Option C is technically defensible but unhelpfully absolute.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate puts participant wellbeing, consent or access needs ahead of schedule, budget or convenience.
- Note whether the candidate acts within their remit while being transparent about its edges — attempting what they can and telling the right person about what they cannot.
- Watch for whether the candidate addresses a problem directly and privately with the person involved, rather than going around them or staying silent.
- Score for whether the candidate's answer both resolves the immediate instance and proposes something that reduces recurrence.
- Note whether the candidate reports their own mistake promptly and fully, rather than waiting to see if it is noticed.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that ranks a request by the seniority of who asked rather than by consequence.
- Missing when a candidate either escalates everything or escalates nothing, rather than attempting what is within their competence and flagging the rest.
- Rewarding silence or deference — "it was not my session", "they are more experienced" — over a direct, private, non-accusatory conversation.
- Accepting a delayed report of a candidate's own mistake as equivalent to a prompt one.
- Missing when a candidate inflates a finding's strength to be more persuasive or helpful to a stakeholder.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and careful checking against the everyday materials of a research team — schedules, consent trackers, screeners and session notes; the numeric habits that keep a study's reporting honest — finding the right denominator, distinguishing a percentage point from a percentage, and noticing when a mean conceals an outlier; the discipline of reading a standard or a stakeholder email exactly as written, including its exceptions and its qualifiers; and the situational judgement that defines a strong associate user researcher — putting participants first, working within remit while being transparent about its edges, and reporting mistakes quickly.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and the strongest answers consistently apply only the information given rather than imported assumptions. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
