# Senior Service Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for senior service designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A senior service designer is an experienced designer who works with minimal support, influences and mentors others, works with service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts, may carry responsibility across complex services, helps set direction and embed good practice within teams, and makes important decisions based on research while understanding how that research affects others. This is a role defined as much by judgement and influence as by craft.

Three features shape this material. First, a candidate is accountable for more than their own work — advising teams, critiquing others' approaches, and embedding good practice — making diagnostic reasoning about somebody else's evidence or design logic a core competence rather than an occasional one. Second, the role works with complexity: complex services, complex evidence such as large data sets, and prototyping complex ideas at appropriate fidelity, meaning genuinely competing constraints and evidence supporting more than one reading. Third, the role builds consensus by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions, and works across profession boundaries with policy and technology colleagues — a negotiating role resting on the evidence behind it being unassailable.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate audits somebody else's reasoning or evidence — diagnosing the flaw rather than only producing sound reasoning of their own — and for whether they read a distribution of failures diagnostically rather than treating each instance in isolation. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward independent professional judgement, capability-building alongside problem-solving, and absorbing organisational pressure that should not land on someone more junior.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At senior level the materials are messier and the reasoning less mechanical: evidence supporting two incompatible readings, designs with a flaw that compromises some conclusions but not others, prioritisation problems with no dominant option, and claims made by other people that need diagnosing rather than accepting or rejecting.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

Three things distinguish this section at this level. First, the shift from producing sound reasoning to auditing it — advising teams, helping teams use design hypotheses effectively, and building consensus by asking difficult questions are each a diagnostic act performed on somebody else's thinking. Second, systemic reasoning: when something goes wrong repeatedly, the informative feature is usually the distribution of failures rather than any single failure — concentrated in one place under one condition tells a different story than scattered failures. Third, comfort with irreducible uncertainty: several questions have no clean answer, only a best one, and the discriminating factor is whether a candidate can identify the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence and state its limits.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing evidence and reasoning** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining complex evidence such as large data sets, and helping a team use design hypotheses effectively.
- **Challenging assumptions** maps to **Design communication**: building consensus around a design approach by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions.
- **Systemic and risk reasoning** maps to **Designing strategically**: using risks, opportunities and constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Designing strategically** and **Iterative design**: adapting designs quickly to changes in requirements, priorities or user needs.
- **Diagnosing exclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**: advising teams on inclusive design and delivering ethical services considering users' personal and social context.
- **Reasoning about positions and incentives** maps to **Designing together**: working across team or profession boundaries.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing a design against a standard**

Standard: every transactional page must use words users would use, state what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid requesting held information, offer a route to help, and work without JavaScript. A page: uses internal terminology; says the case will be assessed; gives no timescale; re-requests a held reference number; has no contact route; degrades gracefully without JavaScript. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Internal terminology fails requirement one; "the case will be assessed" satisfies requirement two, minimally; no timescale fails requirement three; re-requesting held information fails requirement four; no contact route fails requirement five; graceful degradation satisfies requirement six. Four unmet. This tests applying a standard as written rather than as it should have been written — and, in a strong candidate's answer, noticing that absences (the missing help route, the JavaScript behaviour) are the systematic blind spot in any audit.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Rule: "A service may enter public beta only with sign-off from the service owner, unless it replaces an existing service on a like-for-like basis and has been assessed as low risk." A programme lead reports: "We entered public beta last month without service owner sign-off." Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) It was a like-for-like replacement assessed as low risk.
- C) Either it was a low-risk like-for-like replacement, or the standard was breached.
- D) The programme should be paused.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** With the exception compound (like-for-like *and* low risk) and the facts silent on whether it applied, only the disjunction is guaranteed; D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true. This tests reading a compound exception precisely, and framing a follow-up as an enquiry rather than an accusation.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Diagnosing someone else's conclusion**

A designer reports: "Users trust the new identity flow — all 18 participants said they were comfortable with it." Sessions were held in a government building, participants came from the departmental panel, and the comfort question was the facilitator's closing item. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) Eighteen participants is too small for a trust measure.
- B) The setting, the recruitment source and the direct closing question all bias in the same direction towards socially desirable responding, so unanimity is evidence about the conditions rather than about the flow.
- C) Comfort and trust are different constructs.
- D) A validated scale should have been used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unanimity is the alarm, and this tests identifying that three conditions compound rather than partly cancel — a panel selecting for people well-disposed to the department, a government building priming deference, and a closing question inviting the expected answer. Option A misdiagnoses validity as size; option D leaves all three biases untouched.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation across services**

Capacity across several services for one thing this quarter: (1) Service A chooses a delivery model next month, committing four years; (2) Service B is live with a persistent 30% drop-off affecting 250,000 users a year; (3) Service C launches in six weeks with designs signed off; (4) Service D is preparing a business case due in nine months. Ask which is strongest, and why.

- A) B, because it affects the most users.
- B) A, because the decision is imminent, commits four years, and design input is the only thing that could still change it — giving it much the highest value of information.
- C) D, because business cases shape long-term investment.
- D) C, because launching untested carries reputational risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Service A scores highest on decision leverage and remaining time, exactly where design shifts the answer rather than decorating it. This tests ranking by value of information rather than current scale (A) — Service C sounds prudent and is weakest, since testing signed-off designs six weeks from launch produces findings with nowhere to go, and Service D is the profile trap.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Systemic risk in a channel decision**

A programme proposes closing a paper channel carrying 11% of a service's 300,000 annual applications, projecting a 25% processing saving. Ask for the most complete risk analysis.

- A) The risk is that 33,000 people cannot apply.
- B) The first-order risk is exclusion of about 33,000 people a year, but the second-order risks matter as much: those users will contact the service by other routes, raising contact centre volume and cost; some will apply late or not at all, shifting rather than removing cost; and if the paper cohort skews towards people with the greatest need, the policy outcome is affected as well as the operational metric — so the saving may be partly a transfer between budgets.
- C) There is no significant risk; 89% of users are unaffected.
- D) The projected saving is probably overstated.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests following consequences beyond the first order — removing a channel does not remove the people who used it, and the final clause is the most important, since a service may be shedding cost by shedding exactly the people it exists to serve. Option C treats a majority as sufficient.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a colleague's synthesis**

A designer presents insight from eleven studies; one headline — "users want proactive notification across our services" — rests on material from three studies, all of services with statutory deadlines. Ask for the most useful feedback.

- A) The claim is invalid and should be removed.
- B) Note that the supporting evidence comes exclusively from deadline-driven services, so the claim over-reaches as written; suggest narrowing it to that service type, or testing whether it holds where there is no deadline, before it informs investment.
- C) Suggest adding more studies.
- D) Approve it; three studies is a reasonable basis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and the claim built on it reaches further than the evidence goes, especially consequential since it informs investment. This tests preserving the finding while correcting its scope — deadline-driven services supply an obvious motivation (risk of missing something) that may not transfer to voluntary or one-off services.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Prototype fidelity for a complex idea**

A new cross-service concept — a single "tell us once" change of circumstances — needs testing for whether it makes sense to users before any build commitment, with three weeks available. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) A high-fidelity interactive prototype of the full flow.
- B) A low-to-mid fidelity prototype focused on the concept and the moments where it touches other services, plus a simple service walkthrough that includes the offline and cross-organisational steps — because the question is whether the concept coheres across the journey, not whether an interface works.
- C) A written concept description discussed in interviews.
- D) A technical spike to establish feasibility first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question is whether a cross-service concept coheres for people, a journey question rather than an interface one. This tests keeping fidelity low enough to change between sessions while including the offline and cross-organisational steps, where a "tell us once" concept actually succeeds or fails. Option D answers a later question in the wrong order.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study under assurance used a prototype whose save-and-return function was broken throughout. Four findings: (i) participants could not locate the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned when interrupted; (iii) sessions took longer than expected; (iv) participants misread the declaration. Ask which are compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; the fault was incidental.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, since inability to save and return plausibly produced both the abandonment on interruption and the extended durations, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A broken save function makes interruption catastrophic rather than inconvenient, directly manufacturing (ii) and plausibly extending (iii); content comprehension findings are largely untouched. This tests scoping precisely rather than wholesale condemnation (A), which teaches teams that admitting a fault invalidates everything.

**Question 9 (harder) — Reading a good number for the problem inside it**

40% of users who abandon at an evidence step return within seven days and complete; the team treats this as a success and focuses on the 60% who do not. Ask for the strongest observation.

- A) Agree; the 60% is the real problem.
- B) The 40% are also evidence of a problem — they are making two or more attempts at something that should take one, at a cost in time and repeat contact — and the returners are a natural experiment: whatever enabled them to come back and succeed may be the mechanism that would help the 60%.
- C) The 40% should be excluded from the drop-off figure.
- D) Send reminders to everyone who abandons.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A returning user is a user who could not finish first time, almost always because a document was not to hand. This tests treating a comfortable number as still needing investigation and identifying the natural experiment inside it. Option C would conceal the phenomenon entirely.

**Question 10 (harder) — Advising a team that is measuring the wrong thing**

A team reports success because average handling time on a form fell from 14 to 9 minutes after a redesign; contact centre calls about the same service rose 30% over the same period. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The redesign succeeded; handling time is the agreed metric.
- B) The two movements are plausibly connected: a shorter form may be shorter because it explains less, pushing users to telephone for the explanation — so the apparent efficiency may be a cost transfer, and the right measure is end-to-end effort including contact, not time on the form.
- C) The contact centre increase must have another cause.
- D) Both metrics should be reported without interpretation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two metrics moving in opposite directions is a prompt to ask whether they are connected, and this tests naming the measurement error underneath: optimising one step while ignoring what happens around it. Option A accepts a metric because it is agreed, how a service gets locally optimised into a worse whole.

**Question 11 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

A year's review across teams shows accessibility problems reached live in four services, all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all designed by experienced practitioners. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the four cases and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by experienced people points at a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what accessibility assurance actually demands and how long it takes, propose a fast route, and remediate the four cases alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the accessibility requirements.
- D) Introduce a mandatory accessibility gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests reading concentrated failure as diagnostic of a process rather than of conduct, while the remediation clause preserves that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unremediated. Option D imposes friction on every compliant service to catch a localised failure.

**Question 12 (harder) — Working across a profession boundary**

A policy team has designed an eligibility rule requiring evidence that around a fifth of eligible users will struggle to obtain, and is defensive about it, having spent months on the policy. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Present the research showing the difficulty and ask them to change the rule.
- B) Establish first what the rule is protecting against — most likely a specific fraud or error risk — then bring evidence on both sides: how many eligible users the current evidence requirement excludes, and whether an alternative form of evidence would address the same risk; and frame the conversation as jointly solving their problem rather than removing their rule.
- C) Escalate to the service owner.
- D) Design the best possible interface for the existing requirement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The defensiveness is information — the productive move changes what the conversation is about, converting an objection into a design question. This tests bringing the exclusion number as the argument (a fifth of eligible users) rather than asking a policy team to abandon their work on evidence alone (A).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate diagnoses the flaw in somebody else's evidence or reasoning, naming what specific conditions produced a result.
- Note whether the candidate reads a distribution of failures diagnostically (concentrated versus scattered) rather than treating each instance in isolation.
- Watch for whether the candidate ranks priority by value of information (decision leverage and remaining time) rather than current scale or sponsor seniority.
- Score for whether the candidate follows a decision's consequences beyond the first order.
- Note whether the candidate scopes a methodological flaw precisely rather than condemning or excusing a study wholesale.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an audit that catches wrong requirements but misses absent ones.
- Missing when a candidate applies a standard more strictly or loosely than its own wording supports.
- Rewarding a reminder or training proposed as the response to a systemic, distribution-level problem.
- Accepting friction added for a compliant majority to address a small number of non-compliant, concentrated cases.
- Missing when a candidate accepts an agreed metric uncritically rather than checking whether it has been locally optimised.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At senior level the emphasis shifts decisively from calculating to interrogating — candidates still need to compute correctly, but the harder items ask whether a figure means what somebody claims, often the question only the candidate in the room is positioned to ask.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided. Distractors are built from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value used as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting, vendor or benchmark metrics compared to internal ones without checking definitions.

Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority — a number in a board paper is treated as fact while a design argument is treated as a view, regardless of the rigour behind either. Senior designers who cannot interrogate a figure confidently lose arguments they should win, and occasionally win arguments they should lose.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Analysing complex evidence** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining complex evidence such as large data sets.
- **Comparing rates between groups** maps to **Designing for everyone**: gaps between groups are the first numerical trace of exclusion.
- **Judging what a measurement can establish** maps to **Evidence-based design**: helping teams use design hypotheses effectively.
- **Quantifying risk and opportunity** maps to **Designing strategically**: scale determines what matters.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**: documenting decisions, risks and unresolved issues.
- **Interrogating others' claims** maps to **Design communication**: building consensus by asking difficult questions.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Across a programme, 36 of 48 participants completed the core task unaided. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 36÷48=75%. This tests basic percentage calculation — and, in a strong candidate's answer, asking the prior question of whether aggregating 48 participants across a programme is meaningful, which requires the studies to have tested comparable tasks with comparable populations.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off against the right base**

A step is reached by 7,200 users and passed by 5,400. Ask for the drop-off rate.

- A) 25%
- B) 33%
- C) 75%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Drop-off 1,800÷7,200=25%. This tests measuring against arrivals, not against those who continued (option B) — if two steps are computed with different denominators, the ranking of where design effort should go can be simply wrong.

**Question 3 (easy) — Disaggregated rates**

Completion: Digitally confident 5,400/6,000; Low digital confidence 1,500/3,000; Assistive technology users 250/1,000. Ask for the overall completion rate and the gap between the highest and lowest group rates.

- A) 60% overall; 50 point gap
- B) 71.5% overall; 65 point gap
- C) 71.5% overall; 50 point gap
- D) 65% overall; 65 point gap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Overall: 7,150÷10,000=71.5%. Group rates: 90%, 50%, 25%; gap 65 points. This tests not mistaking the overall figure for representative of any group — the 71.5% describes no group in the data and conceals that assistive technology users fail three times in four, which is why disaggregated reporting by the groups most likely to be failing matters.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Eleven sessions' task times in minutes: 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 71. Ask which summary is most defensible.

- A) Mean 15.6 minutes
- B) Median 10 minutes, range 6 to 71, with the 71-minute case described separately
- C) Median 10 minutes
- D) Mean 15.6 minutes, range 6 to 71

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean exceeds nine of eleven observations; the median (10) describes the group well but alone conceals a session over four times the typical duration — likely the most informative observation. This tests supplying centre, spread, and explicit treatment of an outlier.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four studies: 10 participants at 50%; 20 at 65%; 10 at 70%; 20 at 80%. Ask for the overall rate across all 60.

- A) 66%
- B) 68%
- C) 69%
- D) 71%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 5+13+7+16=41÷60≈68%. This tests weighting by participant count rather than averaging the four rates directly (66.25%, option A) — and, in a strong candidate's answer, asking whether the four studies tested comparable tasks and populations before combining them.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 100,000 start, 75,000 pass identity, 45,000 upload evidence, 40,500 submit. Ask what proportion of those who passed identity went on to submit.

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 40,500÷75,000=54%. This tests identifying the denominator the question names; the funnel shows losses of 25,000 at identity and 30,000 between identity and upload, against 4,500 at submission.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A benefits claim**

A benefits paper states a redesign raised completion from 55% to 66%, describing this as "a 20% improvement." Ask whether the description is accurate.

- A) No; it is an 11% improvement.
- B) Yes as arithmetic — 11 ÷ 55 = 20% relative increase — but the expression is ambiguous, and the paper should say "20% relative increase" or "11 percentage points."
- C) No; it is 11 percentage points, and calling it 20% is wrong.
- D) No; it should be 16.7%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relative increase (11÷55=20%) is defensible arithmetic; the problem is labelling ambiguity. This tests distinguishing an arithmetic error from a labelling one — requiring both figures as a standing expectation is the cheapest control available, being a rule about documents rather than a challenge to a person.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Quantifying an exclusion**

A service receives 480,000 applications a year, 7% on paper; a programme proposes closing the paper channel. Ask how many people a year would need another route.

- A) 3,360
- B) 33,600
- C) 34,300
- D) 336,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 0.07×480,000=33,600. This tests converting a percentage into an affected-people count — "7% of applications" sounds like a rounding error, "33,600 people a year" does not, and the number enables the next question: how many will telephone, apply late, or not apply at all.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims a 4-percentage-point completion improvement on 900,000 annual starts is worth £4.32 million a year. Ask for the implied value per additional completed application.

- A) £4.80
- B) £48
- C) £120
- D) £96

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 0.04×900,000=36,000 additional completions; £4,320,000÷36,000=£120. This tests reconstructing an implied unit value in seconds to check a business case's plausibility, enabling a specific, answerable challenge that accuses nobody.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A dashboard reports "average time to complete: 12 minutes" for a service the candidate's research shows most users take over forty minutes across several sittings to complete. Ask for the likely explanation and the right intervention.

- A) The dashboard is wrong and should be corrected.
- B) The metric almost certainly measures single sessions rather than end-to-end journeys, so multi-sitting completions are excluded or counted only for their final short visit, and abandoned sessions may be included — ask for the definition and the distribution, and propose an end-to-end measure alongside it.
- C) The research sample was atypical.
- D) The two figures cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Session-based analytics and journey-based reality diverge systematically for any multi-sitting service, always flattering it. This tests naming the mechanism and proposing a constructive intervention rather than picking a fight over a figure that is probably calculated correctly.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A team proposes measuring whether a redesign improved completion using 40 moderated sessions before and 40 after; baseline is 58%, a 6-point improvement considered meaningful. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 80 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 6 point difference on 40 per group is about two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate through live analytics across the full user base, and spending the 80 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 60 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Forty per group cannot distinguish a six-point difference from chance. This tests redirecting to the right instrument for each question — option D is the most damaging at this level, since caveated findings from underpowered comparisons get cited without their caveats, and an organisation that accepts one such comparison will be arguing against the precedent for years.

**Question 12 (harder) — Two metrics moving apart**

Average handling time on a form fell from 15 to 10 minutes after a redesign; contact centre calls about the service rose 28% and completion was unchanged over the same period. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The redesign succeeded on its metric; the call increase needs separate investigation.
- B) The pattern is consistent with content having been removed to shorten the form, pushing users to telephone for the explanation — an apparent efficiency that is probably a cost transfer, with unchanged completion suggesting no benefit to users at all; the right measure is end-to-end effort including contact.
- C) The call increase must have another cause.
- D) Report both metrics without interpretation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three facts fit together, pointing at cost transfer rather than genuine improvement, with unchanged completion the decisive detail. This tests naming the mechanism and the measurement error beneath it — option A accepts a metric because it was agreed, how services get locally optimised into worse wholes.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator and questions whether compared figures are defined comparably before aggregating.
- Note whether the candidate reconstructs an implied unit value to sanity-check a business case's plausibility.
- Watch for whether the candidate insists on disaggregation rather than accepting a single blended rate.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change and flags an ambiguous label.
- Note whether the candidate assesses whether a proposed sample size, or a metric's own definition, can plausibly support the conclusion being drawn from it.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an average of rates that is not weighted by the underlying counts.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a reported aggregate that could and should be disaggregated.
- Rewarding an underpowered comparison reported "with caveats" as an adequate answer.
- Accepting figures aggregated across studies or sources without checking they are comparably defined.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a metric's face value without asking what population or session definition it actually measures.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. In a senior role that runs on documents — service standards, accessibility regulations, policy submissions, evaluation reports, business cases, contracts and the words users say — reading accuracy is the substrate everything else rests on. A misread exception clause becomes a compliance failure across a programme; a misread finding in a colleague's report becomes advice given confidently to a programme director.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to mark True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, and expertise actively costs marks at this level — the more standards and policy documents a candidate has read, the more readily they supply the clause they expect. Cannot Say is the answer experienced practitioners avoid most.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**: ensuring a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing and clearly explaining complex evidence.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: clearly explaining complex problems and ideas to teams or stakeholders outside design.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: using constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**: noticing a framework never mentions access needs has an ethical consequence.
- **Documenting risks and unresolved issues accurately** maps to **Design communication**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "All new public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before entering public beta, unless an exemption has been agreed in writing by the accessibility lead. Where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, the team must publish an accessibility statement setting out the gap and the plan to close it. Services already in live operation must meet the standard within 18 months of this policy taking effect. Internal-facing tools are outside scope, although teams are encouraged to apply the same standard."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Every new public-facing service must meet the accessibility standard before public beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception — a written exemption agreed by the accessibility lead. This tests catching the absolute-term-versus-exception pattern; note the passage carries two distinct mechanisms easily conflated: an exemption route (removing the requirement) and an accessibility statement route (handling an unmet requirement while the obligation remains), material to Question 4.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Internal tools must meet the accessibility standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Internal tools are outside scope, and teams are only "encouraged," not required. This tests catching a soft aspiration alongside hard requirements, misreported in both directions.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A service that entered public beta two years ago without meeting the standard breached the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage never states when the policy took effect, so whether a service entering beta two years ago was covered at the time cannot be determined. This tests checking whether a deadline's reference point is actually given.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "A team that publishes an accessibility statement no longer needs to meet the requirement it describes."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The statement route requires setting out the gap and the plan to close it, presupposing the requirement still stands; the exemption route is the mechanism that removes an obligation. This tests distinguishing a disclosure route from an exemption route — conflating the two is precisely how accessibility statements come to be treated as a permanent exemption in practice.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The evaluation of the assisted digital route found that users completed more slowly than online users and required an average of 2.4 contacts with the service. The evaluation notes that the two groups differed in age, first language and prior use of the service. It did not assess whether completed applications were of comparable quality, nor whether the additional contacts affected outcomes. The evaluation recommends that the programme consider whether additional guidance would reduce contact volume."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Assisted digital users required more than two contacts on average."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** 2.4 exceeds two, stated about assisted digital users specifically. This tests the baseline reading — a claim broadened to "users generally" would overstate the passage.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The assisted digital route causes slower completion."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage names three confounds (age, first language, prior use) that could equally explain the difference. This tests treating declared confounds as a warning against inferring causation — the design consequence differs entirely depending on the actual cause.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "Additional contacts with the service worsen outcomes for users."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states explicitly this was not assessed. This tests resisting the intuitive reading — more contact might mean more help received, not more friction, and a candidate supplying the intuitive direction has substituted a plausible assumption for the text.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "The evaluation recommends providing additional guidance."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evaluation recommends the programme "consider whether" additional guidance would help, including concluding it would not. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X."

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Teams should offer users a choice of channel wherever practicable. A digital channel must not be the only option for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Where a team withdraws a channel, it must assess the impact on affected users and record the assessment; records are reviewed annually by the accessibility lead. Users who need assistance must be able to reach a person by telephone during published service hours."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Users must be able to reach a person at any time."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage specifies "who need assistance" and "during published service hours," both dropped by the statement. This tests catching dropped qualifiers.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A team that withdraws a channel without recording an impact assessment has breached the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The requirement is unconditional. This tests distinguishing a rule with an escape route (Passage A) from an unconditional rule, where failing it straightforwardly is a breach.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "A digital-only design for a general-population service complies with this passage."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The hard prohibition does not bite for a general-population service, but the softer "should offer a choice wherever practicable" has not necessarily been discharged, since practicability is not stated. This tests holding two provisions in mind at once — being out of scope of a prohibition is not the same as being compliant.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Digital services are unsuitable for many users.
- B) Users should be able to reach services through a channel that works for them, with digital-only ruled out where exclusion is likely, withdrawals assessed, recorded and reviewed, and human help available during service hours.
- C) Teams must record an impact assessment when withdrawing a channel.
- D) Users must be able to speak to a person.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary spanning the whole passage without inventing anything — A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement, and C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a passage before concluding what a provision requires, resisting the pull of their own professional expertise.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a disclosure route (an accessibility statement) from an exemption route.
- Watch for whether the candidate resists the intuitive reading where a passage's declared silence points the other way.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes a rule with an escape route from an unconditional one.
- Note whether the candidate recognises that falling outside a hard prohibition's scope does not equal full compliance with a softer expectation.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False from the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate treats a disclosure mechanism (an accessibility statement) as an exemption.
- Rewarding an association read as causation despite a declared confound.
- Accepting "consider whether X" read as "recommends X."
- Missing when a candidate assumes being outside a hard prohibition's scope means full compliance.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. A senior service designer works with minimal support, influences and mentors others, works with service managers and programme directors, may carry responsibility across complex services, helps set direction and embed good practice, and makes important decisions based on research while understanding how that research affects others. The strongest answers involve independent professional judgement, responsibility for other people's practice as well as a candidate's own, holding positions under genuine organisational pressure, and developing capability rather than solving everything personally.

That last point separates this level from a competent practitioner's more sharply than anything else — a service designer who fixes a colleague's flawed approach has solved a problem; a senior service designer who fixes it without the colleague learning anything has done half the job. Four principles carry most scenarios: users come first, specifically users struggling most; protect the integrity of the evidence and of the people whose job it is to protect it; develop capability while solving the problem; and escalate what genuinely requires authority not held, handling everything else.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Building consensus and challenging assumptions** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Advising on inclusive and ethical design** maps to **Designing for everyone**: delivering ethical services considering users' personal and social context.
- **Running and adapting sessions, and coaching others** maps to **Designing together**.
- **Working across profession boundaries** maps to **Designing together**: working with policy and other teams.
- **Embedding practice and mentoring** maps to **Leading design** and **Iterative design**.
- **Documenting risk honestly** maps to **Design communication**: documenting decisions, related risks and unresolved issues.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A designer under pressure from a director**

A mentee reports that a director has asked them to remove an accessibility finding from a board summary because it "muddies the message," and they are unsure what to do. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell them to leave it in and say you said so.
- B) Talk it through so they understand what is at stake, agree together how the finding should be presented — including a constructive framing with proposed mitigation — offer to attend the meeting, and take the request itself up with the director yourself.
- C) Contact the director and instruct them not to make such requests.
- D) Escalate to the head of profession.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the finding survives, and the mentee emerges more capable and less exposed. This tests taking the request up with the director personally — a power differential this role exists to absorb. Option A is least effective, leaving them defending by proxy, teaching nothing.

**Question 2 (easy) — Embedding practice in a resistant team**

A candidate is asked to embed user-centred design practice in a delivery team that has never worked with a designer, whose delivery manager says design is welcome "as long as it doesn't slow us down." Ask for the strongest first move.

- A) Present the service standard's design requirements.
- B) Find the team's nearest consequential decision, offer to answer that specific question inside their sprint, and get the whole team observing sessions — so the value is demonstrated in their terms before more is asked.
- C) Ask the programme director to mandate design involvement.
- D) Run a full discovery to establish user needs properly first.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A team with no reason to believe design repays its cost is convinced by watching a user struggle with something they built. This tests demonstrating value in the team's own terms before asking for more. Option C is least effective, making mandated involvement performed rather than used.

**Question 3 (easy) — A design session producing one voice**

Twenty minutes into a session with senior stakeholders and frontline staff, two stakeholders dominate and no frontline staff have spoken. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it run; the stakeholders know the service.
- B) Change the structure — write first then round the table, or split into pairs — so quieter participants contribute without anyone being told to stop.
- C) Ask the stakeholders directly to let others speak.
- D) Follow up with the frontline staff separately.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The session is producing one perspective when it was convened for several. This tests adapting a session's format to achieve a useful outcome — option A is least effective, wasting frontline staff's time and producing a design based on how senior people imagine the service works.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Advising on work that is not good enough**

A designer in another team, who does not report to the candidate, has produced a service map whose central claim about user behaviour is not supported by the research cited, already shared with stakeholders. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct the record with the stakeholders.
- B) Speak to the designer first, privately and specifically, and agree how the claim should be corrected and communicated to those already relying on it — with a clear next step if they decline.
- C) Say nothing; it is not your team.
- D) Raise it as a general point about evidence standards in the design community.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Going to the designer first is not merely courteous — they may hold context, or the correction lands better from them. This tests including a next step and communicating to those already relying on the claim. Option C is least effective, since stakeholders are currently acting on an unsupported claim regardless of team boundaries.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to predetermine a conclusion**

A programme director says, before work starts, that design work "needs to show the new digital-only channel will work, because that decision is effectively made." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Focus the work on the channel's strengths.
- B) State plainly that the work will report what users experience, and reframe around what they actually need — if the decision is made, the useful questions become who will struggle, what support they will need, and what would signal the change is failing.
- C) Agree, then report honestly and handle the fallout.
- D) Refuse and report the conversation.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The request asks the candidate to predetermine a conclusion, which would make the work worthless. This tests declining the premise without confrontation and redirecting to genuinely useful questions. Option A is least effective, designing to demonstrate a conclusion — a rigged exercise, and rigged exercises surface.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Developing someone whose practice is weak**

A mentee consistently overstates findings — small samples described as "users," preferences reported as needs — despite general feedback raised twice. Ask for the most effective next step.

- A) Rewrite their outputs before they circulate.
- B) Move from general feedback to something structured: work through one of their outputs together, line by line, agreeing what each claim can support; then agree they will apply that test themselves before the next one, and review it together.
- C) Raise it in their performance conversation.
- D) Reduce their responsibility for reporting.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Twice-repeated general feedback failing usually means the person cannot yet see the problem in their own writing. This tests making the abstract concrete and building a portable, repeatable test. Option A is least effective, removing the problem from view while guaranteeing it persists and signalling the candidate does not think they can learn.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A constraint from a policy team**

A policy team requires evidence that about a fifth of eligible users will struggle to obtain, and is defensive, having spent months on the rule. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Present the research and ask them to change the rule.
- B) Establish first what the rule protects against, then bring evidence on both sides — how many eligible users the requirement excludes, and whether an alternative form of evidence addresses the same risk — framing it as jointly solving their problem rather than removing their rule.
- C) Escalate to the service owner.
- D) Design the best possible interface for the existing requirement.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** This tests converting a policy objection into a design conversation by naming the risk the rule protects against and bringing the exclusion number as the argument. Option C is least effective as a first move, escalating a solvable disagreement across a profession boundary the candidate needs repeatedly.

**Question 8 (harder) — An accessibility blocker close to launch**

Two weeks before launch, a key step is found unable to be completed with a screen reader. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with the delivery manager and service owner, describe precisely what cannot be done and by whom, ask for an explicit recorded decision, and offer any interim route that would let affected users complete the journey another way.
- C) Attempt the fix yourself in the two weeks.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** An unrecorded decision drifts; a recorded one makes the trade-off owned. This tests securing an explicit decision and an interim route — option A is least effective, sending a genuine blocker to a backlog where it will remain unfixed.

**Question 9 (harder) — Evidence against a public commitment**

Evidence shows a publicly announced channel closure will substantially disadvantage users without reliable digital access; colleagues propose presenting this as "opportunities for future enhancement." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the framing; the commitment is public.
- B) Report the findings accurately and do the constructive work alongside — quantify who is affected, specify what assisted provision would mitigate it, and present finding and mitigation together so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report starkly and let the organisation respond.
- D) Escalate before acting.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The proposed framing protects the organisation from discomfort while the disadvantage occurs anyway. This tests keeping the finding intact and making it actionable with quantification and mitigation. Option A is least effective, the precise failure professional standards exist to prevent.

**Question 10 (harder) — Consensus that would harm users**

A team the candidate advises has converged on a solution everyone likes, which the evidence indicates will fail the users who struggle most; reopening it reverses a decision people are relieved to have made. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go; the team has decided.
- B) Raise it once, clearly and specifically, showing what the evidence indicates will happen to those users and what mitigation might look like — then if the team proceeds, ensure the risk is documented and propose measuring the outcome after release.
- C) Escalate to the service owner over the team's head.
- D) Keep raising it until the team changes course.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests threading a professional obligation to say the thing against the need to keep advising the same team — raising once with evidence and mitigation, then documenting the risk and proposing measurement. Option A is least effective, since consensus harming the users who struggle most is not a value.

**Question 11 (harder) — A flaw in work you endorsed**

Three months ago, a design decision was endorsed based on research whose screener excluded anyone who had contacted the support desk; the design is now live. Ask what should happen.

- A) Note it in guidance for future assurance.
- B) Tell the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are compromised and which stand, propose targeted follow-up with the excluded group, and review why the candidate's own check did not catch it.
- C) Commission follow-up quietly and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Raise it with your head of profession and ask how to proceed.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A live design is running on a compromised sample. This tests precise scoping, targeted follow-up, and, distinctively at this level, reviewing why the candidate's own earlier check did not surface the flaw. Option C is least effective, concealing a known flaw while a live design proceeds.

**Question 12 (harder) — A recurring problem across teams**

Across three teams advised, design work consistently arrives after build decisions are taken; each team is appreciative and nothing changes. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Work with each team separately to speed up design delivery.
- B) Gather specific examples across all three showing what each late input would have changed, take them to the programme's governance forum with a proposed change to how decisions are sequenced, and secure a director's support for the change.
- C) Raise it in each team's retrospective.
- D) Reduce the depth of design work so it can be delivered sooner.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Three teams with the same problem is a programme-level sequencing issue. This tests gathering evidence across all three and securing sponsorship for a structural change. Option D is least effective, trading the value of the work for its timeliness.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate develops capability in others alongside solving the immediate problem, rather than solving it alone.
- Note whether the candidate absorbs organisational pressure that should not land on a more junior colleague.
- Watch for whether the candidate escalates only what genuinely requires authority not held, having engaged directly with the person who can resolve it first.
- Score for whether an own or endorsed flaw is disclosed promptly, with precise scoping of what is and is not affected, and a review of why the process that should have caught it did not.
- Note whether the candidate looks for the programme-level or systemic version of a problem repeated across multiple teams rather than treating each instance separately.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response where the candidate solves a problem personally without the person or team involved learning anything.
- Missing when a candidate softens a finding to accommodate a prior commitment or public announcement.
- Rewarding mandated practice introduced before value has been demonstrated.
- Accepting a colleague's work corrected by going straight to stakeholders rather than to the colleague first.
- Missing when a candidate treats consensus as a terminal value even where it harms the users who struggle most.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and diagnostic auditing of somebody else's evidence and reasoning across service standards, colleague syntheses, and cross-service concepts; the arithmetic that supports senior-level design argument — disaggregating a blended rate to find the group it conceals, reconstructing an implied unit value to check a business case, and declining to endorse a comparison too small to detect what it was built to find; the discipline of reading service standards, evaluation reports and governance prose at exactly the strength the text supports, especially distinguishing a disclosure route from an exemption route; and the situational judgement that defines a strong senior service designer — developing capability alongside solving problems, absorbing pressure that should not land on someone more junior, and acting on the mechanism behind a recurring problem rather than the instance in front of them.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answers are consistently the ones honest about the limits of the evidence, including where the evidence would support something the candidate wants to be true. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
