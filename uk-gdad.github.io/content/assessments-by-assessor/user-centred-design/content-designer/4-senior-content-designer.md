# Senior Content Designer - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for senior content designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A senior content designer is an expert practitioner responsible for content quality across a team, not just their own pages: developing content strategy for large-scale problems and high-profile events, assuring the quality of colleagues' work through review, leading cross-government content projects, mentoring the content designers around them, writing and mapping user stories, negotiating with stakeholders who want content to say something the evidence does not support, and turning quantitative and qualitative research into confident content decisions.

Three features of the role shape what the assessment is built to test. First, this role is comfortable using evidence to make content decisions, so the assessment tests whether a candidate knows what a piece of evidence supports, not merely whether they can read it. Second, this role influences stakeholders across government, a negotiating skill resting on unassailable evidence and explicable reasoning. Third, this role works across the end-to-end journey, so the unit of responsibility is rarely the page in front of them.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate deduces only what given facts guarantee, checks a claim's attribution before accepting it, and applies given rules exactly as written even where the result feels unsatisfying. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a response that solves the immediate problem, addresses its underlying cause, and preserves the relationships involved — candidates fail in two symmetrical ways at this level: doing the junior's job by quietly rewriting or silently absorbing everything, and floating above the work by noting concerns and deferring decisions upward. Score for whether a response diagnoses before it escalates, and sizes a problem with craft before fighting about it.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes this role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles — content patterns, user story maps, style guide rules, journey diagrams, research findings, and review comments.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

For a senior content designer, cognitive ability is particularly relevant because professional value rests on structured thinking applied to language and journeys — holding an end-to-end service in mind while editing a single sentence within it, checking that a content pattern established in one part of a service has been applied consistently everywhere else, deducing what a change to eligibility rules means for every page that mentions eligibility.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**: establishing content patterns, then testing and iterating them, means recognising recurring structures and instantly noticing when a page departs from the pattern.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **User-centred content design** and **Strategic thinking (content design)**: reasoning validly about what follows when one step, rule, or piece of content changes.
- **Error checking** maps to **User-centred content design**: assuring the quality of content from more junior colleagues means spotting inconsistencies between a page and the style guide, between two pages in the same journey, or between content and underlying policy.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)** and **Agile working (content design)**: rapidly ranking what matters most against user needs and objectives.
- **Problem solving** maps to **User focus (content design)**: formulating hypotheses, gaining insights from research, and making decisions based on findings.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a content pattern library**

A pattern library specifies every "check before you start" page follows: (1) service name as H1, (2) a lead paragraph stating what the user can do, (3) a bulleted list of what the user needs, (4) a green "Start now" button. Four draft pages: (1) H1, lead, list, button; (2) H1, list, lead, button; (3) H1, lead, list, button; (4) H1, lead, list, button. Ask which page breaks the pattern.

- A) Page 1
- B) Page 2
- C) Page 3
- D) Page 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Page 2 inverts elements (2) and (3), presenting the requirements list before telling the user what the service lets them do. This tests spotting a transposed element quickly and explaining why the order matters for users (context before requirements reduces cognitive load) — the difference between correcting work and coaching through it.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a style rule**

Style guide: "Use 'sign in', not 'log in', in all service content unless the content refers to a third-party system that itself uses 'log in'." A page under review says "log in" in a sentence about the department's own tax account service. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The page breaches the style guide.
- B) The junior designer who wrote it does not know the style guide.
- C) The page should use "login" as one word instead.
- D) The sentence refers to a third-party system.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The exception applies only to third-party systems using "log in," which this sentence does not concern. This tests deducing only what the given information guarantees — option B goes beyond the evidence (the writer may know the rule and have slipped), separating "this breaks the rule" from unfounded theories about why.

**Question 3 (easy) — Prioritisation of review comments**

A quality review of a high-profile page, publishing in one hour, finds four issues: (A) "applicants must apply by 31 June" — a date that does not exist; (B) two headings use title case instead of sentence case; (C) a 68-word paragraph would read better split; (D) "utilise" where "use" would be plainer. Ask which to fix first.

- A) A
- B) B
- C) C
- D) D

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Only A is factually wrong in a way that directly harms users — an impossible date creates genuine ambiguity about the real deadline, generating failure demand on a high-profile page. This tests sequencing by user harm — factual accuracy first, then clarity, then style polish — under a fixed time constraint.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Error checking across a journey**

A four-screen journey: Screen 1 says "You'll need your National Insurance number and a recent payslip"; Screen 2 asks for the National Insurance number; Screen 3 asks the user to upload a P60; Screen 4 confirms "We've received your National Insurance number and payslip." The service genuinely requires a P60, not a payslip. Ask how many screens must change.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Screens 1 and 4 both wrongly reference a payslip; Screens 2 and 3 are correct. This tests tracing every screen touching the same fact through an entire journey — the tempting error is stopping after fixing the first mention, since content errors rarely live on a single page.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Logical deduction from eligibility rules**

Rules: (i) applicants 66 or over are eligible; (ii) applicants under 66 are eligible only if they receive a qualifying benefit; (iii) nobody may apply on behalf of another except an appointed representative. A stakeholder proposes: "If you are 65 and do not receive a qualifying benefit, you may still be eligible — contact us to check." Ask whether this is consistent with the rules.

- A) Yes — contacting to check is always allowed.
- B) No — under the rules a 65-year-old without a qualifying benefit is not eligible, so "may still be eligible" is misleading.
- C) Yes — rules always have discretionary exceptions.
- D) Cannot say — the rules do not mention 65-year-olds.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 65-year-old falls under rule (ii) (under 66), and without a qualifying benefit is not eligible — the proposed sentence contradicts this. This tests applying rules to a specific case precisely — option D wrongly treats "not named specifically" as "not covered," when the "under 66" condition covers it explicitly, and C invents unstated discretion.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in user research findings**

Five usability rounds of a benefit application recorded first hesitation points: Round 1 — evidence upload; Round 2 — evidence upload; Round 3 — declaration wording; Round 4 — evidence upload; Round 5 — evidence upload. Budget exists to redesign one step before the next release. Ask for the most defensible reading.

- A) The declaration wording is the priority — it is the most recent kind of problem to appear only once, so it is emerging.
- B) The evidence upload step is the priority — four of five rounds independently surfaced it as the first point of hesitation.
- C) Neither is a priority — five rounds is too small a sample to conclude anything.
- D) Both must be redesigned before release regardless of budget.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four independent rounds converging on the same step is a strong qualitative signal — in usability research, recurrence across independently-run sessions matters more than raw sample size. This tests recognising that small-sample usability testing is designed to surface repeated blockers (C over-applies quantitative sample-size instincts to qualitative evidence) rather than acting on a single occurrence (A) or ignoring a stated capacity constraint (D).

**Question 7 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a mixed workload**

Monday morning, four items: (1) ministerial announcement content publishing Wednesday, with three disputed fact-check sentences arriving overnight; (2) a mentee's first solo journey map, needing review before Friday's show-and-tell; (3) the cross-government content community needs confirmation by tomorrow of a presentation slot; (4) Thursday's backlog refinement needs two epics' user stories drafted. Ask for the most defensible sequencing.

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4 — publication deadline first, then the quick commitment, then the review, then refinement prep
- B) 3, 1, 2, 4 — clear the smallest task first
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — strict order of importance
- D) 2, 1, 4, 3 — developing others always comes first

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is both urgent and high impact; item 3 is nearly effortless but has the earliest deadline and blocks someone else's planning, fitting well as a quick dispatch after the morning's priority; item 2 deserves unhurried attention with a Friday horizon; item 4 fits after. This tests sequencing by impact-weighted urgency rather than task size (B), rigid importance ranking (C, which leaves a colleague waiting on a two-minute answer), or over-weighting one duty (D, mentoring matters, and Friday allows it to be done well).

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a content hypothesis**

40% of users abandon a passport-renewal journey on the photo requirements page. Three candidate explanations: (i) requirements are genuinely hard to meet at that moment (users leave to take a photo and return); (ii) unclear content causes users to give up; (iii) the page loads slowly. Ask which observed evidence would most strongly support explanation (i) over the others.

- A) A high proportion of the abandoning users return and complete the journey within 48 hours.
- B) Session recordings show users scrolling up and down the page repeatedly before leaving.
- C) The page's average load time is 9 seconds.
- D) Users who reach the following page complete the journey at high rates.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Explanation (i) predicts a distinctive signature — abandonment that is really postponement, with the same users reappearing once they have a compliant photo — which A observes and is hard to reconcile with (ii) or (iii). This tests identifying evidence that discriminates between competing hypotheses, since B supports (ii), C supports (iii), and D is consistent with all three explanations and discriminates between none.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling of a content sprint**

A cross-government project, four tasks in a two-week sprint (days 1–10): audit (A), pattern proposal (P), stakeholder walkthrough (W), usability testing (T). Constraints: A must finish before P starts; A takes 3 days, starts day 1; W must happen day 6 or 7 (stakeholder availability) and requires P complete; T requires two full days after W for recruitment, then occupies one day. Ask for the earliest day T can take place.

- A) Day 8
- B) Day 9
- C) Day 10
- D) T cannot fit in this sprint

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A occupies days 1–3; P can complete by day 4 or 5; W runs day 6 (its earliest slot); T requires days 7 and 8 to elapse for recruitment, then runs day 9. This tests working forward through a dependency chain to its earliest feasible point — the tempting error (day 8) misreads "two full days after W" as requiring only one day to elapse.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Error checking a user story map**

A story map for "apply for a fishing licence" contains: (1) "As an angler, I can check which licence I need, so that I buy the right one." (2) "As an angler, I can pay by card, so that I complete my purchase online." (3) "As the Environment Agency, we can collect payment data, so that we meet reporting duties." (4) "As an angler, I can receive my licence by email, so that I can prove I hold one." A colleague says story 3 is wrongly formed. Ask whether the colleague is right, and why.

- A) Yes — user stories must never mention organisations.
- B) Partly — the format is arguable, but the real problem is that story 3 describes an organisational need, which is valid to capture, though many teams would express it as a separate business or compliance story rather than mixing it into the user's journey narrative.
- C) No — all four stories are equally well formed.
- D) Yes — story 3 should be deleted because reporting duties are not user needs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Story 3 follows the syntactic template correctly, so it is not malformed mechanically, but it changes actor mid-map from the angler's journey to an organisational obligation — a real need that should be captured differently, not deleted. This tests calibrated judgement over rule-recitation — option D discards a genuine constraint and A applies dogma rather than craft.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Deduction under conflicting instructions**

Three sources: the style guide says FAQs must not be used; a director emails "the public keep asking the same six questions — put up an FAQ page this week"; user research shows users ask those six questions because the guidance page buries the answers below unrelated content. Ask which conclusion follows most strongly from all three sources together.

- A) The director's instruction overrides the style guide, so publish the FAQ page.
- B) The style guide overrides the director, so refuse and do nothing else.
- C) The research explains the questions: the guidance page fails to surface answers users need. Restructuring that page addresses the director's underlying goal without breaching the style guide.
- D) The research is irrelevant because a decision-maker has already decided.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The director's actual goal is "the public stop asking us these questions," not "an FAQ page exists," and the research identifies the mechanism (buried answers) that removes the cause. This tests reasoning to the intervention satisfying all three sources at once — A satisfies the instruction but not the goal, B treats compliance as the end rather than the means, D abandons evidence-based practice.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Applied problem solving at strategy level**

Leading a strategic content improvement project across 400 pages of business guidance: 60 pages generate 80% of all traffic; 200 pages have not been updated in over 3 years; 25 pages have failed their accessibility audit; the 60 high-traffic pages include 10 of the 25 accessibility failures. A sponsor funds a first phase covering at most 70 pages. Ask for the most defensible scoping.

- A) The 200 outdated pages — age is the biggest category of problem.
- B) All 25 accessibility failures plus the 45 highest-traffic remaining pages.
- C) The 60 high-traffic pages only — maximum reach per page improved.
- D) A random sample of 70 pages to avoid bias.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Accessibility failures are legal and ethical obligations affecting real users now regardless of traffic; the remaining 45 slots are best spent where improvements reach the most users. This tests ranking obligations before reach — option C leaves 15 known accessibility failures unaddressed, A treats age as a risk indicator rather than an impact measure, and D confuses a research sampling technique with a prioritisation decision.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate applies a given rule exactly as written, including its exceptions, rather than adding or omitting conditions.
- Note whether the candidate traces every screen or page touching the same fact through an entire journey, not just the first instance found.
- Watch for whether the candidate identifies evidence that discriminates between competing hypotheses rather than evidence consistent with all of them.
- Score for whether prioritisation reasons from impact-weighted urgency and dependency chains rather than task size or seniority alone.
- Note whether a scoping or resourcing decision ranks legal or ethical obligations ahead of reach or convenience.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a "must be true" answer that is merely plausible rather than guaranteed by the given facts.
- Missing when a candidate stops checking a journey after finding the first instance of an error rather than tracing it through every screen.
- Rewarding a small qualitative sample dismissed for its size when recurrence across independent sessions is the relevant signal.
- Accepting a prioritisation ranked by seniority of the requester or task size rather than actual impact and urgency.
- Missing when a candidate proposes an intervention satisfying only one of several conflicting sources rather than reasoning to one that satisfies all.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures a candidate's ability to interpret, manipulate, and draw sound conclusions from numerical information of the kind this role actually generates. It is not a mathematics exam — the arithmetic rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, averages, and rates — page analytics, completion and drop-off rates, usability testing results, search-term volumes, readability measures, support contact statistics, and content audit figures.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, each built around a table, chart, or short data description, calculator usually permitted. Distractors are engineered from predictable mistakes: using the wrong denominator, confusing percentage points with percentages, averaging averages, or reading the wrong row.

For a senior content designer, numeric reasoning is core professional equipment: evaluating quantitative and qualitative data, collaborating with performance analysts, sizing a content problem before committing team effort, and presenting numbers honestly when leading cross-government projects.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentage and rate calculations** map to **User focus (content design)**: evaluating quantitative data means fluently handling completion rates, drop-off rates, task success rates, and changes in each over time.
- **Interpreting tables and charts** maps to **User focus (content design)** and collaboration with performance analysts.
- **Trend and comparison reasoning** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: leading strategic content improvement projects requires before-and-after comparisons, baselines, and honest attribution.
- **Proportional reasoning about effort and scope** maps to **Agile working (content design)** and **User-centred content design**.
- **Sceptical checking of others' figures** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

12,400 users started an online application journey, 9,300 completed it. Ask for the completion rate.

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 93%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 9,300÷12,400=75%. This tests basic percentage calculation — option D preys on digit-matching ("9,300 looks like 93%"), the classic wrong-denominator reflex worth checking for explicitly.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change in support contacts**

After a guidance page rewrite, related calls fell from 1,150 per week to 920. Ask for the percentage reduction.

- A) 15%
- B) 20%
- C) 23%
- D) 25%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (1,150−920)÷1,150=20%. This tests dividing by the original figure — option D divides by the new figure (230÷920=25%), a report-inflating error worth catching both in a candidate's own work and in others'.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a usability testing table**

Task results: "Find eligibility rules" 6/8 succeeding; "Upload evidence" 4/8; "Check application status" 6/6. Ask which task had the lowest success rate.

- A) Find eligibility rules
- B) Upload evidence
- C) Check application status
- D) They cannot be compared because attempt numbers differ

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rates: 75%, 50%, 100%. This tests converting to rates before comparing — option D wrongly treats differing attempt numbers as a barrier to comparison rather than the reason to convert to rates in the first place.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Task success rose from 60% to 69%. A colleague drafts: "Success improved by 9%." Ask whether this is accurate.

- A) Yes — 69 minus 60 is 9, so 9%.
- B) No — success improved by 9 percentage points, which is a 15% relative improvement.
- C) No — success improved by 6 percentage points.
- D) Yes — percentages and percentage points are interchangeable.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 69−60=9 points; 9÷60=15% relative. This tests correctly labelling both the absolute and relative change, catching a mislabelling that is both a numeracy error and a content quality error worth correcting kindly in review.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average across content types**

Content audit, 300 pages: 200 transactional pages averaging 82, 100 guidance pages averaging 64. Ask for the average quality score across all 300 pages.

- A) 73
- B) 76
- C) 78
- D) 80

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (200×82+100×64)÷300=76. This tests weighting by page count rather than averaging the two group averages unweighted (A, understating the estate's quality by over-representing the smaller, weaker group) — a common dashboard error.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Search-term analysis**

45,000 searches last quarter: "renew licence" 9,000; "contact" 6,750; "forms" 4,500; all other terms 24,750. Ask what percentage of searches are for the top three terms combined.

- A) 40%
- B) 45%
- C) 50%
- D) 55%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (9,000+6,750+4,500)÷45,000=45%. This tests basic proportion calculation, checkable against the remainder (24,750÷45,000=55%, summing to 100%).

**Question 7 (moderate) — Reading-time budget for a review round**

18 pieces to 2i-review before Friday: 10 short (20 min each), 8 long (50 min each), with 2 hours per day available. Ask how many working days are needed.

- A) 4 days
- B) 5 days
- C) 6 days
- D) 7 days

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (10×20+8×50)=600 minutes ÷ 120 minutes/day = 5 days. This tests correctly totalling and converting units before dividing — a calculation underpinning honest sprint commitments.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratio reasoning in research recruitment**

An accessibility-focused research round needs participants in the ratio 2 screen-reader users : 3 screen-magnifier users : 5 no assistive technology, recruiting 30 total. Ask how many screen-magnifier users to recruit.

- A) 6
- B) 9
- C) 12
- D) 15

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 2+3+5=10 parts; 30÷10=3 per part; 3 parts×3=9. This tests the standard ratio method applied to the correct row — the distractors are the other groups' counts, punishing a correct method attached to the wrong row.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Baseline comparison with seasonality**

Tax-credits guidance page views: this January 80,000, February 60,000; last year January 82,000, February 61,500. A stakeholder claims: "Views dropped 25% from January to February — your rewrite in late January damaged the page." Ask what the data actually supports.

- A) The stakeholder is right — a 25% drop is a 25% drop.
- B) The January-to-February drop almost exactly matches last year's seasonal pattern (a fall of about 25%), so the data shows seasonality, not damage from the rewrite.
- C) The rewrite improved the page, because February views are only slightly below last February.
- D) The data proves nothing about anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This year's drop is 25%, last year's drop (before the rewrite existed) is also 25% — an identical seasonal pattern. This tests computing a year-on-year baseline comparison to test a causal claim — a stakeholder's raw drop needs the counterfactual computed before it needs a defence.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Cost per contact avoided**

A £24,000 content project rewrote pages; calls fell from 5,200 to 3,700/month over six months, with analysts attributing 80% to the content changes. Each call costs £6. Ask for the approximate payback period.

- A) About 1.4 months
- B) About 2.8 months
- C) About 3.3 months
- D) About 6 months

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** (5,200−3,700)×0.8×£6=£7,200/month saved; £24,000÷£7,200=3.3 months. This tests applying the attribution step (80%, not 100%) before computing payback — skipping it (option B) is precisely the over-claim challenged in a real benefits review.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Sample size and observed difference**

An A/B test: version A, 4,000 users, 62.0% continued; version B, 4,000 users, 62.4% continued. A performance analyst assesses the difference as not statistically significant; a product manager wants to declare B the winner. Ask for the numerically sound position.

- A) B won — 62.4% is higher than 62.0%, and numbers do not lie.
- B) The observed difference is 0.4 percentage points and the analyst has assessed it as within the range expected by chance; the test has not demonstrated a winner, so decide on other grounds or test a bolder variation.
- C) A won — the older version deserves the benefit of the doubt.
- D) Run the same test repeatedly until it reaches significance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 0.4-point difference assessed as consistent with chance means neither version has been shown to be better. This tests deferring to a statistically sound assessment rather than declaring a winner from noise (A, C) — option D is a known malpractice, repeating a test until noise crosses a threshold manufactures false positives.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Multi-step audit planning**

480 pages: 35% need minor edits (1 hour each), 25% need substantial rewrites (4 hours each), 10% should be removed (0.5 hours each), remainder no action. 60 hours/week available. Ask approximately how many weeks the programme takes.

- A) About 8 weeks
- B) About 11 weeks
- C) About 13 weeks
- D) About 16 weeks

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (168×1)+(120×4)+(48×0.5)=672 hours; 672÷60≈11.2 weeks. This tests computing each workstream separately before totalling, and recognising the rewrites dominate effort (480 of 672 hours from only 25% of pages) — the strategic lever if the sponsor needs the programme shortened.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate correctly distinguishes percentage points from relative percentage change and labels each explicitly.
- Note whether the candidate weights an average by group size rather than averaging group averages unweighted.
- Watch for whether an improvement claim includes the attribution step (what fraction is actually due to the intervention) before being used in a business case.
- Score for whether the candidate defers to a statistically sound significance assessment rather than declaring a winner from a difference within the range of chance.
- Note whether a baseline or year-on-year comparison is computed before accepting a causal claim about a metric change.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage change computed against the new value instead of the original.
- Missing when a candidate averages group averages unweighted rather than by group size.
- Rewarding a claimed improvement that skips the attribution step, taking full credit for a partially-caused effect.
- Accepting a statistically insignificant difference declared a winner.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a causal claim about a metric without computing a baseline or seasonal comparison first.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate understands, analyses, and draws conclusions from written material of the kind this role handles daily — a policy extract, a standard, an email, a research summary, a set of guidelines — followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say, or items testing summary, inference, author's purpose, or clear-writing judgement.

Typical format: online and timed, usually 15 to 25 minutes for 20 to 30 items, with adaptive difficulty common. The discipline being measured is precision: reasoning strictly from the passage, not background knowledge, however expert — a specific vulnerability for a professional reader and writer, whose expertise volunteers answers the passage never gave.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading comprehension of dense source material** maps to **User-centred content design**: making things easier for people to understand starts with understanding the source perfectly.
- **True/False/Cannot Say discipline** maps to **User focus (content design)**: distinguishing what evidence establishes from what it merely suggests.
- **Inference and argument evaluation** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: locating a stakeholder's load-bearing claim and responding to that rather than a caricature.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping** and reviewing the work of others.
- **Summarising accurately** maps to **Agile working (content design)**: keeping a team situationally aware depends on faithful compression.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot Say on a standard**

Passage: "The service standard requires teams to make sure everyone can use the service, including people with disabilities and those who lack access to the internet or the skills or confidence to use it. Teams must provide appropriate assisted digital support that is free to use." Statement: "The standard requires assisted digital support to be free to use."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly in the final sentence. This tests the baseline reading, establishing what full textual support looks like before harder items test the boundary of what is merely consistent with a passage rather than stated by it.

**Question 2 (easy) — True/False/Cannot Say on a policy extract**

Passage: "From April, applicants must provide two proofs of address. Applications submitted without the required documents will be placed on hold until the documents are received. Applications on hold for more than 60 days will be closed." Statement: "Applications submitted without two proofs of address are immediately closed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage describes a two-stage process — hold, then closure after 60 days — directly contradicting "immediately closed." This tests reading a procedural sequence precisely — the difference between "closed" and "on hold for up to 60 days" is the difference between panic and a to-do list for a user.

**Question 3 (easy) — Identifying the clearest sentence**

Ask which sentence most clearly tells users what to do.

- A) "It is a requirement that the completion of the declaration form be undertaken by applicants prior to submission."
- B) "Applicants are advised that declarations, once completed, facilitate submission."
- C) "You must complete the declaration form before you submit your application."
- D) "Prior to the submission process, the aforementioned declaration documentation should be completed."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C addresses the user directly, uses an active verb, states the obligation plainly, and orders the actions chronologically. This tests the transferable checklist — direct address, active voice, accurate modal verb, chronological order — against three officialese variants that each obscure whether the step is mandatory.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Cannot Say discipline against expertise**

Passage: "The department's style guidance recommends sentences of no more than 25 words for body text. Guidance pages reviewed this quarter averaged 21 words per sentence." Statement: "The department's guidance pages this quarter met the accessibility requirements of WCAG."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage discusses sentence-length guidance only, saying nothing about WCAG, which covers far more than sentence length. This tests resisting the pull of expertise (knowing short sentences support accessibility) to answer only from what the passage actually asserts or entails.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from a research summary**

Passage: "In moderated testing, 7 of 9 participants completed the renewal journey. Both participants who failed did so at the identity verification step. Four participants described the confirmation email as 'reassuring'. No participant used the help link." Ask which inference is best supported.

- A) The identity verification step caused the most severe completion failures observed in this round.
- B) The help link is unnecessary and should be removed.
- C) The confirmation email is the journey's greatest strength.
- D) 78% of all users will complete the journey.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Both observed failures occurred at identity verification, making it the site of the only — and therefore the most severe — completion failures within this round. This tests staying inside the data — B overreaches on non-use, C converts a comment into an unsupported superlative, D commits the classic sample-to-population generalisation error.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Author's purpose in a stakeholder email**

Email: "Thanks for the proposed rewrite. I recognise the current page is long, and your user research is compelling. However, the legal position on the discretionary payments is genuinely finely balanced, and the two paragraphs you propose removing are the ones our lawyers drafted after the tribunal ruling last year. Could we meet before anything is changed?" Ask what the writer is primarily doing.

- A) Rejecting the rewrite outright.
- B) Accepting the rewrite subject to minor edits.
- C) Flagging a specific legal risk in the proposed cuts and asking to discuss before publication.
- D) Questioning the validity of the user research.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The email concedes length and praises the research, then pivots on "however" to one specific concern, closing with a request to meet before anything changes. This tests reading a stakeholder email's actual moves rather than its emotional register — neither outright rejection nor acceptance is what is stated.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot Say on data ethics guidance**

Passage: "Research participants must give informed consent before sessions are recorded. Consent may be withdrawn at any time, including after the session has taken place. Where consent is withdrawn, recordings must be deleted within 30 days." Statement: "A participant who gave informed consent before a recorded session cannot later require the recording to be deleted."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Consent may be withdrawn "at any time, including after the session," triggering deletion — directly contradicting the statement. This tests assessing a proposition rather than its confident legalistic register, since content designers meet this trap constantly in stakeholder drafts.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Best summary of a passage**

Passage: "The channel-shift programme aimed to move 60% of licence renewals online within two years. Eighteen months in, 52% of renewals are online. Telephone renewals have halved, but paper renewals have barely moved, remaining near 20%. Analysis suggests paper users are disproportionately older and more likely to live in areas with poor connectivity. The programme board has asked for options rather than an extension of the current approach." Ask for the best summary.

- A) The programme has failed and will be cancelled.
- B) Online renewals are ahead of schedule, so the target will certainly be met.
- C) Progress is substantial but has come mainly from telephone users; paper use persists among older and poorly connected users, and the board wants a changed approach.
- D) Older people cannot use online services.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C preserves the passage's structure — overall progress, its composition, the explanatory analysis, and the board's request. This tests faithful compression that preserves the whole skeleton — A invents failure, B converts progress into unwarranted certainty, D turns a statistical association into a capability claim.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Argument evaluation**

A stakeholder argues: "We should publish the full 40-page policy document as one web page. Users complain they cannot find answers, and search engines index long pages well. Splitting content into many short pages just means more clicks. Therefore one long page will solve the findability problem." Ask for the strongest criticism of this argument.

- A) Policy documents should never be published online.
- B) The argument assumes findability fails because content is split across pages, but the complaints are equally consistent with poor structure, headings, or titling — and one 40-page page could worsen within-page findability even if the page itself is easy to find.
- C) Search engines are not important for government content.
- D) Users never complain about findability.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The argument's load-bearing assumption is a diagnosis — that page-splitting causes the findability failure — which B attacks directly, noting the same evidence is consistent with alternatives the argument never rules out. This tests finding the inference the conclusion cannot survive without, rather than denying something absurd or unrequired by the argument (A, C, D).

**Question 10 (challenging) — Precision with qualifiers**

Passage: "Most departments that piloted the new consent pattern reported fewer user errors. No department reported an increase in errors. Two departments have not yet reported." Statement: "Every department that piloted the pattern reported fewer user errors."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two departments have not reported at all, so it cannot be true that every department reported fewer errors — they have not reported anything. This tests tracking quantifiers precisely — the statement fails on the reporting, not the errors, a distinction that matters because content upgrading "most" to "all" is misinformation with good intentions.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference across two sources**

Source 1 (policy): "The discretionary fund is available to applicants who meet the hardship criteria. Decisions are made case by case; meeting the criteria does not guarantee an award." Source 2 (web page): "If you meet the hardship criteria, you will receive a payment from the discretionary fund." Ask which statement about the web page is best supported.

- A) The web page is accurate as written.
- B) The web page overstates the entitlement: it converts case-by-case eligibility into a guarantee, and should say that those who meet the criteria can apply and may receive a payment.
- C) The web page understates the entitlement.
- D) The two sources do not address the same fund.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Source 1 explicitly says meeting criteria does not guarantee an award; the web page asserts "you will receive" — a guarantee. This tests aligning two sources precisely to catch an overstatement — the most consequential reading task in the role, since overpromising discretionary payments creates real appealable expectations and hurt.

**Question 12 (harder) — Clear-writing judgement with accuracy constraints**

Policy rule: applicants must report a change in circumstances within 30 days, and late reporting can lead to a penalty unless there is good reason. Ask which rewrite is both clearest and accurate.

- A) "Report any change in your circumstances within 30 days of the change. If you report late without good reason, you may have to pay a penalty."
- B) "Report changes promptly. Late reporting leads to a penalty."
- C) "Failure to report changes of circumstances within the prescribed 30-day period may render applicants liable to penalties, absent reasonable justification."
- D) "You might want to report changes within about a month, or there could possibly be consequences in some cases."

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A preserves the concrete deadline, the conditional penalty, and the good-reason exception, in direct plain language. This tests achieving both clarity and accuracy simultaneously — B deletes the deadline and the exception, C is accurate but officialese, D is so hedged it fails users needing to know the deadline is real.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate answers strictly from the passage, marking Cannot Say where professional knowledge would fill a genuine gap.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes False (the passage contradicts a statement) from Cannot Say (the passage is merely silent).
- Watch for whether qualifiers and quantifiers ("most," "may," "not yet") are tracked precisely rather than silently upgraded.
- Score summary items on whether the candidate preserves the passage's full structure and qualifiers rather than a true-but-partial detail.
- Note whether the candidate finds an argument's actual load-bearing assumption when asked for the strongest criticism, rather than a peripheral objection.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate confuses False with Cannot Say, or treats a confident register as evidence.
- Rewarding a silently upgraded quantifier ("most" read as "all," "may" read as "will").
- Accepting a summary that improves on or edits the passage's actual claims rather than faithfully compressing them.
- Missing when a candidate criticises a peripheral detail of an argument rather than its load-bearing assumption.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks a candidate to evaluate possible responses — typically choosing the most and least effective, or ranking options. It measures professional judgement: how a candidate balances user needs, stakeholder relationships, quality standards, team development, and delivery pressure when they conflict, which at this level is most weeks.

Typical format: 12 to 25 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed, scored against a key agreed by subject-matter experts and experienced practitioners. Scoring generally rewards responses that solve the immediate problem, address the underlying cause, and preserve or strengthen relationships, while penalising passivity, avoidance, unilateral aggression, and rule-breaking however well-intentioned.

For a senior content designer, situational judgement is arguably the most job-relevant assessment of the four, drawn from genuine pressure points at this level: a stakeholder demanding inaccurate content, a junior colleague's work needing honest critique, a cross-government project stalling, research evidence arriving at an inconvenient moment, accessibility traded against a deadline, and the recurring senior dilemma of doing the work yourself versus developing someone else.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Stakeholder scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: negotiating, influencing decisions, dealing with challenging situations, and removing blockers while keeping user needs central.
- **Quality and review scenarios** map to **User-centred content design**: assuring quality while coaching and guiding colleagues to improve.
- **Evidence scenarios** map to **User focus (content design)**: advocating for users, championing research, and putting accessibility requirements at the heart of the approach even when inconvenient.
- **Delivery and team scenarios** map to **Agile working (content design)**: iterating under pressure, keeping the team situationally aware, learning openly from mistakes.
- **Leadership and community scenarios** map to **Strategic thinking (content design)** and duties to lead cross-government projects and contribute to the content community.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A stakeholder wants promotional language**

A policy stakeholder asks for a guidance page to open with: "This world-leading, award-winning scheme demonstrates the department's commitment to excellence." User research shows users arrive anxious, wanting to know if they qualify and what to do. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Add the sentence — the stakeholder owns the policy.
- B) Refuse by email, citing the style guide, and publish without the sentence.
- C) Share the research showing what users need first, propose an opening that answers eligibility immediately, and suggest where departmental achievements could live if needed elsewhere.
- D) Add the sentence now and quietly remove it in a future iteration.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** C solves the content problem, addresses the underlying cause (a need for recognition met without harming the page), and preserves the relationship through evidence and alternatives. This tests influencing with research while supporting a stakeholder's mutual needs — option D is least effective, dishonest in both directions: publishing content believed wrong, then removing agreed content without consent, poisoning trust when discovered.

**Question 2 (easy) — A junior colleague's weak first draft**

A mentee's first solo eligibility page has structural problems throughout, though good plain-language instincts; their confidence is fragile and the page is not due for two weeks. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Rewrite it yourself tonight so the team is not embarrassed, and send it back with "made a few tweaks".
- B) Book a session: start with what genuinely works (the plain-language instincts), then work through the structural issues together on a shared screen, and agree what they will revise before you review again.
- C) Return it with "needs restructuring — see the style guide" and let them work it out.
- D) Tell their line manager the work is below standard.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** B is coaching as this role defines it — true specific praise, joint working that shows rather than tells, and a clear next step keeping ownership with them. This tests using a comfortable runway as a genuine development opportunity — option A is least effective, deceiving the colleague about the state of their work, teaching them nothing, and quietly converting a mentor into a bottleneck.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Research contradicts your own design**

Usability testing on a content pattern personally designed and championed finds four of six participants misunderstood the key interaction; the researcher presents tomorrow, and a partner team is about to adopt the pattern. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Ask the researcher to soften the findings, since six participants is a small sample.
- B) Attend, thank the researcher publicly, flag the finding to the adopting team yourself before they hear it second-hand, and schedule an iteration of the pattern with follow-up testing.
- C) Say nothing and let the show-and-tell take its course; the pattern may still be adopted.
- D) Withdraw the pattern entirely and apologise to the programme.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** B models championing user research even when it bites, being unafraid to learn from mistakes in public, and keeping teams situationally aware. This tests treating a pattern as something to establish, test, and iterate rather than defend — option A is least effective, crossing an integrity line by corrupting the evidence base every team relies on.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Accessibility versus deadline**

Two days before a high-profile service launches, an accessibility audit finds error messages fail WCAG (colour-only, no fix guidance); the service manager says "launch anyway, fix it in the first sprint after — the minister has announced the date." Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Accept the decision — the service manager owns the launch call.
- B) Size the fix first: error-message content is your craft, so establish with the developers today whether corrected messages can ship within the two days. Present the service manager with that option, and if it truly cannot be done, set out the user impact and legal risk in writing and ask for the deferred fix to be a committed, dated item, escalating if the risk is brushed aside.
- C) Refuse to be associated with the launch and copy the programme director.
- D) Post your concerns in the team channel and leave the decision to others.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Shrinking the dilemma before contesting it is the senior move — establishing whether error-message content can genuinely be fixed within the timeline often dissolves the standoff, since content fixes are often hours, not weeks. This tests sizing the problem before escalating — option A is least effective, quiet acceptance making the candidate complicit, since "fix it next sprint" without challenge routinely becomes "fix it never."

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-government project stalling**

Leading a cross-government project spanning three departments, one representative has missed three consecutive sessions, and their department's pages are drifting from agreed patterns. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Proceed with two departments and publish the patterns as "cross-government" anyway.
- B) Contact the representative directly and with curiosity: find out what is blocking their participation (capacity, mandate, disagreement), adjust the project's demands if possible, and if the blocker is above their level, agree together who in their department to approach.
- C) Email the representative's director to report the missed sessions.
- D) Wait — they will probably return when their workload eases.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Diagnosing before acting usually recovers the relationship and participation together, keeping escalation available but consensual. This tests removing a blocker through curiosity — option C is least effective, escalation as a first resort over the representative's head, converting a probably-recoverable colleague into a defensive one and teaching the wider community that this project generates complaints.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Conflicting feedback from two seniors**

On a high-profile page, a deputy director wants key figures in a prominent table; a legal adviser wants the same figures embedded in caveated prose. Both have emailed with incompatible instructions and a Friday deadline. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Implement the deputy director's version — seniority decides.
- B) Implement the legal adviser's version — legal risk trumps presentation.
- C) Produce a quick prototype showing caveated figures in an accessible table — captions and footnotes carrying the legal qualifications — and convene both stakeholders briefly to agree it against user needs before Friday.
- D) Split the difference by publishing the table on one page and the prose on another, satisfying each email separately.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** A concrete artefact turns an abstract standoff into a joint review — the two underlying needs (findable, legally accurate figures) are compatible even though the two instructions conflict as stated. This tests using prototyping to arbitrate between stakeholders without picking a loser — option D is least effective, serving neither need, letting the two pages drift out of sync, with neither stakeholder actually agreeing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A pattern of missed accessibility in a team you influence**

The same accessibility issues recur across several designers' pages: undescriptive link text and images without meaningful alt text. Each instance is small; the pattern is not. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Fix each instance yourself during 2i reviews — it is quicker than raising it.
- B) Raise the pattern at the team's next retrospective or crit: show the recurring issues without naming culprits, refresh the "why" with a short demonstration (for example, how a screen reader announces bad link text), agree a team checklist, and follow up in future reviews.
- C) Email the team a link to the accessibility guidance.
- D) Add "accessibility" as a standing agenda item and consider the matter handled.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** A recurring cross-designer pattern is a capability gap, not a set of typos, and coaching with a concrete demonstration changes behaviour where a link or a document does not. This tests recognising a systemic pattern and addressing it at the team level — option A is least effective, hiding the feedback loop and permanently tying quality to the reviewer's personal vigilance.

**Question 8 (harder) — Ministerial pressure to publish early**

A minister's office wants guidance on a new scheme published Monday, three days before rules finalise; the policy team says rules are "95% settled" with two eligibility details still possibly changing. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Publish full guidance Monday using the 95%-settled rules; update later if details change.
- B) Publish nothing until the rules are final — accuracy is absolute.
- C) Publish Monday, scoped to what is certain: what the scheme is, who it is broadly for, when full details and applications open, and what users can do now — explicitly excluding the two unsettled eligibility details, with a commitment date for the full guidance, agreed with the minister's office and policy team.
- D) Publish the full guidance with a disclaimer that details may change.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Scoping content to the certain parts serves users' first questions, meets the minister's office's actual need (a Monday publication), and avoids stating an eligibility rule that later reverses — the specific harm that matters most, since eligibility drives user decisions. This tests recognising which specific uncertainty is highest-consequence and scoping around it — option A is least effective, publishing the exact detail most likely to harm users if wrong.

**Question 9 (harder) — Your improvement, their credit**

A senior stakeholder presents a content overhaul's contact-reduction results as "the policy team's simplification work," without mentioning content design, in front of the team; the stakeholder influences next year's funding decisions. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Interrupt during the meeting to correct the record.
- B) Say nothing — credit is vanity, and the users benefited either way.
- C) Afterwards, speak with the stakeholder privately and without accusation: welcome the shared success, note that the results came from the joint policy–content work, and agree how the collaboration is described in future — then make sure your team hears you name their work, and ensure the content contribution is visible in the programme's own reporting.
- D) Email the board members afterwards with evidence that your team did the work.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** A private, generous correction lets the stakeholder adjust without losing face while fixing the record where it counts (reporting) and letting the team hear their work named. This tests addressing team morale, discipline visibility, and the relationship together without manufacturing conflict — option A is least effective, forcing the stakeholder to choose between losing face and defending their version publicly, worsening the outcome on every dimension.

**Question 10 (harder) — Coaching versus delivery under pressure**

A critical page must be finished by tomorrow; the junior owner's draft needs significant rework and they want to finish it themselves — their rework would take two days with feedback, you could rewrite it in three hours, and the deadline cannot move. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Let them finish it — development comes first, and the deadline will just have to absorb the delay.
- B) Rewrite it yourself tonight without discussion; explain afterwards if they ask.
- C) Pair with them for the three hours: you drive the rework at the pace the deadline requires, thinking aloud so the reasoning is visible; they contribute, question, and afterwards take ownership of the follow-up improvements — with a debrief on what changed and why.
- D) Give them written feedback tonight and let them attempt the rework overnight alone.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Pairing meets the immovable deadline while converting the rework into the richest available learning through visible expert reasoning. This tests refusing a false choice between delivering and developing — option A is least effective, treating a stated immovable deadline as negotiable in favour of one colleague's development, teaching them the wrong lesson about deadlines.

**Question 11 (harder) — Community contribution versus team demands**

Asked to co-lead a three-month cross-government working group; the same week, a delivery manager warns the team's next quarter is the heaviest yet and asks everyone to minimise external commitments. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Decline the working group — the team comes first, always.
- B) Accept without telling the delivery manager; you will manage your own diary.
- C) Discuss it openly with the delivery manager: quantify the real commitment, propose how to bound it (for example, a defined day per fortnight, or co-leading with a deputy who gains development from it), name the benefits flowing back to the team, and make the accept/decline decision together with eyes open.
- D) Accept, then quietly delegate all of the working-group work to a junior colleague.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Treating capacity as a shared planning fact — quantified, bounded, negotiated, with returns made explicit — reconciles both duties rather than sacrificing one. This tests transparent capacity negotiation — option B is least effective, breaking the situational awareness Agile teams run on, converting a reasonable negotiation into a trust problem when discovered.

**Question 12 (harder) — Discovering a factual error after publication**

Analytics reveal a page published three weeks ago states a fee as £115 when the correct figure is £151, a transposition that survived 2i review by a mentee; roughly 40,000 users have viewed the page. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Correct the page immediately and quietly; with luck, nobody noticed.
- B) Correct the page immediately; tell the service and policy teams the same day so contact-centre staff and caseworkers can handle affected users; assess with them whether users who acted on the wrong figure need proactive communication; then run a blameless review of how the error passed 2i and strengthen the check — treating the reviewer's involvement as a team process lesson, not an individual failing.
- C) Ask the junior reviewer to correct it and write up what they missed, as a learning exercise.
- D) Report the error and wait for the policy team to decide whether the page changes.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** B stops the harm, manages the consequences with the teams who face affected users, and fixes the system with a blameless review of why the transposition survived. This tests the full ownership arc — stop, manage, fix the system — option C is least effective, converting a collective process failure into individual punishment dressed as development, delaying the correction and teaching the reviewer that errors near them end in public penance.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether a response solves the immediate problem, addresses its underlying cause, and preserves the relationships involved, rather than achieving only one of the three.
- Note whether the candidate diagnoses before escalating and sizes a problem with craft before fighting about it.
- Watch for whether the candidate avoids both symptomatic failure modes at this level: doing the junior's work personally, and floating above the work by deferring or merely noting concerns.
- Score for whether integrity is held under social or delivery pressure (softened research, concealed publication, quiet non-disclosure) rather than traded away.
- Note whether a process or systemic failure is addressed at the system level (a blameless review, a changed check) rather than through individual blame.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a passive response (waiting, hoping, noting concerns, posting in a channel) that delays action while harm continues.
- Missing when a candidate chooses the "heroic" option — quietly rewriting or absorbing everything personally — which hides problems, blocks learning, and does not scale.
- Rewarding a response that trades accessibility, accuracy, or inclusion for smooth relations.
- Accepting a response that singles out an individual for a failure that passed through a shared process.
- Missing when a candidate optimises for personal blamelessness (a covering email, documented dissent, a decision deferred upward) rather than the outcome.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through cognitive reasoning that traces evidence to decisions that hold — reading a set of findings for the cause that generates them, tracing a fact through every screen of a journey it touches, and distinguishing what research establishes from what it makes plausible; the arithmetic that turns a content argument into a decision — proportions of a real user base, rates against volumes, the difference between a metric moving and users being better served, and the attribution step that separates a defensible claim from an overstated one; the discipline of exact reading — "most" is not "all," "may" is not "will," and the trap that catches senior candidates hardest is the summary that improves on the passage rather than the one actually written; and the situational judgement that defines this level, where the strong answers sit between doing the junior's job and floating above the work — diagnosing before escalating, sizing a problem before fighting about it, and choosing responses that solve, address, and preserve all at once.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — this role's influence rests on being trusted in both directions, and a response that is honest but undiplomatic, or diplomatic but concealing, both fail the standard this level requires. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
