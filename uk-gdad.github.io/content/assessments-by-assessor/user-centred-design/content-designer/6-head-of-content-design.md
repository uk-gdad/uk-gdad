# Head of Content Design - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for head of content design. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A head of content design is an expert practitioner with broad industry experience who defines and assures best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others: developing a strategy for content that meets the organisation's objectives, leading a team capable of executing that strategy, working with senior stakeholders to influence organisational strategy, and prioritising and collaborating with counterpart colleagues across government.

Four features shape what the assessment is built to test. First, this role is accountable for content produced by people it did not train, on services it has not seen, judged from output rather than the thinking behind it — core competence in diagnostic reasoning at a distance. Second, this role owns a strategy and is accountable for alignment as well as quality: content that is well written and pulls against organisational objectives is a failure of leadership. Third, this role influences senior stakeholders who substantially outrank it, resting entirely on unassailable evidence and trusted judgement, since content quality is unusually hard to argue about — everybody reads and most people believe they can write. Fourth, this role builds capability and collaborates across government: the practice left behind matters more than any page assured.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate diagnoses organisational mechanisms rather than instances, and interrogates a quantitative or strategic claim rather than accepting it at face value — several items are deliberately built so a confident answer is wrong where the facts call for establishing a mechanism, a denominator, or a scope first. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a response that absorbs pressure downward from very senior stakeholders, revises an unachievable commitment rather than restating it, and negotiates once on behalf of many teams rather than leaving each to have the same conversation separately.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-profession level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic and strategic — content produced by other people, standards applied unevenly, evidence gathered for other purposes, and prioritisation problems whose units are programmes and shared assets.

Typical format: an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

Three things characterise this section at this level. First, reasoning at a distance — rarely seeing the work itself, only a page, a report, or a claim in a board paper. Second, the interesting questions concern mechanisms — bad content is nearly always produced by an unsettled policy, a sign-off process teams route around, a missing template field, a relationship where nobody feels able to say no, or a recruitment market that has left teams without experienced content designers, and fixing the page leaves the mechanism intact. Third, prioritisation is genuinely hard because nothing dominates, and the discriminating factor is reasoning from what input can still change rather than importance or visibility.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing at a distance** maps to this role level's requirement to define and assure best practice.
- **Cross-service synthesis** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: developing a content strategy requires seeing the estate rather than the page.
- **Systemic diagnosis** maps to leading a team capable of executing a strategy, since capability failures are usually structural.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to this role level's requirement to prioritise and to influence organisational strategy.
- **Diagnosing exclusion** maps to **User focus (content design)**.
- **Reasoning about positions and incentives** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: influencing senior stakeholders begins with understanding what they are protecting.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Standard requires transactional pages to: use words users would use, say what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid requesting held information, offer a route to help, and name the responsible team. A page uses internal terminology, says "the case will be assessed," gives no timescale, re-requests a held reference, has a "contact us" footer link, and names no team. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four requirements fail (terminology, timescale, held information, named team); the other two are satisfied thinly. This tests applying a standard exactly as written — if a footer link satisfies "offer a route to help," the standard needs revising, not the audit result. Auditing repeatedly against a standard known to be too loose is how a standard loses its meaning across an organisation.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Rule: "Content may be published without second review only if it is a factual correction approved by the head of content design." A directorate reports publishing without second review. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) It was an approved factual correction.
- C) Either it was an approved factual correction, or the standard was breached.
- D) The content should be withdrawn.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Only if" makes the approved correction the sole route, giving exactly two branches. This tests recognising the precise logical shape — the approval named here is the candidate's own, so if not personally given, the branch is known without asking; if unsure whether it was given informally, that ambiguity is itself a defect in the process owned by this role.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading the thinking behind a page**

A page under assurance is well written, follows the style guide, and answers its heading clearly — and generates the highest contact volume in the service. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) The page must have a defect the style guide does not cover.
- B) A page that reads well and still generates calls is usually answering a question users are not asking, arriving after an expectation set elsewhere, or correctly explaining a rule too conditional for prose — so the diagnostic step is establishing what callers actually say, which the contact centre already knows.
- C) The contact centre needs a better script.
- D) The page should be rewritten more simply.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The combination — well written, compliant, still generating calls — rules out the obvious explanations. This tests naming the realistic candidates and the cheapest way to distinguish them — authorising a script (C) permanently obscures the underlying content problem by making the calls invisible without stopping them.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

Capacity for one thing this quarter: (1) a new service's core journey content, setting patterns reused across eight services; (2) a live page with steady contact volume for a year; (3) exploratory content work for a programme's next-year plan; (4) a directorate wants a style guide refresh. Ask which is strongest.

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will be reused across eight services, and content input is the only thing that could still shape them — and an established pattern is far harder to change than to set.
- C) Item 4, because a style guide affects everything.
- D) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 carries a compounding multiplier the others lack. This tests weighing decisions by reuse and timing rather than current impact — item 4 is the interesting distractor, since refreshing a style guide teams already ignore changes nothing unless a diagnosis has been made, making it lower-value than it appears.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

A team reports users struggling with a benefit rule; a second service's search log shows the rule's phrase as its top term; the contact centre reports queries about it across five services. Ask for the strongest synthesis.

- A) Three teams have found content problems.
- B) One rule that cannot be understood as expressed is appearing across at least five services — so it is a single content problem to be solved once, with an agreed and properly tested form of words, rather than five teams each writing their own.
- C) The rule should be simplified.
- D) Each team should improve its own page.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Five services struggling with the same rule is one problem, not five, and solving it once produces a consistent, tested explanation maintained in one place. This tests reframing the unit of work at organisational scale — option D produces several inconsistent explanations diverging over time, exactly what an audit finds two years later.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on reasoning**

A lead proposes removing a warning paragraph because eight of ten participants called it "off-putting." Ask for the most useful intervention.

- A) Approve it; eight of ten is a clear signal.
- B) Note that "off-putting" describes a reaction to tone rather than evidence that the information is unnecessary, and that the settling question is whether users who skip the warning go on to make the mistake it exists to prevent — so the finding supports rewriting, not removing, unless separate evidence shows the risk is not real.
- C) Reject the proposal.
- D) Suggest testing with more participants.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Disliking a paragraph reveals tone, not whether the information prevents harm — a separable question. This tests distinguishing a reaction from evidence about need, since "users found it off-putting" is among the most common justifications for deleting exactly the content that protects people, and accepting that reasoning once at this level becomes organisational precedent.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Six teams consistently omit timescales, each with a plausible local reason. Ask for the strongest diagnosis and response.

- A) Remind all teams that timescales are required.
- B) Six teams doing the same thing for plausible local reasons is one systemic cause with six rationalisations; establish which it is — the template lacks a field, teams cannot get a timescale confirmed, or the standard is silent on what to publish when a timescale is uncertain — and fix that, since the remedies are entirely different.
- C) Add timescales to the content checklist.
- D) Accept it; some timescales genuinely cannot be given.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Six teams behaving identically for plausible reasons is the signature of a systemic cause, and each candidate implies a distinct fix — the most likely and interesting being a standard silent on what to publish when a timescale is uncertain, resolved with a form of words. This tests naming realistic systemic candidates rather than the weakest available compliance response (A).

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study recruited entirely through the service's online panel. Findings: (i) participants did not understand a legal phrase; (ii) participants found the journey easy to navigate; (iii) participants completed in under ten minutes; (iv) participants did not notice a footer link. Ask which findings are most compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; the sample is adequate.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are most compromised, because a panel recruited through the online service consists of people already comfortable with it, which flatters navigation and speed — while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension and noticing, less dependent on prior familiarity though not wholly independent of it.
- D) Only (i).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A panel recruited through the service is filtered twice, most affecting ease and speed findings. This tests scoping precisely — option A discards findings that survive and, at organisational scale, teaches the whole organisation that declaring a sampling limitation invalidates the work, an incentive to stop disclosing it.

**Question 9 (harder) — Content that should not exist**

A page explaining how to correct a common form error is heavily used and well rated across four services using the same form component. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The page works and should be maintained in all four.
- B) Heavy use of a recovery page measures how often the upstream defect occurs, and the defect is in a shared component used by four services — so the highest-value work is on the component, after which the page shrinks or disappears in all four at once.
- C) Make the page more prominent.
- D) Each service should maintain its own version.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A recovery page's heavy use measures a failure rate; the shared component means one fix removes the problem from all four services at once. This tests recognising both the symptom-page logic and the multiplied leverage of a shared defect — option D entrenches four separately maintained pages for one shared cause, how content estates become unrationalisable.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of a problem**

Seventeen of twenty services complied with a plain-language review standard; the three that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced content designers. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the three and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: three failures in one directorate under identical pressure by experienced people indicates a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what the review demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the three alongside.
- C) Remind all content designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-publication gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Concentrated non-compliance under identical pressure by experienced people points to a process that does not survive a deadline. This tests investigating and fixing the mechanism while remediating live instances — option D imposes friction on seventeen compliant services to catch three, and this candidate would be defending that gate in two years having forgotten why it exists.

**Question 11 (harder) — Strategy and evidence**

A content strategy commits to "one voice across all channels." A review finds letters, which reach the most excluded users, are produced by a separate team on different standards and could not be aligned within two years. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Restate the commitment and press for compliance.
- B) Treat the finding as evidence the strategy was written without knowing what it committed to, and revise it — replacing an unachievable universal with a sequenced commitment that names letters as a priority precisely because they reach the most excluded users, with a realistic horizon.
- C) Exclude letters from the strategy's scope.
- D) Escalate the letters team's non-alignment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A commitment that cannot be met within two years will be quietly ignored, and every commitment beside it loses force. This tests revising a strategy to say something true rather than pressing an impossibility (A) or excluding the exact users who most need the commitment (C, inverting its purpose).

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your organisation**

Two teams consistently produce strong content, six do not; both strong teams run a weekly session reading drafts aloud. Ask for the most useful inference and action.

- A) The six weaker teams need training.
- B) Reading drafts aloud is a cheap, transferable routine that plausibly explains the difference — go and watch a session to establish what it actually does, then build it into the organisation's standards and expectations rather than attributing the difference to talent.
- C) Recruit more content designers like those in the strong teams.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the six weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reading drafts aloud is free, needs no training, and transfers in a way individual skill does not — but the step almost everyone skips is going to watch first, since the session may be doing something other than its name suggests. This tests identifying and propagating a naturally occurring transferable practice rather than positing an unspecified deficit (A) or misattributing a routine to talent (C).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate applies a given standard exactly as written and identifies the standard itself as needing revision, rather than the audit result.
- Note whether the candidate diagnoses a recurring problem across several teams or services as one systemic cause, not several unrelated instances.
- Watch for whether the candidate scopes a sampling or evidence flaw precisely, naming which findings survive and which are compromised.
- Score for whether prioritisation weighs compounding effect (reuse, precedent, strategic drift) rather than only current visible impact.
- Note whether the candidate revises an unachievable strategic commitment rather than restating or narrowing its beneficiaries.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a "must be true" answer that is merely plausible rather than guaranteed by the given facts.
- Missing when a candidate proposes fixing an instance rather than the systemic cause behind a pattern repeated across several teams.
- Rewarding a sampling flaw treated as either wholly disqualifying or entirely negligible.
- Accepting a response that presses an unachievable commitment or narrows its scope to exclude the users who most need it, rather than revising it honestly.
- Missing when a candidate proposes a reminder or checklist addition to a system where the requirement is already known and cannot currently be met.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At this level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation — computing correctly matters, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims, and in most rooms this candidate enters, they may be the only person who will ask.

Typical format: a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided.

Content quality is hard to argue about because everybody reads, leaving content leadership vulnerable to being treated as taste, and content work is chronically under-resourced because its value is hard to express in numbers other people use. A head of content design who can say "two in five calls ask when something will happen, which is 57,600 calls a year at roughly £6 each" makes an argument a director general recognises.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: a content strategy needs a view of the estate.
- **Quantifying content value** maps to influencing organisational strategy and leading a team capable of executing one.
- **Comparing groups fairly** maps to **User focus (content design)**.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User focus**: a wrong measure misdirects content work across several teams at once.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 6,400 users who answered a feedback prompt, 1,600 said they did not find what they needed. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 1,600÷6,400=25%. This tests basic percentage calculation, and, in a strong candidate's answer, recognising this is a rate among those who answered the prompt, not everyone who saw the page — a figure that gets misquoted as a satisfaction rate at board level if the reporting convention does not correct it.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing pages fairly**

Page A: 60,000 views, 1,800 negative. Page B: 12,000 views, 1,440 negative. Page C: 30,000 views, 900 negative. Ask which page has the highest negative rate, and what the pattern tells you.

- A) Page A, 3%, because it has the most negative responses
- B) Page B, 12%, and Pages A and C sitting identically at 3% establishes a baseline that makes 12% interpretable
- C) Page C, 3%
- D) Page A, 30%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 3%; B 12%; C 3%. This tests converting to rates and recognising that two pages at an identical rate establish a baseline — without it, 12% could be typical for this kind of content, so the baseline is what makes it a genuine finding.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a contact log**

Calls: "when will I hear" 4,800; "what documents" 3,000; "how do I cancel" 800; "fee query" 800; "other" 2,600. Ask what percentage concern timescales, and how many calls a year that is.

- A) 40%, 57,600
- B) 40%, 4,800
- C) 36%, 57,600
- D) 45%, 57,600

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total 12,000; 4,800÷12,000=40%; ×12=57,600/year. This tests computing the total, finding the proportion, and annualising — option B stops at the monthly figure, and the annual figure with its associated cost is what converts a missing sentence into a business case a board recognises.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Word counts of nine pages: 130, 140, 150, 155, 165, 170, 180, 200, 3,000. Ask for the most defensible summary.

- A) Mean 476 words
- B) Median 165, range 130 to 3,000, with the 3,000-word page flagged as probably several pages that have not been split
- C) Median 165
- D) Mean 476, range 130 to 3,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean (476.7) is arithmetically correct and describes no page; the median (165) alone conceals the most informative item — a page eighteen times typical length. This tests supplying centre, spread, and content-specific interpretation together, rather than the median alone (C, burying the interesting item).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four pages' negative feedback rates: 4% on 30,000 views, 10% on 6,000, 6% on 12,000, 8% on 12,000. Ask for the overall rate across all 60,000 views.

- A) 5.8%
- B) 6.5%
- C) 7%
- D) 8%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 1,200+600+720+960=3,480 across 60,000 views ≈5.8%. This tests returning to counts before combining rates — option C is the unweighted mean (exactly 7), over-weighting the small pages against the 30,000-view page that should dominate; and, at this level, re-checking such a total before it leaves the candidate's hands, since a figure attributed to the head of profession is one nobody downstream will question.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before: 2,400 of 6,000 answered a prompt negatively. After: 1,800 of 7,200 did. Ask whether the page improved, and by how much in percentage points.

- A) Yes, by 600 responses
- B) Yes, by 15 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 25 percentage points
- D) No, it got worse

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Before 40%, after 25%, improvement 15 points. This tests converting to rates because totals differ — the raw fall (600) actually understates the improvement, a reminder that raw counts can conceal a genuine win as easily as flatter a false one when reviewing a team's own reporting.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change and interpretation**

Views of a guidance page fell from 100,000 to 75,000 a month after a form was simplified. Ask for the change, and what it most likely means.

- A) 25% decrease, and the guidance is failing to reach people
- B) 25% decrease, which may be the intended outcome — if the form no longer produces the confusion the guidance resolved, fewer people needing it is success
- C) 33% decrease, and the guidance is failing
- D) 33% decrease, which may be success

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (100,000−75,000)÷100,000=25% (C, D divide by the new figure). This tests correctly interpreting the sign of a decline for content that exists to resolve confusion — establishing this with a board before the numbers arrive protects teams from a metric that would otherwise punish them for succeeding.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims reducing contact volume by 50,000 calls a year is worth £300,000. Ask for the implied cost per call, and what it tells you.

- A) £6 per call, a plausible order of magnitude for a short telephone interaction
- B) £60 per call, implausibly high
- C) £0.60 per call, implausibly low
- D) £600 per call

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £300,000÷50,000=£6. This tests reconstructing an implied unit value in thirty seconds to check whether a case is grounded — and, in a strong answer, recognising an understated benefit hurts a resourcing argument as much as an inflated one hurts credibility.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

Journey: 100,000 start, 80,000 reach the evidence page, 60,000 reach the declaration, 57,000 submit. Ask what proportion of those reaching the declaration submitted.

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 57,000÷60,000=95%. This tests identifying the group the question names as the denominator — option A uses 100,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked, and at this level establishing the actual loss points (20,000 before evidence, 20,000 before declaration) in seconds redirects a team's plan in the meeting rather than a quarter later.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that hides a split**

Average time on a guidance page is 50 seconds; sessions show participants either glancing for a few seconds or reading carefully for two to three minutes. Ask for the most useful response.

- A) The average is wrong.
- B) The average is probably correct and uninformative: it sits between two clusters and describes neither, so ask for the distribution — and note that a single edit may serve one group at the other's expense, which is the argument for structure rather than length.
- C) The participants were unrepresentative.
- D) The page should be shorter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A mean landing between two behavioural clusters describes neither group. This tests asking for the distribution before acting — "make it shorter" (D) is the instruction that arrives from someone who has seen only the average, and helps glancers while removing what careful readers needed.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

A page's usefulness score rose from 50% to 62%. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) A 12% increase.
- B) A 12 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 24% relative increase.
- C) A 24 percentage point increase.
- D) A 19.4% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 62−50=12 points; 12÷50=24% relative. This tests labelling both figures correctly, and, at this level, establishing a standing reporting convention requiring both — protecting the whole organisation's content reporting from the accusation that a flattering framing was chosen.

**Question 12 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A directorate proposes 30 moderated sessions before and after a rewrite; baseline 60%, with a 5-point improvement meaningful. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 60 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 30 per group is one or two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users and spending the 60 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 50 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 30 per group cannot reliably detect a 5-point difference from chance. This tests recognising an underpowered comparison and redirecting to the right method — option D is particularly damaging at this level, setting a precedent that content effectiveness can be judged on thirty people, later cited against a team's genuine improvement.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate annualises or otherwise scales a figure appropriately before using it in a resourcing or business case.
- Note whether the candidate weights an average by group size rather than averaging group averages unweighted.
- Watch for whether the candidate reconstructs or sanity-checks an implied unit value in a claimed business case before accepting it.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from relative percentage change and establishes a reporting convention requiring both.
- Note whether the candidate assesses whether a proposed sample size can actually detect the effect it claims to measure.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage change computed against the new value instead of the original.
- Missing when a candidate averages group averages unweighted rather than by group size.
- Rewarding a resourcing or business case claim accepted without reconstructing its implied unit value.
- Accepting a fall in a metric treated automatically as bad without considering the content's actual purpose.
- Missing when a candidate endorses an underpowered comparison as sufficient to detect a stated effect.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. For most disciplines it is a proxy skill; for this one it is close to the job itself, which makes it the section where a candidate should score best and where a settled habit costs most.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say — regardless of professional knowledge, which actively costs marks at this level, since the more governance and policy prose a candidate has read, the more readily they supply the clause they expect rather than the one actually present. Cannot Say is the answer experienced leaders avoid most, particularly where the claim is one they would like to be true — a specific trap for someone who owns a strategy.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **User focus (content design)**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to the core task teams perform and this role sets the standard for.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: knowing what is required versus recommended tells the organisation where it has room.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **User focus**.
- **Reading cross-government prose accurately** maps to this role level's collaboration with counterparts across government.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Organisations must submit an annual content assurance return to the cross-government profession board, unless all content within the organisation has been produced under a centrally managed programme. Returns must confirm that plain-language review was completed where required and list any services where it was not. Where an organisation identifies content published without required review, it must notify the board within ten working days of identifying it, separately from the annual return. Returns are published across government; notifications are not."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Every organisation must submit an annual content assurance return."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An exception exists for organisations where all content was produced centrally. This tests the most reliable pattern in the section, and, in a strong answer, noting the exception says "all," not "most" — a narrow condition that Question 3 turns on.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Notifications of missed review are published across government."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence directly contradicts this — returns are published, notifications are not. This tests reading past where a sentence appears to finish, since the operative distinction sits in a semicolon clause governance prose regularly places at the end.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "An organisation where nineteen of twenty services produced content under a centrally managed programme must submit a return."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where *all* content was produced centrally; nineteen of twenty does not satisfy it. This tests carrying "all" from the exemption clause to a scenario several lines later — partial satisfaction of an exception is non-compliance, a recurring organisational error worth catching before a return is late.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Statement: "Content published without required review must be notified within ten working days of publication."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Notification runs from *identifying* the content, not publishing it — potentially months apart. This tests establishing what starts the clock before anything else whenever a passage specifies a deadline, since misreading a trigger produces either unnecessary alarm or false comfort.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The cross-government review of content assurance found considerable variation between departments. Five departments operate peer review before publication; the remainder rely on individual sign-off. Departments with peer review reported fewer post-publication corrections, although the review did not examine whether their content was more accurate. The review recommends that the profession board consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Some departments do not operate peer review before publication."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests the baseline reading, confirmed by the passage's own description of what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Peer review produces more accurate content."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly states accuracy was not examined, despite reporting fewer post-publication corrections. This tests the specific trap of a claim a candidate at this level would like to be true and might well cite when arguing for a common approach.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common assurance approach."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the board "consider whether" a common approach would be proportionate, not that one be introduced. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X" — a candidate at this level is likely both the recipient of such a recommendation and the person who reports what was done about it.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "Individual sign-off is a weaker form of assurance than peer review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports one difference in outcome while declining to assess accuracy, making no judgement about relative strength. This tests resisting a passage's structural lean (peer review mentioned first, associated with a favourable outcome) rather than its actual assertions.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Content must not be altered to reflect the preferences of stakeholders where doing so would make it inaccurate or materially incomplete. Presentation and tone may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. Where a content designer is asked to alter content in a way they believe breaches this, the matter must be raised with the head of content design, who is accountable for the integrity of published content. Where content cannot be reviewed before publication, the reason must be recorded; records are reviewed annually."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "Tone may be adapted for different audiences."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, and though the statement omits the substance condition, a weaker true statement the passage still supports is True. This tests not confusing "does not say everything" with "says something wrong."

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "A content designer asked to make content less complete must refuse."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes a duty to escalate, not a duty to personally refuse. This tests recognising that an escalation route exists precisely so an individual is not left to refuse alone — a protection this role's accountability is meant to provide.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "Removing any information from a page at a stakeholder's request breaches the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The prohibition is conditional twice over — inaccurate or *materially* incomplete — and tone/presentation adaptation is expressly permitted. This tests reading a doubly conditional prohibition precisely: read as absolute, the clause makes every stakeholder edit suspect and teams begin ignoring it entirely.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Content must never be changed at a stakeholder's request.
- B) The substance of published content must remain accurate and materially complete regardless of stakeholder preference, with tone and presentation adaptable, escalation provided where a designer believes that line is being crossed, accountability located with the head of content design, and honesty required where review cannot happen in time.
- C) The head of content design is accountable for content integrity.
- D) Reasons must be recorded when content cannot be reviewed before publication.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the whole passage while adding nothing. This tests preserving "materially," which option A discards, and every other qualifier — writing a summary that keeps every qualifier and adds nothing is precisely the standard this role defines for others.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads past the apparent end of a sentence to a qualifying clause that follows it (often after a semicolon).
- Note whether the candidate correctly identifies what triggers a deadline in governance prose, rather than assuming the more obvious trigger.
- Watch for whether Cannot Say is used correctly even where the alternative would support a strategic claim the candidate might want to be true.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes a duty to escalate from a duty to personally refuse.
- Note whether the candidate reads a doubly conditional prohibition (two qualifying conditions, e.g. "inaccurate or materially incomplete") at its actual narrow scope.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate treats partial satisfaction of an exception ("all," "every") as sufficient.
- Rewarding a conditional prohibition read as absolute, or a proxy outcome (fewer corrections) read as the underlying goal (greater accuracy).
- Accepting a response to a passage's implied stance rather than what it actually asserts.
- Missing when a candidate marks True on a claim that happens to support a strategic position they favour.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do. It measures whether judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. This role defines and assures best practice, develops a content strategy meeting organisational objectives, leads a team capable of executing it, works with senior stakeholders to influence organisational strategy, and collaborates with counterparts across government.

Three features distinguish this level: almost nothing is executed personally, so an option solving a problem through personal intervention is usually second-best; content leadership is chronically vulnerable to being treated as taste, making evidence the primary instrument; and this role absorbs pressure downwards — when a director general leans on a content designer, that designer should not be holding the line alone.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Defining and assuring best practice** maps to this role level's core accountability.
- **Developing and defending a strategy** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)** and influencing organisational strategy.
- **Leading a capable team** maps to this role level's requirement to lead a team able to execute the strategy, and to **Agile working (content design)**.
- **Influence with senior stakeholders** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Protecting users** maps to **User focus (content design)** and **User-centred content design**.
- **Cross-government collaboration** maps to this role level's named prioritisation and collaboration with counterparts.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A content designer under pressure**

A lead reports a director general has asked a content designer to remove a warning from a page because it "undermines the message." Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Tell the lead to instruct their designer to refuse.
- B) Agree with the lead how the warning should be presented — including whether the tone can change while the substance stays — and take the conversation with the director general yourself, since a request of that kind at that level is a matter for you.
- C) Contact the director general and cite the standard.
- D) Approve the removal.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Considering whether tone can change while substance stays often resolves this, and taking the conversation personally is what absorbing a power differential of this size means. This tests protecting content that protects users while resolving the underlying objection — option D is least effective, removing protective content because of who asked, establishing that requests at that level succeed.

**Question 2 (easy) — A strategy nobody uses**

An inherited content strategy, thorough and two years old, is not referred to by teams. Ask for the strongest and least effective approach.

- A) Republish it with a message from you.
- B) Find out why it is not used — most likely that it describes principles rather than decisions, or commits to things nobody resourced, or was written without the teams — and rewrite it around the decisions teams actually face, with the people who face them.
- C) Introduce compliance reporting against the strategy.
- D) Run sessions explaining the strategy.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A strategy nobody refers to has already failed, and rewriting around actual decisions produces a document people open because it answers something. This tests diagnosing before rewriting — option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act while changing nothing.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for the team**

Content designers embedded one per service across nine services, pulled into delivery firefighting, no time for critique, rarely see each other; three have left in eight months. Ask for the strongest and least effective response.

- A) Discuss workload with each delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as structural: establish a protected routine bringing content designers together, negotiate the time with delivery leadership by showing what the pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how people are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Single embedding removes the discipline's most effective quality mechanism — a peer to read drafts — damaging both people and work. This tests addressing a structural pattern on multiple fronts — option D reproduces the attrition on a lag in a competitive market where reputation as an employer travels fast.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate not managed by the candidate has published a live page with a wrong eligibility claim. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Correct it yourself.
- B) Contact the directorate's content lead immediately with the specific error and the evidence, agree who corrects it and by when, and check whether the claim appears elsewhere — while making clear a live eligibility error needs fixing today.
- C) Raise it at the content community.
- D) Report it to the director.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Going directly to the people who can fix it, with an agreed deadline, addresses the actual harm speedily. This tests moving quickly through the right people — option C is least effective, deferring a live error to the wrong forum while broadcasting a team's mistake before they can fix it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A stakeholder who will not accept evidence**

A director general dismisses research showing users don't understand a key term, saying participants were unrepresentative and the term is standard in the sector. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Concede and keep the term.
- B) Acknowledge the sample's limits honestly, point out that a term being standard among practitioners says nothing about the public and is if anything weak evidence the other way, and propose a check they will accept — the site search log, showing whether users search for that term or a different one.
- C) Insist on the change.
- D) Escalate.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Separating the two claims of different quality and offering acceptable evidence converts disagreement into a resolvable question. This tests distinguishing a fair objection from a weak one at very senior level — option A is least effective, conceding to an objection that does not survive examination and teaching a senior stakeholder that it works.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capability you cannot buy**

Experienced content designers cannot be recruited; posts stay vacant for months, services covered by people working beyond their level. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Keep advertising and escalate the recruitment difficulty.
- B) Treat it as capability design rather than recruitment: deploy experienced content designers across services rather than embedding singly, invest in structured development for the people you have, and build entry routes — apprenticeships, secondments, conversion from adjacent roles — while continuing to recruit.
- C) Lower the stated experience requirements.
- D) Use contractors.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Redeploying scarce expertise across services makes it go further and gives less experienced designers someone to read their drafts, the mechanism that develops them. This tests redesigning around a market constraint rather than recruiting harder — option C is least effective, setting people up to fail without changing the support structure.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Strategy meets reality**

A strategy commits to "one voice across all channels"; a review finds letters, reaching the most excluded users, are produced separately and could not align within two years. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Restate the commitment and press for compliance.
- B) Revise the strategy: replace an unachievable universal with a sequenced commitment naming letters as the priority precisely because they reach the most excluded users, with a realistic horizon and the resource it would need.
- C) Exclude letters from scope.
- D) Escalate the letters team's non-alignment.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Sequencing by exclusion-reach makes letters the highest priority rather than the awkward exception, and naming the resource makes it a proposal rather than an aspiration. This tests revising an unachievable commitment honestly — option C is least effective, solving the embarrassment by excluding the users who most need the commitment.

**Question 8 (harder) — Content that may cause harm**

A page across four services omits that applying may reduce a benefit already received; correcting it needs policy sign-off. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Add it to the content backlog for the four services.
- B) Raise it immediately with the relevant service owners, stating specifically that users may reduce their income without knowing, ask for an explicit recorded decision on whether the services continue unchanged meanwhile, and have your team draft the wording for policy so sign-off is as fast as possible.
- C) Instruct the four teams to add a sentence based on their understanding.
- D) Raise it at the next governance board.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Drafting wording for policy to approve removes the commonest cause of delay, and asking for a recorded decision ensures the risk is knowingly held rather than drifting. This tests raising a specific, actionable, multi-service financial-harm concern — option C is least effective, instructing four teams to publish unapproved accounts of a benefit interaction and normalising the practice.

**Question 9 (harder) — A recurring problem across teams**

Content across six teams consistently omits timescales, each with a plausible local reason. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Add timescales to the content checklist.
- B) Establish the common cause — most likely that the standard is silent on what to publish when a timescale is uncertain — and fix it by agreeing a form of words such as "we usually reply within X and will tell you if it will take longer", then negotiating once, on all six teams' behalf, for the figures.
- C) Remind the six teams.
- D) Accept it; some timescales cannot be given.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Supplying the wording that makes an uncertain timescale publishable, and negotiating once with policy on everyone's behalf, is a leadership-level fix a single content designer cannot deliver. This tests identifying and fixing a shared cause once — option C is least effective, the weakest available response to any compliance problem.

**Question 10 (harder) — Making the case at a board**

A board is considering halving content design resource, believing content is writing that can be absorbed by other roles. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Explain the discipline and what content designers do.
- B) Lead with evidence in the board's currency — contact volume attributable to content gaps, calls avoided by specific past changes, the cost per call — then set out what halving the resource would predictably cost, tying each claim to a measurable outcome and naming what you would stop doing.
- C) Point to the service standard's requirements.
- D) Ask the director general to intervene.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A board that believes content is writing has already discounted the premise, and naming what would stop signals an honest trade-off rather than a defensive brief. This tests leading with numbers a board already uses — option A is least effective for feeling natural, confirming the board's view that this is a craft preference.

**Question 11 (harder) — A failure in something you approved**

A form of words approved six months ago, used across six services, is found to misstate a deadline in the two services with a different statutory period. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Note it for the next review.
- B) Notify all six now, scope precisely which two are affected and which four are not, correct the two immediately, and examine why the approval did not ask whether the deadline varied — then change what the approval process requires.
- C) Amend the wording quietly in the two affected services.
- D) Withdraw the agreed wording from all six.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Precise scoping matters as much as disclosure, since four services are correct and should not be told otherwise. This tests fixing both the instance and the process that let it through — option C is least effective, correcting quietly and leaving the approval process's blind spot for the next shared asset.

**Question 12 (harder) — Cross-government collaboration**

Three departments each explain the same common benefit rule differently, and users move between all three, with no authority over the other two. Ask for the strongest and least effective response.

- A) Fix your own department's version.
- B) Convene the three departments' heads of content, establish whether the rule is genuinely the same, and if so propose one tested form of words with the evidence behind it and an honest note on what was and was not tested — accepting that you are building consensus rather than exercising authority.
- C) Ask the cross-government profession board to mandate a common form of words.
- D) Publish your version and invite others to adopt it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Convening rather than mandating is calibrated to the actual authority available, and the honest note on testing limits prevents the wording acquiring unearned authority. This tests building consensus within real authority limits — option A is least effective, fixing one third of a problem visible whole.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate absorbs pressure from very senior stakeholders personally rather than leaving a junior colleague to hold a contested position alone.
- Note whether the candidate revises an unachievable strategic commitment honestly rather than restating it or narrowing its beneficiaries.
- Watch for whether a resourcing or capability argument is made in the audience's own currency, with an honest trade-off named, rather than an explanation of the discipline.
- Score for whether the candidate negotiates once on behalf of many teams facing a shared recurring problem, rather than leaving each to repeat the conversation.
- Note whether cross-government collaboration is calibrated to actual authority (convening, consensus-building) rather than assuming a mandate that does not exist.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that leaves a junior colleague to defend a contested position against a very senior stakeholder alone.
- Missing when a candidate presses an unachievable commitment or narrows its scope to exclude the users who most need it, rather than revising it.
- Rewarding a resourcing argument that explains the discipline rather than leading with evidence in the audience's terms and naming a genuine trade-off.
- Accepting an over- or under-scoped correction to a shared asset used across many services.
- Missing when a candidate attempts to mandate cross-government alignment beyond their actual authority, rather than building consensus.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through organisational-scale diagnostic reasoning — tracing a compliant, well-written page's call volume to something outside the page, diagnosing several teams' shared failure as one systemic cause, recognising a heavily used recovery page as a measure of a shared component's failure rate, and revising a strategy commitment that could not be met rather than restating it; the arithmetic that turns a content argument into a resourcing decision at board scale — annualising, weighting by group size, reconstructing an implied unit value, and recognising that a fall in a metric can be success; the discipline of reading governance, policy and cross-government prose at exactly the strength the text supports, distinguishing a conditional prohibition from an absolute one and an escalation duty from a duty to personally refuse; and the situational judgement that defines this level — absorbing pressure from very senior stakeholders, revising unachievable commitments honestly, and negotiating once on behalf of many teams.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — this role's authority rests on evidence more than craft judgement, and the errors that cost most at this level are made fluently, in areas where experience runs deep enough that checking feels unnecessary and nobody else is positioned to catch them. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
