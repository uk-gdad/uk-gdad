# Junior Content Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for junior content designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A junior content designer makes things easier for people to understand and use: explaining content decisions, working collaboratively with user researchers, service designers and interaction designers, identifying user issues and needs, and supporting a team's administration and publishing processes. The assessments described here use exactly those materials — style guides, draft pages, user feedback, publishing schedules, research findings, and stakeholder emails.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate deduces only what the given rules or facts guarantee, and applies every stated rule fully rather than stopping at the first fault found. Score situational judgement for a working balance appropriate to this level: the strongest answers combine competent, independent action with prompt, honest communication upwards — neither silent over-reach beyond what a junior can competently decide, nor reflexive escalation of things that could simply be fixed.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking processes this role actually demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract shapes and sequences — style guide rules, draft page content, publishing checklists, content inventories, and user feedback.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a similar level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

For a junior content designer, so much of the role's value comes from noticing things: a draft that breaks the style guide, two pages that contradict each other, a missed publishing checklist step, a pattern in user feedback that suggests a page is confusing.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **User-centred content design**: demonstrating basic but decent editorial standards and showing an awareness of style standards means recognising when content follows an established pattern — and, more importantly, when it quietly breaks one.
- **Error checking** also maps to **User-centred content design** and to supporting content team administration and publishing processes.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **User focus (content design)**: seeing the purpose of user stories and focusing on user needs means reasoning correctly from evidence without jumping to conclusions the evidence does not support.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile working (content design)**: working in an Agile team means constantly deciding what to do first, adapting to feedback, and being transparent about why.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping** and independently identifying user issues and needs.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a style guide**

A style guide says page titles must: start with a verb, use sentence case (only the first word capitalised, except proper nouns), and contain no full stop. Four titles: (1) "Apply for a fishing licence"; (2) "Renew your passport"; (3) "Check your State Pension"; (4) "How to report a lost passport." Ask which title breaks the most style guide rules.

- A) Title 1
- B) Title 2
- C) Title 3
- D) Title 4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Titles 1–3 are compliant (State Pension in title 3 is a proper noun); title 4 breaks two rules — it starts with "How" rather than a verb, and ends with a full stop. This tests checking every rule against every title systematically rather than stopping at the first fault found — the question asks which title breaks the most rules, requiring a full count for each option.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a publishing rule**

Team process: "A page may only be published if it has been second-checked by another content designer, unless it is a minor fix approved by the lead content designer." A page was published this morning and was not second-checked. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The team broke the publishing process.
- B) The page was a minor fix approved by the lead content designer.
- C) Either the page was an approved minor fix, or the process was broken.
- D) The page contained an error.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one permitted exception, and the given facts do not establish whether the exception applies. This tests deducing only what the facts guarantee, rather than assuming a branch (A, B) or introducing unsupported information (D — an unchecked page is not automatically a wrong page).

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a draft against instructions**

A senior content designer requested exactly three changes: (i) "commence" to "start"; (ii) "in order to" to "to"; (iii) add a bullet list of three required documents. A peer's updated draft shows all three changes made, plus the page title changed from "Apply for a permit" to "Permit applications." Ask how many of the changes made were requested.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Four changes were made, three requested; the title change was extra. This tests distinguishing what was requested from what was done — an unrequested change (which here no longer starts with a verb, breaking the style rule from Question 1) should be flagged rather than let slip through.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation of a morning's tasks**

At 9:30am, four items: (1) a factual error on a live page states the wrong deadline for a benefit application, users may act on it today; (2) a 10:00 stand-up with an unupdated ticket; (3) a colleague's afternoon pairing request wants a read first; (4) the weekly content community newsletter. Ask what should happen first.

- A) Update the ticket so the board is accurate for stand-up.
- B) Start fixing, or escalate, the factual error on the live page.
- C) Read the colleague's draft while it is fresh.
- D) Read the newsletter quickly before the day gets busy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A wrong deadline on a live page can cause real users to miss an application today, a harm outranking everything else. This tests weighing impact and urgency together, ranking harm to users above internal tidiness and internal tidiness above nice-to-haves — a junior may not fix it alone, but raising it comes first regardless.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across user feedback**

Four pages, similar traffic, "No" responses to "Is this page useful?": Page A 8%, Page B 9%, Page C 31%, Page D 7%. Ask, as a junior content designer, for the most reasonable first interpretation of Page C's figure.

- A) Page C is badly written and should be rewritten immediately.
- B) Page C is an outlier worth investigating — the cause could be the content, the placement in the journey, or something else entirely.
- C) Pages A, B and D must be unusually good, so Page C is probably fine.
- D) The survey is unreliable and should be ignored.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three pages cluster at 7–9%, one sits at 31%, more than three times the cluster. This tests treating an outlier as a prompt for inquiry rather than a conclusion — option A jumps to a solution (rewriting) before understanding the cause, which might instead be a broken link or an out-of-date page.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction about content formats**

Guidance: (i) content with step-by-step instructions must use a numbered list; (ii) numbered lists must have each step start with a verb; (iii) eligibility content must never use step-by-step instructions. Reviewing an eligibility page for a travel document. Ask which conclusion must be true about the page based only on these rules.

- A) It must use a numbered list.
- B) It must not contain step-by-step instructions.
- C) Each of its list items must start with a verb.
- D) It must not contain any lists at all.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rule (iii) applies directly to eligibility content, forbidding step-by-step instructions. This tests applying rules exactly as written without over-extending them — options A and C would apply only if the page had step-by-step instructions, which it must not, and D bans lists entirely when only a specific list type (numbered, for steps) is prohibited.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a content inventory**

Rules: every row must have a unique page ID; "Last reviewed" must not be a future date; "Owner" must name a team, not a person. Today is 1 June. Extract: (101) 12 May, Licensing team; (102) 3 July, Licensing team; (103) 28 April, Sarah Okafor; (102) 15 May, Licensing team. Ask how many rows contain at least one error.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Row 1 is clean; row 2 (3 July) is a future date; row 3 names a person, not a team; row 4 duplicates the ID 102. This tests checking each rule against each row systematically without letting one found error distract from finding the next.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritising feedback during iteration**

Four pieces of feedback on a draft: (i) a user researcher reports test participants could not find the "continue" action; (ii) a policy colleague says a legal term was simplified in a way that changes its meaning; (iii) a fellow content designer suggests a shorter synonym for one word; (iv) a stakeholder wants the page to "sound more impressive." Ask for the most defensible order for addressing the feedback.

- A) iv, iii, ii, i — handle the stakeholder first to keep them happy
- B) ii, i, iii, iv — fix accuracy, then findability, then polish, then discuss tone
- C) i, ii, iii, iv — user research always comes first, no matter what
- D) iii, i, ii, iv — start with the quickest fix to build momentum

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Feedback (ii) means the page may be legally inaccurate and must be corrected first; (i) blocks task completion entirely and is next; (iii) is worthwhile polish; (iv) needs a conversation about the evidence for plain language rather than a fix. This tests weighing consequence rather than convenience, hierarchy, or a rigid rule (C) — an accuracy error that changes legal meaning is the rare thing that outranks even user research findings.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a confusing page**

A page explaining how to appeal a decision has a high drop-out rate at one step, which contains a 62-word sentence, three unexplained legal terms, and a link labelled "click here." One change can be made today and tested. Ask, following an evidence-led iteration approach, for the best single first experiment.

- A) Rewrite the entire page from scratch so all problems are fixed at once.
- B) Break the 62-word sentence into shorter sentences, since sentence length affects the whole step's readability.
- C) Change the link label from "click here" to a descriptive label, since the drop-out happens where users must follow the link.
- D) Add a glossary page defining all legal terms used across the service.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The drop-out happens specifically where users must follow the link, and "click here" tells users nothing about where it goes — particularly poor for screen reader users navigating by links. This tests forming a targeted, testable hypothesis aimed at the observed failure point — option A abandons iteration entirely, since changing everything at once means never knowing which change worked.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-rule deduction for content placement**

Placement rules: (i) "Start" pages must link to exactly one "Apply" page; (ii) "Apply" pages must not link back to "Start" pages; (iii) "Guidance" pages may link to any page type, including other guidance pages; (iv) every page must be reachable from "Start" by following links. Map: Start→Apply; Apply→Confirmation; Guidance A→Apply; Guidance B→Guidance A. Ask which rule is broken.

- A) Rule (i)
- B) Rule (ii)
- C) Rule (iii)
- D) Rule (iv)

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Rules (i)–(iii) are all satisfied by the map; tracing reachability from Start reaches only Apply and Confirmation, since nothing links to either guidance page from Start's reachable set. This tests systematically tracing which pages can actually be reached, rather than looking for a violation in the links guidance pages send out (the tempting distractor given rule iii mentions them) when the real problem is the links they never receive.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Reconciling contradictory evidence**

A page explains two submission methods: online or post. Evidence: (i) analytics show 85% of users choose online; (ii) research finds several low-digital-confidence participants could not find the postal option, mentioned only in the final paragraph; (iii) a stakeholder wants the postal option removed to encourage digital uptake. Ask for the strongest recommendation.

- A) Remove the postal option from the page, because 85% of users choose online anyway.
- B) Move the postal option higher on the page, because users who need it cannot find it, and the page must work for everyone including those with low digital confidence.
- C) Leave the page as it is, because the evidence is contradictory so no change is safe.
- D) Create a separate page for the postal option, because separating channels keeps each page simpler.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The 85% figure says nothing about whether the remaining 15% matter, while the research finding is direct evidence of a failure for a specific vulnerable group. This tests recognising that a stakeholder's preference is not evidence and does not contradict the research finding — option C mistakes tension between evidence and opinion for genuine contradiction between two pieces of evidence, which are actually consistent.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Scheduling a content task under constraints**

Four tasks, one per day, Monday–Friday: draft (D), factcheck by a policy expert (F), revise after factcheck (R), support the fortnightly publishing run (P). Constraints: F cannot start until D is complete; R cannot start until F is complete; the policy expert is available only Tuesday and Thursday; the publishing run is fixed Wednesday; one task per day maximum. Ask on which day D should happen to finish R as early as possible.

- A) Monday
- B) Tuesday
- C) Wednesday
- D) Thursday

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** D on Monday enables F on Tuesday (the expert's earliest slot); Wednesday is blocked by P; R follows on Thursday — one day earlier than if D happened any later, which would push F to Thursday and R to Friday. This tests working forward through a chain of dependencies and resource availability to find the earliest feasible schedule.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate checks every stated rule against every item systematically rather than stopping at the first fault found.
- Note whether the candidate deduces only what given facts guarantee, resisting a plausible but unproven conclusion.
- Watch for whether prioritisation weighs consequence and urgency together rather than a rigid rule or convenience.
- Score for whether the candidate forms a targeted, testable hypothesis aimed at an observed failure point rather than a broad, unfocused fix.
- Note whether the candidate traces a multi-step dependency or reachability chain systematically rather than checking rules in isolation.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a "which breaks the most rules" answer that stops at the first fault found rather than a full count.
- Missing when a candidate confuses "could be true" with "must be true" in a deduction item.
- Rewarding a prioritisation ranked by a rigid category rule ("user research always first") rather than actual consequence.
- Accepting a stakeholder preference treated as evidence that contradicts a genuine research finding.
- Missing when a candidate fails to trace a full dependency chain or reachability map before answering.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how comfortably a candidate works with numbers in everyday job situations. It is not an advanced maths exam — the numbers involved are the ones content teams genuinely see: page views, task completion rates, feedback survey percentages, research participant counts, readability scores, and publishing statistics. The mathematics never goes beyond percentages, averages, ratios, simple differences, and reading tables and charts accurately.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions, each presenting a small table, chart or short scenario with numbers, followed by a multiple-choice question. A basic on-screen calculator is often allowed.

Content design is evidence-led: many user issues first appear as numbers — a completion rate that dips, a feedback score that drifts, a page view pattern that looks odd. A candidate who reads those numbers correctly spots real problems earlier and avoids being misled by noisy data.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Percentages and proportions** map to **User focus (content design)**: understanding user experience analysis means interpreting completion rates, drop-out percentages and feedback scores without misreading them.
- **Averages and comparisons** map to **User-centred content design**: using evidence to review content — comparing before-and-after readability figures, or one page's performance against similar pages.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to supporting content team administration and publishing processes.
- **Simple rates and trends** map to **Agile working (content design)**: spotting whether a number is genuinely rising, falling or wobbling helps a team adapt and respond to feedback.
- **Sense-checking figures** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: noticing when a reported number cannot be right before anyone acts on it.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading a feedback table**

"Was this page useful?" responses for "Apply for a permit": 320 Yes, 80 No. Ask what percentage said Yes.

- A) 75%
- B) 80%
- C) 84%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 320÷400=80%. This tests building the correct total before computing a percentage — a common error is dividing Yes by No directly, or using the wrong row.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing two completion rates**

April: 1,200 started, 900 completed. May: 1,500 started, 1,200 completed. Ask by how many percentage points the completion rate improved.

- A) 3 percentage points
- B) 5 percentage points
- C) 10 percentage points
- D) 25 percentage points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** April 75%, May 80%, difference 5 points. This tests computing a rate change correctly rather than confusing it with the relative rise in raw completions (900 to 1,200 is a 33% rise, a different figure entirely, the trap in D).

**Question 3 (easy) — Averaging research session lengths**

Session lengths: 28, 32, 35, 25, 30 minutes. Ask for the mean.

- A) 28 minutes
- B) 29 minutes
- C) 30 minutes
- D) 32 minutes

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 150÷5=30. This tests basic averaging, checkable by confirming the result sits between the smallest and largest values.

**Question 4 (easy) — Publishing workload ratio**

24 content updates split between two junior content designers in the ratio 3:1. Ask how many updates the larger share receives.

- A) 6
- B) 12
- C) 16
- D) 18

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 3+1=4 parts; 24÷4=6 per part; 3×6=18. This tests the standard ratio method — total the parts, size one part, multiply — rather than dividing the total directly by one of the ratio numbers.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Percentage change in page views**

Views: 4,500 before a rewrite, 5,850 after (both over four-week periods). Ask for the percentage increase.

- A) 13%
- B) 23%
- C) 30%
- D) 35%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** (5,850−4,500)÷4,500=30%. This tests dividing by the original value — option B divides by the new value (1,350÷5,850≈23%), the classic percentage-change error.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reading a two-way table**

Content audit, 200 pages: Up to date — 70 under 1 year, 50 over 1 year (total 120); Needs review — 30 under 1 year, 50 over 1 year (total 80). Ask what percentage of pages over 1 year old need review.

- A) 25%
- B) 40%
- C) 50%
- D) 62.5%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 50÷100=50% (of the 100 pages over 1 year old). This tests identifying the correct subgroup total as the denominator — option A divides by the grand total (200), and B by all pages needing review, answering different questions entirely.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Readability scores before and after**

Four pages' readability scores (target 60+): (48→63), (55→61), (42→58), (51→66). Ask for the average improvement and how many pages now meet the target.

- A) Average improvement 13; 3 pages meet the target
- B) Average improvement 13; 4 pages meet the target
- C) Average improvement 15; 3 pages meet the target
- D) Average improvement 12; 2 pages meet the target

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Improvements: 15, 6, 16, 15, averaging 13; scores after are 63, 61, 58, 66 — three meet the 60+ target (page 3 at 58 falls short). This tests computing two related figures cleanly and separately, and, in a strong answer, noting that improvement and attainment are different measures — Page 3 improved the most yet still misses the target.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Estimating a publishing backlog rate**

Queue of 36 items; team publishes 12 a week, 8 new items join each week. Ask how many weeks to clear the queue.

- A) 3 weeks
- B) 6 weeks
- C) 9 weeks
- D) The queue will never clear

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Net clearance 12−8=4 per week; 36÷4=9 weeks. This tests using the net rate rather than the gross publishing rate alone — option A ignores new arrivals entirely, the most common real-world backlog-planning mistake.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Survey sample sense-check**

"Only 5 of 10 tested users completed the task — but our page gets 40,000 visits a month, so 20,000 users a month must be failing." Ask which statement best describes the numerical reasoning.

- A) The arithmetic and the conclusion are both sound.
- B) The arithmetic (50% of 40,000 = 20,000) is correct, but projecting from 10 test participants to 40,000 visitors states false precision — the small sample suggests a problem worth investigating, not an exact failure count.
- C) The stakeholder should have multiplied by 10, not divided by 2.
- D) Ten participants is too few to conclude anything at all, so the test result should be ignored.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The multiplication is correct, but projecting a small-sample rate onto the full population claims a precision the data cannot support. This tests holding both facts at once — the finding is a real, useful signal (D over-corrects and dismisses it entirely) — rather than accepting the exact projected count at face value (A).

**Question 10 (challenging) — Weighted average feedback score**

Three pages, star ratings 1–5: Guidance overview 4.0 average (300 ratings); How to apply 3.0 average (100 ratings); After you apply 4.5 average (100 ratings). Ask for the overall average across all three pages combined.

- A) 3.83
- B) 3.90
- C) 3.95
- D) 4.00

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total points: (4.0×300)+(3.0×100)+(4.5×100)=1,950, over 500 ratings = 3.90. This tests weighting by group size rather than averaging the three averages unweighted (A, the trap, since the pages received different numbers of ratings) — a busy page's score should count for more than a quiet page's.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Interpreting a trend with a seasonal dip**

Monthly views of a school admissions page: March 10,000, April 9,500, May 9,000, June 8,500, July 4,000, August 4,200. A colleague says: "Views have collapsed — the July redesign must have failed." Ask for the strongest numerical objection to this claim.

- A) Views fell every month from March, so the decline started long before July's redesign; and a school admissions page would be expected to dip sharply during the summer holidays regardless.
- B) The redesign clearly worked, because views rose from 4,000 in July to 4,200 in August.
- C) You cannot compare months with different numbers of days.
- D) 4,000 is 40% of 10,000, which is not a collapse.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A gentle pre-existing decline plus a plausible seasonal explanation (the admissions cycle quietening over summer) means the redesign cannot be blamed on this evidence alone. This tests asking what else could explain a number before accepting a single causal story — option B over-reads a tiny 5% rise as vindication, the same leap-to-conclusions error in the opposite direction.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Checking a stakeholder's summary against source data**

Stakeholder slide: "Task completion increased by 20%, and user complaints halved." Source: completion rose from 60% to 72%; complaints fell from 48 to 30 per month. Ask which parts of the slide are accurate.

- A) Both claims are accurate.
- B) The completion claim is accurate; the complaints claim is not.
- C) The complaints claim is accurate; the completion claim is not.
- D) Neither claim is accurate.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Completion: (72−60)÷60=20% relative increase, defensible as stated; complaints: (48−30)÷48=37.5% fall, not a halving (which would require 24). This tests verifying each claim independently against source data rather than accepting a stakeholder's summary at face value.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate always identifies the correct base or denominator before computing a percentage.
- Note whether the candidate weights an average by group size rather than averaging averages unweighted.
- Watch for whether a net rate (arrivals minus clearances) is used in backlog or queue questions rather than the gross rate alone.
- Score for whether a small-sample or short-period finding is treated as a real signal without claiming false precision.
- Note whether the candidate verifies each part of a summary or claim independently against the underlying source data.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage computed using the wrong denominator (grand total instead of a named subgroup, or vice versa).
- Missing when a candidate averages group averages unweighted rather than weighting by group size.
- Rewarding a backlog or queue calculation that ignores ongoing arrivals.
- Accepting a small-sample percentage projected onto a full population as though it were precise.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a stakeholder's summary claim without checking it against the source figures.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads, understands and draws conclusions from written information. For most roles this is one useful skill among several; for a content designer it is close to the heart of the job — every day involves reading policy notes, style guidance, research summaries, stakeholder emails and existing pages, and extracting exactly what they say, no more and no less.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes, presenting a short passage followed by statements to judge True (the passage supports it), False (the passage contradicts it) or Cannot say (the passage gives too little information) — regardless of outside knowledge. Other question types test comprehension, inference, and clear-writing judgement.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to **User-centred content design**: demonstrating decent editorial standards begins with reading source material accurately.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **User focus (content design)**: distinguishing what evidence actually says from what is hoped or assumed.
- **Inference** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: reading guidance and strategy documents and drawing only the conclusions they genuinely support.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **User-centred content design** and explaining content decisions.
- **Reading stakeholder communications accurately** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "Our style guide requires plain language on all public-facing pages. Aim for short sentences, and prefer common words over formal ones — for example, use 'help' rather than 'assistance'. Technical terms may be used where the audience is specialist, but each technical term must be explained at first use on a page. Legal terms that have a precise meaning must not be replaced with everyday synonyms; instead, explain them in a following sentence. This guidance applies to new content immediately, and to existing content as pages come up for review."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "The style guide allows technical terms on pages written for a specialist audience."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True only for legal content

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, with a condition (explain at first use) that does not turn permission into prohibition. This tests locating the governing sentence and comparing the statement's claim to it precisely — a condition attached to a permission does not defeat the permission.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Legal terms should be swapped for simpler everyday words."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for new content only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states the opposite — legal terms with a precise meaning must not be replaced with synonyms, only explained. This tests catching a plausible-sounding statement that fits the general theme of plain language but contradicts the specific rule.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "All existing pages must be rewritten in plain language within twelve months."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the guidance applies immediately

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says guidance applies to existing content "as pages come up for review" with no stated review frequency or deadline. This tests recognising that the passage neither confirms nor rules out a twelve-month timeline — the honest answer is that it is silent, and "immediately" (option D) applies only to new content.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Main point comprehension**

Ask which option best summarises the passage's overall position on word choice.

- A) Always use the simplest word available, without exception.
- B) Prefer common words, but preserve technical and legal terms where precision matters — supported by explanation.
- C) Avoid technical terms entirely on public-facing pages.
- D) Formal words are acceptable if sentences are kept short.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage prefers common words, permits technical terms with explanation, and protects legal terms from inaccurate substitution. This tests weighing the whole passage rather than one sentence — A overstates by ignoring the legal-terms exception, C contradicts the technical-terms permission, D invents a trade-off never offered.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 7.* "Thanks for the draft. Legal have confirmed the eligibility rules changed on 6 April: applicants must now have lived in the UK for 3 years, not 5. Please update the eligibility page before Friday, as the old figure is still live. Separately, the team would like the application page to mention our new helpline, but the helpline number is not yet confirmed — do not publish it until I send confirmation. I'll be on leave next week, so direct any questions to Priya."

**Question 5 (easy) — Identifying the required action**

Ask which action is requested by Friday.

- A) Publish the new helpline number.
- B) Update the residence requirement on the eligibility page from 5 years to 3 years.
- C) Redirect all questions to Priya.
- D) Update both the eligibility page and the application page.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The Friday deadline attaches only to the eligibility page update; the helpline mention is explicitly embargoed ("do not publish it until I send confirmation"). This tests correctly attaching a deadline and a condition to their respective, separate requests — option D bundles the blocked task with the required one.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The eligibility page currently tells users the wrong residence requirement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True only after Friday

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two stated facts chain together — the rules changed to 3 years, and "the old figure is still live" (5 years). This tests a fair inference built entirely from stated facts, distinguishable from a genuine information gap like the review timetable in Question 3.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inference about communication**

The eligibility page is updated Thursday; the following Tuesday a question arises about the helpline wording. Ask what should be done, based only on the email.

- A) Publish the helpline number, since a week has passed.
- B) Email the stakeholder and wait for their return.
- C) Direct the question to Priya.
- D) Ask Legal, since they confirmed the eligibility rules.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The email explicitly names this contingency: "I'll be on leave next week, so direct any questions to Priya." This tests registering a contingency instruction on first reading — option A wrongly treats time passing as equivalent to the required confirmation being sent, and D confuses which team owns which matter.

*Passage C — for Questions 8 to 10.* "We tested the draft 'Report a repair' page with eight social housing tenants. Six completed the task without help. Two participants, both using screen readers, could not identify where to enter the property reference number, because the field label was read out as 'reference' with no further context. Participants generally described the page as 'clear' and 'quick', though three said they were unsure whether they would receive a confirmation message after submitting. We recommend clarifying the field label and adding a sentence about what happens after submission. We did not test the page on mobile devices."

**Question 8 (easy)**

Statement: "All participants completed the task without help."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for screen reader users

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Six of eight completed without help; two did not. This tests hunting for an exception whenever a statement contains an absolute word ("all") — a single counter-example makes it false.

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: "The page works well on mobile devices."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because screen reader users struggled

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly states mobile devices were not tested. This tests treating declared silence as neither confirming nor contradicting — option D commits the subtler error of borrowing evidence about a different dimension (screen readers) and applying it to mobile, which the passage never links.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Drawing the right conclusion from findings**

Ask which conclusion is best supported by the passage.

- A) The page should be rebuilt from scratch because it fails accessibility standards.
- B) The label change should be prioritised because it blocked task completion for assistive technology users, and a post-submission sentence should be added to address the confirmation uncertainty.
- C) No changes are needed because most participants succeeded and described the page as clear.
- D) The confirmation message issue is more serious than the label issue because three participants raised it versus two.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The label problem completely blocked two users; the confirmation uncertainty caused doubt but not failure for the three who raised it. This tests weighing findings by severity rather than headcount — option D counts heads while ignoring that three mildly unsure participants do not outweigh two fully blocked ones, and C commits the majority fallacy, excusing a barrier that excluded users with access needs.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement**

Draft: "In the event that the applicant is in receipt of Universal Credit, the applicant may be eligible for a reduction in respect of the fee payable." Ask which rewrite best preserves the meaning while making it plain.

- A) "If you get Universal Credit, you may pay a smaller fee."
- B) "If you get Universal Credit, you will not pay the fee."
- C) "Universal Credit recipients always receive fee reductions."
- D) "You might pay less, depending on your benefits."

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The meaning has two load-bearing parts — the condition and the possibility ("may," not a guarantee) — and A preserves both while converting stiff constructions into everyday English. This tests preserving both meaning-bearing elements of a sentence during simplification — B upgrades a possibility into a false promise, C converts "may" into "always," and D blurs which specific benefit matters.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling two documents**

Note A (January): "Users must verify their identity before viewing their application status." Note B (March): "Following the service update, users can view their application status without signing in; identity verification is now required only when users wish to change their details." Ask, based only on the two notes, what the current rule is.

- A) Users must verify their identity to view their status, as note A states.
- B) The notes conflict, so no rule can be stated until someone decides.
- C) Note B is more recent and describes the position "following the service update": viewing status requires no sign-in, and verification is needed only to change details — though it would be sensible to confirm nothing has changed since March.
- D) Users never need to verify their identity.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Note B is dated later and explicitly frames itself as describing the position after an update, accounting for why note A is now out of date. This tests checking whether a later document supersedes an earlier one rather than treating a dated, explained update as an unresolvable contradiction (B) or over-extending the newer note (D, which still requires verification for changes).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate answers strictly from the passage, marking Cannot Say where the text is genuinely silent.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a statement that fits the general theme of a passage from one that actually matches its specific wording.
- Watch for whether deadlines and conditions attached to separate parts of a multi-instruction passage are kept correctly separated.
- Score for whether the candidate weighs research findings by severity (blocked vs merely uncertain) rather than by headcount alone.
- Note whether a clear-writing rewrite preserves every meaning-bearing element of the original rather than simplifying one away.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True because it fits the theme of a passage while contradicting its specific wording.
- Missing when a candidate treats declared silence (a test not run, a fact not stated) as evidence rather than as neither confirming nor denying.
- Rewarding a multi-instruction email or passage answered without separating each instruction's own deadline and conditions.
- Accepting a headcount-based reading of research findings that ignores severity.
- Missing when a candidate selects a clear-writing rewrite that drops or overstates part of the original meaning.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks how effective different responses would be. There are no sums to solve and no passages to parse — it measures professional instincts: how a candidate balances user needs against stakeholder pressure, when they ask for help, how they handle mistakes, and how they behave in a team.

Typical format: an online test of 20 to 40 minutes, usually untimed or generously timed, with 10 to 20 scenarios, asking for the most effective response, the least effective, a full ranking, or independent ratings.

For a junior content designer, situational judgement matters enormously because attitude counts more than experience at this level: putting users first, communicating honestly, asking for direction at the right moments, owning mistakes quickly, collaborating generously, and treating everyone with respect.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **User-first judgement** maps to **User focus (content design)**: keeping the user's need in view when a stakeholder, a deadline, or convenience pulls the other way.
- **Collaboration and openness** map to **Agile working (content design)** and **Content concepts and prototyping**.
- **Knowing when to escalate** maps to this level's defining balance: working independently after being given direction by more senior content designers.
- **Handling stakeholders respectfully** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Ownership and honesty** map to **User-centred content design** and publishing duties.
- **Community contribution** maps to engaging with and contributing to the cross-government content community.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A stakeholder wants jargon restored**

A page was simplified in line with the style guide, changing "remuneration" to "pay." A policy stakeholder emails asking for it to be changed back, saying "remuneration" is correct and "pay" sounds unprofessional. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Change it back immediately — stakeholders outrank style guides.
- B) Reply explaining the evidence for plain language, note that the style guide requires common words, offer to discuss, and let a senior content designer know about the conversation.
- C) Ignore the email — the style guide settles the matter.
- D) Change it back, but keep "pay" in brackets afterwards as a compromise.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Option B engages respectfully, explains the content decision with evidence, keeps the door open, and loops in a senior — appropriate backup for a standards disagreement with a stakeholder. This tests holding a user-centred line kindly — option C wins the point technically and poisons the relationship through silence, the opposite of managing stakeholder relationships.

**Question 2 (easy) — Spotting your own published mistake**

Ten minutes after publishing an update, an error is noticed: the page now says applications close on "31 June," a date that does not exist. Nobody else has noticed. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Fix it quietly — no harm done, no need to mention it.
- B) Fix it (or start the fix immediately), tell a senior content designer what happened, and check whether the correct date is 30 June or something else before republishing.
- C) Leave it until the next scheduled publishing run to avoid making extra work.
- D) Report it to a senior and wait for them to tell you what to do.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Option B fixes the user-facing problem fast, is honest about it (which helps a team fix any underlying process gap), and verifies the correct fact before republishing rather than guessing. This tests fast, honest correction with verification — option C is least effective, leaving a nonexistent date live in front of users for no defensible reason.

**Question 3 (easy) — Unclear direction from a senior**

A senior content designer asks for a page to be "tidied up" before Thursday, then goes into back-to-back meetings; reading the page reveals it needs far more than tidying — confusing structure and possibly out-of-date content. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Do a light tidy only — that is what was literally asked.
- B) Rewrite the whole page thoroughly — they will be pleased you took initiative.
- C) Send a short message: work on the tidy-up has started, the page appears to have deeper problems (with one or two examples), and ask whether a bigger rework is wanted now or the tidy first — then continue with the tidy while awaiting a reply.
- D) Wait until Thursday to ask, since they are busy.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Working independently after direction includes recognising when the direction no longer fits what was found, and saying so early with a precise question a busy senior can handle between meetings. This tests making progress while surfacing new information — option D sits on important information for days, and B silently expands scope, risking duplication or changes needing policy checks not known about.

**Question 4 (easy) — Pairing invitation when behind**

A fellow junior invites you to pair on their draft this afternoon, while you are behind on your own ticket due tomorrow. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Decline without explanation and work on your ticket.
- B) Accept and pair all afternoon — collaboration always comes first.
- C) Explain being behind on a ticket due tomorrow, offer a shorter slot (say, 45 minutes) or tomorrow afternoon, and flag the workload at stand-up if the ticket is at risk.
- D) Accept, but secretly work on your own ticket during the session.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Option C is honest about the constraint, still offers real collaboration, and uses stand-up — the team's own mechanism — to make workload pressure visible. This tests honest, proportionate trade-offs made visible to the team — option D is least effective, faking attention and shortchanging both tasks, damaging trust more than an honest no ever would.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to skip the second check**

At 4:40pm, a stakeholder urgently needs a correction published today; the process requires a second check, and the only available checker left at 4:30. A senior is reachable by phone. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Publish without the check — it is a small change and the stakeholder said it is urgent.
- B) Tell the stakeholder it will be published tomorrow morning, without exploring alternatives.
- C) Call a senior, explain the situation, and ask whether they can second-check remotely or approve an exception — then follow whatever the process allows.
- D) Ask the stakeholder to confirm in writing that they take responsibility, then publish unchecked.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Publishing processes catch wrong dates, broken links and legal inaccuracies, and option C finds the legitimate flexibility (a remote check or a lead-approved exception) rather than either bypassing the process or refusing outright. This tests resourceful escalation to the person who holds process authority — option D is least effective, since a stakeholder's email cannot transfer accountability for content quality — paperwork does not catch errors.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A research finding you disagree with**

User research shows participants struggled with a page structure the candidate designed and was proud of; the researcher recommends restructuring, but the candidate privately thinks two of five participants misunderstood the task. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the recommendation without mentioning your concern — challenging research looks defensive.
- B) In the meeting or shortly after, thank the researcher, raise the observation about the two participants as a question, and offer to work with them on what a follow-up test or revision should look like.
- C) Explain to the team why the research is flawed and the structure should stay.
- D) Say nothing, but quietly keep the structure unchanged.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Option B engages with the findings rather than defending territory, raises a legitimate methodological question as a question, and moves toward collaboration rather than a standoff. This tests responding constructively to disagreeable feedback about one's own work — option D is least effective, ignoring evidence, the team's decision process, and honesty all at once.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Accessibility versus a launch deadline**

Two days before launch, an infographic is found to contain essential eligibility information as text within the image with no text alternative; the designer is busy and a product manager says accessibility can be fixed after launch. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the product manager's call — they own the timeline.
- B) Explain that the missing information is essential and launching would exclude screen reader users; propose a fast fix (adding the eligibility information as body text below the image before launch) and involve a senior if the deadline pressure continues.
- C) Refuse to be involved with the launch and tell the team it is illegal.
- D) Add a note to the backlog so the problem is fixed in a future sprint.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The conflict is largely false — the fix (duplicating essential information as real text) takes minutes, protecting both users and the deadline. This tests refusing a false either/or and offering a genuine third path — option D is least effective, knowingly shipping exclusion of essential eligibility information.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Credit for your work**

At a show-and-tell, before/after content comparisons written by the candidate are presented by a senior colleague without attribution; a stakeholder later emails the senior praising "your excellent rewrite." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Reply-all to the email correcting the record.
- B) Say nothing — juniors should not expect credit.
- C) Mention it privately to the senior colleague: pleased the work landed well, and would appreciate being named when the work is shown, since visibility matters for development.
- D) Raise it as a formal grievance with HR.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** A private, constructive conversation lets the colleague fix what is probably an oversight gracefully, and names a legitimate professional interest — visibility matters for career development at this level. This tests calibrated self-advocacy — option B teaches invisibility and lets a small unfairness calcify into a pattern.

**Question 9 (moderate) — A community request during a busy week**

A content designer in another department asks the cross-government content community whether anyone has written content for reporting a lost document; the candidate wrote exactly that last quarter but is having a busy week. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Ignore the post — your own team's work comes first this week.
- B) Post a short reply linking to the published pages and offering a 20-minute call next week, and mention anything learned the hard way.
- C) Spend half a day writing them a detailed guide.
- D) Reply saying you did this work, but that they should figure it out themselves since your version may not fit their context.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Linking existing published work costs minutes, and a deferred short call fits within a busy week — this is exactly how the community creates value: reuse instead of reinvention. This tests generous but proportionate community contribution — option A is least effective, hoarding knowledge the taxpayer already paid for once.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Conflicting instructions from two seniors**

A senior content designer directed a page restructure into three shorter pages on Monday; on Wednesday, while that senior is away, a delivery manager instructs putting the page back to one for the launch next week. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Follow the delivery manager's instruction — they are present and the launch is imminent.
- B) Follow the senior's original instruction — content decisions belong to content designers.
- C) Explain that the senior directed the split, ask the delivery manager for the reason behind the single-page need, and contact the senior (message or call) to resolve the conflict before undoing the work — while making sure the launch deadline is flagged in that message.
- D) Do neither and work on other tickets until the senior returns.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Option C is transparent with the delivery manager, gathers missing information (there may be a real constraint the senior does not know, or vice versa), and puts the decision back where it belongs, urgently. This tests surfacing a conflict to the people who own it rather than silently picking a side — option D is least effective, paralysis leaving the conflict undiscovered until the week of launch.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Least effective: a tempting shortcut with data**

Preparing a summary for a stakeholder meeting tomorrow, the dashboard shows mixed data: feedback scores improved, task completion is flat, and one page's drop-out rate worsened. Ask which response would be LEAST effective.

- A) Present all three findings, with a sentence on what the team plans to investigate about the worsened page.
- B) Present the improved feedback scores prominently and omit the flat completion and worsened drop-out, since the meeting needs a positive story.
- C) Ask a team lead whether they want a one-slide summary or a fuller picture, and mention now that the data is mixed so they are not surprised tomorrow.
- D) Present the findings and note honestly that four weeks of data may be too little to judge some of the changes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Selectively reporting only favourable metrics is a small act of dishonesty with a long tail — the omitted numbers are on a dashboard anyone can open, and discovery later damages durable trust in everything a team reports. This tests recognising the one option that breaches a professional principle (honesty with evidence) against several genuinely defensible alternatives.

**Question 12 (challenging) — A user complaint that crosses teams**

A member of the public reports being told they could apply by phone, when the phone line (run by another directorate, which changed its policy without notice) now says applications are online only; the candidate's team's page still says "you can apply by phone." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Reply to the user explaining the other directorate caused the error, and forward the complaint to them.
- B) Update or flag the incorrect page urgently, tell a senior what was found, make sure someone sends the user an apology with the correct application route, and suggest the team establish a way to be notified when the phone service's policies change.
- C) Update the page and consider the matter closed.
- D) Log the complaint in the backlog for prioritisation at the next sprint planning.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Option B stops the harm (the page is actively misdirecting users today), repairs the user's experience with an apology and correct route, and addresses the systemic cause (a missing cross-directorate notification channel) that would otherwise let the failure recur. This tests handling immediate harm, user repair, and root cause together — option D is least effective, treating a live, harm-causing inaccuracy as routine backlog work rather than something needing urgent action.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate's strongest responses combine competent independent action with prompt, honest communication upward, rather than either silent struggle or reflexive escalation.
- Note whether a response includes the human step (thanking a colleague, apologising to a user, looping back to a stakeholder) alongside the technical fix.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes escalating a genuine authority conflict from quietly absorbing one that should be surfaced.
- Score for whether integrity is held under social or delivery pressure (selective reporting, silent non-compliance) rather than traded away.
- Note whether a response offers a genuine third option when a scenario presents a false either/or (deadline versus quality, stakeholder versus user).

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an over-escalated response for a decision well within the candidate's own competence to make.
- Missing when a candidate under-escalates a genuine authority conflict or accessibility risk by quietly absorbing it.
- Rewarding a "heroic" response — unrequested wholesale rewriting, disproportionate unpaid effort, refusing to participate dramatically.
- Accepting selective or dishonest reporting of data to preserve a positive story.
- Missing when a candidate's technically correct fix omits the human step (an apology, an acknowledgement, restoring credit).

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and error checking against a style guide, a publishing rule, and a content inventory; the arithmetic that makes content evidence defensible — weighting averages by group size, distinguishing net from gross rates, and verifying a stakeholder's summary claim against the underlying data; the discipline of reading policy prose, research summaries and stakeholder emails at exactly the strength the text supports; and the situational judgement that defines a strong junior content designer — competent independent action paired with prompt, honest communication, and a genuine third option offered wherever a scenario presents a false either/or.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answer is very often one that acts within remit while being transparent at its edges. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
