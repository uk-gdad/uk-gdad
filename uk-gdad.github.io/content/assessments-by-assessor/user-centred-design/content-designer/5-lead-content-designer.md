# Lead Content Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for lead content designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A lead content designer is an expert practitioner and leader who directs a team of content designers, assures content quality across whole teams, and makes sure content aligns to strategy and objectives while meeting the needs of users; works closely with service managers, programme directors and senior stakeholders to resource teams, resolve problems and develop future projects; and promotes the content design discipline, engages with the cross-government community, and keeps up to date with industry changes.

Three features of the role shape what the assessment is built to test. First, this role assures content it did not write, frequently on services it does not work on, judged from output rather than the thinking behind it — a core competence in diagnostic reasoning at a distance. Second, this role is accountable for alignment as well as quality: content that is well written and pulls against organisational objectives is a failure of leadership rather than of the writer. Third, this role resources teams and resolves problems with senior stakeholders, meaning much of the work is negotiation conducted from informal authority, resting on unassailable evidence — content quality is unusually hard to argue about, because everybody reads and most people believe they can write.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate diagnoses mechanisms rather than instances — a recurring problem across several teams is usually one systemic cause, not several unrelated failures — and interrogates a quantitative claim rather than accepting it at face value. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a response that absorbs pressure downward from senior stakeholders rather than leaving a junior colleague to hold a line alone, negotiates once on behalf of several teams rather than leaving each to have the same conversation separately, and fixes the process behind an error rather than only the instance.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic — content produced by other people, standards applied unevenly, evidence gathered for other purposes, and prioritisation problems whose units are teams and services rather than pages.

Typical format: an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

Three things characterise this section at this level. First, reasoning at a distance — rarely seeing the work itself, only a page, a report, or a claim in a board paper, and judging from visible traces whether the thinking behind it holds. Second, the interesting questions concern mechanisms — bad content is nearly always produced by something (an unsettled policy, a slow sign-off process, a template missing a field, a relationship where nobody feels able to say no), and fixing the page leaves the mechanism intact. Third, prioritisation is genuinely hard because nothing dominates, and the discriminating factor is reasoning from what input can still change rather than from importance or visibility.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing at a distance** maps to assuring content quality across whole teams.
- **Cross-service synthesis** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: alignment requires seeing across the content estate rather than page by page.
- **Systemic diagnosis** maps to **Agile working (content design)**: improving how teams work is part of leading them.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to this role level's requirement to resource teams and develop future projects.
- **Diagnosing exclusion** maps to **User focus (content design)**: content that fails a group is a failure whether or not the group appears in the data.
- **Reasoning about positions and incentives** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: resolving problems with senior stakeholders begins with understanding what they are protecting.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Standard requires transactional pages to: use words users would use, say what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid requesting held information, offer a route to help, and name the responsible team. A page uses internal terminology, says "the case will be assessed," gives no timescale, re-requests a held reference, has a "contact us" footer link, and names no team. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Internal terminology, no timescale, re-requested information, and no named team fail four requirements; "the case will be assessed" and the footer link satisfy the other two, thinly. This tests applying a standard exactly as written even where satisfaction feels thin — a footer link satisfying "offer a route to help" is a signal to revise the standard's precision, not to mark it a failure by fiat.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Rule: "Content may be published without second review only if it is a factual correction approved by the content lead." A team reports publishing without second review. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) It was an approved factual correction.
- C) Either it was an approved factual correction, or the standard was breached.
- D) The content should be withdrawn.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Only if" makes the exception the sole route to compliance, giving exactly two branches with no third possibility. This tests recognising the precise logical shape of "only if" as distinct from "unless" — options A and B each assume a branch, and D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading the thinking behind a page**

A page under assurance is well written, follows the style guide, and answers its heading clearly — and generates the highest contact volume in the service. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) The page must have a defect the style guide does not cover.
- B) A page that reads well and still generates calls is usually answering a question users are not asking, arriving after an expectation has been set elsewhere, or correctly explaining a rule too conditional to be explained in prose — so the diagnostic step is finding out what callers actually say, which the contact centre already knows.
- C) The contact centre needs a better script.
- D) The page should be rewritten more simply.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The combination of well written, compliant, clear, and still generating calls rules out the obvious explanations and points outside the page. This tests identifying the cheapest way to distinguish causes — a script (C) permanently obscures the underlying content problem, and rewriting (D) targets a page that already works.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

Capacity for one thing this quarter: (1) a new service's core journey content, setting patterns reused across six related services; (2) a live page with steady contact volume for a year; (3) exploratory content work for a programme's next-year plan; (4) revisiting a page written six weeks ago after minor design changes. Ask which is strongest.

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will be reused across six services, and content input is the only thing that could still shape them — and an established pattern is far harder to change than to set.
- C) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.
- D) Item 4, because unverified changes are a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack — a pattern reused six times embeds either good or bad decisions six times over, and undoing it later means coordinating six teams who have adopted it. This tests weighing decisions by their compounding reuse rather than current impact alone (A), which misses this exact effect.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

A team reports users struggling with a benefit rule; a second service's site search shows the same rule's key phrase as its top term; the contact centre reports queries about the rule across four services. Ask for the strongest synthesis.

- A) Three teams have found content problems.
- B) The organisation has one rule that cannot be understood as currently expressed, appearing across at least four services — so it is a single content problem to be solved once, most likely with an agreed form of words, rather than four teams each writing their own explanation.
- C) The rule should be simplified.
- D) Each team should improve its own page.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four services struggling with the same rule is one problem, not four, and solving it once produces a consistent, properly tested explanation maintained in one place. This tests reframing the unit of work from page to organisation-wide problem — option D is the default that produces four inconsistent explanations diverging further over time.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a colleague's reasoning**

A content designer proposes removing a warning paragraph because eight of ten participants said it was "off-putting." Ask for the most useful feedback.

- A) Approve it; eight of ten is a clear signal.
- B) Note that "off-putting" is a reaction to tone rather than evidence that the information is unnecessary, and that the useful question is whether users who did not read it went on to make the mistake the warning exists to prevent — so the finding supports rewriting the paragraph, not removing it, unless separate evidence shows the risk it describes is not real.
- C) Reject the proposal.
- D) Suggest testing with more participants.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Participants disliking a paragraph reveals its tone, not whether the information prevents harm — separable questions. This tests distinguishing a reaction from evidence about need — "users found it off-putting" is one of the most common justifications for deleting exactly the content that protects people, since a page without a warning tests more pleasantly.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Five teams consistently omit timescales, each with a plausible local reason (uncertain timescale, policy won't confirm, no template field). Ask for the strongest diagnosis and response.

- A) Remind all teams that timescales are required.
- B) Five teams doing the same thing for plausible local reasons is one systemic cause with five rationalisations; establish which it is — the template genuinely lacks a field, or teams cannot get a timescale confirmed, or the standard does not say what to publish when a timescale is uncertain — and fix that, since the remedies are entirely different.
- C) Add timescales to the content checklist.
- D) Accept it; some timescales genuinely cannot be given.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Five teams behaving identically for individually plausible reasons is the signature of a systemic cause, and each candidate implies a completely different fix — the most interesting being a standard silent on what to publish when a timescale is genuinely uncertain, resolved with a form of words like "we usually reply within X." This tests naming realistic systemic candidates rather than the weakest available response (A) to any compliance problem.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study recruited entirely through the service's online panel. Findings: (i) participants did not understand "relevant supporting evidence"; (ii) participants found the journey easy to navigate; (iii) participants completed in under ten minutes; (iv) participants did not notice a footer link. Ask which findings are most compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; the sample is adequate.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are most compromised, because a panel recruited through the online service consists of people already comfortable with it, which flatters navigation and speed — while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of a phrase and noticing an element, less dependent on prior familiarity though not wholly independent of it.
- D) Only (i).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A panel recruited through the service is filtered twice — for success and for volunteering — most affecting ease and speed findings. This tests scoping a sampling flaw precisely — option A discards findings that survive and teaches teams that admitting a limitation invalidates their work, an incentive to stop disclosing it, which is a specifically organisational cost at this level.

**Question 9 (harder) — Content that should not exist**

A page explaining how to correct a common form error is heavily used and well rated across three services using the same form component. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The page works and should be maintained in all three services.
- B) Heavy use of a recovery page measures how often the upstream defect occurs, and the defect is in a shared form component used by three services — so the highest-value work is on the component, after which the page should shrink or disappear in all three at once.
- C) The page should be made more prominent.
- D) Each service should maintain its own version.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A recovery page's heavy use measures a failure rate; the shared component means one fix removes the problem from all three services simultaneously. This tests recognising both the symptom-page logic and the multiplied leverage of a shared defect — option D is the worst structural answer, entrenching three separately maintained pages for one shared defect.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of a problem**

Seventeen of twenty services complied with a plain-language review standard; the three that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced content designers. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the three and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: three failures in one directorate under identical pressure by experienced people indicates a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what the review demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the three alongside.
- C) Remind all content designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-publication gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Concentrated non-compliance under identical pressure by experienced people points to a process that does not survive a deadline, not to conduct or capability. This tests investigating and fixing the mechanism while still remediating the live instances — option D imposes friction on seventeen compliant services to catch three, accumulating organisational burden.

**Question 11 (harder) — Alignment as well as quality**

A team's content is clear, well tested, popular — and describes a service in terms conflicting with the organisation's strategy. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Approve it; the content works for users.
- B) Establish which description is right before deciding — if the strategy is aspirational and the content describes the service as it is, the content is correct and the strategy is out of date; if the content misdescribes what the service actually does, that is an accuracy problem regardless of testing well.
- C) Require the content to match the strategy.
- D) Escalate the discrepancy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Alignment and quality can genuinely conflict, and the resolution is establishing which document is factually right, not automatically deferring to either. This tests recognising that user testing settles usability, not accuracy of description (A wrongly conflates the two) — the content may be right and the strategy may have drifted, or vice versa.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your own teams**

Two teams consistently produce strong content, five do not; both strong teams have a content designer who runs a weekly session reading drafts aloud. Ask for the most useful inference and action.

- A) The five weaker teams need training.
- B) Reading drafts aloud is a cheap, transferable routine that plausibly explains the difference — go and watch one session to establish what it actually does, then introduce it as a routine rather than attributing the difference to individual talent.
- C) Recruit more content designers like those two.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the five weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reading drafts aloud is free, requires no training, and transfers in a way individual skill does not — but the step almost everyone skips is going to watch first, since the session may be doing something other than its name suggests. This tests identifying a naturally occurring, transferable practice rather than positing an unspecified knowledge deficit (A) or misattributing a routine to individual talent (C).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate applies a given standard exactly as written and flags the standard itself, rather than the audit result, as what needs revision.
- Note whether the candidate diagnoses a recurring problem across several teams as one systemic cause with several rationalisations, rather than several unrelated instances.
- Watch for whether the candidate scopes a sampling or evidence flaw precisely — naming which findings survive and which are compromised.
- Score for whether prioritisation weighs a decision's compounding effect (reuse across services, patterns adopted elsewhere) rather than only current visible impact.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a reaction to tone or presentation from evidence about actual user need.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a "must be true" answer that is merely plausible rather than guaranteed by the given facts.
- Missing when a candidate proposes fixing an instance (a page, a single team) rather than the systemic cause behind a recurring pattern.
- Rewarding a sampling flaw treated as either wholly disqualifying or entirely negligible, rather than scoped to specific findings.
- Accepting user testing or popularity treated as evidence of factual accuracy rather than usability.
- Missing when a candidate proposes adding a rule or reminder to a system where the requirement is already known and cannot currently be met.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At lead level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation — computing correctly matters, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims.

Typical format: a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided.

Content work is chronically under-prioritised because its value is hard to express in numbers other people use, and content quality is unusually hard to argue about because everybody reads. A lead content designer who can say "two in five calls to this service ask when something will happen, which is 28,800 calls a year" wins resourcing arguments that a strong view about clarity does not.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to assuring content quality across whole teams.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: alignment requires seeing the estate rather than the page.
- **Quantifying content value** maps to this role level's requirement to resource teams and develop future projects.
- **Comparing groups fairly** maps to **User focus (content design)**.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User focus**: a wrong measure sends content work to the wrong place across several teams at once.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 4,800 users who answered a feedback prompt, 1,200 said they did not find what they needed. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 1,200÷4,800=25%. This tests basic percentage calculation, and, in a strong candidate's answer, recognising this is the rate among people who answered the prompt, not everyone who saw the page.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing pages fairly**

Page A: 40,000 views, 1,200 negative. Page B: 8,000 views, 960 negative. Page C: 20,000 views, 600 negative. Ask which page has the highest negative rate, and how many users it disappoints.

- A) Page A, 3%, 1,200 users
- B) Page B, 12%, 960 users
- C) Page C, 3%, 600 users
- D) Page A, 12%, 1,200 users

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 3%; B 12%; C 3%. This tests converting to rates rather than raw counts — and, in a strong answer, noting Pages A and C sitting identically at 3% establishes a baseline that makes Page B's 12% genuinely interpretable.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a contact log**

Calls: "when will I hear" 3,600; "what documents" 2,250; "how do I cancel" 600; "fee query" 600; "other" 1,950. Ask what percentage concern timescales, and how many calls a year that is.

- A) 40%, 43,200
- B) 40%, 3,600
- C) 36%, 43,200
- D) 45%, 43,200

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total 9,000; 3,600÷9,000=40%; ×12=43,200/year. This tests computing the total, finding the proportion, and annualising — option B stops at the monthly figure, the commonest error, and the annual figure is what a programme director and finance colleague both recognise as a resourcing argument.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Word counts of seven pages: 130, 145, 150, 160, 170, 185, 2,600. Ask for the most defensible summary.

- A) Mean 506 words
- B) Median 160, range 130 to 2,600, with the 2,600-word page flagged as probably several pages that have not been split
- C) Median 160
- D) Mean 506, range 130 to 2,600

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean (505.7) is arithmetically correct and describes no page; the median (160) describes the guide well but alone conceals the most informative item — a page sixteen times typical length. This tests supplying centre, spread, and the content-specific interpretation together, rather than the median alone (C, burying the interesting item).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four pages' negative feedback rates: 4% on 25,000 views, 10% on 5,000, 6% on 10,000, 8% on 10,000. Ask for the overall negative rate across all 50,000 views.

- A) 5.8%
- B) 6.5%
- C) 7%
- D) 8%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 1,000+500+600+800=2,900 across 50,000 views ≈5.8%. This tests returning to counts before combining rates — option C is the unweighted mean (exactly 7), over-weighting the two small pages against the 25,000-view page that should dominate.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before: 1,600 of 4,000 answered a prompt negatively. After: 1,200 of 4,800 did. Ask whether the page improved, and by how much in percentage points.

- A) Yes, by 400 responses
- B) Yes, by 15 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 25 percentage points
- D) No, it got worse

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Before 40%, after 25%, improvement 15 points. This tests converting to rates because totals differ — option A compares raw counts, where here the raw fall (400) actually understates the improvement since more people answered after the change.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change and its interpretation**

Views of a guidance page fell from 80,000 to 60,000 a month after a form was simplified. Ask for the change, and what it most likely means.

- A) 25% decrease, and the guidance is failing to reach people
- B) 25% decrease, which may be the intended outcome — if the form no longer produces the confusion the guidance resolved, fewer people needing it is success
- C) 33% decrease, and the guidance is failing
- D) 33% decrease, which may be success

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (80,000−60,000)÷80,000=25% (C, D divide by the new figure). This tests correctly interpreting the sign of a decline for content that exists to resolve confusion — a fall in views can be exactly the intended outcome, worth establishing with a programme director before the numbers arrive rather than explaining afterwards.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims reducing contact volume by 30,000 calls a year is worth £180,000. Ask for the implied cost per call, and whether it is plausible.

- A) £6 per call, which is plausible for a short telephone interaction
- B) £60 per call, which would be implausibly high
- C) £0.60 per call, which would be implausibly low
- D) £600 per call

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £180,000÷30,000=£6. This tests the thirty-second check of reconstructing an implied unit value to assess whether a business case is grounded — a routine worth applying to every figure presented as settled before it is repeated onward.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

Journey: 80,000 start, 64,000 reach the evidence page, 48,000 reach the declaration, 45,600 submit. Ask what proportion of those reaching the declaration submitted.

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 45,600÷48,000=95%. This tests identifying the group the question names as the denominator — option A uses 80,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked, and at this level being able to establish the actual loss points (16,000 before evidence, 16,000 before declaration) in seconds redirects a team's plan in the meeting.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that hides a split**

Average time on a guidance page is 45 seconds; sessions show participants either glancing for a few seconds or reading carefully for two to three minutes. Ask for the most useful response.

- A) The average is wrong.
- B) The average is probably correct and uninformative: it sits between two clusters and describes neither, so ask for the distribution — and note that a single edit may serve one group at the other's expense, which is the argument for structure rather than length.
- C) The participants were unrepresentative.
- D) The page should be shorter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A mean landing between two behavioural clusters describes neither group. This tests asking for the distribution before acting — shortening the page (D, the instruction a stakeholder gives after seeing only the average) helps glancers and removes what careful readers needed.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

A page's usefulness score rose from 50% to 62%. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) A 12% increase.
- B) A 12 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 24% relative increase.
- C) A 24 percentage point increase.
- D) A 19.4% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 62−50=12 points; 12÷50=24% relative. This tests labelling both figures correctly and, at this level, establishing a standing reporting convention requiring both — protecting teams from the accusation of choosing the flattering framing.

**Question 12 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A team proposes 25 moderated sessions before and after a rewrite; baseline 60%, with a 5-point improvement meaningful. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 50 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 25 per group is one or two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users and spending the 50 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 40 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 25 per group cannot reliably detect a 5-point difference from chance. This tests recognising an underpowered comparison and redirecting to the right method for each question — option D is particularly damaging at this level, setting a precedent that content effectiveness can be judged on a handful of sessions, later used against a team's genuine improvement.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate annualises or otherwise scales a figure appropriately before using it in a resourcing argument.
- Note whether the candidate weights an average by group size rather than averaging group averages unweighted.
- Watch for whether the candidate reconstructs or sanity-checks an implied unit value in a claimed business case before accepting it.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from relative percentage change and applies both consistently as a reporting standard.
- Note whether the candidate assesses whether a proposed sample size can actually detect the effect it claims to measure.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a percentage change computed against the new value instead of the original.
- Missing when a candidate averages group averages unweighted rather than by group size.
- Rewarding a resourcing or business case claim accepted without reconstructing its implied unit value.
- Accepting a fall in a metric (views, time on page) treated automatically as bad without considering the content's actual purpose.
- Missing when a candidate endorses an underpowered comparison as sufficient to detect a stated effect.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. For most disciplines it is a proxy skill; for this one it is close to the job itself, which makes it the section where a candidate should score best and where a settled habit costs most.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say — regardless of professional knowledge, which actively costs marks at this level, since the more governance prose a candidate has translated, the more readily they supply the clause they expect rather than the one actually present.

The payoff is direct: teams are handed policy prose and asked what it requires so they can write the plain-language version, and this role assures the result. Getting it wrong in either direction is serious.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to assuring content quality across whole teams.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **User focus (content design)**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to the core task teams perform and this role assures.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: knowing what is required versus recommended tells the organisation where it has room.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **User focus**: noticing a source never states a timescale is what stops a page being published without one.
- **Reading stakeholder and governance prose accurately** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "All content published on the service must be reviewed against the style guide before publication, unless an exemption has been agreed in writing by the head of content design. Where content is a factual correction of published material, it may be published immediately and reviewed within five working days. Content including legal wording supplied by the legal team must not be altered without the legal team's agreement, although surrounding explanatory content may be edited freely. All published content is reviewed annually."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Every piece of content must be reviewed against the style guide before publication."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two distinct routes around the general rule exist — a written exemption, and immediate publication of factual corrections. This tests recognising the statement's "every" is contradicted twice over, and, in a strong answer, distinguishing an exemption (removing the requirement) from a deferral (delaying it), which the next questions turn on.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Legal wording may never be altered."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage prohibits alteration "without the legal team's agreement," permitting it with agreement. This tests recognising a conditional prohibition rather than an absolute one — content designers who believe legal wording is untouchable never ask, and legal teams are often willing to agree clearer formulations.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A factual correction is exempt from style guide review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A factual correction "may be published immediately and reviewed within five working days" — the obligation is deferred, not removed. This tests distinguishing an exemption from a deferral — a team believing corrections are exempt will publish and never review them, producing content that never passes through the style guide at all.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "A page containing legal wording is reviewed annually."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The final sentence applies to all published content without exception. This tests noticing the annual review carries no carve-out for legal content despite the strong alteration constraint — reviewing is not altering, and the passage keeps them separate.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The review of content practice across the department found considerable variation in how teams assure quality. Four teams operate peer review before publication; the remainder rely on individual sign-off. Teams with peer review reported fewer post-publication corrections, although the review did not examine whether their content was more accurate. The review recommends that the head of content design consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Some teams do not operate peer review before publication."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests the baseline reading, confirmed by the passage's own description of what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Peer review produces more accurate content."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer post-publication corrections and explicitly states accuracy was not examined — different findings, since peer-reviewed teams might catch errors before publication rather than being genuinely more accurate. This tests the specific trap of a claim a candidate would like to be true (an argument for a common approach).

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common assurance approach."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the head of content design "consider whether" a common approach would be proportionate, not that one be introduced. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X" — a candidate at this level is likely to be the person the recommendation is addressed to.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "Individual sign-off is a weaker form of assurance than peer review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports one difference in outcome while explicitly declining to assess accuracy, making no judgement about relative strength. This tests resisting the passage's structural lean (peer review mentioned first, associated with a favourable outcome) — noticing implied stance versus actual assertion, which applies directly to review reports generally.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Content must be written so that it can be understood by users with a reading age of 9 where the subject matter allows. Where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided, they must be explained at first use. Content must not rely on colour alone to convey meaning. Headings must describe the content that follows and be used in order without skipping levels. These requirements apply to public-facing content and to internal guidance used by more than fifty staff."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "All content must be understandable by users with a reading age of 9."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The requirement is qualified by "where the subject matter allows." This tests catching a dropped qualifier that must be read exactly — at this level a candidate adjudicates whether a team's claim of difficult subject matter is honest, requiring the rule read precisely rather than as a slogan in either direction.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "Technical terms must be avoided."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage requires explanation "where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided," presupposing such cases exist. This tests reading a conditional rule correctly rather than as a prohibition — producing arguments that a term must be removed when it is merely required to be explained.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: "These requirements apply to internal guidance used by twenty staff."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states where the requirements apply (over fifty staff) without stating they are excluded below that threshold. This tests distinguishing a scoping statement from an exemption — a lead who tells a small team they are exempt when nobody has said so will eventually have to withdraw it.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Content must be written for a reading age of 9.
- B) Public-facing content and widely used internal guidance must be comprehensible and navigable without specialist knowledge or reliance on visual cues — through plain language where the subject allows, explanation where terms are unavoidable, meaning carried in more than colour, and headings that describe and are correctly ordered.
- C) Headings must be used in order.
- D) Technical terms must be explained at first use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage while adding nothing. This tests identifying a summary preserving every qualifier and the scope clause — the exact skill this role assures in others' work.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes an exemption (removes an obligation) from a deferral (delays it) in governance prose.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a conditional prohibition ("not without agreement") from an absolute one ("never").
- Watch for whether the candidate resists responding to a passage's implied stance or structural lean rather than its actual assertions.
- Score for whether Cannot Say is used correctly even where the alternative would support a claim the candidate might otherwise want to make.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes "consider whether X" from "recommend X" in institutional or governance language.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate reads a deferral as an exemption, or a conditional prohibition as absolute.
- Rewarding a scope statement (where a rule applies) converted into an exemption (where it explicitly does not).
- Accepting a response to a passage's implied stance rather than what it actually asserts.
- Missing when a candidate marks True on a claim that happens to support their own case or preference.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do. It measures whether judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. This role directs a team of content designers, assures quality across teams, aligns content to strategy, works with service managers and programme directors to resource teams and resolve problems, and promotes the discipline.

Three features distinguish this level: most of what is achieved happens through other people, so an option solving a problem through personal intervention is usually second-best; this role absorbs pressure downwards, so when a programme director leans on a content designer to publish something they should not, that designer should not hold the line alone; and content quality is unusually hard to argue about, so evidence is the primary instrument.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Assuring quality across teams** maps to this role level's core accountability.
- **Directing and developing a team** maps to **Agile working (content design)** and leading content designers.
- **Resourcing and resolving problems with senior stakeholders** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Aligning content to strategy** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**.
- **Protecting users** maps to **User focus (content design)** and **User-centred content design**.
- **Promoting the discipline** maps to this role level's named engagement with the cross-government community.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A content designer under pressure**

A content designer reports a programme director has asked them to publish before the plain-language review, since a launch is tomorrow, and they are unsure what to do. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Tell them to refuse and cite your instruction.
- B) Work out with them what is actually possible — whether the review can happen in the next hour — and take the conversation with the programme director yourself, since a director pressing a content designer to skip assurance is a matter at your level.
- C) Approve the exception.
- D) Tell them to publish and review afterwards.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Establishing whether a fast review is possible often dissolves the conflict, and taking the conversation personally is what absorbing a power differential means at this level. This tests protecting a junior colleague while ensuring the substantive issue is resolved — option A is least effective, leaving the colleague to refuse a director by proxy.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard nobody follows**

An organisational content standard is routinely ignored by teams who describe it as unrealistic. Ask for the strongest and least effective approach.

- A) Reissue it with a message from you.
- B) Review recent content with the teams to establish which requirements are genuinely unworkable and which merely unclear, revise it with them, and pair it with a lightweight routine — such as reading drafts aloud before sign-off — that gives compliance a moment of application and produces something the teams want anyway.
- C) Introduce mandatory compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the standard.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Treating widespread non-compliance as data and revising with the teams produces authorship, and therefore defenders. This tests fixing a genuinely unworkable standard rather than reissuing it unchanged — option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act while changing nothing.

**Question 3 (easy) — Resourcing a team**

Content designers spread one per service across seven services, pulled into delivery firefighting, no time for critique, rarely see each other; two have left in six months. Ask for the strongest and least effective response.

- A) Discuss workload with each delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as structural: establish a protected routine bringing content designers together, negotiate the time with delivery leadership by showing what the current pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how people are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Single embedding removes a content designer's most effective quality mechanism — a peer to read their drafts — damaging both people and work directly. This tests addressing a structural pattern on multiple fronts — option D is least effective, reproducing the attrition on a lag in a competitive market.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring another team's content**

A team not managed by the candidate has published a live page with a wrong eligibility claim. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Correct it yourself.
- B) Contact the team's content designer and lead immediately with the specific error and the evidence, agree who corrects it and by when, and check whether the same claim appears elsewhere in their service or in yours — while making clear that a live eligibility error needs fixing today rather than in a sprint.
- C) Raise it at the next content community session.
- D) Report it to the service owner.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Going directly to the people who can fix it, agreeing ownership and a deadline, and checking where else the claim appears addresses the actual harm speedily. This tests moving quickly through the right people — option C is least effective, deferring a live factual error to the wrong forum and broadcasting a team's mistake before they can fix it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A stakeholder who will not accept evidence**

A programme director dismisses research showing users don't understand a key term, saying participants were unrepresentative and the term is standard in the sector. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Concede and keep the term.
- B) Acknowledge the sample's limits honestly, point out that a term being standard among practitioners says nothing about the public and is if anything weak evidence the other way, and propose a cheap check the director will accept — the site search log, which shows whether users search for that term or a different one.
- C) Insist on the change.
- D) Escalate to your head of profession.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Conceding the fair part of the objection while dismantling the weak part, then proposing evidence the director will actually accept, converts a disagreement into a resolvable question. This tests separating claims of different quality — option A is least effective, conceding to an objection that does not survive examination.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Developing someone whose work is weak**

A content designer consistently over-writes; general feedback raised twice has not changed this. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Edit their work before it publishes.
- B) Move from general feedback to something structured: sit with them and read one of their own pages aloud, so they hear where a reader would stumble; then agree they will read every draft aloud before sign-off, and review the next one together.
- C) Raise it in their performance conversation.
- D) Move them to work that involves less writing.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Hearing one's own sentence read aloud makes a persistent problem obvious in a way abstract feedback has not. This tests escalating the coaching technique after general feedback has failed twice — option A is least effective, removing the problem from view while guaranteeing it persists and making the candidate a permanent bottleneck.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Content that conflicts with strategy**

A team's content is clear, well tested, popular — and conflicts with the organisation's published strategy description of the service. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Approve it; it works for users.
- B) Establish which description is right before deciding — if the strategy is aspirational and the content describes the service as it is, the content stands and somebody should know the strategy has drifted; if the content misdescribes what the service does, that is an accuracy problem regardless of how well it tests.
- C) Require the content to match the strategy.
- D) Escalate the discrepancy.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Establishing which is factually right resolves the actual question rather than picking a side by default. This tests recognising that quality and alignment can genuinely conflict and require diagnosis — option C is least effective, requiring content to match a document over reality, which users will discover is untrue.

**Question 8 (harder) — Content that may cause harm**

Two days before launch, a page is found to omit that applying may reduce an existing benefit; adding it needs policy sign-off and may delay launch. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Launch and add it in the next update.
- B) Raise it immediately with the service owner, stating specifically that users may reduce their income without knowing, ask for an explicit recorded decision, and have your team draft the wording for policy to approve so the sign-off is as fast as possible.
- C) Have your team add a sentence based on their understanding.
- D) Launch and log it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Drafting the wording for policy to approve removes the commonest cause of sign-off delay. This tests raising a specific financial-harm concern with a ready-made solution — option C is least effective, publishing an unapproved account of a benefit interaction and normalising the practice as authorised by the lead.

**Question 9 (harder) — A recurring problem across teams**

Content across four teams consistently omits timescales, each with a plausible local reason. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Add timescales to the content checklist.
- B) Establish the common cause — most likely that the standard is silent on what to publish when a timescale is uncertain — and fix it, most plausibly by agreeing a form of words such as "we usually reply within X and will tell you if it will take longer", then negotiating with policy once, on all four teams' behalf, for the figures.
- C) Remind the four teams of the requirement.
- D) Accept it; some timescales genuinely cannot be given.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Supplying the wording that makes an uncertain timescale publishable and negotiating once with policy on everyone's behalf is a leadership-level fix a single content designer cannot deliver. This tests identifying and fixing a shared cause once rather than repeatedly — option C is least effective, the weakest available response to any compliance problem.

**Question 10 (harder) — Promoting the discipline**

Asked to explain to a programme board why content design needs three people rather than one, when board members believe content is writing anyone can do. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Explain the discipline and its methods.
- B) Lead with the evidence in their currency — the contact volume attributable to content gaps, the calls avoided by specific past changes, the cost per call — and then explain what three people would do that one cannot, tying each to a measurable outcome.
- C) Point to the service standard's requirements.
- D) Ask the head of profession to make the case.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A board that believes content is writing will not be moved by an explanation of the discipline, since they have already discounted the premise. This tests leading with numbers a board already uses before explaining capacity — option A is least effective for feeling natural, confirming their view this is a craft preference.

**Question 11 (harder) — A failure in something you assured**

A form of words approved six months ago and used across five services is found to misstate a deadline in the two services with a different statutory period. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Note it for the next review.
- B) Notify all five services now, scope precisely which two are affected and which three are not, correct the two immediately, and examine why the approval did not check whether the deadline varied — then change what the approval process asks.
- C) Amend the wording quietly in the two affected services.
- D) Withdraw the agreed wording from all five.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Precise scoping matters as much as disclosure, since three services are actually correct and should not be told otherwise. This tests fixing both the instance and the approval process that let it through — option C is least effective, correcting quietly and leaving the process unchanged for the next shared asset.

**Question 12 (harder) — Cross-government contribution**

A solved content problem (a tested, agreed form of words explaining a common benefit rule) is known to affect several other departments. Ask for the most and least effective response.

- A) Use it in your services and move on.
- B) Share it through the cross-government content community with the evidence behind it and an honest note about what was and was not tested, and offer to work with anyone adapting it — accepting that others may improve it, which is the point.
- C) Publish it as a pattern for others to adopt.
- D) Offer it to the departments you know personally.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Sharing the evidence and an honest note about testing limits lets others judge whether it transfers, without acquiring unearned authority. This tests active, generous, evidence-honest contribution to the wider discipline — option A is least effective, keeping a solution that cost effort while several departments repeat the work.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate absorbs pressure from senior stakeholders personally rather than leaving a junior colleague to hold a contested position alone.
- Note whether the candidate negotiates once on behalf of several teams facing the same recurring problem, rather than letting each have the same conversation separately.
- Watch for whether a process failure behind an assured error is fixed, not just the instance corrected.
- Score for whether the candidate scopes their own errors precisely rather than over- or under-correcting across an entire shared asset.
- Note whether a resourcing or discipline-promotion argument is made in the audience's own currency (numbers, cost, outcomes) rather than an explanation of the craft.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that leaves a junior colleague to defend a contested position against a senior stakeholder alone.
- Missing when a candidate fixes a symptom or instance rather than the systemic or process cause behind a recurring problem.
- Rewarding a resourcing argument that explains the discipline rather than leading with evidence in the audience's terms.
- Accepting an over- or under-scoped correction to a shared asset (a form of words, a pattern) used across multiple services.
- Missing when a candidate keeps a solved cross-government problem to themselves rather than sharing it with its evidence and limitations.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through diagnostic reasoning at a distance — tracing a compliant, well-written page's call volume to something outside the page, diagnosing several teams' shared failure as one systemic cause with several rationalisations, and recognising a heavily used recovery page as a measure of a shared component's failure rate; the arithmetic that turns a content argument into a resourcing decision — annualising, weighting by group size, reconstructing an implied unit value, and recognising that a fall in a metric can be success; the discipline of reading governance and policy prose at exactly the strength the text supports, distinguishing an exemption from a deferral and a scope statement from an exclusion; and the situational judgement that defines this level — absorbing pressure downward, negotiating once on behalf of several teams, and fixing a process rather than only the instance it produced.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — this role's authority rests on evidence far more than craft judgement, and the errors that cost most at this level are made fluently, in areas where experience runs deep enough that checking feels unnecessary. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
