# Associate Content Designer - Psychometric Assessments

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for associate content designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the everyday thinking a particular job actually uses. For an associate content designer that means checking a draft against a style guide and spotting where it diverges, noticing that a page tells users what happened but not what to do next, reading research findings and working out what they mean for a piece of content, interpreting a set of numbers about a page, and making sensible decisions when a stakeholder asks for something unclear.

An associate content designer is a trainee in an entry-level position, working under supervision, expected to show aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role, and to perform basic standard checks and copy edits. Nobody at this level is expected to have redesigned an end-to-end journey or negotiated with a policy team over a legal phrase — the assessment is calibrated to aptitude, potential, understanding and careful checking, and every rule, definition and figure a candidate needs appears inside the question.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and verbal items for whether the candidate concludes only what a stated rule or set of facts guarantees, resisting a plausible-but-unproven answer. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward a response that acts within remit and is transparent at its edges — attempting what can competently be done and promptly telling the right person about what cannot — over either silent struggling or reflexive escalation; the strongest answer at this level is very often one that asks for guidance.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking this role genuinely demands, using practical workplace materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences — a style guide, a draft page, a content inventory, a publishing checklist, a set of user research observations, a search log.

Typical format: an online test lasting fifteen to thirty minutes, with questions clustering into pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a similar level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

For an associate content designer this section matters because so much of the early value of the role comes from noticing: a heading that is a statement where the style guide requires a question, two pages using different words for the same thing, a page that tells users their application has been received but not what happens next, a sentence using a term no member of the public would search for. Each is a small act of comparison or deduction.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **User-centred content design**: showing awareness of style standards means recognising when content follows an established pattern and, more importantly, when it quietly departs from one.
- **Error checking** maps to **User-centred content design** and to the basic standard checks and copy edits this role level names explicitly.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **User focus (content design)**: seeing the purpose of user stories and focusing on user needs means reasoning correctly from evidence rather than jumping to conclusions the evidence does not support.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile working (content design)**: working in an Agile team means constantly deciding what to do first, adapting to feedback, and being transparent about why.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**: exploring ideas about how content might work before committing to it.
- **Understanding what a request is really for** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: knowing what stakeholders find important is the foundation of a useful working relationship.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking against a style guide**

A style guide says page titles must: start with a verb, use sentence case with only proper nouns capitalised, contain no full stop, and be no longer than eight words. Four titles: (1) "Apply for a fishing licence"; (2) "Renew Your Passport"; (3) "Check if you need to pay tax on money you receive from abroad"; (4) "How to report a lost driving licence." Ask how many titles break at least one rule.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Title 1 is compliant; title 2 breaks sentence case ("Your Passport"); title 3 is thirteen words, breaking the length rule; title 4 starts with "How" rather than a verb and ends with a full stop, breaking two rules. Three titles break at least one rule. This tests checking every rule against every item rather than stopping at the first fault, and distinguishing "how many titles" from "how many rules" — there are four breaches across three titles, and confusing the two produces D.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a publishing rule**

Team rule: "Content may only be published if it has been second-checked by another content designer, unless it is a factual correction approved by the content lead." A page was published this morning and was not second-checked. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The team broke the rule.
- B) The page was a factual correction approved by the content lead.
- C) Either the page was an approved factual correction, or the rule was broken.
- D) The page contains an error.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one permitted exception, and the facts do not establish whether the exception applies. This tests deducing only what the given facts guarantee, rather than assuming a branch (A, B) or introducing an unsupported claim (D, an unchecked page is not automatically a wrong page).

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition across feedback**

Five pages of the same service, similar traffic, "No" responses to "Did you find what you needed?": Page A 7%, Page B 8%, Page C 34%, Page D 9%, Page E 6%. Ask for the most reasonable first interpretation of Page C.

- A) Page C is badly written and should be rewritten immediately.
- B) Page C is an outlier worth investigating — the cause could be the content, its position in the journey, a broken link, or something else entirely.
- C) The other four pages must be unusually good, so Page C is probably fine.
- D) The survey is unreliable and should be ignored.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four pages cluster between 6% and 9%; Page C sits at four times the cluster. This tests treating an outlier as a prompt for investigation rather than a conclusion — option A jumps to a solution before understanding the problem, and rewriting a page whose real fault is a broken link achieves nothing.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation of a morning**

At 9:30am, four items: (1) a live page states the wrong deadline for a benefit application, users may act on it today; (2) a 10:00 stand-up with an unupdated ticket; (3) a colleague's draft to review this afternoon; (4) the weekly content community newsletter. Ask what should happen first.

- A) Update the ticket so the board is accurate.
- B) Raise or fix the wrong deadline on the live page.
- C) Read the colleague's draft while it is fresh.
- D) Read the newsletter before the day gets busy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A wrong deadline on a live page can cause real users to miss an application today, a serious and irreversible harm. This tests weighing impact and urgency together, ranking harm to users above internal tidiness and internal tidiness above nice-to-haves — an associate may not fix the page alone, but raising it comes first regardless.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking against instructions**

A senior content designer requested exactly three changes: (i) "commence" to "start"; (ii) "in order to" to "to"; (iii) add a bullet list of three required documents. A peer's updated draft shows all three changes made, plus the page title changed from "Apply for a permit" to "Permit applications." Ask how many of the changes made were requested.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Four changes were made and three were requested; the title change was not on the list. This tests distinguishing what was requested from what was done — an unrequested title change is worth querying since it no longer starts with a verb, breaking the style rule from Question 1, and may break a link or navigation label elsewhere.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about what content must say**

A confirmation page rule requires stating: what has happened, what happens next, when it will happen, and how to get help. A page reads: "We have received your application. We will contact you." Ask for the most accurate assessment.

- A) The page meets all four requirements.
- B) The page states what has happened and what happens next, but not when or how to get help.
- C) The page states only what has happened.
- D) The page fails all four requirements.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both "what has happened" and "what happens next" are met (minimally); no timescale or help route is given. This tests checking every requirement against the text rather than judging the wording's overall quality — option C is a tempting near-miss for anyone treating "we will contact you" as too vague to count as "what happens next," which the rule as stated does not require.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in search data**

Site search terms over one month: "how long does it take" 1,850, "contact" 1,420, "check my application" 1,190, "cancel" 240, "fee" 180. Ask what this pattern most strongly suggests.

- A) The site search is working well.
- B) Users are looking for information about progress, timescales and how to reach someone — which suggests the service is not telling them these things where they expect to find them.
- C) The service should add a cancellation page.
- D) Users do not care about fees.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The top three terms — all versions of "what is happening with my thing" — account for around 4,460 of 4,880 searches. This tests reading a concentration of related search terms as evidence of a missing answer on the page, rather than reading heavy search as success (A) or an unsupported inference from a small figure (D — a low fee-search count could equally mean the fee is already clearly stated).

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking against plain language rules**

Standard: content must use words users would use, explain any term at first use, use active voice, and address the user as "you." Sentence: "Applicants whose circumstances have altered must ensure that notification is submitted to the department within the prescribed period." Ask how many parts of the standard it breaks.

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** "Applicants," "altered," "notification," "prescribed period" are not everyday words; "prescribed period" (the most important information) is unexplained; "notification is submitted" is passive; "applicants" replaces "you." This tests checking each of four rules independently against a dense sentence — a rewrite ("If your circumstances change, tell us within 28 days") is shorter and supplies the deadline the original concealed.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving in a review**

A page is well written, follows the style guide, and answers the question in its heading — but search data and the contact centre both suggest users arrive wanting something else. Ask, as the most junior person in the team, for the most sensible thing to do.

- A) Approve the page; it meets the standard.
- B) Raise the observation with a senior content designer, describing what the search and contact data suggest users are actually asking, and ask whether the page is answering the right question.
- C) Rewrite the page around the question thought to be the right one.
- D) Note it for the next content review in three months.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A page can satisfy every style rule and still fail, because style compliance is about how something is written and this is about what it says. This tests raising an unconfirmed observation through the right channel with evidence attached, framed as a question — option A is the trap of conflating rule-compliance with rightness, and C acts beyond remit on an unconfirmed diagnosis.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Deduction about what evidence supports**

In research with nine participants, seven did not understand the phrase "relevant supporting evidence." Ask which conclusion is best supported by this evidence alone.

- A) 78% of users do not understand the phrase.
- B) The phrase should be deleted.
- C) Seven of nine participants did not understand the phrase, which is a strong signal that it needs replacing with wording that says specifically what documents are needed.
- D) All government content should avoid the word "evidence."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Seven of nine is a strong qualitative signal, stated at that strength, with the mechanism (users did not know what the phrase referred to) pointing at a concrete replacement. This tests stating evidence at its actual strength rather than converting nine participants into a population rate (A), proposing deletion without a replacement (B), or generalising to all content from one phrase in one context (D).

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation with competing pressures**

Senior content designer away; at 10:00: (1) a developer needs final wording for an error message today to finish a story; (2) a content review at 14:00, page not ready; (3) a policy colleague needs confirmation of a legal phrase by 12:00 for a publication; (4) a colleague asked for a draft review this week. Ask for the most sensible order.

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — your own commitments first.
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — unblock the developer, then the policy colleague, then your page, then the review.
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4 — deadlines in time order.
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — as listed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 blocks another person's work today and takes minutes; item 3 has a hard 12:00 deadline and also blocks somebody, plus may need an honest "I'm not the right person to confirm this" said in good time; item 2 is important and can absorb the earlier interruptions; item 4 has a week. This tests ranking by consequence and deadline together, rather than by ownership (A) or by strict time order alone (C, which puts a minutes-long unblocking task behind a 12:00 deadline).

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about a difficult phrase**

A policy team insists a page must contain the exact phrase "you may be liable to a financial penalty," saying it is legally required; research shows users do not understand what it means or what triggers it. Ask, as the most junior person involved, for the strongest analysis.

- A) The phrase must stay, so nothing can be done.
- B) The phrase may need to appear, but nothing prevents the page also explaining in plain words what it means and when it applies — so the useful question is whether the requirement is that this wording appears, or that this wording is all that appears.
- C) The phrase should be replaced with plain language regardless.
- D) The page should link to the legislation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Legal requirements are almost always that certain wording appears, almost never a prohibition on saying anything else, and the apparent deadlock usually dissolves once someone asks. This tests separating a requirement to include wording from a prohibition on explaining it, framed as a question appropriate to raise at this level — option C proposes overriding a legal requirement the candidate is not positioned to override.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate checks every stated rule against every item rather than stopping at the first fault found.
- Note whether the candidate deduces only what given facts guarantee, resisting a plausible but unproven conclusion.
- Watch for whether prioritisation weighs impact and urgency together, rather than ranking by ownership or a fixed list order.
- Score for whether the candidate treats an outlier or anomaly as a prompt to investigate rather than an immediate conclusion.
- Note whether evidence is stated at its actual strength (a raw count from a small sample) rather than converted into a population percentage.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a "how many" answer that stops at the first fault rather than checking every item against every rule.
- Missing when a candidate confuses a count of items with a count of breaches.
- Rewarding a prioritisation ranked by ownership ("my own deadlines first") rather than consequence.
- Accepting a small-sample finding converted into a population percentage.
- Missing when a candidate approves or accepts a page as correct because it meets the style guide, without checking whether it answers the right question.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how well a candidate works with the numbers this role produces. It is not a maths exam — there are no equations to solve and calculators are usually provided. It tests whether a candidate can read a table correctly, pick the right numbers out of a cluttered display, choose the right operation, and interpret the result sensibly.

Typical format: a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item showing a small table, chart, or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question, often around a minute per item.

Content design runs on numbers more than it looks: page views to know what people reach, search terms to know what they cannot find, contact centre volumes to know what a page failed to explain, feedback scores compared across pages. At associate level the content is deliberately modest — percentages, fractions, proportions, simple averages, ratios, and reading values from tables.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **User focus (content design)**: gaining insights from data is part of understanding what users need.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **User focus** and **User-centred content design**: comparing how pages perform is how content teams decide what to fix first.
- **Measuring change** maps to **Agile working (content design)**: adapting and being responsive to feedback means knowing whether a change helped.
- **Understanding scale** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: knowing how many people a piece of content affects is what makes a case for spending time on it.
- **Interpreting figures cautiously** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: a claim built on a misread number is hard to defend twice.
- **Spotting an implausible figure** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**: testing an idea starts with trusting the right data.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Feedback rate**

Of 2,400 users who saw a feedback prompt, 600 answered "No" to "Did you find what you needed?" Ask what percentage answered No.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 600÷2,400=25%. This tests basic percentage calculation, checkable by estimating a quarter first (1,200 would be half), and, in a strong candidate's answer, recognising this is the percentage of people who answered the prompt, not of everyone who saw the page.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing pages fairly**

Page A: 8,000 views, 400 "No" responses. Page B: 2,000 views, 300 "No" responses. Page C: 5,000 views, 250 "No" responses. Ask which page has the highest rate of "No" responses.

- A) Page A
- B) Page B
- C) Page C
- D) They are equal

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A: 5%; B: 15%; C: 5%. This tests converting to rates before comparing pages with very different traffic — the raw counts would tell the opposite story (Page A has the most "No" responses in absolute terms), which is precisely the trap, and both readings (total harm versus proportional harm) answer different questions worth distinguishing explicitly.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a search log**

Site searches: "how long" 900, "contact us" 750, "cancel" 150, "fee" 120, "appeal" 80. Ask what percentage of these searches are for "how long."

- A) 35%
- B) 40%
- C) 45%
- D) 50%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total 2,000; 900÷2,000=45%. This tests computing the total from a list before finding a proportion — the step candidates skip when a total is not handed to them directly.

**Question 4 (easy) — Average reading time**

Five pages: reading times of 40, 55, 45, 60, and 50 seconds. Ask for the mean.

- A) 48 seconds
- B) 50 seconds
- C) 52 seconds
- D) 55 seconds

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum 250÷5=50. This tests basic averaging, checkable via a guess-and-adjust shortcut: differences from 50 (−10, +5, −5, +10, 0) sum to zero, confirming the mean is exactly 50.

**Question 5 (easy) — Scale of a content fix**

A page is viewed 500,000 times a year; a missing timescale generates a phone call from an estimated 2% of viewers. Ask how many calls a year adding the timescale could potentially avoid.

- A) 1,000
- B) 10,000
- C) 12,000
- D) 100,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 2%×500,000=10,000. This tests converting a small percentage into an absolute figure — "two per cent of viewers ring up" sounds like a rounding error, while "ten thousand phone calls a year" is the version that gets a sentence prioritised.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before a rewrite: 480 of 1,200 answered "No." After: 350 of 1,400 answered "No." Ask whether the page improved, and by how much in percentage points.

- A) Yes, by 130 responses
- B) Yes, by 15 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 25 percentage points
- D) No, it got worse

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Before 40%, after 25%, improvement 15 points. This tests converting to rates because the totals differ — option A compares raw counts across different-sized groups, which is meaningless here since more people saw the prompt after the change too.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Views of a guidance page fell from 40,000 to 30,000 a month. Ask for the percentage decrease.

- A) 10%
- B) 25%
- C) 33%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (40,000−30,000)÷40,000=25%. This tests dividing by the original value, not the new one — option C divides by 30,000, the commonest error in this question type, and, in a strong answer, noting a fall in views is not automatically bad news for content if the underlying need has gone.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a total**

12% of users abandon a form at the evidence question, and this is 3,600 people a month. Ask how many reach the evidence question each month.

- A) 4,032
- B) 15,000
- C) 30,000
- D) 43,200

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 3,600÷0.12=30,000. This tests working back from a percentage and a count to the base — verifiable by reversing the calculation (12% of 30,000 is 3,600) — a skill that lets a candidate judge whether a quoted percentage deserves the weight it is being given.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Proportion of a whole journey**

Journey page "No" feedback rates: Page 1 4%, Page 2 6%, Page 3 28%, Page 4 5%, Page 5 4%. Ask which statement is best supported.

- A) The journey has a general content problem.
- B) Page 3 stands out sharply against a consistent baseline of 4 to 6%, which points at something specific to that page rather than a journey-wide issue.
- C) The average rate of 9.4% is the useful figure.
- D) Pages 1, 4 and 5 need attention because they are below average.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four pages cluster at 4–6%, establishing a baseline that makes Page 3 interpretable as an outlier. This tests reading a set of figures for its baseline before its exception — option C computes a mean across pages that behave completely differently, concealing the outlier entirely, and D inverts the logic by treating below-average performance as a problem.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Choosing the right denominator**

Journey: 20,000 start, 16,000 reach the evidence page, 12,000 reach the declaration, 11,400 submit. Ask what percentage of users who reached the declaration went on to submit.

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 11,400÷12,000=95%. This tests identifying the group the question actually names before calculating — option A uses 20,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked, a common trap in funnel data with real consequences for which page a team is told to fix.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Before a rewrite, 30% of users answered "Yes" to a page's usefulness question; afterwards, 42% did. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) Usefulness rose by 12%.
- B) Usefulness rose by 12 percentage points, which is a 40% increase.
- C) Usefulness rose by 40 percentage points.
- D) Usefulness rose by 28.6%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 42−30=12 percentage points; 12÷30=40% relative increase. This tests correctly labelling both figures — option A uses "12%" ambiguously (usually read as relative change), and the same underlying change can be described as either "12 percentage points" or "a 40% increase," with the second sounding more than three times as impressive.

**Question 12 (harder) — When an average hides something**

Six pages' word counts: 120, 140, 150, 160, 180, 2,400. A colleague reports "the average page is 525 words." Ask for the best comment.

- A) The calculation is wrong; the average is 155 words.
- B) The calculation is right, but the mean is misleading: five of six pages are between 120 and 180 words and one very long page is pulling the figure up. Report the median of 155 with the range, and treat the 2,400-word page as a separate question — it is probably several pages that have not been split.
- C) The figure is fine as reported.
- D) The 2,400-word page should be excluded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The sum is 3,150÷6=525, so the mean is arithmetically correct (ruling out A) but describes no actual page. This tests recognising when a mean is skewed by an outlier and reporting the median instead, plus treating the outlier itself as a lead — a 2,400-word page among 150-word pages is very likely doing several jobs at once and a candidate for splitting.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate converts raw counts to rates before comparing groups of different sizes.
- Note whether the candidate identifies the correct base or denominator the question actually names before calculating.
- Watch for whether percentage change is measured against the original value consistently, not the new one.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change, and labels each correctly.
- Note whether a mean skewed by an outlier is recognised and reported alongside or replaced by a more representative statistic (the median).

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a comparison of raw counts across groups of different sizes rather than rates.
- Missing when a candidate uses the wrong denominator in a funnel or multi-step data question.
- Rewarding a percentage change computed against the new value instead of the original.
- Accepting "percentage points" and "percentage" used interchangeably without distinguishing them.
- Missing when a candidate reports a mean without checking whether an outlier makes it unrepresentative.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. For most roles that is a useful proxy skill; for this one it is close to the whole job.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily; False means the passage contradicts it; Cannot Say means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it — regardless of what a candidate otherwise knows. Alongside these, comprehension questions, main-point questions, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention because candidates avoid it, reaching for True or False to seem decisive when the honest answer is that the passage does not settle it. Watch the small words, since they carry the meaning: "all" is not "most," "may" is not "must," "should" is not "will," and "where practicable" or "unless" flag conditions governing everything around them.

This assessment has a direct payoff for content design: the role frequently involves being handed a paragraph of policy prose and asked what it actually requires, so that the plain-language version can be written. Getting that wrong in either direction is serious — understate a requirement and users miss something that matters; overstate it and an obligation government does not actually impose gets invented.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **User-centred content design**: awareness of standards means applying them as written.
- **Distinguishing statement from inference** maps to **User focus (content design)**: reasoning from evidence about users must not add to it.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **User-centred content design** and to turning dense source material into content that says what it actually means.
- **Understanding qualified language** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: knowing whether something is required or recommended tells you how much room there is.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **User focus**: noticing that a source never mentions a timescale is what stops a page publishing without one.
- **Following written direction exactly** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: delivering what was actually asked for is the foundation of trust.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "All content published on the service must be reviewed against the style guide before publication. Where content is a factual correction of published material, it may be published immediately and reviewed within five working days. Content that includes legal wording supplied by the legal team must not be altered without the legal team's agreement, although surrounding explanatory content may be edited freely. Content is reviewed annually thereafter."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "All content must be reviewed against the style guide before publication."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The second sentence attaches an exception for factual corrections. This tests reading to the end of a provision before concluding what it requires — the first sentence read alone says exactly what the statement says, which is precisely why the exception two sentences later matters.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Explanatory content around legal wording may be edited without the legal team's agreement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The third sentence restricts the legal wording itself but explicitly permits editing surrounding explanatory content freely. This tests reading a constraint to its precise scope — the constraint attaches to the legal wording, not the page, which is why content design can usually explain a legal phrase even when the wording itself cannot change.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A factual correction may be published before it has been reviewed against the style guide."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Publishing immediately and reviewing within five working days is exactly what the second sentence describes. This tests holding two sentences together — the general rule and its exception — and, in a strong answer, understanding why the exception exists: a live factual error does active harm every hour it stands.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Statement: "Content is never reviewed after publication."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence and the second sentence both establish post-publication review. This tests using the whole passage rather than the sentence that seems most relevant — a reader fixated on the pre-publication requirement might treat review as exclusively pre-publication.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The review of the guidance found that users who read the eligibility page before starting an application were more likely to complete it. The review notes that these users also differed in other respects, including whether they had applied before. The review did not assess whether the eligibility page was accurate. It recommends that the team consider whether the eligibility page should be more prominent."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Users who read the eligibility page were more likely to complete an application."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests the baseline reading, and, in a strong answer, noting the passage's careful "more likely" rather than "usually completed," which the statement respects.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Reading the eligibility page causes users to complete applications."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and flags a confound (previous applicants differing in other respects). This tests recognising declared confounds as a warning against inferring causation — the content-design consequence is direct, since making the page prominent only helps if the causal claim is true.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The eligibility page is accurate."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly states accuracy was not assessed. This tests treating declared silence as establishing nothing — a page could correlate with completion while being inaccurate and actively harmful.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "The review recommends making the eligibility page more prominent."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the team "consider whether" the page should be more prominent — a recommendation to evaluate, not to act. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X," a distinction content designers are unusually likely to encounter when writing up what a review actually said.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Content must be written so that it can be understood by users with a reading age of 9 where the subject matter allows. Where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided, they must be explained at first use. Content must not rely on colour alone to convey meaning. Headings must describe the content that follows them, and must be used in order without skipping levels."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "All content must be understandable by users with a reading age of 9."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The requirement is qualified by "where the subject matter allows." This tests catching a dropped qualifier — the qualifier is not a general excuse, but it does acknowledge some content genuinely cannot reach that level.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "Technical terms must be avoided."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage requires explanation "where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided" — a rule addressing a situation that presupposes the situation occurs. This tests reading a conditional rule correctly rather than as a prohibition — the actual requirement is explanation, not removal.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "A page using a red asterisk to mark required fields, with no other indication, complies with the passage."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Whichever element is treated as the carrier of meaning — the unexplained asterisk symbol or its colour — the meaning is not conveyed accessibly. This tests reasoning through both possible readings of a marginal case rather than accepting a superficially plausible compliance claim; the fix (the word "required" beside the field) removes the question entirely.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Content must be written for a reading age of 9.
- B) Content must be comprehensible and navigable without specialist knowledge or reliance on visual cues — through plain language where possible, explanation where it is not, meaning carried in more than colour, and headings that describe and are correctly ordered.
- C) Headings must be used in order.
- D) Technical terms must be explained at first use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage while adding nothing. This tests identifying a summary broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough not to overstate any one part — options A, C and D are each a true detail promoted too far, and B is careful to preserve the qualifier ("where possible") that A discards.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a provision before concluding what it requires, rather than stopping at an early absolute-sounding sentence.
- Note whether the candidate treats a declared confound or a declared gap in what was assessed as a genuine limit on what can be concluded.
- Watch for whether qualifiers ("where the subject matter allows," "unless," "may") are preserved rather than dropped when a statement is judged.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes "consider whether X" from "recommend X" in institutional prose.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary that covers the whole passage rather than one true detail promoted above the rest.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate avoids Cannot Say even where the passage is genuinely silent.
- Rewarding a dropped qualifier ("where the subject matter allows," "where X cannot be avoided") treated as though the rule were absolute.
- Accepting an association read as causation despite a declared confound in the passage.
- Missing when a candidate selects a true-but-narrow detail as the main point rather than a summary covering the whole passage.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and specifically whether a candidate's instincts align with what the profession considers good practice at this level — the same scenario has different best answers for a trainee and for a head of profession. An associate content designer who overrules a policy colleague is overstepping; one who never raises anything is under-performing.

Four principles carry most scenarios: users come first, specifically users who will be misled, delayed or excluded by what a page does or does not say; act within remit and be transparent at its edges — the pattern is attempt, then tell; address problems directly and early, with the person involved; and ask what a request is for, since stakeholders very often ask for a specific sentence when what they need is a specific outcome.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Protecting users from misleading or missing content** maps to **User focus (content design)** and **User-centred content design**.
- **Explaining a content decision** maps to **User-centred content design** and **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Handling requests and constraints** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Working in cycles and responding to feedback** maps to **Agile working (content design)**.
- **Exploring how content might work** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**.
- **Understanding where content fits** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Not knowing how to do the task**

A senior content designer asks for "a content audit of the renewals journey by Thursday"; the candidate knows roughly what a content audit is but has never done one. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Say yes, then work it out alone from articles online.
- B) Say yes, and add that this has not been done before and half an hour of guidance would be really useful before starting.
- C) Say it cannot be done because of not having been trained.
- D) Say yes, and quietly ask another associate to help rather than telling the senior.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** This role level names working under supervision and developing skills, so asking for guidance is doing the job correctly. This tests combining willingness with an honest, small, specific request — option C is least effective, declining a development opportunity and handing the work back with nothing offered.

**Question 2 (easy) — Something noticed in live content**

A live page tells users to send documents to an address that changed six months ago. Ask, as the most junior person in the team, for the most effective response.

- A) Say nothing; someone would have noticed by now.
- B) Raise it immediately with a senior content designer, stating clearly that the address on a live page is wrong and users may be posting documents to the wrong place today.
- C) Correct the page directly.
- D) Note it for the next content review.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Documents posted to a former address may be lost entirely, with affected users unaware for weeks. This tests raising a live error immediately, stating the consequence rather than just the fault — option A is least effective, "someone would have noticed" being exactly how errors survive for months.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

A senior content designer requested the content audit finished this morning; a delivery manager then asks for demo copy this morning, calling it urgent. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Do what the delivery manager asked, because they are more senior.
- B) Do what the senior content designer asked and tell the delivery manager you are busy.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask the two of them to confirm the priority.
- D) Try to do both by working through lunch.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Resolving the conflict is not the candidate's call, but surfacing it promptly with a concrete option is. This tests offering a specific workable option rather than bare escalation — option D is least effective, hiding the conflict, setting an unsustainable expectation, and leaving nobody aware the team is over-committing.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A stakeholder asks for specific wording**

A policy colleague asks for a paragraph in the language of a regulation itself, though research shows users do not understand that language. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Add the paragraph as requested.
- B) Ask what the paragraph needs to achieve — whether it is that users understand the rule, that the department is legally protected, or that a specific form of words appears — because those three needs have different solutions and only one requires that exact wording.
- C) Refuse and write a plain-language version instead.
- D) Add the paragraph and a plain-language version underneath.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Establishing what the request is actually for is the characteristic content design move, and the colleague will often agree once it is said aloud. This tests distinguishing genuinely different underlying needs before deciding the format — option A is least effective, publishing something users demonstrably do not understand while believing there was no choice.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Feedback on your own work**

A senior content designer returns a draft with most of it rewritten and one comment: "too wordy." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the rewrite and move on.
- B) Compare the two versions carefully, work out what specifically was cut and why, then ask a couple of targeted questions about the changes you cannot account for.
- C) Ask them to explain every change.
- D) Point out that some of the removed detail was requested by the policy team.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Comparing versions line by line explains most changes and leaves a short list of genuinely puzzling ones. This tests extracting the pattern from feedback before asking about it — option A is least effective, since accepting the rewrite without extracting the pattern means repeating the same choices and receiving the same rewrite next time.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A pattern noticed across pages**

Auditing finds four pages each explaining the same eligibility rule in four different ways, two of which contradict each other. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Fix the two that contradict each other.
- B) Document all four versions with their differences, raise it with a senior content designer, and flag that at least two are inconsistent so somebody with authority to establish the correct rule can do so.
- C) Pick the clearest version and make all four match it.
- D) Note it in the audit and move on.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** At least one page is wrong and nobody currently knows which. This tests documenting a genuine inconsistency rather than deciding it — option C is least effective, since making all four consistent could propagate a wrong rule to four pages instead of one, worse than the original inconsistency.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pressure to publish quickly**

A delivery manager asks for a page to be published today, before the second check the team's process requires, because a campaign launches tomorrow. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Publish it; the deadline is real.
- B) Explain that the check exists and ask what is possible — whether someone can second-check it in the next hour, or whether the page could go live tomorrow morning — and raise it with a senior content designer if no route works.
- C) Refuse and cite the process.
- D) Publish it and arrange the check afterwards without telling anyone.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The deadline and the check are both real, and asking what is possible frequently resolves the tension. This tests engaging with a genuine constraint rather than skipping it or citing it rigidly — option D is least effective, concealing that a page is unchecked and making a decision that was not the candidate's to make.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Content that is not yours**

A confirmation letter, handled by another team, uses completely different wording and a different timescale from the confirmation page. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Ignore it; letters are not your responsibility.
- B) Document the discrepancy — particularly the different timescales, since one of them must be wrong — and raise it with a senior content designer, asking whether there is a route to the team that owns the letter.
- C) Contact the other team directly.
- D) Change the confirmation page to match the letter.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two different timescales for the same thing means at least one is a factual error a user has no way to resolve. This tests documenting a discrepancy and seeking a legitimate route through it — option A is least effective, describing an org chart while the user experiences one contradictory service.

**Question 9 (harder) — Content that may cause harm**

Two days before launch, a page describing a benefit is found not to mention it may affect a different benefit already received; adding it needs policy sign-off and may delay launch. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Launch and add it in the next content update.
- B) Raise it immediately with a senior content designer and the delivery manager, stating specifically that users may take an action that reduces their income without knowing, and ask for a decision.
- C) Add a sentence yourself based on your own understanding of the interaction.
- D) Launch and note the gap in the content backlog.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A financial-harm decision belongs to people with authority to weigh it against a launch date, but raising it precisely and now is entirely within remit. This tests raising a specific, actionable financial-harm concern — option C is the trap for a conscientious trainee, writing unconfirmed content about a benefit interaction and risking publishing something wrong about someone's money.

**Question 10 (harder) — Being asked to soften a message**

A stakeholder asks to change "you must tell us within 28 days or you may have to pay a penalty" to "please let us know as soon as you can," saying the original sounds threatening. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Make the change; the tone matters.
- B) Explain that the original tells users two things they need — the deadline and the consequence — and that removing both to soften the tone leaves people at risk of a penalty they were never warned about; then offer to work on the tone while keeping the deadline and the consequence.
- C) Refuse and keep the original.
- D) Change it to "please tell us within 28 days."

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The tone concern is legitimate and the proposed solution removes the two pieces of information the sentence exists to convey. This tests separating a legitimate concern from a flawed proposed fix — option A is least effective, leaving a user who responds late facing an unwarned penalty.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

A page published last week states a fee of £75 when the correct fee is £57. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct it quietly and say nothing.
- B) Correct it immediately if possible, tell a senior content designer straight away, and flag that users may have been given the wrong figure for a week and may need to be contacted.
- C) Correct it and mention it at the next stand-up.
- D) Check with someone else first whether it is really wrong.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A wrong fee published for a week means users may have budgeted or decided based on incorrect information. This tests disclosing an own error promptly and surfacing whether affected users need contacting — option A is least effective, since a later-discovered concealment becomes the thing people remember rather than the original typo.

**Question 12 (harder) — An assumption in the room**

In a planning session, someone says "we don't need to worry about people who can't read this — they can always ring up," while the contact centre is only open 9 to 5 weekdays and the service is used heavily by shift workers. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go; the team has decided.
- B) Bring the specific facts into the room — that the contact centre is 9 to 5 on weekdays and many users work shifts — and ask what should happen to someone who cannot read the page and cannot ring during those hours.
- C) Argue that all content must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately with a senior content designer afterwards.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The comment rests on an assumption that a phone line is a universal fallback, which collapses against specific facts. This tests contributing concrete information rather than an opinion, at the moment a decision is being made — option A is least effective, letting a factually wrong assumption stand when the facts to correct it were available.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate attempts what can be competently handled and promptly tells the right person about what cannot, rather than struggling silently or escalating reflexively.
- Note whether the candidate states the consequence of a problem, not just the fault, when raising it.
- Watch for whether the candidate asks what a stakeholder's request is actually for before deciding how to satisfy it.
- Score for whether own errors are disclosed promptly, with a check on whether affected users need further action.
- Note whether the candidate resists making inconsistent content "consistent" before establishing which version is actually correct.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting silence justified by newness ("someone would have noticed").
- Missing when a candidate writes unconfirmed content, especially about money or entitlement, rather than raising the gap.
- Rewarding publishing outside an agreed process without disclosing it.
- Accepting a message softened by removing the substantive information it exists to convey.
- Missing when a candidate delays disclosure of their own mistake.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and error checking against a style guide, a publishing rule, and plain language standards; the arithmetic that makes content decisions defensible — converting counts to rates before comparing pages, distinguishing percentage points from percentage change, and recognising when a mean hides an outlier worth investigating; the discipline of reading policy prose, guidance and stakeholder emails at exactly the strength the text supports; and the situational judgement that defines a strong associate content designer — raising problems early with their consequence stated, asking what a request is really for, and disclosing an own mistake promptly rather than concealing it.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several situational and verbal items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answer is very often the one that asks for guidance rather than acting alone. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
