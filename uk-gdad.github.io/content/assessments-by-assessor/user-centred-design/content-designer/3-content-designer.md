# Content Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for content designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A content designer creates, evaluates, updates and reviews content at all stages of the end-to-end user journey: comfortable using evidence, data and research to make content decisions, building relationships across government to focus on user needs and influence stakeholders, contributing to and using style guides and content patterns, and engaging with the cross-government content community. This is a role defined by judgement and influence as much as by craft.

Three features of the role shape what the assessment is built to test. First, this role is comfortable using evidence to make content decisions — the assessment tests whether a candidate knows what a piece of evidence supports, not merely whether they can read it. Second, this role influences stakeholders across government, a negotiating skill resting on unassailable evidence and explicable reasoning. Third, this role works across the end-to-end journey, so the unit of responsibility is rarely the page in front of them — it is the sequence of things a person reads, including the letter, the email, the error message, and what the contact centre says.

Content design is routinely mistaken for writing, and much of the actual work is deciding what should exist. The strongest content decisions are often deletions, reorderings, or the recognition that a page should not exist because the question it answers should never have arisen.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate reasons about causes rather than only symptoms, and states evidence at its actual strength rather than converting a small sample into a population figure. Situational judgement items consistently reward a response that asks what a stakeholder's request is actually for, fixes the instance and then addresses the source, and protects the accuracy of published content over the comfort of the moment.

This assessment is created by AI and is a work in progress for educational purposes. It is not a validated assessment instrument — it has not been piloted, normed or checked for adverse impact — and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision. Before using it, consult your organisation's human resources, people development, occupational psychology or legal compliance colleagues.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At this level the materials get messier and the reasoning less mechanical — evidence supporting two incompatible readings, constraints that turn out to be negotiable and others that do not, and prioritisation problems with no dominant option.

Typical format: an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

Two things distinguish this section at this level. First, reasoning about causes rather than only symptoms — a page generating phone calls, a search term appearing constantly, a step where people abandon are all symptoms, and the useful question is what produces them; frequently the answer is not in the content at all, but in a policy, a form, or a letter sent three weeks earlier. Second, comfort with irreducible uncertainty — several questions have no clean answer, only a best one, and the discriminating factor is whether a candidate can identify the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence and state its limits.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **User focus (content design)**: gaining insights from data and evidence is how content decisions get made.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **User-centred content design**: a content decision explained on an overreaching claim does not survive its first challenge.
- **Error checking** maps to **User-centred content design** and using and contributing to style guides and content patterns.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile working (content design)**: working in short cycles means constantly deciding what to do first.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**: exploring how content might work before committing.
- **Tracing symptoms to causes** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: seeing beyond the immediate task.
- **Reasoning about positions** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: influencing stakeholders begins with understanding what they are protecting.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Standard: every transactional page must use words users would use, say what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid asking for information already held, and offer a route to help. A page uses internal terminology, says "the case will be assessed," gives no timescale, re-requests a held reference number, and has a "contact us" link in the footer. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 4
- D) 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Internal terminology fails requirement one; "the case will be assessed" satisfies requirement two, minimally; no timescale fails three; re-requesting held information fails four; the footer link technically satisfies five as written. Three unmet. This tests applying a standard exactly as written even where it feels thinly satisfied — option C typically comes from rejecting the footer link on a defensible content judgement that is the wrong audit judgement, since if a footer link satisfies the standard, the standard itself needs revising, not the audit result.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a publishing rule**

Rule: "Content may be published without second review only if it is a factual correction approved by the content lead." A colleague published a page this morning without second review. Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The rule was breached.
- B) It was a factual correction approved by the content lead.
- C) Either it was an approved factual correction, or the rule was breached.
- D) The page contains an error.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is conditional with a stated exception, and the given facts do not establish which branch applies. This tests recognising the precise logical shape of "only if" — the sole route to compliance is the exception — as distinct from "unless," which places the exception differently in the rule's structure, resisting an unsupported assumption in either direction (A, B) or an unsupported import (D).

**Question 3 (moderate) — Tracing a symptom to its cause**

A page explaining eligibility generates the highest contact volume in the service, is well written, follows the style guide, and tested well in research. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The page must have a problem the research missed.
- B) A page that reads well and still generates calls is often answering the question correctly for a rule that is genuinely complicated, or arriving after users have formed an expectation elsewhere — so the useful next step is to find out what callers actually ask, which may point at the policy, at an earlier letter, or at a search result that brings people here with the wrong question.
- C) The contact centre should be given a script.
- D) The page should be rewritten more simply.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The combination — well written, well tested, still generating calls — rules out the obvious explanations and points outside the page. This tests identifying the cheapest way to distinguish causes (asking what callers actually say) rather than assuming the research was wrong (A), treating the symptom (C), or rewriting a page that already tests well (D), where a great deal of content effort is wasted.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritising by what can change**

Capacity for one substantial piece of work this quarter: (1) a new service's core journey content, being written next month, setting the pattern for eight related pages; (2) a live page with steady contact volume for a year; (3) exploratory work for a policy team's concept for next year; (4) revisiting a page written six weeks ago after minor design changes. Ask which is strongest, and why.

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will set a pattern for eight pages, and content input is the only thing that could still shape them.
- C) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.
- D) Item 4, because unverified changes are a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 scores highest on both timing and a compounding multiplier — a pattern repeated across eight pages establishes either a good pattern or a bad one eight times, and undoing an established pattern later is far harder than getting it right now. This tests weighing decisions by how much they change as a result of input and by repetition — option A ranks by current impact alone and misses the compounding effect, exactly the strategic thinking this role names.

**Question 5 (moderate) — What the evidence supports**

Nine of twelve participants did not understand a phrase on a page. Ask which conclusion is best supported.

- A) 75% of users do not understand it.
- B) The phrase is a substantial problem, evidenced across most participants, and should be replaced with wording that says specifically what is meant — with a follow-up check on whether the replacement is understood.
- C) The phrase should be deleted.
- D) The page is badly written.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Nine of twelve is a strong qualitative signal stated at that strength, with a specified action and a follow-up check that distinguishes a change from an improvement. This tests stating evidence at its actual strength — option A converts a sample into a population rate, the commonest overreach in content reporting, and D generalises from one phrase to the whole page.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A pattern across sources**

Search shows "how long" as the top term; contact centre data shows "when will I hear" as the top call reason; research participants asked about timescales unprompted in seven of nine sessions; a timescale is published on a page two clicks from the journey. Ask for the strongest conclusion.

- A) The timescale needs to be more prominent.
- B) Three independent sources converge on the same need, and the timescale exists but is not where users look — so the fix is placement rather than creation, and the strongest version puts it at the moment the question arises rather than on a page users must seek out.
- C) The research is unnecessary given the analytics.
- D) The timescale page should be promoted in navigation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three sources of different kinds converging is unusually strong evidence, and the content already existing makes the problem tractable — a placement problem is cheaper to fix than a content one. This tests distinguishing prominence from placement — the effective answer is usually the timescale placed in the confirmation message, email and letter at the moment the question forms, not a navigation change (D), the weakest version of prominence since users mid-wait search or ring rather than navigate.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Designing under a constraint**

A legal team requires the phrase "you may be liable to a financial penalty" to appear verbatim; research shows users do not understand what it means or when it applies. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Use the phrase and accept the outcome.
- B) Establish whether the requirement is that the phrase appears or that nothing else appears, since those are different — then keep the phrase and immediately explain it in plain words, giving the amount and the trigger.
- C) Replace it with plain language.
- D) Link to the legislation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Legal requirements are almost always that certain wording appears, almost never a prohibition on saying anything else, and the deadlock usually dissolves once asked. This tests separating a wording requirement from an implied prohibition, and specifying what the explanation must actually contain (amount and trigger) — option C proposes overriding a legal requirement, refused and costly to credibility on constraints that are actually negotiable.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study's participants were all recruited through the service's own online panel. Findings: (i) participants did not understand "relevant supporting evidence"; (ii) participants found the page easy to navigate; (iii) participants completed the journey in under ten minutes; (iv) participants did not notice a footer link. Ask which findings are most compromised by the recruitment.

- A) All four.
- B) None; the sample is adequate.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are most compromised, because a panel recruited through the online service consists of people already comfortable with it, which flatters navigation and speed — while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of a phrase and noticing an element, which are less dependent on prior familiarity though not wholly independent of it.
- D) Only (i).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A panel recruited through the service is filtered twice — for digital confidence and for goodwill — most affecting findings about ease and speed, exactly what familiarity produces. This tests scoping a sampling flaw precisely rather than discarding all findings (A) or none (B, leaving two flattered findings in circulation, more dangerous than absent ones because they generate false confidence).

**Question 9 (harder) — Content that should not exist**

A page exists solely to explain how to correct a mistake users commonly make on the preceding form, and is well used and well rated. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The page is doing its job and should be maintained.
- B) A well-used page explaining how to fix a common mistake is evidence of a defect upstream: the form is producing the mistake, and the highest-value content work is on the form itself, after which this page should shrink or disappear.
- C) The page should be made more prominent so more people find it.
- D) The page should be merged into the form.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Heavy use is not evidence of success — it measures how often an upstream defect occurs. This tests the reasoning that most distinguishes content design from writing: the correct recommendation makes the candidate's own successful page unnecessary — option A maintains the symptom indefinitely, and C makes the recovery route easier while leaving the failure rate untouched, a real but second-best intervention frequently mistaken for a fix.

**Question 10 (harder) — Two metrics moving apart**

After a rewrite, average time on a page fell from 90 to 50 seconds; contact centre calls about that step rose 22%; completion was unchanged. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The rewrite succeeded on its metric; the calls need separate investigation.
- B) The pattern is consistent with content having been removed that users needed, pushing them to telephone for the explanation — an apparent efficiency that is probably a cost transfer, with unchanged completion suggesting no benefit at all; the right measure is end-to-end effort including contact.
- C) The call increase must have another cause.
- D) Report both without interpretation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three facts fit together — the page got shorter, calls rose, completion did not move — the most economical explanation being content removed that users needed, transferring the cost to telephone rather than eliminating it. This tests recognising "shorter is better" as a heuristic that is wrong in a specific case (removal of needed information looks identical to simplification in a word count) — option A accepts a metric because it was agreed rather than checking what it actually shows.

**Question 11 (harder) — Conflicting evidence**

A satisfaction survey of 1,200 users reports 81% positive about a service's guidance. Ten moderated sessions found seven participants struggling to understand it. The contact centre reports guidance queries as its second-highest call driver. Ask how to synthesise this.

- A) Trust the survey; 1,200 responses outweigh ten sessions.
- B) The sources see different populations and measure different things: satisfaction surveys are typically served at the end of a journey, so people who gave up rarely see them, and satisfaction with an outcome is not comprehension of guidance — while contact centre volume is behavioural evidence at scale that corroborates the sessions. The picture is guidance most people get through, many find hard, and a substantial minority need help with.
- C) The sources conflict irreconcilably.
- D) The guidance is performing well with minor issues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The apparent conflict dissolves once what each source sees and measures is considered — end-of-journey surveys miss people who gave up, and satisfaction differs from comprehension, while contact centre volume corroborates the sessions at scale. This tests reconciling apparently conflicting evidence by examining each source's population and measure — option A defers to scale over relevance, and C abandons an available reconciliation.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in your own work**

Three weeks after a supported rewrite, the underlying research is found to have recruited only users who had completed the service successfully. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Note it as a limitation next time.
- B) Tell the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are affected and which stand, and propose targeted follow-up with users who did not complete — since those are the people the study could not see and the ones the content most needs to serve.
- C) Quietly commission follow-up and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Say nothing; the rewrite was probably an improvement anyway.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A sample of successful completers cannot see the failures the content was meant to address, and a rewrite has already gone live on that basis. This tests disclosing a flaw promptly and scoping the damage precisely — noting which conclusions may still be sound rather than minimising or catastrophising — option D is least defensible, substituting a guess for the evidence relied on.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate applies a standard exactly as written, even where the result feels unsatisfying, and flags the standard itself as the thing needing revision.
- Note whether the candidate traces a symptom (calls, drop-out, a search term) to its actual upstream cause rather than treating or rewriting the symptom directly.
- Watch for whether evidence is stated at its actual strength (a raw count from a sample) rather than converted into a population percentage.
- Score for whether the candidate weighs a decision by its compounding effect (a pattern that will repeat) rather than only its immediate visible impact.
- Note whether a sampling or methodological flaw is scoped precisely — naming which findings are compromised and which survive — rather than discarded wholesale or dismissed entirely.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a small-sample finding ("nine of twelve participants") converted into a population percentage.
- Missing when a candidate proposes rewriting or fixing a symptom (a confusing page, a script for the contact centre) rather than tracing it to an upstream cause.
- Rewarding heavy page usage treated as evidence of success rather than possibly evidence of an upstream failure rate.
- Accepting a sampling flaw treated as either wholly disqualifying or entirely negligible, rather than scoped to specific findings.
- Missing when a candidate accepts "shorter is better" or a similar heuristic without checking what was actually removed or changed.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles the quantitative material this role produces and consumes. At this level the emphasis shifts from calculating to interrogating — computing rates correctly still matters, but the harder items ask whether a figure means what somebody claims.

Typical format: a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided.

Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority — a number in a slide is treated as fact and a content argument as a matter of taste, regardless of the rigour behind either. Content designers who cannot interrogate numbers confidently lose arguments they should win, and content improvements are hard to attribute and easy to dismiss without this fluency.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading analytics, search and contact data** maps to **User focus (content design)**: insights from evidence drive content decisions.
- **Comparing rates fairly** maps to **User-centred content design**: deciding which content to fix first depends on comparing pages honestly.
- **Measuring whether a change worked** maps to **Agile working (content design)**: responsiveness to feedback means knowing whether the feedback loop closed.
- **Quantifying scale** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: making the case for content work depends on expressing its value in numbers other people recognise.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User focus**: a wrong measure sends content work to the wrong page.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Feedback rate**

Of 3,600 users who answered a feedback prompt, 900 said they did not find what they needed. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 900÷3,600=25%. This tests basic percentage calculation, and, in a strong candidate's answer, recognising this is the percentage of people who answered the prompt, not everyone who saw the page — a comparison between pages is more reliable than the absolute level.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing pages fairly**

Page A: 20,000 views, 600 negative feedback. Page B: 4,000 views, 480 negative. Page C: 10,000 views, 300 negative. Ask which page has the highest negative rate, and what it is.

- A) Page A, 3%
- B) Page B, 12%
- C) Page C, 3%
- D) Page A, 30%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 3%; B 12%; C 3%. This tests converting to rates rather than raw counts — the raw counts tell the opposite story (Page A has the most negative responses), and both readings answer genuinely different questions (total harm vs proportional harm) worth stating explicitly.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a contact log**

Calls: "when will I hear" 2,400, "what documents" 1,500, "how do I cancel" 400, "fee query" 400, "other" 1,300. Ask what percentage of calls are about timescales.

- A) 30%
- B) 35%
- C) 40%
- D) 45%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total 6,000; 2,400÷6,000=40%. This tests computing the total from a list before finding a proportion, and, in a strong answer, recognising that two in five calls asking "when" is a content problem with a one-sentence solution worth quantifying this way.

**Question 4 (easy) — Scale of a content fix**

A service handles 400,000 applications a year and receives 6,000 calls a month; adding a timescale could plausibly remove the 40% of contact volume asking about timescales. Ask how many calls a year might be avoided.

- A) 2,400
- B) 28,800
- C) 72,000
- D) 240,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 40%×6,000=2,400/month; ×12=28,800/year. This tests annualising a monthly figure before reporting it — option A stops at the monthly figure, the single most common error, and the annual figure is what makes a compelling business case a finance colleague can act on.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before a rewrite: 1,200 of 3,000 answered a feedback prompt negatively. After: 900 of 3,600 did. Ask whether the page improved, and by how much in percentage points.

- A) Yes, by 300 responses
- B) Yes, by 15 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 25 percentage points
- D) No, it got worse

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Before 40%, after 25%, improvement 15 points. This tests converting to rates because totals differ — option A compares raw counts, and here the raw fall (300) actually understates the improvement, since more people answered the prompt after the change, a reminder that raw counts can conceal a genuine win as easily as flatter a false one.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Views of a guidance page fell from 60,000 to 45,000 a month after a form was simplified. Ask for the percentage decrease, and whether it is necessarily bad.

- A) 25% decrease, and it is bad
- B) 25% decrease, and it may be good — if the form no longer produces the confusion the guidance existed to resolve, fewer people needing it is the intended outcome
- C) 33% decrease, and it is bad
- D) 33% decrease, and it may be good

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** (60,000−45,000)÷60,000=25%. This tests dividing by the original value (C, D divide by the new figure) and correctly interpreting the sign of a change for content that exists to resolve confusion — a decline in views can be exactly the intended outcome once the source of confusion is fixed.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Working back to a base**

15% of users abandon at the evidence question, and this is 5,400 people a month. Ask how many reach the evidence question monthly.

- A) 6,210
- B) 8,100
- C) 36,000
- D) 81,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 5,400÷0.15=36,000. This tests working back from a percentage and a count to the base, checkable by reversing the calculation — a habit that lets a candidate judge whether a quoted percentage deserves the weight given to it.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

Journey: 50,000 start, 40,000 reach the evidence page, 30,000 reach the declaration, 28,500 submit. Ask what percentage of those who reached the declaration submitted.

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** 28,500÷30,000=95%. This tests identifying the group the question actually names as the denominator — option A uses 50,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked, with real consequence for where content effort goes: the losses are actually earlier in the journey, not at the declaration.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Weighted comparison**

Three pages: 5% negative on 20,000 views; 12% on 5,000; 8% on 5,000. Ask for the overall negative rate.

- A) 6.7%
- B) 7.5%
- C) 8.3%
- D) 9%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 1,000+600+400=2,000 across 30,000 views ≈ 6.7%. This tests returning to counts before combining rates — option C is the unweighted mean, over-weighting the two small pages against the 20,000-view page that should dominate; only the weighted figure describes what the average user actually experiences.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that hides a split**

Average time on a guidance page is 40 seconds; sessions showed participants either glancing for a few seconds or reading carefully for two to three minutes. Ask for the most useful response.

- A) The average is wrong.
- B) The average is probably correct and uninformative: it sits between two clusters and describes neither, so ask for the distribution — which will show whether the page has two audiences, and a single change may help one while harming the other.
- C) The participants were unrepresentative.
- D) The page should be shorter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A mean landing in the gap between two behavioural clusters describes neither group. This tests recognising when a mean conceals a split and asking for the distribution before acting — shortening the page (D) helps glancers and removes what careful readers needed, an edit that looks obviously right and may be exactly wrong.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

A page's usefulness score rose from 45% to 54%. Ask which statement is accurate.

- A) A 9% increase.
- B) A 9 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 20% relative increase.
- C) A 20 percentage point increase.
- D) A 16.7% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 54−45=9 points; 9÷45=20% relative. This tests labelling both figures correctly — since content designers frequently write the sentence these numbers appear in, giving both figures honestly protects against a colleague later discovering a flattering figure was chosen and discounting the whole document.

**Question 12 (harder) — What a small comparison can establish**

Baseline completion 60%; a 5-point improvement would be meaningful; a colleague proposes 20 moderated sessions before and 20 after. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 40 sessions is reasonable.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 20 per group is one participant and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users, and spending the 40 sessions on understanding why people succeed or fail, which sessions do uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 40 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With 20 per group, a five-point difference is a single participant, indistinguishable from chance. This tests recognising an underpowered comparison and redirecting to the method suited to each question — analytics for the rate, sessions for the why — rather than endorsing a design that cannot answer its own question (A) or reporting noise with a caveat that will not stop it being acted on (D).

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate converts raw counts to rates before comparing groups of different sizes.
- Note whether the candidate annualises or otherwise scales a figure appropriately before using it to make a case.
- Watch for whether a mean is checked for a hidden bimodal split before being used to justify a single change.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from relative percentage change and labels each correctly.
- Note whether the candidate assesses whether a proposed sample size can actually detect the effect it is meant to measure.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a comparison of raw counts across groups of different sizes rather than rates.
- Missing when a candidate stops at a monthly or partial figure rather than scaling it to the period that matters for the decision.
- Rewarding an unweighted average of rates across groups of unequal size.
- Accepting a fall in a metric (views, time on page) treated automatically as bad without considering what a decline might actually mean for that content.
- Missing when a candidate endorses an underpowered sample or comparison as adequate to detect a stated effect size.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. For most disciplines this is a proxy skill; for this one it is close to the job itself, which makes it the section where a candidate should score best and where a careless habit costs most.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose followed by statements to classify as True, False, or Cannot Say — regardless of professional knowledge, which becomes harder to set aside with experience rather than easier, since familiarity makes it easy to supply the expected clause rather than read the actual one.

The payoff for a content designer is unusually direct: the job frequently consists of being handed a paragraph of legislation or policy and asked what it actually requires, so a plain-language version can be written. Getting it wrong in either direction is serious — understating a requirement lets users miss something that matters, overstating it publishes an obligation government does not impose.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **User-centred content design**: using style guides and content patterns means applying them as written.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **User focus (content design)**: reasoning from evidence must not add to it.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to turning dense source material into content that says what it means and no more.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: knowing whether something is required or recommended tells you how much room there is.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **User focus**: noticing that a source never states a timescale is what stops publishing a page without one.
- **Reading stakeholder correspondence accurately** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "All content published on the service must be reviewed against the style guide before publication. Where content is a factual correction of published material, it may be published immediately and reviewed within five working days. Content that includes legal wording supplied by the legal team must not be altered without the legal team's agreement, although surrounding explanatory content may be edited freely. All published content is reviewed annually."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Explanatory content around legal wording may be edited without the legal team's agreement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly — the constraint attaches to the legal wording, not the page. This tests reading a constraint to its precise scope, the fact that unlocks most content design arguments about legal text.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: "A factual correction may be published before it has been reviewed against the style guide."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The second sentence permits exactly this. This tests holding two sentences together — a general rule and its exception — rather than stopping at the general rule; the exception exists because a live factual error causes ongoing harm.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "Legal wording may never be altered."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage prohibits alteration "without the legal team's agreement," permitting it with agreement — the statement's "never" drops the condition. This tests recognising a conditional prohibition rather than an absolute one, a distinction with real consequences: content designers who believe legal wording is untouchable never ask, and legal teams are often willing to agree clearer wording.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "A page containing legal wording is reviewed annually."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The final sentence applies to all published content without exception. This tests noticing that the annual review carries no carve-out for legal content despite the strong constraint on altering it — reviewing is not altering, and the passage keeps them separate.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The review of the guidance found that users who read the eligibility page before applying were more likely to complete an application. The review notes that these users differed in other respects, including whether they had applied before. The review did not assess whether the eligibility page was accurate. It recommends that the team consider whether the eligibility page should be more prominent."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Users who read the eligibility page were more likely to complete an application."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly. This tests the baseline reading, and, in a strong answer, noting the passage's careful "more likely" rather than "usually completed."

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "Reading the eligibility page causes users to complete applications."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage flags a confound — the groups differed in whether they had applied before, which independently explains higher completion and possibly the causal arrow itself. This tests recognising a declared confound as a warning against inferring causation; the content consequence is direct, since prominence only helps if the causal claim is true.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The eligibility page is accurate."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly states accuracy was not assessed. This tests treating declared silence as establishing nothing — an inaccurate page could correlate with higher completion while causing real harm.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "The review recommends making the eligibility page more prominent."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review recommends the team "consider whether" the page should be more prominent, not that it should be. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X" — content designers are unusually likely to be the person writing the sentence recording what a review said.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Content must be written so that it can be understood by users with a reading age of 9 where the subject matter allows. Where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided, they must be explained at first use. Content must not rely on colour alone to convey meaning. Headings must describe the content that follows and must be used in order without skipping levels. These requirements apply to public-facing content and to internal guidance used by more than fifty staff."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "All content must be understandable by users with a reading age of 9."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The requirement is qualified by "where the subject matter allows." This tests catching a dropped qualifier that is frequently misquoted in both directions — it is not a general excuse, but it does acknowledge genuine cases.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "Technical terms must be avoided."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage requires explanation "where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided," presupposing such cases exist and permitting the terms with explanation. This tests reading a conditional rule correctly rather than as a prohibition.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: "These requirements apply to internal guidance used by twenty staff."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states where the requirements do apply (guidance used by more than fifty staff) but does not say they are excluded below that threshold. This tests distinguishing a scoping statement from an exemption — stating where a rule applies is not stating where it does not.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Content must be written for a reading age of 9.
- B) Public-facing content and widely used internal guidance must be comprehensible and navigable without specialist knowledge or reliance on visual cues — through plain language where the subject allows, explanation where terms are unavoidable, meaning carried in more than colour, and headings that describe and are correctly ordered.
- C) Headings must be used in order.
- D) Technical terms must be explained at first use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage while adding nothing. This tests identifying a summary broad enough to cover every element and its scope — options A, C and D are each a true detail promoted too far, while B preserves both qualifiers and names the scope, which a summary omitting it would wrongly imply is universal.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads a conditional prohibition or requirement as conditional, rather than as absolute in either direction.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a scoping statement (where a rule applies) from an exemption (where it explicitly does not).
- Watch for whether a declared confound or a declared gap in what was assessed is treated as a genuine limit on what can be concluded.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes "consider whether X" from "recommend X" in institutional prose.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary covering the whole passage, including its qualifiers and scope, rather than one true detail promoted above the rest.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement judged True or False on the strength of the candidate's own professional knowledge rather than the passage.
- Missing when a candidate reads a conditional prohibition ("not without agreement") as an absolute one ("never").
- Rewarding a scoping clause converted into an exemption for cases the passage never actually addresses.
- Accepting an association read as causation despite a declared confound in the passage.
- Missing when a candidate selects a true-but-narrow detail as the main point rather than a summary covering the whole passage and its qualifiers.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do. It measures whether judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. This role is responsible for content across the end-to-end journey, comfortable using evidence to make content decisions, building relationships across government and influencing stakeholders, contributing to style guides and patterns, and engaging with the cross-government community.

Four principles carry most scenarios: users come first, specifically users who will be misled, delayed or financially harmed, since content failures are quiet and nobody rings to say they misunderstood something and gave up; protect the accuracy of what is published, since this role is frequently the last person to check whether something is true, complete and comprehensible; ask what a request is for, since stakeholders very often ask for a specific sentence when what they need is a specific outcome; and fix the instance, then the source, since content problems usually recur while the thing producing them is still there.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Protecting accuracy and comprehensibility** maps to **User-centred content design** and **User focus (content design)**.
- **Influencing stakeholders** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Using evidence in argument** maps to **User focus**.
- **Working in cycles** maps to **Agile working (content design)**.
- **Exploring how content might work** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**.
- **Seeing beyond the page** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, and engaging the cross-government community is named at this level.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A stakeholder asks for specific wording**

A policy colleague asks for a paragraph published in the language of the regulation itself; research shows users do not understand it. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Publish it as requested.
- B) Ask what the paragraph needs to achieve — user understanding, legal protection, or the appearance of a specific form of words — because those three needs have different solutions and only one requires that exact wording.
- C) Refuse and publish a plain-language version.
- D) Publish both versions.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Establishing what the request is for before deciding how to satisfy it usually resolves the tension, since the colleague often agrees once the actual need is named. This tests distinguishing genuinely different underlying needs before assuming one solution — option A is least effective, publishing something demonstrably not understood and teaching the colleague that such requests are simply granted.

**Question 2 (easy) — A live inaccuracy**

A live page states a fee that changed two months ago. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Raise a ticket for the content backlog.
- B) Correct it immediately if you have the authority and the correct figure, tell your lead, and check whether the same figure appears on other pages, in the letters, or in the confirmation email.
- C) Correct the page.
- D) Ask the policy team to confirm the new fee before doing anything.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A fee is almost never stated once, and correcting only the page seen leaves a contradiction across the service, which is arguably worse than a consistent error. This tests checking where else a figure appears — the discipline that distinguishes a content designer from a proofreader — option A is least effective, treating a live financial inaccuracy as backlog work.

**Question 3 (easy) — Feedback on your work**

A senior colleague returns a draft heavily rewritten with the comment "too wordy." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the rewrite and move on.
- B) Compare the versions carefully, work out what was cut and why, then ask a couple of targeted questions about the changes that cannot be accounted for.
- C) Ask them to explain every change.
- D) Point out that some removed detail was requested by the policy team.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Comparing versions line by line explains most changes and leaves a short list of genuinely puzzling ones. This tests extracting the pattern from feedback before asking about it — option A is least effective, accepting the rewrite without extracting the pattern and repeating the same choices next time.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Pressure to publish**

A delivery manager asks for publication before the second check the process requires, because a campaign launches tomorrow. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Publish; the deadline is real.
- B) Explain the check and ask what is possible — whether someone can review in the next hour, or whether the page could go live first thing tomorrow — and escalate if no route works.
- C) Refuse and cite the process.
- D) Publish and arrange the check afterwards without telling anyone.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Both the deadline and the check are real, and asking what is possible often resolves it. This tests engaging with the genuine constraint rather than skipping or citing it rigidly — option D is least effective, concealing that a page is unchecked and taking sole responsibility for a decision that was not the candidate's to make.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A pattern you cannot fix locally**

Four services each explain the same benefit rule differently, and at least two are inconsistent. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Fix the two that contradict.
- B) Document all four with their differences, establish through the policy owner which is correct, then propose a single agreed form of words and offer it to the cross-government content community if the rule is not unique to your organisation.
- C) Make all four match the clearest version.
- D) Raise it in your own team's retrospective.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** At least one page is wrong and nobody currently knows which, so establishing correctness must precede establishing consistency, and the community contribution outlasts the individual fix. This tests resolving correctness first and sharing the resolution widely — option C is least effective, making pages consistent could propagate a wrong rule to four pages, worse than the original inconsistency.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Evidence a stakeholder does not accept**

Research shows users do not understand a key term; a stakeholder argues the participants were unrepresentative and the term is standard in the sector. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Concede and keep the term.
- B) Acknowledge the sample's limits honestly, then point out that the sector-standard argument concerns people in the sector while the page is for members of the public — and propose a cheap test: check whether the term appears in the site's search log, since users searching for it would suggest familiarity and users searching for a different word would not.
- C) Defend the research and insist on the change.
- D) Escalate to your lead.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The two claims differ in quality — the sample objection is partly fair, the sector-standard claim is a category error — and separating them plus proposing a cheap, existing-data test converts disagreement into a resolvable question. This tests distinguishing a fair objection from a weak one — option A is least effective, conceding on the strength of an objection that does not survive examination.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Content that belongs to another team**

The letter sent after an application contradicts the timescale on the confirmation page; letters belong to another team. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Ignore it; letters are not yours.
- B) Establish which timescale is correct, correct whichever of the two you control, and raise the other with the owning team — noting that the discrepancy means one of them has been telling users something untrue.
- C) Change your page to match the letter.
- D) Raise it with your lead and leave it there.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two different timescales for one thing means at least one is wrong, producing a phone call at best and a missed deadline at worst. This tests establishing the truth first, fixing what is controlled, and escalating the rest — option A is least effective, describing an org chart while a user experiences one contradictory service, and this role is explicitly defined across the end-to-end journey including offline channels.

**Question 8 (harder) — Content that may cause harm**

Two days before launch, a page is found not to mention that applying may reduce a benefit already received; adding it needs policy sign-off and may delay launch. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Launch and add it in the next update.
- B) Raise it immediately with your lead and the service owner, stating specifically that users may reduce their income without knowing, ask for an explicit recorded decision, and offer a form of words for policy to approve so the sign-off is as fast as possible.
- C) Add a sentence based on your understanding of the interaction.
- D) Launch and note the gap in the backlog.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A financial-harm decision belongs to people with authority to weigh it against a launch date, but drafting wording for policy to approve removes the most common cause of delay. This tests raising a specific, actionable financial-harm concern with a ready-made solution — option C is least effective, the conscientious person's trap of publishing an unapproved, potentially wrong account of a benefit interaction.

**Question 9 (harder) — Being asked to soften a message**

A stakeholder asks to change "you must tell us within 28 days or you may have to pay a penalty" to "please let us know as soon as you can," saying the original sounds threatening. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Make the change; tone matters.
- B) Agree that the tone can be improved, and explain that the sentence carries two things users need — the deadline and the consequence — so removing both leaves people at risk of a penalty they were never warned about; then offer a revised version that keeps both and sounds less punitive.
- C) Refuse and keep the original.
- D) Change it to "please tell us within 28 days."

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The tone concern is legitimate; the proposed solution removes the information the sentence exists to convey. This tests separating a legitimate concern from a flawed fix and offering an alternative that addresses both — option A is least effective, leaving a late-responding user facing an unwarned penalty.

**Question 10 (harder) — A page that should not exist**

A page explaining how to correct a common form error is heavily used and well rated. Ask for the strongest recommendation.

- A) Maintain the page; it is working.
- B) Recommend fixing the form that produces the error, on the reasoning that heavy use of a recovery page measures how often the upstream defect occurs rather than how well the page works — while keeping the page until the form is fixed.
- C) Make the page more prominent.
- D) Merge the page into the form.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Heavy use measures a failure rate, not the page's quality, and the correct recommendation makes the candidate's own successful page unnecessary. This tests recognising a symptom page and prioritising the upstream cause — option A is least effective, treating good ratings as evidence the situation is fine when users are rating being rescued from a problem they should never have had.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

A page published last week states a deadline of 14 days when the correct period is 28. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Correct it quietly.
- B) Correct it immediately, tell your lead, check every other place the deadline appears, and raise whether users who acted on the wrong figure may need contacting — since a shortened deadline may have caused people to rush or to believe they had missed it.
- C) Correct it and mention it at stand-up.
- D) Check with policy whether 28 is right before doing anything.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A deadline stated as half its actual length may have caused users to believe they had already missed it and not applied. This tests disclosing an own error promptly and surfacing whether affected users need contacting — option A is least effective, since a concealment discovered later becomes the thing remembered rather than the original error.

**Question 12 (harder) — An assumption in the room**

In a planning session, someone says "we don't need to worry about people who can't read this — they can always ring up," while the contact centre is open 9 to 5 weekdays and the service is used heavily by shift workers. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go.
- B) Bring the specific facts — that the line is 9 to 5 on weekdays and many users work shifts — and ask what should happen to someone who cannot read the page and cannot ring in those hours, so the decision is made knowingly.
- C) Argue that all content must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately afterwards.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The comment rests on an assumption that collapses against specific facts. This tests contributing concrete information at the moment a decision is being made — option A is least effective, letting a factually wrong assumption stand when the facts to correct it were available.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate establishes what a stakeholder's request is actually for before deciding how to satisfy it.
- Note whether the candidate checks where else a figure or fact appears (fees, deadlines, timescales) before treating a single correction as complete.
- Watch for whether the candidate resolves which of two conflicting versions is correct before making content consistent.
- Score for whether integrity is held under social or delivery pressure (concealed publication, softened warnings, quiet non-disclosure) rather than traded away.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes a symptom (a busy recovery page, a well-used workaround) from its upstream cause and prioritises accordingly.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response that publishes something users demonstrably do not understand because it was requested.
- Missing when a candidate makes inconsistent content consistent before establishing which version is actually correct.
- Rewarding a message softened by removing the substantive information it exists to convey.
- Accepting good usage or good ratings on a recovery or workaround page as evidence the underlying problem does not need fixing.
- Missing when a candidate delays a correction pending confirmation rather than correcting first and confirming in parallel.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through cognitive reasoning that traces a symptom to its cause rather than treating it directly — a well-written page's call volume traced to something outside the page, a heavily used recovery page read as a measure of an upstream failure rate, a panel-recruitment flaw scoped precisely enough to say which findings survive it; the arithmetic that makes content evidence defensible — comparing pages by rate rather than count, returning to counts before combining rates, and recognising that a fall in a metric can be the intended outcome; the discipline of reading policy prose, review reports and stakeholder correspondence at exactly the strength the text supports; and the situational judgement that defines effective content design work — asking what a request is really for, fixing the instance and then the source, and protecting published accuracy under pressure.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several cognitive and situational items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, particularly around what evidence actually establishes and where a content problem's real cause lies. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
