# Senior Interaction Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

This guide is for you: an assessor — a line manager, a panel member or a reviewer — administering a workplace job-specific psychometric assessment to a candidate for senior interaction designer. It is not the candidate's practice material. Every item below carries its worked answer and explanation as your answer key, to be held back and shown to the candidate only at scoring and debrief.

A senior interaction designer is an experienced designer who works with minimal support, influences and mentors others, works with service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts, may carry responsibility across complex services, helps set direction and embed good practice within teams, and makes important decisions based on research while understanding how that research affects others. This is a role defined as much by judgement and influence as by craft.

Three features shape this material. First, a candidate is accountable for more than their own work — advising teams, critiquing others' approaches, identifying and creating new patterns and components, and embedding good practice — making diagnostic reasoning about somebody else's evidence or design logic a core competence rather than an occasional one. Second, the role works with complexity: complex services, complex evidence such as large data sets, and prototyping complex ideas at appropriate fidelity, meaning genuinely competing constraints and evidence supporting more than one reading. Third, the role builds consensus by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions, and works across profession boundaries with policy and technology colleagues — a negotiating role resting on the evidence behind it being unassailable.

At this level a candidate creates patterns and components rather than only using them, which changes the nature of the reasoning: a pattern is a decision made once and paid for many times, in maintenance, in the expectations it sets, and in the accessibility characteristics it propagates. Several items below turn on that asymmetry.

Each of the four sections that follow presents an overview of what it measures and its typical format, a mapping to the role's named skills, twelve graduated practice questions with full worked explanations, then administration tips and common scoring pitfalls.

**How to administer this fairly.** Give every candidate for this role level the same items and the same time allowance, and hold the answer key back until scoring and debrief. Score cognitive and numeric items for whether the candidate audits somebody else's reasoning or evidence — diagnosing the flaw rather than only producing sound reasoning of their own — and for whether they read a distribution of failures diagnostically rather than treating each instance in isolation. Situational judgement items at this level consistently reward independent professional judgement, capability-building alongside problem-solving, and absorbing organisational pressure that should not land on someone more junior.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning this role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At senior level the materials are messier and the reasoning less mechanical: evidence supporting two incompatible readings, designs with a flaw that compromises some conclusions but not others, prioritisation problems with no dominant option, and claims made by other people that need diagnosing rather than accepting or rejecting.

Typical format: an online test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority, often adaptive, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

Three things distinguish this section at this level. First, the shift from producing sound reasoning to auditing it — advising teams, helping teams use design hypotheses effectively, and building consensus by asking difficult questions are each a diagnostic act performed on somebody else's thinking. Second, systemic reasoning: when something goes wrong repeatedly, the informative feature is usually the distribution of failures rather than any single failure — concentrated in one place under one condition tells a different story than scattered failures. Third, the multiplier effect of pattern-level decisions: because this role creates patterns and components rather than only using them, several items ask a candidate to weigh a decision by how many times it will be paid for.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Auditing evidence and reasoning** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining complex evidence such as large data sets, and helping a team use design hypotheses effectively.
- **Challenging assumptions** maps to **Design communication**: building consensus by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions.
- **Systemic and risk reasoning** map to **Designing strategically**: using risks, opportunities and constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design.
- **Pattern-level judgement** maps to **Designing strategically**: identifying and creating new design patterns and components.
- **Diagnosing exclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**: advising teams on inclusive design and delivering ethical services considering users' personal and social context.
- **Reasoning about positions and incentives** maps to **Designing together**: working across team or profession boundaries.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Standard: every transactional page must use words users would use, state what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid requesting held information, offer a route to help, and work without JavaScript. A page: uses internal terminology; says the case will be assessed; gives no timescale; re-requests a held reference; has no contact route; degrades gracefully without JavaScript. Ask how many requirements are unmet.

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Internal terminology fails requirement one; "the case will be assessed" satisfies requirement two, minimally; no timescale fails requirement three; re-requesting held information fails requirement four; no contact route fails requirement five; graceful degradation satisfies requirement six. Four unmet. This tests applying a standard as written rather than as it should have been written, which is what assurance requires — and, in a strong candidate's answer, noticing that absences (the missing help route, the JavaScript behaviour) are the systematic blind spot in any audit, worth leading a checklist with.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Rule: "A custom component may enter a live service only with sign-off from the head of interaction design, unless it replaces an existing custom component on a like-for-like basis and has been assessed as low risk." A team reports: "We shipped a custom component last month without sign-off." Ask which conclusion must be true.

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) It was a low-risk like-for-like replacement.
- C) Either it was a low-risk like-for-like replacement, or the standard was breached.
- D) The component should be withdrawn.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** With the exception compound (like-for-like *and* low risk) and the facts silent on whether it applied, only the disjunction is guaranteed; D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true. This tests reading a compound exception precisely — a component satisfying only one half does not qualify, and the productive opening move is to establish which route was taken, framed as an enquiry rather than an accusation.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Diagnosing a colleague's conclusion**

A designer reports: "Users find the new autocomplete easy — all 18 participants completed the task without difficulty." Sessions were moderated, the facilitator demonstrated the component first, participants were recruited from the departmental panel, and all used a laptop with a mouse. Ask for the most important observation.

- A) Eighteen participants is too small a sample.
- B) The demonstration, the panel recruitment and the mouse-only conditions all remove the difficulties the component is most likely to produce — first encounter, unfamiliarity, and keyboard or assistive technology operation — so the result describes an unusually favourable case rather than the component's usability.
- C) "Easy" is not a measurable construct.
- D) A think-aloud protocol should have been used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unanimity is the alarm, and this tests identifying what the test conditions removed — demonstrating the component eliminates first-encounter difficulty, a departmental panel is comfortable with government interfaces, and mouse-only skips the operation mode where custom autocompletes most often fail. Option A misdiagnoses validity as size — 180 participants under identical conditions would produce the same finding; option D is a technique fix leaving all three conditions in place.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

Capacity across several services for one thing this quarter: (1) an identity check pattern is being decided next month, reused across six services; (2) a live service has a persistent 30% drop-off at one step, affecting 250,000 users a year; (3) a service launches in six weeks with designs signed off; (4) a programme is preparing a business case due in nine months. Ask which is strongest, and why.

- A) Item 2, because it affects the most users.
- B) Item 1, because the decision is imminent, will be reused six times, and design input is the only thing that could still shape it — and a pattern embeds its characteristics, good or bad, into every service that adopts it.
- C) Item 4, because business cases shape long-term investment.
- D) Item 3, because launching untested carries reputational risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack, and the cost of changing a pattern rises with every service that adopts it. This tests weighting by value of information and reuse rather than current scale alone (A); item 3 sounds prudent and is weakest, since signed-off designs six weeks from launch means findings arrive with nowhere to go; item 4 is the profile trap — business cases attract attention, which is not decision leverage.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Systemic risk in a component decision**

A team proposes a custom component used on eleven pages across three services, well designed, maintained by that team alone. Ask for the most complete risk analysis.

- A) The risk is that the component may have accessibility defects.
- B) The immediate risk is defects, but the structural risks matter as much: a component maintained by one team becomes a dependency for three, so its priorities will not match theirs; it will diverge as teams fork it for local needs; and its accessibility characteristics propagate to eleven pages, meaning one undetected defect is eleven defects — so the decision is really about ownership and maintenance, not about this component's quality.
- C) There is no significant risk if it is tested properly.
- D) Custom components should be avoided.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests following consequences beyond the artefact — a well-made component is not the question; a shared dependency with a single owner is. The owning team will deprioritise a fix urgent to another service, at which point other teams fork it, producing divergent versions with different defects. Option C treats testing as sufficient at launch and not thereafter.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a colleague's synthesis**

A designer presents a case for a new pattern drawing on nine studies; the central claim — "users expect to be able to save and return at any point" — is supported by material from three studies, all of services taking more than two weeks to complete. Ask for the most useful feedback.

- A) The claim is invalid and should be removed.
- B) Note that the supporting evidence comes exclusively from multi-week services, so the claim over-reaches as written; suggest narrowing it, or testing whether it holds in short services, before it becomes a pattern that every service is expected to implement.
- C) Suggest adding more studies.
- D) Approve it; three studies is a reasonable basis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and the claim built on it reaches further than the evidence goes — especially consequential here because the claim is destined to become a pattern. This tests preserving a finding while correcting its scope, rather than discarding something real (A) or letting an over-generalised claim become an obligation on every team (D, letting it proceed as an approval).

**Question 7 (moderate) — Prototype fidelity for a complex idea**

A new multi-step interaction — a single control for entering a date range with optional open ends — must be tested for comprehensibility before committing to build, with three weeks available. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) A high-fidelity interactive prototype with full validation.
- B) A low-to-mid fidelity prototype of the control in two or three variants, tested with keyboard and screen reader from the outset rather than only with a mouse — because comprehension is the question, but a novel control's viability depends on whether it can be operated at all, and discovering that late is what makes custom controls expensive.
- C) A written description discussed in interviews.
- D) A technical spike to establish feasibility.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests matching the artefact to the comprehension question while catching a second, hidden viability question — whether a novel control can be operated by keyboard and announced sensibly by a screen reader — by including those modes from the first prototype rather than as a later check. Option A spends the three weeks building and still tests only the mouse path.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study under assurance used a prototype whose "back" function was broken throughout. Four findings: (i) participants could not locate eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned after a wrong turn; (iii) sessions ran longer than expected; (iv) participants misread the declaration. Ask which are compromised.

- A) All four.
- B) None; the fault was incidental.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, since inability to go back plausibly produced both, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A broken back function directly manufactures abandonment and inflated durations; content comprehension findings are largely untouched. This tests scoping precisely rather than wholesale condemnation (A), which at this level does organisational damage — teaching teams that any admitted prototype fault invalidates everything, an incentive to stop mentioning faults.

**Question 9 (harder) — Reading a good number for the problem inside it**

40% of users who abandon at an upload step return within seven days and complete; the team treats this as a success. Ask for the strongest observation.

- A) Agree; the 60% is the real problem.
- B) The 40% are also evidence of a problem — two or more attempts at something that should take one — and they are a natural experiment: something enabled them to return and succeed, and identifying whether that was a saved session, an email, or simply remembering the URL tells you what to build for the 60%.
- C) The 40% should be excluded from the drop-off figure.
- D) Send reminders to everyone who abandons.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests treating a comfortable number as still needing investigation and identifying the natural experiment inside it — the three candidate mechanisms imply completely different builds. Option A accepts the framing that ends well; option C would conceal the phenomenon entirely.

**Question 10 (harder) — A local optimisation**

Time on a form page fell from 90 to 55 seconds after content was removed; contact centre calls about that step rose 25% over the same period, and completion was unchanged. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The redesign succeeded on its metric; the calls need separate investigation.
- B) The pattern is consistent with content having been removed that users needed, pushing them to telephone for the explanation — an apparent efficiency that is probably a cost transfer, with unchanged completion suggesting no benefit at all; the right measure is end-to-end effort including contact.
- C) The call increase must have another cause.
- D) Report both metrics without interpretation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three facts fit together, pointing at cost transfer rather than genuine improvement, with unchanged completion the decisive detail. This tests refusing to accept a metric because it was agreed (A), which is how services get locally optimised into worse wholes, and supplying the interpretation this role exists to provide (D abdicates it).

**Question 11 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

A year's review shows accessibility defects reached live in four services, all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all designed by experienced practitioners. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by experienced people indicates a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what accessibility assurance demands and how long it takes, propose a fast route, and remediate the four alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirements.
- D) Introduce a mandatory accessibility gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests reading concentrated failure as diagnostic of a process rather than of conduct or capability, while the closing clause preserves that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unremediated. Option D imposes friction on every compliant service to catch a localised failure — how organisational burden accumulates and workarounds get invented.

**Question 12 (harder) — Working across a profession boundary**

A technology team has selected a third-party component library not meeting the accessibility standard, chosen for delivery speed, already used to build two services. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Require them to stop using it.
- B) Establish specifically which components fail and how badly, since a library is not uniformly good or bad; propose a shortlist of components to avoid or wrap, and a route to remediate the two existing services — framing it as making their choice workable rather than reversing it.
- C) Escalate to the head of profession.
- D) Accept it and document the risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Does not meet the standard" is almost never true of a whole library, usually a handful of complex components. This tests converting an unwinnable argument about a decision already made into a tractable list — the team cannot start again, so an answer requiring reversal (A) will be refused, spending standing for nothing.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate diagnoses the flaw in somebody else's evidence or reasoning, naming what specific conditions produced a result.
- Note whether the candidate reads a distribution of failures diagnostically (concentrated versus scattered) rather than treating each instance in isolation.
- Watch for whether the candidate weighs a pattern-level decision by how many times it will be reused.
- Score for whether the candidate follows a design decision's consequences beyond the immediate artefact (e.g. ownership and maintenance, not just component quality).
- Note whether the candidate scopes a methodological flaw precisely rather than condemning or excusing a study wholesale.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an audit that catches wrong requirements but misses absent ones.
- Missing when a candidate applies a standard more strictly or loosely than its own wording supports.
- Rewarding a reminder or training proposed as the response to a systemic, distribution-level problem.
- Accepting friction added for a compliant majority to address a small number of non-compliant, concentrated cases.
- Missing when a candidate accepts an agreed metric uncritically rather than checking whether it has been locally optimised.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate handles quantitative material. At senior level the emphasis shifts decisively from calculating to interrogating — candidates still need to compute correctly, but the harder items ask whether a figure means what somebody claims, often the question only the candidate in the room is positioned to ask.

Typical format: a timed online test of 15 to 30 minutes, each item presenting a table, chart, or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question, calculator usually provided. Distractors are built from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value used as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting, vendor or benchmark metrics compared to internal ones without checking definitions.

Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority — a number in a board paper is treated as fact while a design argument is treated as a view, regardless of the rigour behind either. Senior designers who cannot interrogate a figure confidently lose arguments they should win, and occasionally win arguments they should lose.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Analysing complex evidence** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining complex evidence such as large data sets.
- **Comparing rates between groups** maps to **Designing for everyone**: gaps between groups are the first numerical trace of exclusion.
- **Judging what a measurement can establish** maps to **Evidence-based design**: helping teams use design hypotheses effectively.
- **Quantifying risk and reuse** maps to **Designing strategically**: scale determines what matters.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**: documenting decisions, risks and unresolved issues.
- **Interrogating others' claims** maps to **Design communication**: building consensus by asking difficult questions.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 6,400 users who reached a page, 1,600 triggered a validation error on one field. Ask for the percentage.

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 1,600÷6,400=25%. This tests basic percentage calculation — and, in a strong candidate's answer, asking whether the field appears in a shared pattern, in which case the same rate is being produced wherever the pattern is used.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off against the right base**

A page is reached by 8,000 users and passed by 6,000. Ask for the drop-off rate.

- A) 25%
- B) 33%
- C) 75%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Drop-off 2,000÷8,000=25%. This tests measuring against arrivals, not against those who continued (option B) — if teams compute pages with different denominators, the ranking of where design capacity should go is distorted.

**Question 3 (easy) — Disaggregated rates**

Completion: Mouse users 8,100/9,000; Keyboard-only 350/700; Screen reader 75/300. Ask for the overall completion rate and the gap between the highest and lowest group rates.

- A) 85% overall; 50 point gap
- B) 85% overall; 65 point gap
- C) 90% overall; 65 point gap
- D) 88% overall; 40 point gap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Overall: 8,525÷10,000≈85%. Group rates: 90%, 50%, 25%; gap 65 points. This tests not mistaking the dominant group's rate for the overall rate (C, the characteristic error when one group is 90% of the sample) — the aggregate figure of 85% describes no group and conceals that screen reader users fail three times in four.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Eleven participants' times in seconds: 12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 28, 30, 35, 40, 240. Ask which summary is most defensible.

- A) Mean 44.1 seconds
- B) Median 25 seconds, range 12 to 240, with the 240-second case described separately
- C) Median 25 seconds
- D) Mean 44.1 seconds, range 12 to 240

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean exceeds ten of eleven observations; the median (25) describes the group well but alone conceals a session six times the typical duration — likely the most informative data point. This tests supplying centre, spread, and explicit treatment of an outlier rather than burying it (C) or pairing the range with a misleading centre (D).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four studies of the same component: 10 participants at 50%; 20 at 65%; 10 at 70%; 20 at 80%. Ask for the overall rate across all 60.

- A) 66%
- B) 68%
- C) 69%
- D) 71%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Converting to counts: 5+13+7+16=41÷60≈68%. This tests weighting by participant count rather than averaging the four rates directly (66.25%, option A) — and, in a strong candidate's answer, questioning whether the four studies are comparable enough to combine at all, since an arithmetically correct aggregate of incomparable studies is referentially empty.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 100,000 start, 75,000 pass identity, 45,000 upload evidence, 40,500 submit. Ask what proportion of those who passed identity went on to submit.

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 40,500÷75,000=54%. This tests identifying the denominator the question names; the funnel also shows where effort belongs — 25,000 lost at identity, 30,000 between identity and upload, against 4,500 at submission.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A benefits claim**

A benefits paper states a change raised completion from 55% to 66%, describing it as "a 20% improvement." Ask whether the description is accurate.

- A) No; it is an 11% improvement.
- B) Yes as arithmetic — 11 ÷ 55 = 20% relative increase — but the expression is ambiguous, and the paper should say "20% relative increase" or "11 percentage points."
- C) No; it is 11 percentage points, and calling it 20% is wrong.
- D) No; it should be 16.7%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relative increase (11÷55=20%) is defensible arithmetic; the problem is labelling, since many readers would read "20% improvement" as a rise to 75%. This tests distinguishing an arithmetic error from a labelling ambiguity — option C over-corrects into an error of its own, since relative change is legitimate when labelled.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Quantifying a capability requirement**

900,000 submissions a year; a proposed control requires JavaScript, and around 1.2% of sessions run without it. Ask how many people a year would be affected.

- A) 1,080
- B) 10,800
- C) 11,000
- D) 108,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 0.012×900,000=10,800. This tests converting a percentage into an affected-people count — and, in a strong candidate's answer, knowing that population is largely people whose script failed to load rather than a fixed group of holdouts, making the case about robustness rather than accommodating a minority.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims a 4-percentage-point completion improvement on 1.2 million annual starts is worth £5.76 million a year. Ask for the implied value per additional completed application.

- A) £4.80
- B) £48
- C) £120
- D) £96

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 0.04×1,200,000=48,000 additional completions; £5,760,000÷48,000=£120. This tests reconstructing an implied unit value in seconds to check a business case's plausibility — a specific, answerable challenge ("so each additional completion is valued at £120 — is that the right order?") that accuses nobody.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A dashboard reports "average time to complete: 12 minutes" for a service the candidate's evidence shows most users take over forty minutes across several sittings to complete. Ask for the likely explanation and the right intervention.

- A) The dashboard is wrong and should be corrected.
- B) The metric almost certainly measures single sessions rather than end-to-end journeys, so multi-sitting completions are excluded or counted only for their final short visit, and abandoned sessions may be included — ask for the definition and the distribution, and propose an end-to-end measure alongside it.
- C) The research sample was atypical.
- D) The figures cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Session-based analytics and journey-based reality diverge systematically for any multi-sitting service, always flattering the service. This tests naming the mechanism and proposing a constructive intervention rather than picking a fight over a figure that is probably calculated correctly (A) — asking for a definition is not an accusation.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A team proposes measuring whether a redesign improved completion using 40 moderated sessions before and 40 after; baseline is 58%, a 6-point improvement considered meaningful. Ask for the strongest response.

- A) Agree; 80 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 6 point difference on 40 per group is about two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate through live analytics across the full user base, and spending the 80 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 60 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Forty per group cannot distinguish a six-point difference from chance. This tests redirecting to the right instrument for each question rather than merely objecting (C moves towards adequacy without reaching it) — option D is the most damaging, since caveated findings from underpowered comparisons get cited without their caveats, and an organisation that accepts one such comparison will be arguing against the precedent for years.

**Question 12 (harder) — Two metrics moving apart**

Time on a form page fell from 15 to 10 minutes after a redesign; contact centre calls about the service rose 28% over the same period, and completion was unchanged. Ask for the strongest analysis.

- A) The redesign succeeded on its metric; the calls need separate investigation.
- B) The pattern is consistent with content having been removed to shorten the page, pushing users to telephone for the explanation — an apparent efficiency that is probably a cost transfer, with unchanged completion suggesting no benefit to users; the right measure is end-to-end effort including contact.
- C) The call increase must have another cause.
- D) Report both without interpretation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three facts fit together, pointing at cost transfer with unchanged completion as the decisive detail. This tests naming the mechanism and the measurement error beneath it — option A accepts a metric because it was agreed, how services get locally optimised into worse wholes.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate identifies the correct denominator and questions whether compared figures are defined comparably before aggregating.
- Note whether the candidate reconstructs an implied unit value to sanity-check a business case's plausibility.
- Watch for whether the candidate insists on disaggregation by mode of operation (mouse, keyboard, screen reader) rather than accepting a single blended rate.
- Score for whether the candidate distinguishes percentage points from a relative percentage change and flags an ambiguous label rather than merely recalculating.
- Note whether the candidate assesses whether a proposed sample size, or a metric's own definition, can plausibly support the conclusion being drawn from it.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting an average of rates that is not weighted by the underlying counts.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a reported aggregate that could and should be disaggregated.
- Rewarding an underpowered comparison reported "with caveats" as an adequate answer.
- Accepting figures aggregated across studies or sources without checking they are comparably defined.
- Missing when a candidate accepts a metric's face value without asking what population or session definition it actually measures.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately a candidate reads. In a senior role that runs on documents — standards, accessibility regulations, component specifications, technical constraints, policy submissions, contracts — reading accuracy is the substrate everything else rests on. A misread criterion becomes a compliance failure across a programme; a misread finding in a colleague's report becomes advice given confidently to a programme director.

The classic format presents a short passage followed by statements to mark True, False, or Cannot Say. The governing rule is answering from the passage alone, and expertise actively costs marks at this level — the more accessibility criteria and component specifications a candidate has read, the more readily they supply the clause they expect. Cannot Say is the answer experienced practitioners avoid most, since forming a view under incomplete information is what the job consists of.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "Consider whether" is not "recommend". "Solely", "entirely", "unless" and "provided that" govern everything around them.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**: ensuring a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**: analysing and clearly explaining complex evidence.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**: clearly explaining complex problems and ideas to teams or stakeholders outside design.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**: using constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**: noticing a specification never mentions keyboard operation has a consequence.
- **Documenting risks and unresolved issues accurately** maps to **Design communication**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4.* "All new public-facing services must use components from the Design System where a suitable component exists, unless an exemption has been agreed in writing by the head of interaction design. Where no suitable component exists, teams may create a new one, provided it is documented and tested with assistive technology before use in a live service. Components created before this policy took effect must be reviewed within 12 months. Teams are encouraged, but not required, to contribute new components back to the Design System."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: "Every new public-facing service must use Design System components where a suitable one exists."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception — a written exemption agreed by the head of interaction design. This tests catching the absolute-term-versus-exception pattern; note the passage has two distinct mechanisms easily conflated: an exemption (removing the requirement) and the create-a-new-component route (handling absence of a suitable component while the general obligation remains).

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: "Teams must contribute new components back to the Design System."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says teams are "encouraged, but not required" to contribute back. This tests catching a soft aspiration sitting alongside hard requirements (documentation, testing, review) in the same passage, where reading for general tone produces errors in both directions.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: "A component created three years ago must have been reviewed by now."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The 12-month deadline is relative to when the policy took effect, which the passage never states. This tests checking whether a deadline's reference point is actually given — its absence recurs across this section.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: "A team that creates a new component because no suitable Design System component exists needs a written exemption."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies to the requirement to use Design System components "where a suitable component exists"; where none exists, that requirement is not engaged, and a distinct route applies. This tests separating two mechanisms that look similar and do different things — teams frequently seek exemptions they do not need, creating a queue that makes the process look like an obstacle.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8.* "The evaluation of the new autocomplete component found that users completed address entry more quickly than with the previous free-text field, and made fewer formatting errors. The evaluation notes that participants were recruited through the departmental panel and all used a mouse. It did not assess performance with assistive technology or on touch devices. It recommends that the programme consider whether the component should be adopted more widely."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: "Participants made fewer formatting errors with the autocomplete than with the free-text field."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, comparing against the previous free-text field specifically. A claim the component "produces few formatting errors" generally would be different and unsupported.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: "The autocomplete component performs well with assistive technology."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states directly that assistive technology performance was not assessed. This tests resisting a professional suspicion — autocomplete components frequently fail with assistive technology — the passage supports neither True nor False, and marking False would substitute expertise for evidence.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: "The component is suitable for wider adoption."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports favourable findings under specific, favourable conditions, flags two untested modes precisely those that would matter for wide adoption, and recommends only "consider whether." This tests recognising that testing in the single most favourable condition available does not establish general suitability.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: "The evaluation recommends adopting the component more widely."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evaluation recommends the programme "consider whether" wider adoption is appropriate, including concluding it should not be. This tests distinguishing "consider whether X" from "recommend X" — especially costly for a component, since rolling one out widely is far easier than rolling it back.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12.* "Interactive components must be operable using a keyboard alone. Where a component uses a non-standard interaction, the team must provide a documented keyboard alternative. Focus must be visible at all times and must move in an order consistent with the visual presentation. Where a component displays an error, the error must be programmatically associated with the field it relates to; a visual association alone is not sufficient. These requirements apply to public-facing services and to internal tools used by more than fifty staff."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: "A visual association between an error and its field is sufficient."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states directly that a visual association alone is not sufficient. This tests catching the load-bearing word "programmatically" — a message beside a field is obvious to a sighted user and may be absent for a screen reader user encountering fields in markup order.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: "These requirements apply to an internal tool used by fifteen staff."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The threshold is "more than fifty staff"; the passage states where the requirements apply, not where they are excluded. This tests distinguishing stating where a rule applies from stating where it does not — a reader converting the first into the second confidently tells a team they are exempt when nobody has said so.

**Question 11 (harder)**

Statement: "A component whose focus order differs from its visual presentation breaches the requirements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage requires focus to move in an order consistent with the visual presentation, unconditionally. This tests distinguishing an unconditional requirement (a departure is a breach) from a scoping statement (Question 10, where silence leaves the case undetermined) — the first supports a finding and the second does not.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Ask which best captures the main point of Passage C.

- A) Components must never use non-standard interactions.
- B) Components in public services and larger internal tools must remain operable and comprehensible for someone not using a mouse or not seeing the screen — through keyboard operation, visible focus in a consistent order, documented alternatives for unusual interactions, and errors associated in code rather than only visually.
- C) Focus must be visible at all times.
- D) Errors must be programmatically associated with their fields.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests identifying a summary spanning the whole passage, including its scope clause — A overstates a conditional requirement into a prohibition, and C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest, including scope, which matters since a summary omitting it implies the requirements are universal.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate reads to the end of a passage before concluding what a provision requires, resisting the pull of their own professional expertise.
- Note whether the candidate distinguishes an explicit exemption route from a distinct alternative route, where the two look similar and do different things.
- Watch for whether the candidate distinguishes an unconditional requirement from a scoping statement.
- Score for whether declared gaps in what was assessed are treated as genuine limits on what can be concluded, even where professional suspicion points elsewhere.
- Note whether a main-point item is answered with a summary covering the whole passage, including scope, rather than one true-but-narrow detail.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a statement marked False because the candidate suspects the untested result, rather than because the passage states it.
- Missing when a candidate conflates an exemption route with a distinct alternative route.
- Rewarding "consider whether X" read as "recommends X."
- Accepting a scope statement converted into an exemption for a case the passage does not address.
- Missing when a candidate drops the scope clause from a main-point or summary answer.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what a candidate would do — a dilemma of the kind this role produces, four or five possible responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether a candidate's judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at this level. A senior interaction designer works with minimal support, influences and mentors others, works with service managers and programme directors, may carry responsibility across complex services, helps set direction and embed good practice, and makes important decisions based on research while understanding how that research affects others. The strongest answers involve independent professional judgement, responsibility for other people's practice as well as a candidate's own, holding positions under genuine organisational pressure, and developing capability rather than solving everything personally.

That last point separates this level from a competent practitioner's more sharply than anything else — an interaction designer who fixes a colleague's flawed component has solved a problem; a senior interaction designer who fixes it without the colleague learning anything has done half the job. Four principles carry most scenarios: users come first, specifically users who cannot get past something; protect the integrity of the evidence and of the people whose job it is to protect it; develop capability while solving the problem; and escalate what genuinely requires authority not held, handling everything else.

### How this assessment maps to the role

- **Building consensus and challenging assumptions** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Advising on inclusive and ethical design** maps to **Designing for everyone**: delivering ethical services considering users' personal and social context.
- **Running and adapting sessions, and coaching others** maps to **Designing together**.
- **Working across profession boundaries** maps to **Designing together**: working with policy and technology teams.
- **Embedding practice and mentoring** maps to **Leading design** and **Iterative design**.
- **Documenting risk honestly** maps to **Design communication**: documenting decisions, related risks and unresolved issues.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A designer under pressure from a director**

A mentee reports that a director has asked them to remove an accessibility finding from a board summary because it "muddies the message," and they are unsure what to do. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Tell them to leave it in and say you said so.
- B) Talk it through so they understand what is at stake, agree together how the finding should be presented — including a constructive framing with mitigation — offer to attend the meeting, and take the request itself up with the director yourself.
- C) Contact the director and instruct them not to make such requests.
- D) Escalate to the head of profession.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the finding survives, and the mentee emerges more capable and less exposed. This tests absorbing a power differential too large for the mentee to hold alone, while building their judgement rather than substituting for it — option A is least effective, leaving them defending a position by proxy and teaching nothing.

**Question 2 (easy) — Embedding practice in a resistant team**

A candidate is asked to embed good interaction design practice in a delivery team that has never worked with a designer, whose delivery manager says design is welcome "as long as it doesn't slow us down." Ask for the strongest first move.

- A) Present the service standard's design requirements.
- B) Find the team's nearest consequential decision, offer to answer that specific question inside their sprint, and get developers and the delivery manager observing a session — so the value is demonstrated in their terms before more is asked.
- C) Ask the programme director to mandate design involvement.
- D) Run a full discovery first.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A team with no reason to believe design repays its cost is convinced by watching a user fail at something they built, not by an explanation. This tests demonstrating value in the team's own terms before asking for more. Option C is least effective, making mandated involvement performed rather than used, and the candidate the director's enforcement mechanism.

**Question 3 (easy) — A session producing one voice**

Twenty minutes into a session with senior stakeholders and frontline staff, two stakeholders dominate and no frontline staff have spoken. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it run; the stakeholders know the service.
- B) Change the structure — write first then round the table, or split into pairs — so quieter participants contribute without anyone being told to stop.
- C) Ask the stakeholders directly to let others speak.
- D) Follow up with the frontline staff separately.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The session is producing one perspective when it was convened for several. This tests adapting a session's format to achieve a useful outcome — option A is least effective, wasting frontline staff's time and producing a design based on how senior people imagine the work is done, particularly costly since caseworkers know exactly which fields get entered wrongly.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Advising on work that is not good enough**

A designer in another team, who does not report to the candidate, has shipped a custom component whose focus behaviour traps keyboard users, now in two services. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Raise it with the services using it.
- B) Speak to the designer and their lead first, describe the defect precisely with a reproduction, agree how it will be fixed and communicated to the services already using it — with a clear next step if they decline.
- C) Escalate to the head of profession.
- D) Advise the two services to stop using the component.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Going to the designer first is not merely courteous — they may know something the candidate does not, and a fix lands better coming from them. This tests including a reproduction (which gets a defect fixed rather than triaged low) and communicating to services already using it. Option A is least effective, bypassing the person who can fix it and starting a conversation about their competence in rooms they are not in.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to predetermine a conclusion**

A programme director says, before work starts, that design work "needs to show the new single-page form will work, because that decision is effectively made." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Focus the work on the form's strengths.
- B) State plainly that the work will report what users experience, and reframe around what they actually need — if the decision is made, the useful questions become who will struggle with a single long page, what support they will need, and what would signal the change is failing.
- C) Agree, then report honestly and handle the fallout.
- D) Refuse and report the conversation.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The request asks the candidate to predetermine a conclusion, which would make the work worthless to everyone including the director. This tests declining the premise without confrontation and redirecting to genuinely useful questions. Option A is least effective, designing to demonstrate a conclusion — a rigged exercise, and rigged exercises surface.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Developing someone whose practice is weak**

A mentee repeatedly ships components that work with a mouse and fail with a keyboard, despite general feedback twice. Ask for the most effective next step.

- A) Review every component they produce before it ships.
- B) Move from general feedback to something structured: sit with them and operate one of their own components using only a keyboard, so they experience the failure rather than being told about it; then agree they will do that check themselves before anything ships, and review the next one together.
- C) Raise it in their performance conversation.
- D) Reassign component work to someone else.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Twice-repeated general feedback failing usually means the person cannot yet see the problem in their own work. This tests using the mechanism specific to this discipline — experiencing a component's own failure — and making the check portable. Option A is least effective, removing the problem from view while guaranteeing it persists and making the candidate a permanent bottleneck.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A technology constraint**

A technology team has selected a component library whose date picker and modal fail the accessibility standard; two services already use it. Ask for the strongest approach.

- A) Require them to stop using the library.
- B) Establish precisely which components fail and how, propose a shortlist to avoid or wrap with compliant alternatives, and a route to remediate the two existing services — framing it as making their choice workable rather than reversing it.
- C) Escalate to the head of profession.
- D) Accept it and document the risk.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A library is almost never uniformly bad — usually a handful of complex components fail. This tests converting an unwinnable argument into a tractable list. Option D is least effective, documenting a risk with nothing following it, how an exclusion becomes permanent while everyone feels it has been handled.

**Question 8 (harder) — An accessibility blocker close to launch**

Two weeks before launch, a custom component is found unable to be operated with a keyboard. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with the delivery manager and service owner, describe precisely what cannot be done and by whom, ask for an explicit recorded decision, and check whether a standard component could replace the custom one within the fortnight.
- C) Attempt the fix yourself.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** An unrecorded decision drifts into launch by default; a recorded one makes the trade-off owned by someone who can own it. This tests securing an explicit decision and naming the practical step most often missed under pressure. Option A is least effective, sending a genuine blocker to a backlog where it will remain unfixed.

**Question 9 (harder) — Evidence against a public commitment**

Evidence shows a publicly announced change will make a service substantially harder for users with motor impairments; colleagues propose presenting this as "areas for future enhancement." Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Accept the framing; the commitment is public.
- B) Report the findings accurately and do the constructive work alongside — quantify who is affected, specify what mitigation would look like, and present finding and mitigation together so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report starkly and let the organisation respond.
- D) Escalate before acting.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The proposed framing protects the organisation from discomfort while the difficulty occurs anyway and nobody owns it. This tests keeping a finding intact and making it actionable with quantification and mitigation attached. Option A is least effective, the precise failure accessibility standards exist to prevent, falling hardest on users least able to absorb it.

**Question 10 (harder) — Consensus that would harm users**

A team the candidate advises has converged on a control everyone likes, which the evidence indicates will fail users on small screens and users with motor impairments; reopening it reverses a decision people are relieved to have made. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Let it go; the team has decided.
- B) Raise it once, clearly and specifically, showing what the evidence indicates will happen to those users and what mitigation might look like — then if the team proceeds, ensure the risk is documented and propose measuring the outcome after release.
- C) Escalate to the service owner over the team's head.
- D) Keep raising it until the team changes course.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This tests threading a professional obligation to say the thing against the need to keep advising the same team — raising once with evidence and a mitigation, then documenting the risk and proposing measurement. Option A is least effective, since consensus harming users who already have fewer options is not a value.

**Question 11 (harder) — A flaw in something you approved**

A pattern approved three months ago, now used in five services, is found to assume errors appear on the same page as the field — breaking in the two services using it across multiple pages. Ask what should happen.

- A) Note it for the next pattern review.
- B) Tell the pattern's users now, scope precisely which services are affected and which are not, propose a fix to the pattern and an interim workaround for the two broken services, and review why the approval did not catch a multi-page case.
- C) Quietly amend the pattern documentation.
- D) Advise the two services to stop using the pattern.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A pattern used in five services means a flaw is potentially five defects, and precise scoping (three services are fine) prevents unnecessary alarm. This tests notifying, scoping, fixing both immediately and durably, and reviewing why the approval process missed the case. Option C is least effective, amending documentation without telling affected services, leaving live defects while creating a record suggesting they were addressed.

**Question 12 (harder) — A recurring problem across teams**

Across three teams advised, custom components keep being built where Design System components would serve, each team with a plausible local reason. Ask for the most effective response.

- A) Review and challenge each proposal as it arises.
- B) Look for the common cause — most likely that the Design System components are genuinely missing something, or that teams do not know they exist, or that adopting them is harder than building fresh — then address whichever it is, and propose the corresponding change: contributing the missing variant, improving discoverability, or reducing adoption friction.
- C) Introduce a rule requiring approval for any custom component.
- D) Accept it; teams know their own needs.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Three teams doing the same thing for plausible local reasons is a pattern, and the plausibility of each is what makes case-by-case challenge exhausting. This tests naming the realistic common causes, each implying a different remedy. Option D is least effective, accepting three teams independently rebuilding components as accepting three sets of accessibility defects and three maintenance burdens.

### Administration tips

- Score for whether the candidate develops capability in others alongside solving the immediate problem, rather than solving it alone.
- Note whether the candidate absorbs organisational pressure that should not land on a more junior colleague.
- Watch for whether the candidate escalates only what genuinely requires authority not held, having engaged directly with the person who can resolve it first.
- Score for whether an own or approved flaw is disclosed promptly, with precise scoping of what is and is not affected, and a review of why the process that should have caught it did not.
- Note whether the candidate looks for a common cause behind a pattern repeated across multiple teams rather than treating each instance separately.

### Common pitfalls to watch for when scoring

- Crediting a response where the candidate solves a problem personally without the person or team involved learning anything.
- Missing when a candidate becomes a permanent review bottleneck rather than building a portable check.
- Rewarding a finding softened to accommodate a prior commitment or public announcement.
- Accepting documentation of a risk treated as equivalent to managing it, with no action attached.
- Missing when a candidate corrects a colleague's work by going to its users first rather than to the colleague.

## Conclusion

This guide has worked through pattern recognition and diagnostic auditing of somebody else's evidence and reasoning across pages, components, and prototypes; the arithmetic that supports senior-level design argument — disaggregating a blended rate to find the group it conceals, reconstructing an implied unit value to check a business case, and declining to endorse a comparison too small to detect what it was built to find; the discipline of reading standards, evaluation reports and governance prose at exactly the strength the text supports, especially the distinction between an unconditional requirement and a scoping statement; and the situational judgement that defines a strong senior interaction designer — developing capability alongside solving problems, absorbing pressure that should not land on someone more junior, and acting on the mechanism behind a recurring problem rather than the instance in front of them.

**Scoring and debrief.** Score against the worked explanations, not the answer letter alone — several items reward the quality of reasoning as much as the final choice, and at this level the strongest answers are consistently the ones honest about the limits of the evidence, including where the evidence would support something the candidate wants to be true. Debrief promptly, holding back the answer key until scoring is complete for every candidate sitting this role level.

**Records and consistency.** Administer the same items and the same time allowance to every candidate for this role level, and keep a record of scores and the reasoning behind them. Consistency across candidates is what protects the fairness of the process, and what would let this material stand up to scrutiny if a decision it informed were later questioned.

**One assessment among several.** This material is not validated, piloted or normed, and a result from it must never be the sole basis for a hiring, promotion, pay, performance or disciplinary decision — see the Advisory above. Use it alongside structured interviews, work samples and reference checks, and weigh its results accordingly.
